# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**Resilience** in this context is the capacity to adapt processes while preserving core mission objectives (hospital supply, physical flow) and maintaining or compensating for critical controls. It is characterized by *controlled deviation*: explicit triage, manual verification where systems fail, structured evidence capture, and transparent prioritization. Patterns A, B, F, J, and I (when formalized) demonstrate resilience. They absorb shock, maintain service to critical customers, and keep traceability chains intact through manual proxies.

**Concealed noncompliance** occurs when adaptations sacrifice traceability, authorization, or control integrity for speed or convenience, creating hidden risk that only surfaces as audit tails, rework, or regulatory exposure. Patterns C, G, and unstructured H exemplify this. They preserve throughput but destroy real-time compliance. The DFG shows `Skip system authorization check` and `Dispatch without scan` as high-volume deviations, directly contributing to T2 traceability exceptions (9.5%) and T4 audit remediation queues. Post-hoc remediation (L) does not equal real-time compliance; it merely documents what should have been captured, shifting risk into the future.

**Ambiguous adaptations** (D, E, H, I) depend on risk appetite and formalization. E.g., E maintains flow but hides traceability risk unless paper trails are standardized. I protects capacity but harms non-critical customers unless deferral rules are explicit. The distinction lies in whether the adaptation includes *compensating controls* and *structured evidence*. Resilience = controlled adaptation with traceable proxies. Concealed noncompliance = uncontrolled deviation that creates post-hoc cleanup and elevates exception rates above baseline.

# 4. Recovery process model

**Incident Response Process Tree (POWL-style notation)**

```
[TRIGGER] WMS/system unavailable > 2h OR Cyber Alert Level  2
  [TIER 1: CRITICAL] Hospital-critical + Cold-chain/Controlled
      Manual Authorization Check (B) using offline lists + phone verification
      Rolling Inventory Snapshot + Cycle Count (D)
      Manual Pick List (E) with incident ID & timestamp
      Pharmacist QC + Temp/Security Log (F)
      Dispatch with Manual Scan Log + Phone POD (H)
 
  [TIER 2: STANDARD] Pharmacy-standard
      Manual Authorization Check (B)
      Standard Manual Pick/Pack (E)
      Dispatch with Manual Scan Log + POD (H)
 
  [TIER 3: DEFERRED] Wholesale-bulk / Non-urgent
       Defer noncritical order (I) to backlog queue
       Daily prioritization review (J)

[SYSTEM RESTORATION TRIGGER] WMS sync > 95% + Scanning functional
  [OPERATIONAL RESUME] Return to baseline DFG
  [RECONCILIATION] Batch match manual logs vs system (K)
      Flag discrepancies > 2% threshold
      Auto-route to correction queue
  [AUDIT REMEDIATION] Close compliance gaps (L)
       Validate temp/security logs
       Verify authorization trails
       Archive incident evidence; root-cause analysis
```

**Key Design Principles:**
- **Explicit triggers:** Time-based (2h) + system health metrics (sync %, scan success rate).
- **Triage rules:** Hard-coded tier thresholds; no informal prioritization.
- **Minimum controls:** No shipment without (1) offline auth verification, (2) inventory snapshot + cycle check for high-value, (3) temp/security log for cold/controlled.
- **Manual evidence capture:** Standardized incident kits with unique IDs, timestamps, and dual-signature for high-risk.
- **Backlog prioritization:** Algorithmic queue by customer criticality × product risk × expiry date.
- **Reconciliation & audit flow:** Parallel to operations; automated matching; shift-left evidence capture to eliminate post-hoc tail.

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management must accept temporary throughput reduction, structured manual workarounds, and a bounded post-incident audit tail as the cost of maintaining hospital supply and compliance integrity. What must not be accepted are uncontrolled deviations that bypass authorization (C), ship without traceability (G), or rely on informal prioritization that creates fairness and diversion risks. Post-hoc audit remediation (L) is a necessary cleanup mechanism, not a substitute for real-time compliance; treating it as equivalent will mask true incident risk and inflate regulatory exposure. The recommendation to formalize triage, standardize manual evidence, and automate reconciliation should stand unless evidence shows hospital fill rates dropping below 85%, traceability exceptions exceeding 5% during manual execution, or audit queues growing beyond 10% of incident volume, at which point stricter control enforcement or capacity augmentation must be triggered.
