# 2. Integrated explanation

No single mechanism fits. The observed U-shaped error and monotonic service-time decline reflect a *sequence of dominant mechanisms that hand off to each other, plus confounding*:

**Phase 1 (positions 1-5, ~22 min): Complex carryover + stabilization, not inefficiency.** Each shift inherits a backlog (Early 61, Late 70). Workers, especially Seniors, deliberately select high-complexity/high-priority cases first (complexity 0.72, priority 41%). Service time 18.8 min is therefore long because work is genuinely harder, and includes re-orientation/warm-up. Error 3.4% is moderate, not worst. Interpreting this as “slow start” mistakes selection for learning.

**Phase 2 (6-30, ~74-151 min): Apparent learning is largely case-mix + genuine warm-up.** Service time falls to 15.213.6 min and error to 2.82.5%. Part is real individual warm-up: Seniors/Mid/Juniors all speed up first 20 events even under controlled conditions, and Night staff show same. But ~40% of the decline is explained by falling complexity (0.610.54) and queue easing (3933). Under low WIP, error is flat for Seniors/Mid later (2.12.2%), indicating warm-up improves *speed* more than *quality* for experienced staff; Juniors improve on both (5.14.4%) because early complexity hurts them most.

**Phase 3 (31-50, ~257 min mid-shift): Plateau under favorable conditions.** Complexity lowest-so-far (0.50), queue low (31), priority 25%. This is the system’s “sweet spot”: service time 12.9 min, errors 2.7% stable. True steady-state performance without strong pressure or circadian penalty. Differences by experience persist but are narrow.

**Phase 4 (51-76+, ~378-459 min): Queue-pressure-driven speed–quality tradeoff, amplified by fatigue, batching, and automation over-reliance, masked as learning.** WIP surges to 6288, especially Day shift (3875). Mean service time *continues* to fall to 11.410.8 min but errors *double* to 4.67.9% (rework 8.213.7%). The continued fall is *not* learning – complexity is lowest (0.460.43), so cases *should* be faster, but stratified tables show even *within low complexity* high WIP cuts time 12.79.4 min and raises error 2.15.8%; within high complexity 22.416.8 min, error 4.210.9%. This is effort withdrawal / corner-cutting under pressure. Fatigue contributes *conditionally*: under low WIP late errors stay low, under high WIP they explode (Seniors 2.24.9%, Juniors 4.412.4%), suggesting fatigue *lowers resistance* to pressure rather than directly causing errors. Batching (Juniors, Document Review) and deferred Quality Check bursts in last 45 min artificially compress service times and defer error detection to rework. Bot_Risk corrections rise under WIP>70, indicating verification is skipped when busy.

**Circadian and shift structure** shape *when* pressure occurs rather than causing the error directly. Night has high complexity share but low throughput; its slower late service (15.2 min) shows circadian caution. Day/Late inherit and generate WIP; Early clears backlog. Without controlling for hour and shift, time-on-task effects are confounded.

Therefore **service time falls throughout because complexity falls *and* queue pressure rewards speed, while error is U-shaped because early complexity and late pressure both elevate risk, with a low-error window only when complexity, WIP, and fatigue are jointly low**. Ignoring either selection (early) or pressure (late) yields the misleading “learning curve then fatigue” story.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Worker-level conclusions are unfair without risk adjustment.** Raw late-shift error rates (7.9% at 76+, 7.5% Day late) reflect system conditions, not individual competence. Juniors’ 12.4% high-WIP late error vs Seniors’ 4.9% is shown *only* under high WIP; under low WIP late error is 4.4% vs 2.2% – narrower. Punishing based on raw rates punishes those scheduled into high-pressure windows and those assigned complex backlog (Early, Night). Night staff handle more urgent exceptions (56% high-complexity first hour) – higher errors are expected.

**Selection bias** contaminates performance ranking. Seniors self-select or are assigned complex cases early; faster late workers get easier cases; batching creates artificial productivity leaders (Junior_H/I). Without random or adjusted comparisons, “top performer” may be the worker who avoided complex work or batched superficial reviews. Using `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, `activity_family` for adjustment is essential; otherwise rewards cherry-picking.

**Measurement error** distorts service time and error. Service time defined as `time:timestamp - start_timestamp` depends on correct start logging; batching and queue-ownership changes can make start times appear short. `error_flag` is human-labeled, likely under-reported under pressure (more errors *and* less reporting). Quality Check is delayed to shift end, so errors are detected late or by next shift – attribution to originating resource is noisy. Rework within 7 days is a lagging, downstream proxy. Do not treat flags as objective ground truth.

**Queue pressure is management-created.** WIP 88 and Day shift 3875 buildup reflect staffing, arrival management, and backlog carryover decisions, not worker slacking. Workers speeding to 9.4 min under high WIP are responding rationally to implicit throughput incentives. Holding workers accountable for errors created by WIP >70 while penalizing them for slower service creates a double bind. Ethical response is to fix system load, not individual output.

**Automation quality and automation bias.** Bot_Risk instantaneous classification tempts over-trust when busy; correction rate rises at WIP>70. If bot degrades with drift or is trained on low-WIP data, humans are set up to fail. Monitoring must include bot accuracy, not just human correction rate, and ensure human verification time is protected in high WIP.

**Punitive use of error rates causes harm.** Penalties incentivize gaming (deferring complex cases, batching superficial work, suppressing error flags, deferring quality checks), increase stress and turnover, and obscure true improvement. It also violates fairness given unequal experience (Juniors need support, not penalties) and circadian vulnerability. Use risk-adjusted metrics for learning, coaching, and system redesign; never for pay or discipline without due process and WIP/complexity adjustment.

**Operational caution:** Do not optimize a single metric. Cutting service time without guardrails (e.g., removing Quality Check) improves throughput but increases rework, customer harm, and total cost. Any intervention must track balanced scorecard: speed, quality, waiting, rework, and fairness.
