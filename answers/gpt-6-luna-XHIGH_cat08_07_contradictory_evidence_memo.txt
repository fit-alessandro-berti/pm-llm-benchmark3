# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive leadership  
**Decision:** Adopt a guarded hybrid (E): retain coordinator ownership and the merged validation/scheduling path for simple, low-risk houses; do not scale Redesign R nationally. Suspend survey skipping and any billing start before a successful activation test while running a risk-stratified regional experiment and improving measurement.

The pilot shows a real speed and handover benefit for simple houses: median cycle time fell 3.4 days overall, handovers of three or more fell from 34% to 12%, and simple-house first-time-right performance improved. Those results support keeping the parts of R that remove avoidable coordination for that segment. They do not justify applying the same path to all customers. Overall p90/p95 duration, first-time-right rate, complaints, second visits, manual activation, and SLA breaches worsened. Visible cost fell, while the incomplete estimate including rework and complaints rose. The deterioration is concentrated in apartments and permit-sensitive older buildings, and the pilot regions had a less favorable building mix than baseline.

The process-mining results reinforce caution but do not by themselves establish causation: high fitness means the discovered model can replay many pilot traces; low precision means it permits many behaviors beyond those actually observed. Alignments and DFG changes highlight billing before successful activation, missing permit checks, more failed and repeat visits, and more complaint escalation after billing. These are operational signals to control and investigate, not proof that every case followed the same failure mechanism.

Keep surveys and required permit checks for higher-risk addresses, require successful activation before billing, and create an approved exception path with monitoring. Relieve coordinator bottlenecks with capacity limits, shared case notes, and backup coverage. Compare survey skipping against the standard path only in a controlled, risk-stratified trial after the predictive rule is validated. Capture full downstream cost and outcome data before expanding.

This preserves the demonstrated simple-house benefit while limiting exposure to the worsening tail, rework, customer harm, and activation-control failures. Broader rollout should require adjusted, segment-level evidence that tail time, first-time-right, complaints, full cost, and required process controls meet predeclared guardrails, without recreating coordinator overload.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision:** Fitness measures how well the discovered model can replay the observed pilot traces. It can be high when many traces fit at least one allowed path. Precision penalizes a model that permits many paths unsupported by observed behavior. The pilot’s 0.94 fitness with 0.51 precision, low generalization, and alignments around skipped surveys, manual activation, and billing are consistent with a flexible model that accommodates observed behavior without describing a tight, reliable process. Neither score alone proves process quality or redesign success.
- **Improved median but worse tail:** The median describes the middle case, while p90/p95 expose delays among the slowest cases. Simple houses improved substantially, but apartment, permit-sensitive, business, and rural cases show worse tails. A quick standard path for many cases can lower the median even as rework, permits, repeat visits, or capacity constraints leave a smaller group waiting much longer.
- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Reducing transfers can make routine ownership clearer. It can also centralize context in coordinators. At 93% utilization, an absent coordinator can leave work stalled if knowledge is not shared. A simpler handover network therefore does not guarantee resilient collaboration or continuity.
- **Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost:** The visible-cost measure excludes some complaint handling and is measured before later downstream costs. Fewer surveys or less coordination may lower the immediate cost per case while increasing second visits, manual exceptions, complaints, and billing disputes. The estimated full cost is already higher, but remains incomplete, so neither estimate settles lifecycle economics.
- **Simple-house gains but apartment/old-building deterioration:** The merged validation/scheduling path appears to suit simple, low-risk houses; the survey-skip rule is often wrong for medium-risk apartments, and permit-sensitive buildings need checks the simplified path may omit. The pilot also has more apartments and older buildings than baseline. Segment results support differentiated policies, but incomplete early risk data and regional mix prevent attributing all differences to R.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | When reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures the simple-case speed and handover benefits quickly; standardizes coordinator ownership. | Exposes all segments to worse tail time, rework, complaints, manual activation, and possible missing permit/activation controls; coordinator and second-visit capacity are already strained. | Only after risk-adjusted, multi-region evidence shows acceptable tail, service quality, full cost, control compliance, and operational capacity. Current evidence does not meet that bar. |
| **B. Roll back completely** | Restores familiar surveys, activation sequence, and distributed ownership; avoids broad exposure to current failure modes. | Discards demonstrated gains for simple houses and may restore unnecessary handovers and slower appointments; does not establish which R components caused harm. | Reasonable if controls cannot be enforced immediately, serious customer/control failures continue, or a safe targeted path cannot be operated during the experiment. |
| **C. Keep coordinator ownership, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation** | Preserves a plausible ownership/coordination gain while removing the clearest risky process changes. | May apply coordinator ownership too broadly; high utilization and absence-related stalls may persist. It does not independently validate skipping or solve segment-specific process needs. | Reasonable as a short-term containment policy where implementation must be simple and the organization can add backup coverage and workload limits. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better risk measurement** | Produces a more credible comparison, measures case mix, and can isolate effects by risk stratum before commitment. | Delays broader benefit; poorly designed randomization or weak safeguards could expose high-risk customers; needs adequate sample and clean outcome capture. | Reasonable for testing survey skipping and component effects, with activation/permit guardrails, risk stratification, and sufficient follow-up. |
| **E. Hybrid: use R for simple houses, add controls for higher-risk cases, measure before wider scale** | Retains the clearest segment benefit, limits exposure where outcomes deteriorated, and creates a practical path to learn. | Depends on reliable risk classification, execution of controls, and enough capacity for exceptions; misclassification can still harm customers. | Best fit to current evidence if simple-house eligibility is conservative, controls are auditable, and uncertain segments remain under standard safeguards pending trials. |

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by how directly the result could change the rollout decision, while accounting for the immediate need to control process deviations:

1. **M7 — Conformance monitoring for billing before activation and missing permit checks.** Establishes frequency, segment, and persistence of the most consequential process deviations. If common, it supports keeping the affected path paused and triggering remediation; if rare and valid exceptions are documented, targeted rollout may be safer. Monitoring should start immediately.
2. **M1 — Randomized survey-skipping pilot by building-risk stratum.** Directly estimates whether skipping surveys causes net benefit or harm in each risk group. A benefit confined to demonstrably low-risk cases could support a narrow rule; excess rework or tail delay in any stratum would argue against skipping there.
3. **M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.** Quantifies misclassification and whether the rule can safely identify cases that do not need a survey. Poor predictive performance would rule out using it for eligibility; strong calibrated performance would inform M1 and a future controlled rollout.
4. **M4 — Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing.** Resolves whether immediate savings survive complaint and revisit costs. Lower complete cost could strengthen the case for targeted scale; higher cost would favor redesigning or stopping the costly paths.
5. **M3 — Structured reasons for failed visits, manual activation, complaints, and second visits.** Identifies mechanisms behind worsening quality and whether they are preventable, segment-specific, or unrelated to R. It can redirect intervention toward staffing, access/appointment issues, activation controls, or building risk.
6. **M2 — Start timestamps for installation, activation, and manual activation.** Separates waiting from work time and locates the source of apartment activation delays and manual work. If the bottleneck is capacity rather than survey policy, management may change staffing or sequencing instead of rolling back the whole design.
7. **M5 — Compare coordinator ownership with shared-team ownership during coordinator absence.** Tests whether central ownership creates avoidable stalls and whether backup/shared context restores resilience. A robust coverage design could make coordinator ownership scalable; continued stalls would limit it.
8. **M8 — Separate customer experience for appointment speed and working service after installation.** Clarifies the trade-off hidden in aggregate NPS and complaint measures. Better appointment experience paired with poor working-service experience would support keeping speed improvements only where activation quality is protected.

These are complementary: start M7 as an operating control; design M1 with M6’s risk validation and collect M2/M3/M4/M8 outcomes in the same trial where feasible. M5 can be evaluated alongside rollout readiness for coordinator ownership.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: E, supported by a controlled regional experiment (D).**

- **Immediate action:** Keep coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule only for conservatively identified simple, low-risk houses. Require documented permits where required and a successful activation test before billing. Add case notes and backup ownership so an absent coordinator does not stop a case. Monitor second-visit capacity and coordinator workload.
- **Pause:** Suspend survey skipping for apartments, medium-risk addresses, old or permit-sensitive buildings, and any segment without validated risk classification. Suspend post-billing activation as a routine path; permit any genuine exception only through a documented, monitored approval process. Do not begin national rollout.
- **Scale now:** The simple-house path only, with eligibility checks, capacity monitoring, and a rollback trigger if service quality or tail time deteriorates.
- **Measure next:** Start M7 monitoring immediately. Validate the risk rule (M6), run M1 by risk stratum with standard-process controls, and capture full 60-day cost (M4), reason codes (M3), stage start times (M2), and segment-specific customer experience (M8). Test absence coverage under M5 before expanding coordinator ownership.
- **Broader-rollout gate:** Before each segment expands, require a sufficiently powered, risk-adjusted comparison across multiple regions with predeclared non-inferiority margins. As proposed management guardrails, require no increase versus baseline in that segment’s p90/p95, first-time-right failures, complaints, or full downstream cost beyond the agreed margins; all required permits and activation-before-billing controls must be met; and staffing/absence tests must show sustainable capacity. The simple-house benefit should persist. If these conditions fail for a segment, retain its safeguarded process and revise the design rather than extending R to it.
