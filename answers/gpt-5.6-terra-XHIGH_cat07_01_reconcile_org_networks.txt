# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

## Handover-of-work network

A handover-of-work network records successive events in the same case performed by different resources. For Mira, the high out-degree (0.82), high betweenness (0.77), and prominent flows from `Bot_Rules` to her and from her to adjusters and specialists indicate that she occupies an important routing position in the observed event sequence.

The strongest interpretation is that Mira is an intermediary in exception-oriented work: the rules engine routes cases to her, she sends work to medical, legal, or adjuster resources, and specialist results often return to her. Her prominence is especially meaningful because she appears in only 22% of all claims but in 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints.

This does **not** by itself establish that she is a formal manager, that she makes all substantive decisions, or that she causes delay. A high handover score can arise from efficient dispatching as well as from queueing, rework, or system-generated routing events. The elapsed time on an edge also includes waiting for the next resource, not just Mira’s own work. For example, waits involving `Vendor_Verify`, `Vendor_Pay`, medical, and legal review may be driven by external or specialist capacity rather than by the coordinator.

## Working-together network

A working-together network measures resource co-occurrence in the same cases. It captures durable case-level collaboration or membership in recurring work groups better than it captures brief routing interventions.

Mira’s low weighted degree (0.16) and low pairwise co-case rates are consistent with her appearing briefly in many different exception cases rather than repeatedly working alongside a stable group. By contrast, the Alice–Carla, Alice–Elise, Bruno–Diego, and Bruno–Farid pairs look like relatively stable North and South operating clusters.

Thus, Mira’s peripheral position in this network does **not** contradict her handover centrality. She can be structurally central as a short-lived bridge between groups while remaining peripheral in durable co-case communities. Her handovers are spread across multiple destination groups: medical, legal, several adjusters, both team leads, and temporary staff. No one of those pairings needs to be frequent enough to make her a core member of a single working-together cluster.

Working-together results also do **not** prove that Mira has weak influence, poor relationships, or little operational value. The measure does not observe off-log consultation, queue review, messaging, policy interpretation, or decisions implemented by another person.

## Subcontracting network

With \(n=2\), the subcontracting pattern identifies sequences in which resource A performs work, resource B appears, and A appears again within two later events. For Mira, the recurring Mira  specialist  Mira patterns are strong evidence of a “dispatch, obtain expert input, resume coordination” structure.

The medical and legal fragments are particularly informative:

- Mira  Omar  Mira: missing-information or medical-exception coordination.
- Mira  Priya  Mira: complaint/legal escalation followed by reopening or reassignment.

This makes Mira look like a coordinator of a return loop rather than simply a sender of work. It also supports the view that specialists may be consulted for bounded expertise while Mira retains responsibility for the next operational step.

However, “subcontracting” is a process-mining pattern name, not evidence of contractual subcontracting, managerial authority, or inappropriate delegation. A Mira–specialist–Mira sequence could reflect legitimate review, rework, a required control step, event-ordering artifacts, or a shared queue. It should be interpreted alongside activity labels, lifecycle semantics, and audit data.

## Performance/time statistics

Mira’s median recorded service time is short (4.2 minutes), while her case-cycle-time contribution is high variance. This is compatible with short coordination actions that trigger long waits elsewhere. It does not support a simple claim that she personally performs lengthy work.

Several indicators suggest that her work is disproportionately associated with difficult cases:

- 48% of her cases have `exception_flag=True`.
- She is heavily represented in reopened claims and complaints.
- Medical and legal reviews have long service times and high exception shares.
- The absence period was associated with a rise in the 90th percentile cycle time for exception cases from 8.4 to 13.9 days, while ordinary low-value claims were unchanged.

The absence evidence is important but not fully causal proof. The six-day period may also have had unusual incoming work, specialist capacity changes, vendor delays, or unobserved seasonal effects. Still, the contrast between ordinary and exception claims makes an exception-specific coordination dependency plausible.

The automated and vendor resources are essential context. `Bot_Ingest` and `Bot_Rules` create large volumes of short, deterministic transitions; they can amplify apparent routing centrality. `Vendor_Verify` and `Vendor_Pay` have substantial waiting-time components, so long claim duration should not automatically be attributed to human coordination.

## Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity

Activity-based similarity and organizational-role discovery would likely separate at least four broad profiles:

1. Automated intake/routing: `Bot_Ingest`, `Bot_Rules`.
2. Team-based coordination and approval: Alice and Bruno.
3. Assessment and negotiation: adjusters.
4. Exception coordination and specialist routing: Mira, potentially as a distinctive singleton role.

Mira’s activity mix—missing-information handling, reopening, complaints, and specialist assignment—is materially different from the team leads’ mix of triage and settlement approval, and from the specialists’ substantive review activities. This supports a role distinction even if her formal job title is disputed.

It does **not** prove that the discovered role is official, appropriately designed, or consistently performed. Also, the data contains an activity-label concern: `Assign Specialist` appears in Mira’s profile and sequence fragments but is absent from the stated activity list. That discrepancy should be reconciled before drawing strong conclusions about responsibility boundaries.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

The hypotheses are not mutually exclusive. Mira may perform elements of several roles.

## Hypothesis A: Formal coordinator / exception desk

**Mechanism.** Mira is an intentionally designed exception-desk resource. Automated rules send non-standard cases to her; she requests clarification, selects the appropriate operational path, and coordinates returns from specialists.

**Evidence supporting it.**

- `Bot_Rules  Mira_Coordinator` is a major handover edge.
- Mira has high involvement in exceptions, reopened claims, and complaints.
- Her recorded activities are operationally consistent with coordination: missing information, reopening, escalation, and specialist assignment.
- Her outbound handovers are distributed across medical, legal, and adjuster resources.
- Her absence affected exception-case tail performance but not routine low-value claims.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.**

- The process owner explicitly denies that she is a “real team lead,” which may mean she lacks formal supervisory authority.
- The log does not show a formal assignment policy, queue ownership field, authorization matrix, or decision rights.
- Some routing may be automated or prompted by rules, with Mira merely recording the result.

**Observable predictions in future event logs.**

- Exception cases should show a repeatable pattern: rules engine  Mira  designated specialist/adjuster.
- Mira’s routing destinations should follow documented attributes such as case type, region, amount band, priority, and exception type.
- During planned coverage by another coordinator, the substitute should reproduce similar routing patterns and exception cycle times.
- Her activity profile should remain distinct from both team leads and adjusters.

## Hypothesis B: Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

**Mechanism.** Mira may be the only person with enough tacit knowledge, authority, or system access to resolve exceptions. Cases may wait for her review or for her decision on the next destination.

**Evidence supporting it.**

- Her handover and subcontracting betweenness are the highest among human resources.
- She touches a large share of reopened claims and complaints despite touching only 22% of all claims.
- Exception-case 90th percentile cycle time worsened materially during her absence.
- Specialist return loops frequently pass back through her rather than directly to team leads or adjusters.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.**

- Her short median service time suggests that her own effort is not necessarily the direct source of long delays.
- The observed delay may instead be due to specialist capacity, `Vendor_Verify`, `Vendor_Pay`, incoming work mix, or backlog already present before her absence.
- High centrality can reflect a deliberately efficient single dispatch point rather than a capacity-constrained bottleneck.
- There is no direct queue-length, arrival-rate, availability, or workload-capacity evidence.

**Observable predictions in future event logs.**

- Waiting time from `Bot_Rules` to the next meaningful action should rise when Mira is unavailable or overloaded.
- Cases assigned to an identified backup should have either longer waits, different routing patterns, or greater rework if the function depends on tacit knowledge.
- Mira’s queue age, work-in-progress, and incoming exception rate should predict later exception delays after controlling for case complexity.
- If she is a true bottleneck, adding qualified backup capacity should reduce queueing without increasing rework or escalation rates.

## Hypothesis C: Organizational boundary spanner or triage broker

**Mechanism.** Mira bridges organizational silos: automated intake, North and South teams, temporary staff, medical, legal, and possibly vendor-related exception handling. Her value lies in knowing where difficult work should go, rather than in owning the substantive work.

**Evidence supporting it.**

- She connects `Bot_Rules` to multiple human destination groups.
- She routes to both senior and junior adjusters, not only to one team.
- She has strong return-loop patterns with both medical and legal specialists.
- Her low working-together centrality is exactly what can be expected from a broker who interacts briefly across many groups rather than remaining embedded in one group.
- Adjusters’ description of her as “the person who knows where hard cases should go” fits this mechanism.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.**

- The handover graph could reflect a codified routing table rather than personal boundary-spanning knowledge.
- Her low co-case rate might also result from sparse event logging rather than a genuine broker role.
- The team leads still have meaningful cross-stage roles in routing, approvals, and payment release.

**Observable predictions in future event logs.**

- Mira’s routing destination entropy should be higher than that of team leads and adjusters, especially for exception cases.
- Her cases should cross organizational or activity boundaries more often than routine claims.
- After controlling for case attributes, she should reduce inappropriate specialist referrals or repeated reassignment relative to a purely rules-based route.
- If a policy change replaces her judgment with routing rules, her brokerage centrality should decline and direct bot-to-specialist or bot-to-team flows should increase.

## Hypothesis D: Quality gatekeeper or control point

**Mechanism.** Mira may act as an informal quality or compliance gate: identifying incomplete evidence, fraud-related ambiguity, complaints, or reconsideration conditions before sending work onward.

**Evidence supporting it.**

- Her activity profile includes missing-information requests, reopenings, and complaint escalation.
- `Bot_Rules  Mira  Bot_Rules` patterns after fraud-screen updates suggest interaction between automated screening and manual exception treatment.
- Her cases are exception-heavy.
- Medical and legal work returns to her, suggesting that expert conclusions may require controlled operational follow-up.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.**

- The log does not establish whether she detects issues, merely receives cases already flagged by automation, or performs clerical routing.
- There is no outcome evidence such as reduced payment errors, complaint substantiation, fraud findings, overturned decisions, or audit findings.
- The available data does not show whether her actions are required controls or informal workarounds.

**Observable predictions in future event logs.**

- Cases routed through Mira should have clear reason codes and structured evidence for the exception type.
- After risk-adjustment, her involvement may be associated with fewer later reopenings, fewer invalid specialist referrals, or fewer post-payment corrections if she is an effective gatekeeper.
- Audit review should find a traceable basis for each specialist-selection and reopening action.
- If she is only a recorder of automated decisions, decision codes and destination choices should closely match the rules-engine recommendation.

## Hypothesis E: Data or workflow artifact / automation monitor

**Mechanism.** Some of Mira’s apparent centrality could be caused by the way the case-management system records queue routing, task reassignment, shared inbox ownership, or bot-triggered updates under her resource identifier.

**Evidence supporting it.**

- Her events are short and often appear as routing, assignment, reopen, or clarification actions.
- She may appear immediately after `Bot_Rules` and then disappear for several events.
- The `Assign Specialist` activity-label inconsistency suggests possible data-model or extraction ambiguity.
- High event-sequence centrality can result from system-mediated assignment events rather than substantive coordination.

**Evidence contradicting or limiting it.**

- The absence period has an operationally meaningful pattern specifically for exception claims.
- The specialist return loops and broad set of downstream destinations are consistent with real coordination, not merely a static system account.
- Mira’s activities include judgment-adjacent work such as complaint escalation and reopening.

**Observable predictions in future event logs.**

- Mira’s events may cluster in timestamp bursts, occur at implausibly high concurrency, or align with batch jobs if they are system-generated.
- Her resource identifier may be shared by multiple staff or automation accounts.
- Detailed audit logs may show that another user, rule, or API actually initiated the routing decision.
- After separating manual from system-generated event sources, her human centrality may be lower than the current resource-level graph suggests.

# 3. Risk assessment

## Bottleneck risk

There is a credible exception-specific bottleneck risk. Mira is a common bridge after `Bot_Rules`, before specialists, and after specialist responses. The deterioration in exception tail cycle time during her absence is consistent with queue accumulation around that bridge.

The evidence does not yet show whether the constraining factor is Mira’s capacity, her availability, specialist availability, vendor turnaround, or delayed recording. The immediate operational risk is therefore not simply “Mira is slow”; it is that the exception path may have insufficient resilient capacity.

## Coordination risk

Central coordination can reduce inappropriate escalations and specialist overload, as the operations manager suggests. It can also create routing risk if criteria are undocumented, inconsistent, or dependent on informal knowledge.

Potential coordination failures include:

- wrong specialist selection;
- unnecessary reassignment and rework loops;
- delayed return from specialist review to the operational owner;
- unclear ownership after a reopening or complaint;
- loss of context when temporary staff or vendors are involved.

The Mira  specialist  Mira patterns are not inherently problematic. They become concerning if they generate avoidable loops, repeated requests for the same information, or inconsistent outcomes for similar cases.

## Key-person risk

Mira appears to be a potential single point of failure for complex work. This is distinct from a current bottleneck: even if she processes cases quickly, losing her case-routing knowledge or system permissions may impair resilience.

The six-day absence is an early warning signal, not final proof. Management should test substitute coverage, documented routing rules, and escalation ownership before a longer absence, turnover event, or surge in complaints exposes the dependency more severely.

## Compliance and control risk

The compliance concern is legitimate: a person who identifies an issue and chooses the next specialist can exercise discretion over the path of a regulated claim. That discretion may be entirely appropriate, but it should be governed.

There is no direct evidence of misconduct, biased routing, or improper claim outcomes. The present risk is a control-design risk:

- Are exception criteria explicit?
- Is the rationale for selecting medical, legal, adjuster, or other review recorded?
- Are reopening and complaint-escalation decisions authorized and auditable?
- Are there segregation-of-duties requirements between detection, specialist selection, approval, and payment?

The current evidence suggests that Alice and Bruno, rather than Mira, are associated with approval and payment release to `Vendor_Pay`; that separation should be verified rather than assumed.

## Workload fairness risk

Mira routes substantial volumes to Carla, Diego, Elise, and Farid. Uneven allocation may be justified by region, case type, skill, seniority, amount band, or capacity. It may also produce hidden workload concentration, especially if complex exception work is repeatedly sent to the same senior adjusters or specialists.

A fairness assessment should compare assignment rates and outcomes after adjusting for `case_type`, `priority`, `amount_band`, `region`, `channel`, and exception category. Raw counts alone are not sufficient. Temporary staff should also be examined separately because their lower exception share may reflect restricted assignments rather than lower capability.

## Data-quality risk

Several measurement risks could distort the interpretation:

- `Assign Specialist` is referenced in activity distributions and fragments but is absent from the activity list.
- Resource identifiers may represent individuals, shared queues, bots, or vendor systems inconsistently.
- Lifecycle transitions must be verified before treating elapsed time as service time.
- Event timestamps may record system updates rather than actual human action.
- `exception_flag` may be a case-level status, an event-level state, or a status added after the fact.
- Vendor timestamps may represent request and acknowledgement times rather than real external work start and completion.
- Handover and subcontracting measures depend on the event-ordering rule, especially for simultaneous timestamps.

These issues do not invalidate the evidence, but they argue for validation before redesigning the organization.

# 5. Recommendation

## What should be changed immediately

Do not remove Mira or eliminate the exception-coordination function. Instead, make the function resilient and auditable.

1. **Establish named backup coverage.** Create at least one trained backup coordinator and an explicit escalation rota for absences, peaks, and complaint surges.
2. **Document routing criteria.** Define when a case goes to medical, legal, senior adjustment, temporary validation, vendor verification, or a team lead.
3. **Require structured rationale codes.** For specialist assignment, reopening, complaint escalation, and repeated missing-information requests, record the reason, recommended destination, and final destination.
4. **Clarify decision rights.** Distinguish detection, routing, substantive review, approval, payment release, and closure. Confirm that payment and settlement controls remain separated from exception routing where required.
5. **Create an exception queue dashboard.** Include queue age, work-in-progress, overdue items, rework loops, specialist-return delays, and cases without a next owner.

## What should be monitored

Monitor weekly and by case segment:

- exception queue age and 90th percentile cycle time;
- wait time before and after Mira events;
- routing volumes and destination mix by coordinator and backup;
- reopen and complaint outcomes;
- repeat missing-information requests;
- specialist and vendor waiting components;
- case load and complexity-adjusted allocation across adjusters, specialists, temporary staff, and teams;
- differences in outcomes by region, channel, amount band, and priority;
- bot-to-human and human-to-vendor handover patterns.

Monitoring should distinguish a healthy broker role from a harmful bottleneck. The key question is whether coordination reduces rework and inappropriate escalation enough to justify its capacity and control requirements.

## What should not be changed until more evidence is available

Do not conclude that Mira is merely a “queue cleaner,” an inappropriate decision-maker, or the sole cause of exception delays. Do not centralize all claims through her, remove her, or redistribute her work indiscriminately until the event-log validation and matched exception analysis are complete.

If management ultimately replaces the role, the replacement must include: documented routing rules, system-supported case classification, a staffed backup/rotation model, explicit ownership after specialist review, and sufficient specialist capacity. Simply removing Mira would likely remove coordination without replacing it and could increase formal escalations, rework, and exception backlog.

Mira’s event pattern is consistent with an exception-routing function, not ordinary line supervision. She touches relatively few claims overall but a disproportionate share of reopened and complaint cases, connects the rules engine to several human specialists, and often receives work back after review. That produces high handover and return-loop centrality even though she rarely remains on a case long enough to co-occur with any one worker.
