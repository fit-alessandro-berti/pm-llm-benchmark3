# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness here is not whether groups receive identical raw outcomes. It is whether similarly situated applicants face comparable rules, waits, handovers, and decision quality after legitimate complexity is accounted for. The log and tables support assessment on eight dimensions.

**Outcome parity.** Raw outcomes are unequal. Approval is 78% for Group A versus 63% (B), 69% (C), and 58% (D). Appeal rates are 6% versus 13%, 10%, and 16%. Rework is 11% versus 22%, 24%, and 20%. These gaps describe disparate impact, not discrimination. Groups differ in risk band, documentation completeness, channel, language need, accommodation requests, and prior-case history. Parity is a starting diagnostic, not a verdict.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for risk band, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior-case count, region, and month, residual gaps remain: approval −5.5 pp (B), −2.2 pp (C), −3.1 pp (D); cycle time +2.6 d (B), +4.1 d (C), +0.9 d (D); appeal +4.4 pp (B) and +5.7 pp (D). Residual disparity is smaller than raw disparity, so case mix explains a large share of the raw gap. It does not explain all of it. Residual gaps are consistent with leftover process differences, omitted variables, or biased controls; they are not proof of intent.

**Procedural burden.** Groups do not travel the same paths. Group A is concentrated on V1 (42%), the short completeness-to-eligibility path (median 5.8 days). Group B is over-represented on V2 (document request) and V4 (language support). Group C is over-represented on V5 (accommodation review plus supervisor review). Group D is over-represented on V3 (fraud/supervisor) and V6 (appeal). Extra steps can be rights-preserving. They are still burden if they add handovers, repeated evidence requests, and longer queues that similarly complete, similarly risky cases in Group A do not face.

**Timeliness.** Median cycle time is 8.1 days (A) versus 13.7 (B), 15.9 (C), and 12.1 (D). The 90th percentile is 21.4 versus 34.2, 38.8, and 29.5 days. Tail delay is especially large for B and C. Waiting-time tables locate much of the extra delay at Language Support Contact → Eligibility (3.8 days, B) and Accommodation Review → Eligibility (4.7 days, C), plus Completeness → Request Missing Documents for B (1.6 vs 0.4 days). Policy treats those steps as rights, but SLAs do not time them separately. Rights that systematically delay the people who need them are a timeliness-fairness problem even if the step itself is required.

**Consistency of routing.** Some routing is policy-consistent: high risk to Fraud Review (V3 share 28% in D vs 9% in A; high-risk share 51% vs 18%); accommodation requests to Accommodation Review (V5 31% in C); language need to Language Support (V4 24% in B). Other routing is less clearly justified after controls: Supervisor Review remains +8.0 pp for B and +5.5 pp for C; Request Missing Documents remains +9.5 pp for B and +4.2 pp for C. Those residuals suggest either unmeasured complexity or inconsistent thresholds. Handover patterns also differ by group and region: B more often general → bilingual → general; C more often general → specialist → supervisor.

**Quality and accessibility of support.** Language and accommodation steps exist, which is better than omitting them. Access quality is weak. Only seven bilingual caseworkers serve Group B; five accommodation specialists serve Group C, two of whom also do supervisor review. Group B is also loaded onto three overloaded regional officers. Support is therefore scarce, queued, and coupled to other scarce roles. The process grants a right and then under-resources the path that implements it.

**Appeal and rework burden.** Appeal and reopen rates are higher for B, C, and D. After adjustment, appeal remains elevated for B (+4.4 pp) and especially D (+5.7 pp). V6 is 13% of D and 8% of B versus 4% of A, with a 30.7-day median. Appeals can be a corrective right. Persistent extra appeals after risk and documentation controls can also mean first-instance decisions are less accurate, less explained, or less trusted for some groups. Rework (22–24% for B and C) implies repeated evidence cycles, which is a second burden layered on the first decision.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Group A is spread across 52 caseworkers. B and C are concentrated on small specialist pools. Concentration produces longer queues, less substitution when staff are absent, and more multi-hop handovers. Two-region supervisor backlogs add geographic inequity on top of group inequity. Workload is observed only daily, so we cannot separate “this group is harder” from “this group is assigned to already overloaded people.” Resource assignment is therefore both a mechanism and a fairness object in its own right.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should separate four claims: *disparity* (groups differ), *legitimate routing* (policy requires different steps given observed attributes), *discrimination or unjustified treatment* (similarly situated cases are treated differently), and *uncertainty* (controls may be biased or incomplete). No single regression or variant table can collapse those claims.

**Matching or stratification variables.** Stratify or match on the preliminary controls: `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and month. Add `disability_accommodation_requested` and `language_preference` as *routing covariates*, not as “complexity” that excuses delay. Within each stratum, compare approval, cycle time, supervisor-review incidence, document-request incidence, appeal, and rework. Use coarsened exact matching or exact stratification where cells are large (especially Group A vs others on low-risk, complete, online cases). Where cells are thin (high-risk × accommodation × in-person), report uncertainty rather than a pooled residual.

**Path and variant comparison.** Compare variant distributions *inside* strata, not only globally. The key contrast is whether a low-risk, complete, online Group B or C case still avoids V1 and enters V2/V4/V5/supervisor more often than a matched Group A case. Use DFGs and performance DFGs by group and by stratum. Compare directly-follows frequencies for Completeness → Request Missing Documents, Eligibility → Supervisor Review, and Risk Screening → Fraud Review. Use Declare or log-skeleton constraints to test whether “language support implies later eligibility” and “accommodation review implies later supervisor review” are near-invariants for B/C but not for matched A cases.

**Resource-assignment comparison.** Build handover-of-work and working-together networks by group and region. Measure: number of distinct resources per case; share of cases touching the 7 bilingual workers or 5 specialists; path length in the handover graph; and concentration (e.g., Gini of case load). Compare activity-based resource similarity to see whether B/C cases are siloed onto a different role set. Organizational-role discovery should recover a bilingual role and an accommodation-specialist role; fairness analysis then asks whether those roles are bottlenecks *and* whether generalist roles could legally perform more of the surrounding work.

**Waiting-time decomposition.** Insert service and waiting times. Decompose cycle time into: (i) queue before first touch, (ii) service time of core steps, (iii) wait after support steps (language, accommodation), (iv) wait after control steps (fraud, supervisor), (v) wait after document request (applicant-side plus agency-side), and (vi) appeal tail. Compare medians and 90th percentiles of each component within strata. Filter paths by performance to isolate slow Completeness → Request Docs and support → eligibility edges. This distinguishes “the case is complex, so work takes longer” from “the case waits in a specialist queue.”

**Sensitivity analysis for biased covariates.** Treat `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` as potentially post-treatment or historically biased. Re-estimate residuals: (a) dropping risk band; (b) replacing risk band with only prior-case count and current documentation; (c) dropping documentation completeness, or instrumenting it with channel and language; (d) bounding exercises that reclassify a share of B/C “incomplete” cases as language-induced incompleteness. If Group B’s residual approval and document-request gaps shrink when documentation is removed, part of the “adjusted” disparity was control-variable contamination. If they persist, the concern is stronger.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel.** Repeat the full stack in each region and separately for online vs in-person. The notes already flag two-region supervisor backlog and longer in-person waits for identity verification. If B’s residual is concentrated in those two regions or in in-person intake, the mechanism is local capacity and channel design, not a global rule. If the residual appears in online, complete, low-risk cells across regions, the mechanism is closer to inconsistent discretionary routing.

Decision rule for interpretation: a difference that disappears inside matched cells and tracks mandated steps is *legitimate routing*. A difference that remains inside matched cells, loads onto discretionary steps (supervisor, extra document requests) or support queues, and is robust to de-controlling biased covariates is *unjustified procedural disparity*. Everything else stays in *uncertainty*.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The most important procedural differences are path mix, specialist queues, and concentrated assignment.

**Likely justified by observed complexity.** V3 (risk screening → fraud → supervisor) is 28% of Group D versus 9% of A, and 51% of D is high-risk versus 18% of A. Residual cycle-time difference for D is only +0.9 days, and supervisor-review residual is only +2.0 pp. Fraud-queue waits are similar across groups (2.0–2.8 days). Group D’s heavier control path is therefore largely consistent with stated policy. That does not make the risk score itself neutral (Section 5), but given the observed band, routing is aligned.

V5 for Group C and V4 for Group B are also policy-aligned *as presence of a step*. Applicants who request accommodation should get Accommodation Review; applicants who need a minority language should get Language Support Contact. Comparing those groups to A on the share of V1 is therefore not, by itself, evidence of wrongful routing.

**Still concerning after complexity is considered.**

1. *Support steps are timed like optional extras.* Language Support → Eligibility waits 3.8 days; Accommodation Review → Eligibility waits 4.7 days. These are the largest median waits in the table and fall almost entirely on B and C. Adjusted cycle time remains +2.6 days (B) and +4.1 days (C)—the largest residual delay is for the accommodation group. Policy calls the steps rights-preserving, but SLAs do not clock them. The burden of exercising a right is delay.

2. *Document-request and supervisor residuals for B (and C).* After controls, Request Missing Documents is still +9.5 pp for B and +4.2 pp for C; Supervisor Review is +8.0 pp and +5.5 pp. V2 is 34% of B versus 24% of A. Completeness → Request Docs wait is 1.6 days for B versus 0.4 for A, explicitly tied to waiting for bilingual staff. That pattern is not explained by the incomplete-documentation share alone, because the model already conditions on documentation completeness. Either completeness is mismeasured for language-minority applicants, or bilingual capacity turns ordinary completeness work into a slower, more request-heavy path.

3. *Handover multiplication.* B cases in two regions bounce generalist → bilingual → generalist. C cases bounce generalist → specialist → supervisor, and two of five specialists also supervise. Extra handovers add waiting, re-explanation, and error risk. They are not required by the right to support; they are an organizational design.

4. *Resource concentration.* A is spread over 52 workers; B depends on 7 bilingual workers plus 3 overloaded officers; C depends on 5 specialists. Concentration predicts exactly the queues and 90th-percentile blow-ups observed (34–39 days for B and C). This is allocation unfairness even if every individual routing decision follows a rule.

5. *Appeal tail, especially D and B.* Adjusted appeal remains +5.7 pp (D) and +4.4 pp (B). V6 is long (30.7 days). For D, some extra appeals may follow from more adverse first decisions on higher-risk cases. For B, extra appeals sit alongside extra document requests and language queues, which is more consistent with first-instance friction than with risk alone. C’s adjusted appeal gap is small (+1.1 pp) even though rework is high (24%), suggesting C’s burden is delay and looping inside the first case, not formal appeal.

**Net.** Group D’s extra control intensity is the most policy-consistent pattern. Groups B and C show a different pattern: mandated support plus scarce specialists plus extra discretionary review and document requests. That combination is the core procedural-fairness concern. It can exist even if some of the remaining approval gap is residual confounding.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

**Legitimate: risk-based control.** High `risk_score_band` and high prior-case count legally trigger Risk Screening and Fraud Review. Group D’s V3 share and similar fraud waits across groups fit this mechanism. Some of D’s lower approval and higher appeal is the expected output of a tighter control path, not necessarily unequal rules.

**Legitimate: rights-based branching.** Language preference and disability-accommodation flags require extra activities. V4 and V5 are the process implementing legal duties. Extra activities increase cycle time even under perfect good faith. This mechanism explains *why B and C leave V1*, not why those branches wait 4 days or attract extra supervisor review.

**Concerning: under-capacity for rights-preserving work.** Seven bilingual workers and five specialists (two dual-hatted as supervisors) create queues that only B and C sit in. Completeness work for B waits on bilingual staff; C waits on a specialized accommodation team. The same policy that creates the step fails to staff it. Delay is then correlated with protected or vulnerability-related attributes even when each case is “correctly” routed.

**Concerning: control-variable contamination and language-induced incompleteness.** Documentation completeness is 46% incomplete for B versus 21% for A. Language barriers, in-person channel (38% of B), and poorly designed forms can produce “incomplete” labels that then justify Request Missing Documents. If the completeness field is partly an effect of inaccessible process design, conditioning on it hides discrimination and makes extra V2 routing look legitimate. Risk scores can similarly encode historical enforcement intensity against Group D.

**Concerning: discretionary over-routing to supervisor and multi-handover design.** Residual supervisor-review gaps for B and C, dual-role specialists, and generalist–specialist–generalist chains suggest a practice of escalating supported cases rather than letting specialists decide. Escalation may be informal risk aversion: “if language or disability is involved, get a supervisor.” That is consistent treatment of a label, not consistent treatment of case facts.

**Mixed: channel and region congestion.** In-person identity verification waits longer; two regions have supervisor backlog; B is more in-person and more present in overloaded regional offices. This can be a neutral capacity shock that happens to hit B harder, or a failure to provision offices that serve minority-language populations. Subgroup splits are needed to tell those apart.

**Mixed: appeal as voice versus appeal as repair of poor first-instance process.** Higher appeal in D may be rational response to more denials on higher-risk files. Higher appeal in B, after controls, may reflect unexplained denials, poor notice language, or distrust after long document loops. The log’s `appeal_flag` cannot distinguish those.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The attribute set is richer than a typical event log and still insufficient for a discrimination finding.

**Measurement bias in controls.** `risk_score_band` may be trained or calibrated on historical outcomes that already under-approved or over-reviewed some groups. Using it as a neutral complexity control will understate disparity. `documentation_completeness` may measure the agency’s form language and channel design as much as the applicant’s true packet. `language_preference` and `disability_accommodation_requested` capture stated requests, not need; people who do not know they can request support are coded as not needing it and then compared as if they were simple Group A cases.

**Missing applicant needs and treatment quality.** There is no measure of explanation quality, interpreter quality, whether accommodation was actually provided, literacy, digital access, or dignity of treatment. `case_outcome` is a coarse approve/deny-style field. Two approved cases can be unfair if one required four document loops and an opaque letter. Two denied cases can be fair if reasons were accurate and reviewable. None of that is in the log.

**Unobserved resource workload and skill.** Assignment is visible; true caseload, backlog age, leave, and competence are not, except as daily aggregates. We cannot test whether a Group B case assigned to a bilingual worker waited because the worker was overloaded or because the case was hard. Dual-hat specialists confound accommodation delay with supervisor delay.

**Appeal quality and rework semantics.** `appeal_flag` and reopen/rework rates do not say who won, whether the original decision was reversed, or whether rework was applicant delay versus agency rework. V6’s 30.7-day median mixes waiting for a fair hearing with waiting caused by a sloppy first decision.

**Historical and temporal bias.** Month is controlled, but not policy changes, staffing shocks, or score-model refreshes. Prior-case count can reflect previous unfair denials. Region can proxy office culture. Channel is partly chosen and partly constrained by digital access.

**Independence and leakage.** Several “case-level” attributes may be updated mid-case (completeness after Request Missing Documents; risk band after Fraud Review). If they are stored as case attributes rather than event attributes, adjusted models can condition on post-treatment information.

These limits mean residual gaps are signals for audit, not standalone proof of unlawful discrimination. They also mean *absence* of a residual gap would not prove fairness, because biased controls can absorb a real disparity.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

**Add capacity for language support and accommodation review.** Hire or cross-train so bilingual and accommodation work is not bottled into 7 and 5 people. Expected effect: shorter support-to-eligibility waits, fewer 90th-percentile outliers for B and C, less dual-hat conflict. Side effects: cost; if hiring is slow, temporary contractors may reduce consistency; if generalists are lightly trained, quality of accommodation and interpretation can fall. Do not reduce the existence of the steps.

**Service-level targets for support steps.** Clock Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, and Completeness → Request Docs with the same seriousness as Eligibility Assessment. Publish group-stratified wait times. Side effects: staff may rush interviews or deny support requests to hit the clock; gaming via earlier “start” timestamps; conflict with thorough accommodation. Pair SLAs with quality sampling so speed is not purchased by hollowing out the right.

**Routing redesign to reduce handovers.** Allow bilingual and accommodation specialists to complete eligibility (and, where competent, decision draft) instead of returning the file to a generalist or automatically escalating to a supervisor. Keep fraud/supervisor triggers on risk and exception rules, not on “this case touched a support step.” Side effects: specialists become even more loaded unless capacity is added; role boundaries and audit trails must be rewritten; supervisors may resist losing visibility. The goal is fewer hops, not fewer rights.

**Decision-explanation improvements.** Standardize plain-language and translated decision notices; require documented reasons tied to eligibility criteria; offer a structured chance to cure missing documents before denial. This targets B’s residual appeals and document loops. Side effects: slightly longer decision-draft time; more carefully worded letters can become boilerplate unless audited; better explanations may *increase* meritorious appeals in the short run (a feature if first-instance quality is the problem).

**Monitor adjusted disparities.** Institutionalize the stratification/matching stack: approval, waits by step, supervisor and document-request rates, handovers, and appeals, inside complexity cells, by region and channel. Review residuals quarterly. Side effects: teams may avoid hard cases or avoid recording language/disability flags to look equal (“equality by erasure”); Goodhart pressure on the residual. Mitigate by monitoring under-flagging and by keeping raw and adjusted views side by side.

**Audit `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`.** Reconstruct whether the score has disparate error rates; whether completeness codes track form language and channel rather than missing facts; whether either field is updated after the decision path is chosen. Recalibrate or replace biased inputs. Side effects: a fairer score may *increase* Group D’s measured risk if under-enforcement was the historical pattern, or decrease it if over-enforcement was; either shift will be politically contested. Temporarily dropping a biased score without a substitute can create inconsistent human risking. Documentation redesign (translated checklists, in-person help at intake) should accompany recoding so B’s incompleteness rate can actually fall.

Additional practical complement: staff the two backlog regions and in-person identity-verification desks, because channel/region congestion is a documented amplifier for B. Side effect: resources moved from faster online-A offices can worsen A’s 90th percentile if not funded as net-new capacity.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is **not shown to be procedurally fair**, and it is **not proven to be group-targeted discrimination**. The best-supported conclusion is **partially explained disparity with remaining, material procedural unfairness concentrated in how rights-preserving support is staffed and sequenced**.

Case mix and legitimate routing explain a large part of the raw gaps: Group D’s fraud-heavy path tracks measured risk; Groups B and C leave the short V1 path because language and accommodation rules require extra activities. Equal raw approval rates were never a reasonable fairness standard here.

After those factors, the evidence still shows a patterned burden. Group B and Group C wait days in specialist queues that Group A never enters; they receive more document requests and supervisor review than the preliminary controls account for; they are assigned to small, overloaded role sets with extra handovers; and service standards do not clock the very steps the policy calls rights-preserving. Group D’s extra control intensity looks more policy-consistent, but elevated appeals and possible historical bias in `risk_score_band` keep that conclusion provisional.

Because completeness and risk may themselves encode prior inequity, and because explanation quality, true need, and real-time workload are unobserved, several residual points remain **indeterminate**. The operational implication does not. Capacity, SLAs, and handover design for language and accommodation work are first-order fairness issues even if every support step should continue to exist.
