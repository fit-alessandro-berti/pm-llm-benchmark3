# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

In this case, resilience is not "anything that kept boxes moving." It is the ability to preserve the mission, especially hospital supply, while preserving the intent of critical controls through visible compensating mechanisms. Concealed noncompliance is different: it preserves apparent throughput by dropping controls, delaying evidence, or shifting unfinished compliance work into a later queue that is not visible in frontline cycle-time metrics.

The clearest examples of true resilience are B, I, and K, plus J when governed well. Manual authorization checks preserved the actual eligibility control when the system was unavailable. Deferring noncritical orders protected scarce capacity for hospitals, which is a legitimate resilience move when it is explicit and time-bounded. Reconcile manual reservation is necessary recovery work because it restores data integrity rather than pretending the damage did not occur. Backlog prioritization is also resilient when the rule set is explicit and monitored for fairness.

The clearest examples of concealed noncompliance are C and G. Skipping authorization preserved flow by removing a control rather than replacing it. Dispatch without scan preserved throughput by removing the custody event that supports traceability, recall execution, controlled-substance accountability, and sometimes cold-chain evidence. H also becomes concealed noncompliance if management treats phone proof of delivery as equivalent to signed or system-captured proof for high-risk products.

Several patterns are conditionally resilient and conditionally risky: A, D, E, F, H, J, and L. The decision test is simple. If the adaptation keeps the control intent visible, bounded, and evidenced, it is resilience. If it turns an unavailable digital control into an unproven assumption or into after-the-fact reconstruction, it is concealed noncompliance. The stabilization data makes this distinction concrete: hospital-critical median cycle time returned to baseline by T4, but traceability exceptions were still 2.2%, rework 5.8%, and 2,300 cases still flowed from invoice close into audit remediation. That is not failure of service resilience, but it is proof that operational recovery and control recovery were not the same thing.

# 4. Recovery process model

Use the following degraded-mode recovery model in future disruptions. Here, `->` means sequence and `[ ]` means a decision or routing gate.

**Explicit trigger conditions**

- Enter degraded mode immediately if any of the following occurs:
  - WMS unavailable for more than 15 minutes.
  - Scan success drops below 98% for 30 minutes.
  - Inventory synchronization lag exceeds 15 minutes.
  - Authorization, temperature, lot/serial traceability, or controlled-substance custody events cannot be recorded electronically.
- Escalate to high-risk degraded mode if any high-risk control is unavailable for controlled, cold-chain, or recall-sensitive products.
- Exit degraded mode only when:
  - WMS is stable for one full shift.
  - Scan success is above 99%.
  - Inventory sync lag is below 5 minutes.
  - No high-risk case remains open with missing critical evidence.

**Redesigned flow**

```text
START
-> Receive order
-> [Classify customer criticality and product risk]
-> [Route by triage rules]
   P1: Hospital-critical + controlled / cold-chain / recall-sensitive
   P2: Hospital-critical ambient + pharmacy-standard high-risk
   P3: Pharmacy-standard ambient
   P4: Wholesale-bulk ambient
-> Authorization gate
-> Inventory confidence gate
-> Manual or offline pick/pack
-> Release control
-> Dispatch handoff capture
-> Proof of delivery capture
-> Provisional invoice hold
-> [System restored?]
   No -> continue in degraded mode with aging review
   Yes -> Reconciliation queue
-> [Critical evidence complete?]
   Yes -> Invoice close -> END
   No, noncritical gap -> Audit remediation with SLA -> END
   No, critical gap -> Compliance hold / investigation -> END
```

**Triage rules**

- Priority is determined first by clinical/customer criticality, then by product risk, then by age and promised service date.
- Hospital-critical orders go first, but priority does not waive authorization, custody, temperature, or release controls.
- Wholesale-bulk and nonurgent pharmacy orders may be deferred only under explicit rules and customer communication.
- Fairness guardrail: no segment should be repeatedly deferred without aging review. Pharmacy-standard orders need a maximum deferment threshold; wholesale-bulk orders need reconfirmation if the deferment window is exceeded.

**Minimum controls by product/customer risk**

| risk class | minimum controls that cannot be skipped |
|---|---|
| All orders | Unique outage case ID; recorded customer/product decision; recorded picker and checker; documented reason code for degraded handling. |
| Hospital-critical, any product | Queue priority plus all controls required by the product class; no priority-based control waivers. |
| Ambient, lower-risk orders | Offline authorization using approved lists or documented phone validation; inventory snapshot within freshness window; manual pick/pack record; dispatch handoff log; POD captured before final close. |
| Cold-chain | Offline authorization; fresh inventory and lot confirmation; pack-out time and temperature-device ID; courier capability confirmation; handoff record; recipient acceptance evidence. Phone-only POD is not enough. |
| Controlled-substance | Offline authorization; physical inventory confirmation; dual-control sign-off; secure chain-of-custody log; named courier handoff; signed recipient proof. No dispatch without a custody event. |
| Recall-sensitive | Lot/batch capture before dispatch; no later reconstruction as a substitute. If lot cannot be recorded, order is held. |

**Manual evidence capture**

- Use a pre-numbered outage packet or offline app, not free-form spreadsheets alone.
- Mandatory fields: order ID, customer, product, lot/batch/serial where applicable, quantity, location, picker, checker, release approver, timestamp, courier, handoff time, recipient evidence, and exception reason code.
- For cold-chain, record temperature-device ID, seal ID, pack-out time, and any exception at handoff.
- For controlled substances, record the two-person release check and the full custody chain.
- If a mandatory field for a high-risk product is missing, the case cannot move to normal close.

**Backlog prioritization**

- Use an explicit score that combines:
  - customer criticality,
  - product risk,
  - backlog age,
  - promised service date breach,
  - perishability or recall sensitivity.
- Publish the rule set in the incident playbook.
- Review backlog aging at least every shift.
- Require approval for repeat deferments of the same customer group or product family.

**Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow**

- Reconciliation starts as soon as systems are stable enough to accept event replay.
- Sequence:
  - replay manual authorization, pick, pack, dispatch, and POD events,
  - reconcile manual reservations against actual inventory,
  - classify exceptions into documentation gap, inventory mismatch, potential compliance breach, or suspected diversion/loss,
  - route high-risk exceptions to compliance hold or investigation,
  - route low-risk documentation gaps to audit remediation with SLA.
- Audit remediation must be measured as open work, not hidden after invoice close.
- Declare operational recovery only when both service flow and exception queues are within threshold, not when median cycle time alone recovers.

# 5. Resilience indicators

| indicator | type | why it matters | suggested incident use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hospital-critical fill rate | Leading | Shows whether the primary mission is being protected. | Monitor hourly during degraded mode. |
| Median and p90 cycle time by segment | Lagging | Distinguishes who is bearing delay and whether variance is exploding. | Track by hospital-critical, pharmacy-standard, and wholesale-bulk each shift. |
| Share of orders routed through approved degraded-mode paths | Leading | Measures whether teams are using sanctioned compensating controls rather than improvising. | Alert if unsanctioned paths appear. |
| Authorization completeness at release | Leading | Confirms that throughput is not being achieved by dropping eligibility controls. | Must be 100% for controlled, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive products. |
| Traceability completeness at dispatch | Leading | Early warning for later recall and audit failure. | Track scan or manual custody-event completeness before handoff. |
| Cold-chain documentation completeness | Leading and lagging | Reveals whether temperature control is preserved in real time and in final records. | Zero tolerance for missing high-risk fields at close. |
| Controlled-substance dual-control completion | Leading and lagging | Captures whether the most sensitive custody control survived the outage. | Target 100%; any miss triggers investigation. |
| Inventory snapshot age at pick | Leading | Measures how stale the manual inventory basis has become. | Alert when freshness windows are exceeded. |
| Backlog age by segment and priority class | Leading | Prevents hidden starvation of lower-priority groups. | Review every shift with explicit escalation thresholds. |
| Deferment and cancellation rate by customer group | Lagging | Shows whether resilience for one group is being bought with excessive harm to another. | Compare hospital, pharmacy, and wholesale outcomes. |
| Rework / correction case rate | Lagging | Measures the operational cost of degraded execution and stale information. | Rising trend indicates unsafe improvisation. |
| Open reconciliation queue and its age | Leading and lagging | Makes hidden recovery work visible. | Recovery is not complete while this queue remains elevated. |
| Open audit-remediation queue and its age | Lagging | Detects false "back to normal" claims. | Report alongside service metrics until cleared. |
| Percent of invoices closed with unresolved critical evidence | Lagging | Tests whether financial close is outrunning compliance reality. | Target 0% for high-risk products. |
| Fairness ratio across customer groups | Leading and lagging | Quantifies whether prioritization is explicit and proportionate rather than arbitrary. | Compare p90 delay, deferment, and cancellation ratios across groups. |

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| priority | recommendation | implementation effort | expected benefit | residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Codify a degraded-mode playbook with explicit trigger conditions, nonnegotiable controls, and role ownership. | Medium | Prevents improvised shortcuts such as C and G; improves decision speed at T1. | A playbook still depends on staff discipline under pressure. |
| 2 | Replace free-form workaround evidence with a pre-numbered outage packet or offline capture tool covering authorization, lot, temperature, dispatch, and POD. | Medium to high | Preserves traceability when systems fail and reduces reconstruction burden in T3-T4. | Manual entry errors remain possible; offline tools must themselves be tested. |
| 3 | Build a resilient offline data cache for customer/product authorization, product risk flags, and latest inventory snapshot with visible freshness timestamps. | High | Makes B and D faster and safer, and removes the temptation for C. | Cached data can still become stale in a prolonged incident. |
| 4 | Enforce hard-stop rules for high-risk products: no ship without authorization, no controlled-substance release without dual-control, no cold-chain ship without temperature evidence, no dispatch without custody capture. | Low to medium | Prevents the highest-consequence noncompliance even when throughput suffers. | Some orders will be delayed or canceled during severe outages. |
| 5 | Formalize triage and backlog prioritization with fairness guardrails, aging caps, and mandatory customer communication. | Medium | Preserves mission focus without letting pharmacy or wholesale customers become an invisible sacrifice zone. | Some dissatisfaction remains when capacity is genuinely scarce. |
| 6 | Stand up a dedicated reconciliation and audit-tail cell during recovery, with separate SLAs and daily reporting to management. | Medium | Prevents T4-style hidden workload and makes real recovery visible. | Some evidence may still be irrecoverable after the fact. |
| 7 | Cross-train compliance, pharmacy, warehouse, and transport staff for degraded-mode roles; run tabletop and live drills. | Medium | Increases the capacity and quality of B, F, K, and L under stress. | Human performance will still vary in a real incident. |
| 8 | Instrument a resilience dashboard that reports service, controls, backlog, and fairness together, not cycle time alone. | Medium | Stops management from mistaking visible flow recovery for full recovery. | Thresholds need tuning over time. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower cycle times, explicit deferral of lower-priority work, and temporary invoice holds in order to preserve hospital supply and maintain core product-risk controls. Management should not accept shipping based on assumed authorization, dispatch without a custody event, missing cold-chain or controlled-substance evidence, or the normalization of post-close audit remediation as if it were equivalent to real-time compliance. This recommendation would change only if the company can demonstrate, through validated offline controls and drills, that its degraded-mode process preserves authorization, traceability, temperature, and custody evidence for high-risk products at near-baseline reliability even when core systems are unavailable.
