# 1. Data audit and estimands

We consider a single pooled queue at a service center open from 07:00 to 19:00 (12 opening hours per day). Each row in the daily table aggregates one calendar day’s activity for that queue.

**Queueing quantities and units (observations)**  
- **Arrival count per day**: `arrivals` = number of new cases entering the queue during opening hours \([0,12)\) hours from 07:00. A natural *arrival intensity* estimate for day \(d\) is \(\lambda_d = \text{arrivals}_d / 12\) cases per hour.  
- **Completion count per day**: `completed` = number of cases finishing service during opening hours that day. A *completion intensity* estimate is \(\mu_d = \text{completed}_d / 12\) per hour. Completions can include cases that arrived earlier days.  
- **Service-time summaries**: `median_service_min` and `p90_service_min` are the median and 90th percentile of **active handling time per case** (in minutes) for cases completed that day. They exclude waiting and do *not* represent mean flow/sojourn time.  
- **Work-in-process (WIP)**: `mean_wip` = mean number of cases “in system” (waiting or in service) over the opening window, derived from 15-minute snapshots. Units are “cases”. It is a time-average over working hours only; WIP during closure is not measured.  
- **Capacity proxy**: `staff_fte` = mean concurrently available full-time-equivalent capacity over the day. This is an average, not a schedule; intraday staffing variation is not visible.  
- **Case mix**: `complex_pct` = percentage of arrivals that follow a multi-step “complex” variant that plausibly consumes more capacity per case.  
- **Outage**: `outage_min` = minutes within opening hours when service was unavailable. Only 2026‑04‑15 has 180 minutes of outage, consistent with completions-by-hour.  
- **Intraday peak concentration**: `peak_08_10_pct` = percentage of that day’s arrivals between 08:00 and 09:59. Increasing values indicate more concentrated morning peaks.  

**Key estimands for analysis (observations)**  
- **Regime-specific arrival and completion intensities**: daily or multi-day averages \(\bar\lambda, \bar\mu\) (per hour) for distinct operating regimes.  
- **Regime-specific service-time behavior**: robust summaries of active service-time distributions (median, p90), not flow time.  
- **Regime-specific WIP and capacity pressure**: typical `mean_wip` and `mean_wip / staff_fte` per regime, along with the gap `arrivals - completed` as a simple backlog drift signal.  
- **Burstiness / overdispersion**: dispersion of hourly counts relative to Poisson (Fano factor) for different representative days.  
- **Serial dependence and change points**: how today’s WIP, mix, and arrivals depend on previous days (e.g., backlog carry-over, structural breaks).  

**At least five threats to naïve inference (observations & assumptions)**  
1. **Unknown opening and closing backlogs (observation + assumption A1)**  
   - Observation: The data do not provide the backlog at 07:00 or 19:00 for each day; only `mean_wip` over open hours is given. Cases can carry over overnight and no service occurs while closed.  
   - Assumption A1: Except after the outage and during the clearly backlog-building period, opening backlog is of similar order to closing backlog within each short baseline window. This assumption is required even to *approximate* Little’s Law over multi-day windows. Without it, \(L = \lambda W\) is not identifiable from the aggregates alone.

2. **Pooling arrivals and completions over strongly non-stationary intraday patterns (observation)**  
   - Representative arrivals-by-hour for 2026‑04‑07, 2026‑04‑15, 2026‑04‑21, and 2026‑04‑25 show large hourly variation and clear morning peaks, especially the bursty 2026‑04‑21.  
   - For 2026‑04‑07, arrivals per hour have mean ≈ 8.4 and variance ≈ 25, giving a Fano factor ≈ 3 (variance ≫ mean), far from a homogeneous Poisson process.  
   - For the high-mix 2026‑04‑21, the arrivals vector \([2,3,8,34,8,5,24,5,3,14,5,4]\) has mean ≈ 9.6 and variance ≈ 89, Fano factor ≈ 9.3, indicating extreme burstiness.  
   - Any single daily average intensity hides these intraday spikes and troughs.

3. **Structural breaks: outage and staffing intervention (observation)**  
   - On 2026‑04‑15, `outage_min = 180`, `completed = 58` versus `arrivals = 105`, median service jumps to 31 minutes and p90 to 68, and `mean_wip` jumps to 47.  
   - In late April, `staff_fte` increases (notably to 7.0 FTE on 2026‑04‑16 and routinely from 2026‑04‑29 onwards), and regime-level behavior changes from backlog-building to backlog-draining.  
   - Treating all days as a single stationary process ignores these breaks and will misattribute outage and intervention effects to “average performance”.

4. **Evolving case mix and potential coding change (observation)**  
   - `complex_pct` averages ≈ 22% over 2026‑04‑06–2026‑04‑19 (sum 314 over 14 days), then ≈ 43% over 2026‑04‑20–2026‑05‑03 (sum 606 over 14 days).  
   - The prompt explicitly warns that this may mix real case-mix change and coding change. A pooled model that ignores this will conflate demand mix with service speed and backlog.  

5. **Serial dependence and backlog carry-over (observation)**  
   - Arrivals total 2,519 over 28 days (≈ 90/day), while completions total 2,409 (≈ 86/day), a net surplus of 110 arrivals.  
   - `mean_wip` drifts from ≈ 18–23 in early April to ≈ 37–50 after 2026‑04‑20, then partially back down after the staffing intervention, indicating that days are not independent; each day’s backlog is influenced by the previous regime.  
   - Naïvely regressing daily `mean_wip` on same-day arrivals would attribute backlog to that day’s demand, ignoring prior carry-over.

6. **Using medians and p90s as if they were means (observation)**  
   - The service-time summaries are medians and p90s, not means, and refer only to active service time. With heavy-tailed service distributions, the mean can be much larger than the median.  
   - Replacing mean flow time in Little’s Law by `median_service_min` would systematically understate actual sojourn times, especially for complex or backlogged regimes.

7. **Weekend versus weekday heterogeneity (observation)**  
   - Weekends (8 days) see ≈ 45 arrivals/day and ≈ 43 completions/day, while weekdays (20 days) see ≈ 108 arrivals/day and ≈ 103 completions/day.  
   - Pooling weekends and weekdays gives ≈ 90 arrivals/day, which is not representative of either group. Staffing or SLA decisions based on this pooled value would overstaff weekends or understaff peak weekdays.

8. **Measurement window limited to opening hours (observation + assumption A2)**  
   - WIP and throughput are only measured 07:00–19:00. If a large fraction of work arrives near closing or if completion spikes occur just before closing, closing backlog could be systematically positive.  
   - Assumption A2: For quasi-steady windows, unobserved backlog drift across the nightly closure does not dominate the observed intra-day dynamics. This is needed for any steady-state flow-time approximation.

Together, these threats mean that a single pooled queueing model (e.g., one M/M/c-type approximation with constant rates) would both understate variability and misattribute structural shifts.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

An alternative segmentation emphasizes **backlog state and flow balance** rather than calendar structure. Using the daily net flow `delta = arrivals - completed` and `mean_wip`, we can define:

- **B1 – Roughly balanced days (low net change)**  
  - Definition: \(|\text{arrivals} - \text{completed}| \le 5\) and `mean_wip < 30`.  
  - Examples: Many early days such as 2026‑04‑06 (96–94), 2026‑04‑07 (101–99), 2026‑04‑08 (98–100), 2026‑04‑09 (104–103), 2026‑04‑11–12, 2026‑04‑18–19, 2026‑04‑26, 2026‑05‑02–03.  
  - Operational story: The queue is approximately in local equilibrium, with modest WIP and no strong drift in backlog.

- **B2 – Backlog-building days (positive net flow)**  
  - Definition: `arrivals - completed > 5`.  
  - Examples: 2026‑04‑20 (108–94 = +14), 2026‑04‑21 (+14), 2026‑04‑22 (+16), 2026‑04‑23 (+12), 2026‑04‑24 (+10), 2026‑04‑25 (+8), 2026‑04‑27 (+16), 2026‑04‑28 (+12), plus the outage day 2026‑04‑15 (+47) as an extreme.  
  - Operational story: Demand (especially complex cases after 2026‑04‑20) persistently exceeds effective service capacity, so backlog ratchets up even if each individual day’s arrivals look similar to earlier ones.

- **B3 – Backlog-draining days (negative net flow)**  
  - Definition: `arrivals - completed < -5`.  
  - Examples: 2026‑04‑16 (112–129 = −17), 2026‑04‑17 (−13), and post-intervention days 2026‑04‑29 (−5, marginally draining), 2026‑04‑30 (−6), 2026‑05‑01 (−7).  
  - Operational story: These days are used to “catch up” after shocks or after a period of sustained backlog growth. They rely on elevated staffing (7.0–6.5 FTE) or recovery from outage.

- **B4 – Shock outage day**  
  - Definition: `outage_min > 0` (only 2026‑04‑15).  
  - Operational story: An exogenous service stoppage creates a one-day anomaly with severe backlog growth, which then propagates into B3 and subsequent B2 days.

**Strengths of backlog-based segmentation (analysis)**  
- Focuses directly on **net backlog drift**, which is central for service-level risk.  
- Groups together seemingly different calendar days (e.g., 2026‑04‑25 weekend and 2026‑04‑22 weekday) that all contribute to backlog growth, supporting targeted remedial actions (overtime, re-prioritisation).  
- Makes it clear that the post-intervention regime (2026‑04‑29–05‑01) is primarily a **draining** regime, not just “another high-volume week”.

**Weaknesses relative to the primary segmentation (analysis)**  
- It can conflate **root causes**: a backlog-building day driven by an outage (2026‑04‑15) vs. one driven by sustained high complex demand (2026‑04‑22) vs. a weekend with understaffing (2026‑04‑25) may all fall into B2. Operational countermeasures differ even though the net drift sign is the same.  
- It obscures calendar-based planning concerns (weekday vs weekend, pre- vs post-intervention staffing) that are essential for rostering.  
- It does not explicitly isolate the **case-mix shift**; a day with balanced flow but much higher `complex_pct` could be lumped with low-complex days.  

In summary, the backlog-based segmentation is valuable for **control and monitoring** (“are we catching up or falling behind?”), while the primary segmentation is better for **structural modeling** and for understanding causes (outage, mix shift, staffing intervention).


# 4. Pooled versus segmented statistics

This section illustrates how pooling over regimes obscures key behaviors and can induce Simpson’s-paradox-like misinterpretations.

## 4.1 Overall pooled statistics (observations)

- **Whole-period averages (28 days)**  
  - Total arrivals = 2,519 ⇒ **≈ 90 arrivals/day**.  
  - Total completions = 2,409 ⇒ **≈ 86 completions/day**.  
  - Overall complex case share: sum of `complex_pct` = 920 ⇒ **≈ 33% complex arrivals on an average day**.  
  - Rough average median service time: \((384 + 483)/28 ≈ 31\) minutes (where 384 and 483 are sums of daily medians before and after 2026‑04‑20).  
  - Average `mean_wip` is not given directly but visually drifts upward from high teens to high 40s and back to 30s.

A naïve planner might thus conclude that the system faces about **90 arrivals/day**, with about **one-third complex cases**, **median service ≈ 31 minutes**, and **a slight capacity deficit** (86 completions/day).

## 4.2 Weekday versus weekend composition (observations)

- **Weekdays (20 days)**  
  - Arrivals: 2,160 ⇒ **≈ 108/day**.  
  - Completions: 2,063 ⇒ **≈ 103/day**.  
  - Staffing: usually 6.0 FTE pre-intervention, then 7.0 or 6.5 FTE post-intervention.  
  - Interpretation: Weekdays are the true capacity drivers, with intense peaks and heavy backlog dynamics.

- **Weekends (8 days)**  
  - Arrivals: 359 ⇒ **≈ 45/day**.  
  - Completions: 346 ⇒ **≈ 43/day**.  
  - Staffing: 2.5 FTE initially, rising to 3.0 FTE after the intervention.  
  - Interpretation: Weekends have about **40%** of the average weekday arrival rate but slightly higher WIP per FTE and longer inferred flow times.

**Pooling effect:**  
The pooled 90 arrivals/day is neither representative of the ~108 weekday nor the ~45 weekend regime. Using the pooled arrival rate to size capacity might **overstaff weekends** or **understaff weekdays**, depending on which is treated as the “norm”.

## 4.3 Complex-case mix and service times (observations and estimates)

- **Before 2026‑04‑20 (14 days)**:  
  - Average `complex_pct` ≈ 314/14 ≈ **22%**.  
  - Average median service ≈ 384/14 ≈ **27.4 minutes**.  

- **From 2026‑04‑20 onward (14 days)**:  
  - Average `complex_pct` ≈ 606/14 ≈ **43%** (almost double).  
  - Average median service ≈ 483/14 ≈ **34.5 minutes**.  

- **Pooled over the full 28 days**:  
  - Complex cases ≈ 33%, median service ≈ 31 minutes.  

**Simpson’s-paradox-like risk:**  
If we fit a single pooled queueing model with median service ≈ 31 minutes and 33% of cases labeled complex, we might infer that **complexity has only modest impact**, or that service times increased only slightly over the month. Segmenting shows that:
- In the early regime, the system processed mostly simpler cases with much faster service times and lower WIP.  
- In the later regime, the system faced many more complex cases with substantially slower active service and higher WIP, even after adding staff.  

A pooled model might therefore underestimate the **true increase in service demand per arrival** and overestimate the **benefit of the staffing intervention**, because it mixes low- and high-complex regimes.

## 4.4 Outage and staffing intervention (observations and estimates)

**Outage day versus surrounding days**  
- On 2026‑04‑15, completions drop to 58, far below the ~100 weekday norm, due to 3 hours of outage. `mean_wip` jumps to 47.  
- The next two weekdays (2026‑04‑16–17) show **completions of 129 and 112**, exceeding arrivals and using 7.0 and 6.5 FTE, clearly a **backlog recovery regime**.  

**Staffing intervention window (from 2026‑04‑29)**  
- Before 2026‑04‑29 in the high-complex regime (R5), weekday arrivals average ≈ 114/day and completions ≈ 101/day. WIP is ≈ 44 on weekdays.  
- After 2026‑04‑29 (R6), weekday arrivals remain high at ≈ 115/day, but completions rise to ≈ 121/day and WIP falls to ≈ 37.  

**Pooling effect:**  
- If we pool 2026‑04‑20–2026‑05‑03, we might see “arrivals ≈ completions” and conclude that capacity is roughly adequate for the new mix. In reality, the system **ran in backlog-building mode** for over a week and then **drained backlog** only after staffing increased.  
- Similarly, including the outage day in a pooled estimate of “typical” throughput systematically underestimates capacity and overstates typical WIP, potentially justifying excessive permanent staffing rather than targeted resilience to outages.


# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## 5.1 Representative hourly vectors and overdispersion (observations & estimates)

Using the four representative arrival/completion profiles (12 one-hour bins from 07:00 to 19:00):

- **Ordinary weekday candidate (2026‑04‑07)**  
  - Arrivals: \([3,5,12,20,16,10,8,7,6,5,5,4]\) sum to 101.  
  - Mean ≈ 8.4, variance ≈ 25 ⇒ **Fano factor ≈ 3**, indicating overdispersion relative to a Poisson process.  
  - There is a pronounced mid-morning peak (20 arrivals in 10–11), followed by a gradual decline.

- **Outage day (2026‑04‑15)**  
  - Arrivals remain structurally similar to 2026‑04‑07 (slightly higher counts), but completions show zeros during outage hours (≈ 11:00–14:00) and then some catch-up.  
  - The completion process is **strongly zero-inflated and bursty**, with overdispersion driven by service stoppage and restart, not by demand variability alone.

- **Bursty post-mix-change weekday (2026‑04‑21)**  
  - Arrivals: \([2,3,8,34,8,5,24,5,3,14,5,4]\) sum to 115.  
  - Mean ≈ 9.6, variance ≈ 89 ⇒ **Fano factor ≈ 9.3**, far from stationarity/Poisson.  
  - There is an extreme spike at 10–11 (34 arrivals) and a secondary spike at 13–14 (24 arrivals), implying strong short-term overload around those hours even if the daily total looks routine.

- **Weekend candidate (2026‑04‑25)**  
  - Arrivals: \([1,2,4,7,8,7,6,5,4,3,2,2]\), sum 51.  
  - Mean ≈ 4.25, variance ≈ 5.0 ⇒ **Fano factor ≈ 1.2**, much closer to Poisson and with a smoother curve.  
  - This suggests that **weekend arrivals are both lower and more regular**, making weekend regimes closer to stationary in a coarse sense.

These hourly patterns show that **burstiness is regime-dependent**: high-mix weekdays after the case-mix shift exhibit strong overdispersion, while weekends remain relatively regular. Any single daily-intensity parameter will miss this.

## 5.2 Daily sequence, autocorrelation, and change-point evidence (observations)

- **Trend and autocorrelation in WIP and mix**  
  - `mean_wip` rises from ≈ 18–23 in early April to ≈ 37–50 after 2026‑04‑20, then falls back toward the low 30s after the intervention.  
  - `complex_pct` moves from ≈ 19–25 early in the month to ≈ 40–47 later, a clear step change around 2026‑04‑20.  
  - Visually, high WIP days cluster (e.g., 2026‑04‑20–24, 2026‑04‑27–28), showing positive lag-1 dependence; a large backlog on one day makes a large backlog on the next day more likely.

- **Change points**  
  - Around **2026‑04‑15**, the outage produces a sharp jump in WIP and a sharp fall in completions; 2026‑04‑16–17 then show elevated completions, indicating a structural disturbance rather than normal fluctuation.  
  - Around **2026‑04‑20**, there is a joint increase in `complex_pct`, median and p90 service times, `mean_wip`, and `peak_08_10_pct`, marking the onset of the high-complex regime.  
  - Around **2026‑04‑29**, `staff_fte` increases and `mean_wip` begins to decline while completions overtake arrivals, indicating a staffing intervention and regime shift.

- **Stationarity assessment**  
  - Over short periods such as R1 or R2, the system is closer to a stationary queue with moderate intraday variability.  
  - Across the full 4-week horizon, **non-stationarity is dominant**: both demand mix and capacity change, an outage occurs, and backlog drifts significantly.

Because we have only four representative hourly profiles, we **cannot** estimate full stochastic arrival or service processes (e.g., interarrival distributions, serial correlation of hourly counts) with confidence. These vectors are best treated as **illustrative shapes** for different regimes, not as complete empirical evidence.


# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law \(L = \lambda W\) requires (i) a stable system over the chosen window (backlog not drifting), (ii) consistent boundaries (arrivals and departures measured for the same population), and (iii) consistent WIP measurement. Here we evaluate candidate windows.

For intensity \(\lambda\), we use **completions per minute** (throughput) within opening hours, because completions reflect processed work, but we note when this is questionable (e.g., outage days).

Approximate implied flow times are estimated as:
\[
W \approx \frac{\bar{L}}{\bar{\mu}}, \quad \bar{L} = \text{mean of `mean_wip`}, \quad \bar{\mu} = \frac{\text{total completions}}{\text{total opening minutes}}.
\]
Units: minutes of **sojourn time within opening hours** (excluding overnight closure); interpretation must therefore be cautious.

### 6.1 Matrix (estimates and qualitative verdicts)

# 7. Operational implications

## 7.1 Staffing and capacity planning (recommendations)

1. **Differentiate weekday and weekend staffing**  
   - Observation: Weekdays have ≈ 108 arrivals/day versus ≈ 45 on weekends, with higher WIP and much more bursty intraday arrival profiles.  
   - Recommendation: Plan **separate staffing templates** for weekdays and weekends rather than applying a pooled average. Weekdays should plan around the high-complex, high-burst regime (R5/R6), with explicit focus on the 08:00–11:00 and early-afternoon peaks; weekends can use lighter but still resilient staffing.

2. **Account explicitly for the complex-case shift**  
   - Observation: `complex_pct` nearly doubles from ≈ 22% to ≈ 43% and coincides with increases in median and p90 service times and WIP.  
   - Recommendation: Treat the late-April configuration as a **new case-mix regime**, not as a minor perturbation. Staffing, training, and routing should assume that complex work is the norm, potentially including dedicated complex-case specialists or separate queues/skills-based routing to avoid complex cases blocking short ones.

3. **Design explicit outage and recovery playbooks**  
   - Observation: The 3-hour outage on 2026‑04‑15 creates a one-day backlog spike that requires multiple days of elevated throughput to clear.  
   - Recommendation: Create **predefined recovery plans** (additional shifts, cross-trained staff, overtime windows) triggered when an outage is detected, rather than treating recovery days as normal operation. This avoids misinterpreting temporarily high completions as evidence that base capacity is sufficient.

4. **Target staffing intervention around high-burst windows**  
   - Observation: Post-mix-change weekdays (e.g., representative 2026‑04‑21) have extremely bursty arrivals with Fano factor ≈ 9 and strong mid-morning spikes.  
   - Recommendation: Use **short-term flexible capacity** (e.g., staggered breaks, part-time or floating staff) concentrated around the peak hours, rather than uniform 7.0 FTE across the entire day. This can reduce peak waiting times without significantly increasing total staffing.

5. **Policy that looks safe under pooled averages but is risky when segmented (example)**  
   - Pooled view: 90 arrivals/day, 86 completions/day, median service ≈ 31 min suggest that simply **maintaining 6.0 FTE** could be adequate across the month.  
   - Segmented view: In the high-complex pre-intervention regime (R5), arrivals average ≈ 114/day with longer service times and WIP ≈ 44, clearly overloading 6.0 FTE. Without the staffing intervention (R6), backlog would continue to grow.  
   - Risk: A policy of “keep staffing at the long-run average” would **systematically under-staff the high-complex regime**, leading to chronic backlog and SLA breaches.

## 7.2 Backlog management and peak smoothing (recommendations)

6. **Monitor net-flow regimes in real time**  
   - Recommendation: Implement daily dashboards tracking `arrivals - completed` and `mean_wip`, classifying days into backlog-building vs backlog-draining states (B2 vs B3). Trigger corrective actions (e.g., overtime, reprioritisation) when several backlog-building days occur in a row.

7. **Peak smoothing and appointment design**  
   - Observation: High-mix weekdays have concentrated demand spikes (e.g., 34 arrivals in one hour on a 115-arrival day).  
   - Recommendation: Use appointments, arrival windows, or channel steering (e.g., digital pre-processing) to **spread complex arrivals away from the most congested hours**, especially just before lunch when outages or breaks are more likely. Even small reductions in the largest hourly peaks can materially reduce queue lengths.

## 7.3 Data-collection and modeling (recommendations)

8. **Collect detailed event logs rather than relying on aggregated medians**  
   - Recommendation: Capture **case-level arrival and completion timestamps, service start and end times, and variant labels**. This would enable estimation of full flow-time distributions, path-specific service times, and more detailed non-stationarity (e.g., via PM4Py-based temporal feature extraction).  
   - With such data, the organization could estimate **separate queueing models per regime** (e.g., baseline weekday, high-complex weekday, weekend, outage/recovery) and properly evaluate policies like skill-based routing or priority rules.

9. **Explicitly log intervention timings**  
   - Observation: The staffing intervention “began during 2026‑04‑29” but the exact clock time is unknown.  
   - Recommendation: In future, **time-stamp intervention starts and ends** (e.g., schedule changes, policy switches). This would allow intraday analysis of pre/post-intervention performance and much sharper identification of causal effects.

    arr = np.asarray(counts, dtype=float)

    cv = arr.std(ddof=0) / m if m > 0 else np.nan
    return pd.Series({"mean": m, "var": v, "fano": fano, "cv": cv})
