# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Queueing quantities
- `arrivals`: daily count of arrivals during opening hours.
- `completed`: daily count of completed cases during opening hours.
- `median_service_min` and `p90_service_min`: median and p90 of active handling times, respectively.
- `mean_wip`: mean number of cases in the system from 15-minute snapshots.
- `staff_fte`: mean concurrently available full-time-equivalent capacity.
- `complex_pct`: percentage of arrivals following a multi-step complex variant.
- `peak_08_10_pct`: percentage of daily arrivals observed from 08:00 through 09:59.

## Opening-hours convention
Opening hours are from 07:00 to 19:00.

## Limitations of the aggregates
1. **Backlog state**: The backlog at 07:00 is not supplied for every day.
2. **Carry-over backlog**: Cases can carry over overnight, although no service occurs while closed.
3. **Non-independence**: Daily points are not independent as cases can carry over from one day to the next.
4. **Outage periods**: Outages (e.g., from 11:00 to 14:00 on 2026-04-15) are not explicitly indicated in the daily data.
5. **Staffing intervention**: The exact implementation time of the temporary staffing intervention on 2026-04-29 is not logged.

## Threats to naive inference
1. **Backlog effects**: Ignoring the backlog state can lead to underestimation of WIP and capacity pressure.
2. **Non-stationarity**: The data exhibits varying patterns across weekdays, weekends, and times of day, which pooling may mask.
3. **Carry-over effects**: Cases that arrive on one day but are completed on another can blur daily patterns.
4. **Outage periods**: Excluding outage periods from the analysis could skew the observed arrival and completion rates.
5. **Complex-case mix**: The presence of a higher percentage of complex cases on certain days can affect service times and resource utilization.

The positive autocorrelation suggests some level of serial dependence in the arrival patterns.

## Change-point evidence

- **Evidence**: Significant drop in arrivals and completion rates during the outage, followed by a return to normal patterns post-intervention.

## Stationarity assessment

- Four representative days are insufficient to establish a full stochastic model, as they do not capture the full range of variations and change points.

# 7. Operational implications

## Recommendations based on segmented analysis

1. **Staffing and capacity planning**:
   - Increase staffing during normal weekdays (primary regime) and post-intervention periods to handle higher arrival rates.
   - Maintain reduced staffing levels during outage periods, but be prepared for a temporary surge in arrivals post-recovery.
   - Adjust staffing levels based on the proportion of complex cases, as seen in the complex case mix regime.

2. **Backlog management**:
   - Implement a backlog monitoring system that accounts for carry-over cases, particularly during outage periods.
   - Use predictive analytics to anticipate backlog build-up during high-complexity days and proactively allocate resources.

3. **Peak smoothing**:
   - Deploy additional resources during peak hours (08:00-09:59), especially on days with high `peak_08_10_pct` values.
   - Consider staggered staffing to smooth out peak periods and reduce congestion.

4. **Data collection and analysis**:
   - Improve data collection to include backlog states at the start and end of each day.
   - Collect detailed timestamps for case arrivals and completions to capture intraday patterns and improve the granularity of the analysis.
   - Consider implementing PM4Py's `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` to extract more detailed temporal features from the event log data.

## Risk under pooled averages
- **Staffing decisions**: Relying on pooled averages could lead to overstaffing during normal periods and understaffing during complex case mix or outage periods.
- **Resource allocation**: Pooling may obscure the need for additional resources during complex case days or post-intervention periods, leading to suboptimal resource utilization.
