# 2. Integrated explanation

Service time declines across the work period are **not** the product of a single mechanism but emerge from the interleaving of learning/warmup, casemix selection, and queue pressure. Early in the shift (positions1–5), mean service time is longest (18.8min) because the queue is populated by “backlog from previous shift, complex carryover” – seniors deliberately take complex cases, and highpriority items dominate (41% highpriority, complexity0.72). As the shift progresses, two concurrent forces reduce service time: (1) a learning/warmup effect where resources gain familiarity with the day’s case mix, and (2) the progressive processing of simpler cases, leaving a disproportionate share of complex work for later or for handoff. The observed complexity decline (0.72  0.43) partly reflects this selection, not merely skill acquisition.

Error rates, however, do **not** move monotonically with the servicetime decline. They stay low (2.5–3.4%) while WIP is moderate (30–70) and complexity is manageable, then rise sharply once WIP exceeds 70 (5.8% for lowcomplexity, 10.9% for highcomplexity). Queue pressure thus modulates error risk: faster throughput under high WIP comes at the cost of quality, especially for complex cases. Fatiguerelated error escalation appears in the last third of shifts (positions51–75 and76+), but its magnitude depends on resource experience—Seniors’ errors only double from 2.2% to 4.9% under high WIP, while Juniors rise from 4.4% to 12.4%. Circadian effects further shape the baseline: Night shift starts with lower WIP (29) and fewer events but handles a higher share of urgent exceptions (56% highcomplexity in first hour), yielding a distinct errorservice tradeoff (15.2min service, 5.1% error in last two hours).

Batching and automation reshape these patterns without being root causes. Documentreview batches of 9–18 consecutive events with very short service times artificially compress apparent throughput late in the Day shift, while Bot_Risk instant classification concentrates correction workload when WIP>70, pushing errors upward in those segments. Quality Check delays until shift end create a burst of rework corrections in the final 45minutes, inflating both servicetime variance and 7day rework rates (13.7% in the 76+ position bin).

The pattern therefore resolves as: **service time falls due to a combination of learning, casemix thinning, and increasing queue pressure; error rates rise conditionally when WIP is high, fatigue sets in late in the shift, or circadian misalignment occurs, with resource experience and automation mediating the severity.** No single driver explains the full profile, and interventions that target only one aspect (e.g., “learning” via faster onboarding) will likely miss the interaction points where quality degrades.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

**Selection bias** – Earlyshift cases are systematically more complex and higherpriority, often assigned to senior resources. Attributing better earlyshift performance solely to learning ignores this preexisting casemix advantage. Conversely, lateshift error spikes may reflect the accumulation of simpler cases after complex ones are filtered out, not purely fatigue.

**Measurement error** – Event logs capture timestamps and flags but not true cognitive load, interruption frequency, or decision difficulty. `minutes_since_shift_start` and `sequence_position_within_shift` are proxies that assume uniform event pacing, which the data explicitly violate (batching, circadian dips, queue surges). Servicetime averages mask intraevent variability (e.g., a 10minute case vs a 20minute case within the same bin).

**Queue pressure created by management** – Interventions that cap shift length or add alerts may shift pressure downstream rather than reduce it. For example, strictly capping hours could force more handovers, increasing interface errors; WIPalert thresholds may prompt rushed decisions when the alert triggers, paradoxically raising errors. Managementinduced pressure must be measured (e.g., changes in `wip_at_start` distribution) and not assumed to be static.

**Automation quality** – Bot_Risk instant classification improves speed but its correction demand (`queue_length_at_start`>70) indicates that automation quality is contingent on system state. Penalizing workers for errors that stem from botcorrection workload misallocates responsibility. Any performance metric must distinguish humanorigin errors from automationinduced rework.

**Punitive use of error rates** – Raw lateshift error rates are a conflation of fatigue, queue pressure, casemix, and circadian effects. Using them directly for performance appraisal or punishment is methodologically unsound and ethically risky. If errorbased incentives are desired, they must be calibrated against residuals from multivariate models that control for the mechanisms identified here, and even then, should supplement—not replace—systemlevel improvements.
