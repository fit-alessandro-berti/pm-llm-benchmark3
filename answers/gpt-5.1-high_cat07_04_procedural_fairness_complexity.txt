# 1. Fairness dimensions

Outcome parity  
Raw outcomes show substantially lower approval rates and worse cycle times for Groups B, C and D than for Group A. For example, Group B has a 63% approval rate vs 78% for Group A and a median cycle time of 13.7 vs 8.1 days, with much longer 90th percentile times and higher appeal and rework rates. Taken alone, these gaps signal potential fairness concerns but do not, by themselves, establish discrimination because case mix, routing, and support needs clearly differ across groups.

Adjusted outcome disparity  
The preliminary adjusted comparison, which conditions on risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region and month, still shows lower approval probabilities for all three non‑reference groups (e.g., Group B −5.5 percentage points) and longer cycle times (e.g., Group C +4.1 days). It also shows higher probabilities of Supervisor Review, Request Missing Documents, and Appeal for these groups. This suggests that even after accounting for observed complexity, there remain residual disparities in how cases are treated, pointing toward potential procedural unfairness or unmeasured differences in need/complexity.

Procedural burden  
Procedural burden captures how many extra steps, reworks, and handovers applicants face, as well as how much effort they must expend to get a decision. Groups B and C are more often routed through additional rights‑preserving steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review) and through Request Missing Documents and Supervisor Review, increasing complexity and burden. Higher appeal and reopen/rework rates for B and C (and for D on appeals) also indicate that these applicants more often have to contest or revisit decisions, adding effort and stress. This burden can be unfair even if the final approval rates are partially explained by complexity, especially when the burden is systematically concentrated on protected or vulnerable groups.

Timeliness  
Timeliness is a key procedural dimension. Raw cycle times are substantially longer for Groups B and C, and the 90th percentile cycle times show much heavier tails. Waiting‑time decomposition indicates that much of the delay arises in queues for language support, accommodation review, and supervisor review: e.g., Group B waits 3.8 days from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment and 1.6 days from Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents; Group C waits 4.7 days from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment. Because these steps are rights‑preserving (intended to ensure access and accommodation), it is procedurally problematic that they systematically lengthen timelines for the groups that need them most. Timeliness disparities therefore remain a central fairness concern even after acknowledging legitimate additional steps.

Consistency of routing  
Routing fairness concerns whether cases with similar observable characteristics follow similar paths. Variant distributions show that Groups B and C are under‑represented in the simple “straight‑through” variant V1 and over‑represented in more complex paths with additional checks and support (V2, V4, V5, V6). Group D is heavily represented in V3 (Risk Screening → Fraud Review → Supervisor Review) and in appeal‑heavy variants like V6, consistent with higher measured risk and prior cases. Some of this routing is policy‑driven and may be legitimate, but the adjusted results (higher probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents even after controlling for risk, documentation and channel) suggest that routing is not fully explained by measured complexity. That residual routing disparity—especially if systematic by applicant_group—raises concerns that similar cases may be treated differently.

Quality and accessibility of support  
Quality/accessibility includes whether support steps (language, accommodation) actually help applicants navigate the system with dignity and understanding. The process treats language support and accommodation as rights‑preserving, but they are not included in service‑level targets and appear to create substantial additional waiting time. Group B applicants, who most often need language support and who are more frequently in‑person, are concentrated with a small pool of bilingual caseworkers and regional officers who are overloaded, increasing wait times and handovers. The data do not directly capture quality of explanations, ease of communication, or whether interpreters/accommodation specialists are available at convenient times, so we cannot fully assess support quality; however, the combination of long waits and high appeal/rework rates for supported groups suggests that support may not be fully effective or accessible.

Appeal and rework burden  
Appeal and reopen/rework rates reflect how often applicants must contest or redo parts of the process. Groups B and D have higher appeal rates (13% and 16% vs 6% for Group A), and Groups B and C have higher rework rates (22% and 24% vs 11% for Group A). After adjustment, the probability of Appeal remains higher for Groups B (+4.4 percentage points) and D (+5.7 points), implying that even controlling for observed complexity, their cases more often lead to contestation. This imposes additional emotional and administrative burden on these groups and may indicate that first‑instance decisions or communications are less clear or less accurate for them.

Resource‑allocation fairness  
Resource allocation examines whether staffing, workloads, and expertise are distributed in a way that does not systematically disadvantage certain applicant groups. Group A’s cases are spread across 52 caseworkers, while Group B’s are concentrated among 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers, and Group C’s among 5 accommodation specialists (two of whom also do supervisor review). This concentration, combined with long waits at support steps and more handovers (e.g., general → bilingual → general for Group B; general → accommodation specialist → supervisor for Group C), suggests that resource shortages and assignment patterns are an important driver of unequal burdens. Fairness is compromised if vulnerable applicants systematically face longer queues and more fragmented responsibility because the organization has not staffed support functions adequately.


# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

Matching or stratification variables  
To distinguish disparity from discrimination and legitimate routing, we would begin with stratification or matching on observable complexity and context: risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel (online vs in‑person), language_preference, disability_accommodation_requested, prior_case_count, household_size_band, region, and time (month). Within these strata—or using propensity‑score or coarsened exact matching—we can compare outcomes and process metrics (e.g., probability of Supervisor Review, request for missing documents, appeal, cycle time) across applicant_group for cases that are otherwise similar. This helps control for legitimate differences in case mix while highlighting residual disparities.

Path/variant comparison  
Using `pm4py.get_variants`, we can map each case to a variant and then, within matched or stratified subsets, compare variant probabilities across groups. For example, among cases with medium risk_score_band, complete documentation, online channel, and no accommodation request, we would test whether Groups B and C are still more likely than Group A to follow high‑burden variants (e.g., those including Request Missing Documents, Supervisor Review, Language Support Contact). Persistent differences in routing within these controlled strata would be evidence of procedural disparity not fully explained by measured complexity. Conversely, if group differences disappear once we hold variant constant, then disparities may be driven mainly by routing and resource allocation rather than differential treatment within a given path.

Resource‑assignment comparison  
Using handover‑of‑work and working‑together networks, we can analyze which resources and teams are involved by group, controlling for complexity. For example, within homogeneous strata, we can compare: (a) the probability that a case is handled by overloaded vs non‑overloaded workers; (b) the number and pattern of handovers; and (c) the distribution of supervisor interventions across groups. If, after conditioning on complexity and path, cases from certain groups are still routed more often to heavily backlogged staff or experience more handovers, that suggests a resource‑allocation fairness issue. If differences disappear after controlling for both complexity and staff load, that supports a more benign explanation rooted in neutral operational constraints.

Waiting‑time decomposition  
With service and waiting times inserted into the event log, we can decompose total cycle time into waiting vs processing time by activity and by group. The key is to compare, within similar variants and complexity levels, how much extra delay is incurred at specific steps, especially rights‑preserving support steps and discretionary reviews. For example, among cases that require language support, we can see whether Group B’s additional waiting time at Language Support Contact and subsequent transitions remains higher than for comparable Group A cases (if any) or than for other groups needing language support under similar queue conditions. This decomposition helps determine whether observed timeliness disparities are driven by legitimate extra steps, operational capacity limits, or potentially inequitable prioritization of support queues.

Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates  
Risk_score_band and documentation_completeness may themselves be biased: risk scores can encode historical disparities or model misspecification, and documentation completeness can reflect barriers to understanding the process. To avoid “over‑controlling” away inequity, we would run multiple specifications:  
- Models that treat risk_score_band and documentation_completeness as covariates.  
- Models that omit or coarsen these variables (e.g., only high vs non‑high risk) to test how sensitive disparities are to them.  
- Models that include interaction terms between applicant_group and these covariates to see whether their meaning or predictive value differs by group.  
If adjusted disparities are highly sensitive to inclusion of these variables, that indicates uncertainty about whether they reflect legitimate complexity or embedded bias.

Subgroup checks by region and channel  
Subgroup analyses would stratify by region, channel, and support needs. For example, we would separately analyze online vs in‑person cases, cases with vs without language support, and cases with vs without accommodation requests. Regional stratification would allow us to identify offices where disparities are particularly large, which may point to local practices or resource constraints. Disparities that are consistent across regions and channels might indicate structural issues (e.g., system‑wide policies or scoring models), while disparities concentrated in specific regions or channels may reflect localized problems or opportunities for targeted interventions.

Distinguishing disparity, discrimination, legitimate routing, and uncertainty  
Bringing these elements together, we can classify findings as:  
- Likely legitimate routing when disparities align with policy‑mandated steps and vanish after conditioning on unbiased complexity measures and path.  
- Potential discrimination (individual or systemic) when residual disparities in routing, waiting time, or review intensity persist after careful adjustment and cannot be plausibly attributed to unobserved complexity alone.  
- Mixed/uncertain when results are sensitive to modeling choices (e.g., treatment of risk_score_band) or when data limitations prevent ruling out benign explanations.  
The conclusion should clearly label which disparities fall into each category rather than inferring motive from raw differences alone.


# 3. Path and resource disparities

Path disparities  
Variant distributions show clear differences in procedural paths. Group A is most likely to follow the straightforward V1 path (42%), while Groups B, C, and D are less likely (19%, 22%, 18%), indicating they more often experience additional steps. Group B is especially concentrated in V2 and V4, which include Request Missing Documents and Language Support Contact; Group C is heavily represented in V5, involving Accommodation Review and Supervisor Review; Group D is prominent in V3 (risk and fraud review) and V6 (appeal‑related path). These patterns align partly with case‑mix indicators (higher risk for D, more accommodation requests for C, more language support for B), but the adjusted model shows that, even among cases with similar measured complexity, Groups B and C still face higher probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents. This suggests that some extra routing is not fully justified by observed risk or documentation status.

Waiting‑time disparities  
Waiting‑time data indicate that Groups B and C face systematically longer waits at multiple steps, especially those tied to support or discretionary reviews. Group B waits longer than Group A from submission to identity verification (1.4 vs 0.8 days), from completeness check to request for missing documents (1.6 vs 0.4 days), and from supervisor review to decision draft (1.9 vs 1.0 days). Group C faces especially long waits between Accommodation Review and Eligibility Assessment (4.7 days) and also longer waits at supervisor review. These extra waits, layered on additional process steps, produce substantially higher cycle times and heavier tails (90th percentile) for B and C. While some delay may be inevitable when specialized staff are scarce, the fact that these steps are rights‑preserving and concentrated on vulnerable groups means that the operational design currently imposes disproportionate temporal burdens on those groups.

Resource‑assignment disparities  
Resource assignment is highly uneven. Group A cases are distributed across a broad pool of 52 caseworkers, which can buffer individual workloads. By contrast, Group B cases are concentrated among 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers; Group C cases are funneled to 5 accommodation specialists, two also performing supervisor review. This concentration, combined with queues and handovers, magnifies the risk that small capacity shortfalls will disproportionately harm Groups B and C. In addition, Groups B and C experience more handovers (general → bilingual → general; general → accommodation specialist → supervisor), which can increase the chance of miscommunication, delays, and inconsistent treatment. Group D’s stronger routing to the fraud queue is consistent with its higher observed risk scores, but given possible bias in those scores, we cannot treat this as purely legitimate without further auditing.

Which disparities may be legitimate vs concerning  
Some disparities can plausibly be justified by real differences in complexity: Group D’s higher risk_score_band share and prior_case_count justify more frequent Risk Screening and Fraud Review; Group C’s high rate of accommodation requests legitimately requires Accommodation Review; Group B’s minority‑language status legitimately drives Language Support Contact. However, even after controlling for these factors, Groups B, C, and D still exhibit worse adjusted approval probabilities, longer adjusted cycle times, and elevated probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents. The long waits and high concentration of burden in support queues, coupled with higher appeal and rework rates, are particularly concerning as they suggest that needed support is being delivered in a way that systematically slows and complicates the process for vulnerable groups. Overall, the evidence points to a mix of legitimate complexity‑driven differences and residual procedural inequities that warrant attention.


# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. Legitimate complexity and risk differences  
Higher risk_score_band and prior_case_count for Group D, as well as higher rates of incomplete documentation for Groups B and C and more frequent accommodation requests for Group C, can legitimately justify additional checks such as Fraud Review, Supervisor Review, and Accommodation Review. These mechanisms are intended to protect program integrity and ensure appropriate benefits levels, and some of the observed routing and longer processing times may be necessary to manage genuinely more complex cases.

2. Capacity constraints and queueing in support functions  
Limited numbers of bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists, combined with the concentration of B and C cases among them, likely create bottlenecks. The long waits after Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review indicate that even when support is available in principle, insufficient capacity and lack of explicit service‑level targets for these steps leads to longer queues. This mechanism is procedurally problematic because it makes exercising a right (language support or accommodation) effectively costly in time, and it is avoidable through better staffing and planning.

3. Discretionary escalation and documentation demands influenced by bias or risk aversion  
Caseworkers and supervisors may exercise discretion in deciding when to request missing documents or escalate to Supervisor Review, especially when documentation_completeness and risk_score_band are imperfect proxies for true complexity. If staff are more likely—consciously or unconsciously—to question documentation or escalate cases from minority‑language applicants, applicants with disabilities, or those with prior cases, that can produce higher adjusted probabilities of Request Missing Documents and Supervisor Review for those groups even after controlling for observable complexity. This mechanisms points toward systemic or implicit discrimination in process usage, not necessarily explicit policy bias.

4. Measurement bias in risk_score_band and documentation_completeness  
Risk scores may be trained on historical data that already embed disparities (e.g., stronger enforcement in certain neighborhoods or among certain groups), and documentation_completeness may reflect unequal access to assistance, digital literacy, or clarity of instructions rather than true diligence. If these variables are biased upward for some groups, then both routing decisions (e.g., to Fraud Review) and adjusted models that control for these covariates will understate true disparities. This mechanism is primarily concerning because it can make disparity appear justified or “explained” when in fact the underlying measures are unfair.

5. Policy design that undervalues timeliness of support steps  
Because language support and accommodation review are framed as rights‑preserving but are excluded from explicit service‑level targets, frontline managers may prioritize meeting timeliness standards on the main eligibility path while allowing support queues to grow. This can lead to systematic delays for applicants who invoke their rights, reinforcing a perception that asking for help slows you down. While not necessarily driven by animus, this design choice creates structural unfairness.

6. Regional and organizational variation  
Backlogs in certain regions and differences in local practices (e.g., thresholds for Supervisor Review or fraud referral) may contribute to group disparities if certain groups are over‑represented in those regions or offices. For instance, minority‑language communities may be spatially concentrated where bilingual staff are scarce or where regional officers are overloaded. This mechanism suggests that some disparities may be mitigated by targeted regional interventions rather than purely system‑wide redesign.


# 5. Limitations of available attributes

Measurement bias in key covariates  
Risk_score_band is derived from a scoring model that may encode historical enforcement patterns, biased data sources, or proxy variables correlated with protected characteristics. Documentation_completeness is influenced by language barriers, access to assistance, literacy, and digital access, not just applicant diligence. Treating these variables as fully neutral measures of true risk or complexity risks underestimating unfairness: disadvantaged groups may appear “objectively riskier” or “less complete” because the system is harder for them to navigate, not because their claims are less meritorious.

Missing applicant needs and contextual factors  
The log includes whether accommodation was requested and language_preference, but it does not capture the severity or type of disability, the quality of communication, or the applicant’s ability to self‑advocate. Nor does it measure socioeconomic stress, housing instability, or other factors that might shape both outcomes and process needs. As a result, we cannot fully adjust for genuine differences in need, nor can we distinguish between cases where extra steps reflect appropriate individualized support versus bureaucratic friction.

Unobserved resource workload and staffing patterns  
Resource workload is only observed at daily granularity and we lack detailed measures of caseworker availability, task prioritization, and concurrent responsibilities. We know that certain workers are overloaded, but not precisely how their queues evolve during the day or how tasks are triaged. Without finer‑grained workload data, it is difficult to disentangle unfair assignment (certain groups being systematically allocated to overloaded staff) from unavoidable congestion due to overall resource scarcity.

Appeal quality and downstream justice  
The event log records an appeal_flag and some appeal‑related activities, but not the quality of appeal handling, representation, or outcomes (e.g., whether appeals are successful, how much the decision changes, or whether explanations are improved). High appeal rates for certain groups may indicate that initial decisions or explanations are weaker for them, but without detailed appeal content we cannot know whether appeals effectively correct injustices or simply add more burden. Thus, we can only partially infer procedural fairness from the presence of appeals.

Historical bias and incomplete coverage of fairness dimensions  
The attributes primarily cover operational and administrative variables, with limited insight into applicants’ experiences of dignity, respect, or stigma. Historical bias may have shaped current policies (e.g., stricter scrutiny of certain neighborhoods or demographics) in ways not fully captured by risk_score_band or region. Because we cannot observe protected characteristics beyond applicant_group, nor the broader social context, our assessment of fairness is necessarily constrained and must remain cautious about attributing causes or ruling out discrimination.


# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Adding capacity for language support or accommodation review  
Increasing the number of bilingual caseworkers and accommodation specialists, or cross‑training more general caseworkers in these functions, would reduce queues at Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review, lowering timeliness disparities and procedural burden for Groups B and C. Side effects include budgetary and hiring constraints, potential dilution of specialized expertise if cross‑training is superficial, and the risk that increased capacity could be used primarily to process more cases without improving quality if not coupled with explicit fairness objectives.

Service‑level targets for support steps  
Establishing explicit timeliness targets for rights‑preserving steps (e.g., maximum wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment, from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment) would signal that these steps are as important as core eligibility processing. This can prevent de‑prioritization of vulnerable applicants in operational decisions. However, if targets are enforced rigidly without added capacity, managers may respond by discouraging use of support steps or rushing through them, potentially harming quality; careful design and monitoring are needed to avoid such unintended consequences.

Routing redesign to reduce handovers  
Redesigning routing to minimize unnecessary handovers—for instance, by assigning a single primary caseworker who can coordinate language support or accommodation specialists, or by embedding bilingual and accommodation expertise within general teams—could reduce delays, miscommunication, and fragmentation for B and C cases. A trade‑off is that consolidating responsibility may concentrate discretion in fewer hands and require stronger training and oversight to prevent biased escalation or documentation demands; additionally, reconfiguring teams can be disruptive in the short term.

Decision‑explanation improvements  
Improving the clarity, accessibility, and linguistic appropriateness of decision explanations—especially for denials and partial approvals—can reduce appeal and rework burdens. This includes using plain language, providing translated materials, explaining what evidence was missing, and offering clear next steps. Side effects may include longer drafting time per decision and the need for staff training or tooling (e.g., templates), but better explanations can also improve trust and reduce repetitive contacts over time, potentially offsetting initial costs.

Monitoring adjusted disparities  
Institutionalizing routine monitoring of adjusted disparities in approval rates, cycle times, routing patterns, and appeal/rework by applicant_group (and by region, channel, and support usage) can help detect emerging fairness issues. This should include dashboards that present both raw and adjusted metrics, plus confidence intervals and sensitivity analyses to avoid over‑interpreting noise. Side effects could include “metric gaming” if staff feel pressured to meet targets without adequate support, and the risk that complex models obscure underlying fairness concerns; transparency and stakeholder engagement are therefore important.

Auditing risk_score_band and documentation_completeness  
Audits of the models and processes that generate risk_score_band and documentation_completeness classifications should evaluate predictive performance and bias across groups, including counterfactual or reweighting analyses. Potential mitigation includes recalibrating risk thresholds, removing problematic predictors, or supplementing automated assessments with structured human review for borderline cases. Side effects include the cost and complexity of model redevelopment, possible short‑term increases in processing time, and the risk that loosening scrutiny inappropriately could increase fraud or error if not carefully designed.

Additional cross‑cutting options  
Other options include targeted training on implicit bias and equitable documentation practices, standardizing criteria for Supervisor Review triggers, and involving community advisory groups in redesigning forms and guidance. These interventions can improve procedural justice but require sustained commitment and may face organizational resistance if perceived as additional burdens without clear operational benefits.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

After controlling for observed complexity, the process still shows meaningful residual disparities in approval probability, cycle time, use of discretionary reviews, and appeal rates across applicant groups, with Groups B and C in particular bearing greater procedural burden and longer waits, especially in rights‑preserving support queues. Some differences are plausibly attributable to genuine complexity (e.g., higher measured risk and prior‑case counts for Group D, more accommodation requests for Group C, and language needs for Group B), but capacity constraints, routing patterns, and possible bias in risk and documentation measures mean that not all disparities can be confidently deemed legitimate. Given data limitations and potential measurement bias, we cannot definitively label the process discriminatory, yet the evidence is strong enough to conclude that it is not procedurally fair in its current form, especially for minority‑language applicants and applicants requiring accommodation. A cautious but clear bottom line is that the system exhibits structurally unfair burdens and timeliness gaps that warrant mitigation, ongoing monitoring, and deeper audit rather than being written off as mere reflections of case mix.
