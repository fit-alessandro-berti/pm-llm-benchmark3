# 2. Scenario forecasts

## Optimistic Scenario
**Conditions:** Shared service is right-sized; routing rules work; local staff adapt quickly; no absence shocks.

### Handover pattern
- Nominal handover reductions materialize: local tech loops drop from 31 to ~15 freq/100.
- Cross-team handovers drop from 6 to 3 freq/100 (resilience gain).
- New central queues are short (median wait 4.0h  3.8h achieved); no tail buildup.

### Queue/waiting-time pattern
- Technical Shared Service absorbs 41 cases/100 without queue formation.
- Waiting time at shared approval queue: median 3.0h, 90th percentile 5.0h (acceptable).
- Service utilization rises to 85-88% (safe from oscillation).

### Cycle-time distribution
- Median overall cycle time: 6.8d  6.5d (slight improvement due to standardized approvals).
- 90th percentile: 16.4d  14.2d (tail improves due to fewer ad hoc reworks).
- High-complexity cases: 10d  11.5d (queue wait offset by fewer local rework loops).

### Rework and quality pattern
- Rework rate stable: 8-9% (no increase).
- Specialist rework: 9%  8% (central specialists are better trained).
- Informal advice prevention yields cleaner referrals  lower error rate.

### Role clarity and accountability
- Routing rules are explicit and followed.
- Shared approval queue establishes clear decision ownership.
- Local caseworkers understand escalation paths.
- Escalation time post-approval is predictable.

### Fairness implications
- East gains 1.5-2.0d cycle time improvement (was bottlenecked by E_Tech).
- North/South stable (lost local loops, but higher specialist quality compensates).
- High-complexity cases see modest wait, offset by better decisions (fewer reworks).
- Customer satisfaction: 4.1-4.3/5 across all teams.

### Early warning signs (triggers for exit)
- Shared service queue backlog exceeds 10 cases; median wait climbs above 5h.
- Rework rate in high-complexity cases jumps above 12%.
- Supervisor escalation frequency rises above 15% of cases (indicates role confusion).
- East caseworker satisfaction drops below 3.5/5 (indicates loss of local knowledge).

## Central Scenario

### Handover pattern
- Local tech loops: 31  22 freq/100 (partial compression; some informal loops persist).
- Cross-team handovers: 6  4 freq/100 (slight improvement, not full gain).
- Central queue becomes visible: 41 cases/100 to shared service, but queue length varies.

### Queue/waiting-time pattern
- Technical Shared Service median wait: 4.0h  4.8h (as forecast indicates).
- 90th percentile wait: 6.0h  9.2h (first bottleneck warning).
- Shared approval queue: median 3.0h, 90th percentile 6.5h.
- Waiting time variability increases (coefficient of variation  40%).

### Cycle-time distribution
- Median overall: 6.8d  7.1d (slight degradation, not improvement).
- 90th percentile: 16.4d  17.8d (tail lengthens due to queue buildups).
- High-complexity cases: 10d  12.5d (queue wait + context loss).
- Complexity-driven variance: cases with `case_complexity` = "high" show 90th percentile 14d  18d.

### Rework and quality pattern
- Rework rate: 8.2%  10.1% (modest increase post-specialist handover).
- Specialist rework: 9%  11% (context loss during queue; returned advice needs rework).
- Quality checks by shared service: longer TAT, more batch processing  inconsistency.
- Rework clustering: 60% of rework occurs after specialist handover (vs. 40% currently).

### Role clarity and accountability
- Routing rules are followed, but edge cases emerge ("is this complex enough?").
- Shared approval queue has clear ownership, but supervisor escalations linger (3-4 days median).
- Local caseworkers report uncertainty: "Is the specialist going to contact me?"
- Accountability diffusion: "Did we fix the root cause or did shared service miss something?"

### Fairness implications

### Early warning signs (triggers for adjustment)

- Rework rate post-specialist climbs to 11% (week 4-5).
- 90th percentile cycle time for high-complexity cases exceeds 18d (week 5-6).
- Supervisor escalation rate reaches 12% of cases (role confusion spreading).

## Adverse Scenario
**Conditions:** Shared service undersized; high arrival variability; absence during ramp-up; local expertise atrophies; customer complaints cascade.

### Handover pattern
- Local tech loops: 31  28 freq/100 (staff work around central bottleneck; hidden loops form).
- Cross-team handovers: 6  8 freq/100 (teams try to find faster paths; workarounds emerge).
- Central queue overflows: 41 cases/100 arrive; avg. service rate ~30 cases/100  backlog grows.
- Handover-of-work network shows new edges (workarounds) not in routing rules.

### Queue/waiting-time pattern
- Technical Shared Service median wait: 4.0h  8.2h (bottleneck + non-linear queueing).
- 90th percentile wait: 6.0h  18.5h (severe tail).
- Shared approval queue: median 3.0h, 90th percentile 12.0h (cascading backlog).
- Queue length reaches 8-12 cases (observable in activity log as "Pending Specialist" status).
- Waiting time oscillates: some days fast (3h), others slow (14h) due to specialist absence/focus shifts.

### Cycle-time distribution
- Median overall: 6.8d  8.4d (material degradation).
- 90th percentile: 16.4d  24.6d (SLA violations common).
- High-complexity cases: 10d  16.5d (queue + rework).
- SLA miss rate: 6.5% (North), 7.2% (South), 9.8% (East)  14%, 16%, 11% respectively.
- Cases over 20d grow from ~2% to ~12% of volume.

### Rework and quality pattern
- Rework rate: 8.2%  14.5% (severe; context loss + rushing).
- Specialist rework: 9%  17% (specialist returns case for missing info  local rework  re-submit  wait again).
- Quality check rework: 5%  9% (QC backlog; checks become cursory).
- Rework loops: cases re-enter shared queue 2-3 times (visible as `reassignment_count` > 2).
- Outcome = "Rework" frequency rises sharply (week 4-8).

### Role clarity and accountability
- Routing rules are ignored or contested ("This should be simple, not complex").
- Shared approval queue loses traction; informal approvals bypass it (workarounds).
- Local supervisors overrule routing rules to get cases through faster (role confusion).
- Specialist ownership is unclear: "Did they really look at this?" Escalations rise.
- Accountability: "Specialist said it's wrong, but we don't know how to fix it"  loop.

### Fairness implications
- East gains 0.5d (modest; still bottlenecked by shared service).
- North/South: cycle time  (6.8d  8.2d+), rework   customer satisfaction 4.3/5  2.9/5 (severe).
- High-complexity (enterprise): 10d  16.5d; satisfaction 4.0/5  2.5/5 (churn risk).
- Low-complexity (SMB): 6.5d  8.0d; satisfaction 3.8/5  3.0/5 (acceptable but eroded).
- Regional/language customers: longest waits (shared service not briefed); satisfaction  2.2/5 (at risk).
- Fairness: restructuring hurts North/South and high-value customers most (opposite of stated goal).

### Early warning signs (triggers for rollback)
- Shared service queue median wait exceeds 8h (week 2-3) ** ALERT**.
- Rework rate climbs above 12% (week 3-4) ** ALERT**.
- 90th percentile cycle time exceeds 22d (week 3-4) ** ALERT**.
- SLA miss rate jumps above 12% (week 4-5) ** CRITICAL**.
- Supervisor escalation rate exceeds 15% (week 3-4).
- Customer complaints (NPS, satisfaction surveys) drop below 3.0/5 (week 5-6) ** ROLLBACK**.
- Staff feedback: "This isn't working" appears in >20% of feedback (week 3-4).
- Workaround handovers (unrouted cross-team transfers) emerge (week 2-3).

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

## Risk 1: Tail Latency and SLA Violation Spike

**Mechanism:** Average cycle time may improve (6.8d  6.5d in optimistic scenario) because the modal case type (low/medium complexity) is routed faster through streamlined approval. However, high-complexity cases accumulate in the shared-service queue, pushing their 90th percentile latency from 12d  18d (central scenario) or 20d+ (adverse scenario).

**Why it's hidden:** Management reporting typically shows median or mean cycle time. A 6.5d median conceals that 10% of cases now take 20d (vs. 16d previously). High-value or enterprise cases cluster in the high-complexity segment; they experience disproportionate delays. SLA thresholds (e.g., 15d for enterprise) are breached at 8-12% rate (vs. 6-7% now), triggering financial penalties or contract violations.

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**
```python
# pm4py filtering & percentile analysis
high_complexity_cases = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log, 'case_complexity', ['high'])
cycle_times_hc = pm4py.get_service_time(high_complexity_cases)
# percentiles = [10, 25, 50, 75, 90]
# Plot: pre-restructuring 90th = 12d; post-restructuring 90th = 18d
# Even if median(cycle_times) improves, percentile(0.90) worsens.
```

**Mitigation:**
- Monitor 90th percentile latency by case_complexity and customer_segment separately.
- Set SLA-miss-rate as a guardrail metric (trigger rollback if >10%).
- Presume specialist queue capacity is 30-36 cases/week; if arrival > 40 cases/week, queue forms.

## Risk 2: Queueing Cliff and Non-linear Behavior

**Mechanism:** Shared-service capacity is a hard resource limit. Under arrival rates 41 cases/100/week and service capacity ~36-40 cases/100/week, the system is operating near critical utilization (  1.0–1.1). In queueing theory (M/M/1 or M/M/c models), waiting time grows non-linearly as  approaches 1.0: W  W / (1 - ). A 10% increase in arrival rate (41  45 cases/100) causes waiting time to double or triple, not scale linearly.

**Why it's hidden:** Week 1-2, the shared service has spare capacity (  0.8-0.9); average wait = 3.5h. Week 3-4, as backlog accumulates and   1.0, a case arriving on Day 21 may wait 10h, while Day 22's case waits 2h (oscillation). Management sees "average wait = 5.5h" and doesn't realize the system is unstable; a single absence or a high-complexity case batch triggers a collapse.

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**
```python
# Time-series analysis of waiting times
import pandas as pd
waits = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(log)
# Group by date; extract median wait per day
daily_median_wait = waits.groupby(
    pd.to_datetime(waits['time:timestamp']).dt.date
)['case_service_waiting_time'].median()
# Plot: if curve shows hockey-stick or exponential rise after week 2-3, system is approaching saturation.
# Coefficient of variation (std / mean) of daily wait > 0.5 = oscillation/instability.
```

**Mitigation:**
- Perform capacity simulation before rollout (e.g., discrete-event simulation with M/M/3 queue).
- Monitor daily or 8-hourly median wait time; if exceeds 5h, escalate.
- Ensure shared service can add a 4th specialist by week 2 if needed (contingency plan).

## Risk 3: Context Loss and Silent Rework

**Mechanism:** Local caseworkers who interact with embedded specialist (N_Tech) have rich contextual knowledge: "This customer always disputes final invoices" or "The regulatory database was updated last week." When a case goes to a central shared-service queue, the specialist receives a case summary, not context. The specialist issues advice; the local caseworker implements it. However, the advice is generic: "Re-check bank account validation." The local caseworker re-does the work, discovers an issue missed by the specialist, and sends the case back. This rework loop is now central  local  central, not local  specialist  local. Rework rate increases subtly, and the root cause is invisible in activity logs.

**Why it's hidden:** Event logs record activities and handovers, but not the information asymmetry. A case appears to be "Specialist Review  Implementation  Quality Check  Rework" (visible), but the real cause is "Specialist lacked context about recent regulatory change" (unobservable). Rework rate post-specialist-handover rises from 9%  12%, but it's attributed to "quality issues" rather than "context loss." By the time staff realize the pattern (week 6-8), hundreds of cases have reworked.

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**
```python
# Rework rate by handover source
rework_post_specialist = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log, 'handover_reason', ['specialist'])
rework_cases = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(rework_post_specialist)
# Filter for Specialist Review activity
rework_rate_specialist = len(rework_cases[rework_cases['activity'] == 'Specialist Review']) / len(rework_post_specialist)
# Pre: 9%  Post: 12%+ = signal

# Textual analysis: if case notes include phrases like "Specialist missed X" or "Context unclear", count them
# Text mining on "outcome" or "notes" fields
```

**Mitigation:**
- Require shared-service specialists to review case context (e.g., customer history, recent rule changes).
- Implement structured case summaries (template) to encode context.
- Monitor rework rate by handover source and by specialist (if one specialist causes 15%+ rework, flag).
- Run 2-3 week pilot with one specialist; measure rework carefully.

## Risk 4: Fairness Divergence Across Teams and Customer Segments

**Mechanism:** The restructuring is intended to standardize and improve service. However, it has asymmetric effects:

- **Team divergence:** East was bottlenecked by overloaded E_Tech (82% util.). Centralizing specialists removes this constraint; East teams gain 1-2 days. North/South had smooth local loops (N_Tech 71%, S_Tech 68%); they lose local expertise access. In the central scenario, North/South median cycle time stalls or rises, while East improves. Fairness metric: Gini coefficient of cycle times across teams rises (inequality increases).

- **Customer-segment divergence:** High-complexity cases (enterprise, regulatory) queue at shared service. Low-complexity cases (SMB, standard) stay local. High-complexity cases experience 4-6h wait; low-complexity cases wait <1h. A fairness assumption is that high-value customers should be served first, but this structure serves them last (queue discipline is FIFO by default).

- **Regional/language divergence:** Customers needing language support or regional expertise (e.g., compliance in Spain vs. Germany) are served by a central, English-speaking shared service. They experience longer handover chains (local  shared  local) and less contextual accuracy. Satisfaction drops.

**Why it's hidden:** Monthly reports show "North cycle time = 7.0d, South = 7.2d, East = 7.8d." Averages are similar, so fairness seems OK. However, 90th percentile for North high-complexity = 20d, South = 19d, East = 15d. East is now fastest and happiest; North/South are slowest and less happy. Satisfaction surveys may show overall NPS stable (because East's +1.5 points offsets North's -0.8 points), but customer-segment satisfaction reveals the bias.

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**
```python
# Cycle time by team and complexity
for team in ['North', 'South', 'East']:
    for complexity in ['low', 'medium', 'high']:
        filtered = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(log, 'team', [team])
        filtered = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(filtered, 'case_complexity', [complexity])
        ct = pm4py.get_service_time(filtered)

# If gini_post > gini_pre, inequality increased.

# Satisfaction by customer_segment

satisfaction.groupby('customer_segment')['satisfaction'].mean()
# Enterprise pre: 4.0  post: 3.6 (decline); SMB pre: 3.8  post: 3.7 (stable)
```

**Mitigation:**
- Define fairness targets: e.g., "90th percentile cycle time shall not exceed current level by team."
- Monitor metrics separately by team and customer segment; flag if any segment declines.
- Implement priority queueing (high-value cases jump queue) if queue forms.
- Ensure shared-service team includes regional expertise (e.g., one specialist fluent in Spanish).

## Risk 5: Learning and Skill Atrophy

**Mechanism:** North and South caseworkers (N_Case1/2, S_Case1/2) currently learn from embedded specialists (N_Tech, S_Tech) through osmosis: they observe how specialists handle edge cases, ask questions, and gradually build expertise. Over 1-2 years, caseworkers can escalate only 30-40% of cases; over 3 years, they escalate 20% (growing competence).

With centralization, caseworkers stop interacting with technical specialists. They no longer learn. More problematically, *they expect the specialist to solve it*, so they stop trying. After 6 months, caseworkers forget how to troubleshoot; after 1 year, they cannot recognize obvious errors. Specialist rework rate increases because caseworkers submit incomplete or incorrect information. Specialists become a dumping ground, not a training resource.

Long-term effect: the organization becomes brittle. Specialists are overloaded (because caseworkers atrophied). If a specialist leaves, no one internally can replace them. Caseworkers cannot move into specialist roles because they lack the gradual skill-building path.

**Why it's hidden:** This emerges over months 3-6, after the pilot or initial rollout. Early metrics (weeks 1-4) show no learning effect. By month 3, rework rate may rise slightly (11% vs. 9%), but it's attributed to "shared service quality" rather than "caseworker competence declined." Staff satisfaction surveys in month 6 reveal: "I don't feel like I'm learning anymore," but HR doesn't connect this to restructuring. A year later, North/South have lost 2-3 technical escalation roles and cannot backfill them (no internal pipeline).

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**


**Mitigation:**
- Intentionally pair caseworkers with shared-service specialists for 1-2 weeks post-restructuring (onboarding).
- Create "clinic" sessions: 1h/week where a specialist reviews 3-4 recent caseworker decisions (feedback loop).
- Monitor escalation rate per caseworker; if rising, escalate to manager.
- Include "training" as a KPI for shared service (not just throughput).

## Risk 6: Accountability Diffusion and Cascading Escalations

**Mechanism:** Under the current structure, a case is owned by a local team from intake to close. If a quality check fails, the local supervisor is accountable. They know the caseworker, the customer, and the issue; they can give direct feedback and oversee rework.

Under the proposed structure, accountability is diffused across boundaries:
- Local caseworker owns intake, eligibility, initial assessment.
- Shared specialist owns specialist review.
- Shared approval queue owns approval.
- Local team owns implementation and quality check.

If quality check fails after specialist review, who is accountable? The specialist says, "I gave correct advice, but the local team implemented it wrong." The caseworker says, "The specialist's advice was incomplete, so I had to guess." The supervisor (one removed) is not present to mediate.

Result: cases linger in escalation loops. A rework case needs to be resubmitted to shared specialist, but the specialist says, "I already reviewed this; escalate to shared approval." Shared approval says, "This is a specialist issue, not an approval issue." Case bounces back to local team. This loop can last 5-10 days, adding invisible latency.

**Why it's hidden:** Activity logs show the case moved from A  B  C  A  D, but the *reason* (accountability diffusion) is not recorded. Escalation frequency rises from 8% to 15% of cases (measurable), but the root cause is organizational, not procedural. Management may interpret it as "teams are not following routing rules" rather than "no one is owning the case."

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**
```python
# Escalation/rework cycles
rework_cases = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
rework_cases['cycle_count'] = rework_cases.groupby('case:concept:name').cumcount()
# If cycle_count > 1, case has reworked multiple times (escalation loops)

# Time between rework and next specialist review
rework_to_specialist = log[log['outcome'] == 'Rework'].merge(
    log[log['activity'] == 'Specialist Review'],
    on='case:concept:name'
)
rework_to_specialist['escalation_time'] = (
    rework_to_specialist['time:timestamp_y'] - rework_to_specialist['time:timestamp_x']
)
# If median escalation_time > 2d, cases are stuck in loops

# Supervisor approval rework
supervisor_reworks = log[(log['activity'] == 'Supervisor Approval') & (log['outcome'] == 'Rework')].shape[0]
# If this increases post-restructuring, approval is ineffective
```

**Mitigation:**
- Assign a "case owner" (caseworker or supervisor) throughout the case lifecycle.
- Implement a case-status update protocol: specialist must contact caseworker after advice (not just queue output).
- Create an escalation SLA: if a case is in escalation loop for >3 days, escalate to director.
- Reduce number of handovers per case; design for "one touch" as much as possible.

## Risk 7: Absence and Resilience Fragility

**Mechanism:** Centralizing 3 specialists (N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech) into one shared-service pool is intended to improve resilience: if N_Tech is absent, S_Tech or E_Tech can cover. However, this is naive.

Reality: Specialists have specialized skills. N_Tech may focus on regulatory compliance; S_Tech on customer data validation; E_Tech on system integration. When N_Tech is absent, S_Tech cannot simply cover 100% of North's cases. Shared service must route cases to the "right" specialist, but during absence, the right specialist is unavailable. Cases either queue or are routed to a non-expert, increasing error rates.

Furthermore, shared service expertise is opaque to local teams. When N_Tech was embedded in North, everyone knew, "N_Tech is the go-to for compliance issues; contact them directly if urgent." With centralized shared service, no one knows who to ask. Cases are queued anonymously, routed by algorithm, and processed by whoever is available.

Under an absence event, queue growth is non-linear. If N_Tech is absent and 36% of North's cases require specialist review, 36% of North's cases enter the shared queue. If shared service has 2 available specialists (one absent), and they can process 18 cases/day, but queue receives 20 cases/day from North alone (plus South and East), the system spirals. Absence creates a cascading bottleneck that persists for days after the specialist returns (backlog clearance).

**Why it's hidden:** Absence events are episodic. Post-restructuring week 2, if a specialist is absent, queue waits spike to 12h for a day, then return to 4h. Management says, "The spike was temporary; system is resilient." But the backlog (cases waiting in queue) persists for 3-5 days. Cases that entered queue on the absence day leave queue 3 days late, delaying downstream work. The latency is real but diffuse (spread across multiple cases over several days), not concentrated.

**Observable signal (PM4Py):**
```python
# Identify absence events: days with 0 or 1 specialist activity
specialist_activities = log[log['org:resource'].isin(['N_Tech', 'S_Tech', 'E_Tech', 'TechSharedService'])]
activities_per_day = specialist_activities.groupby(
    pd.to_datetime(specialist_activities['time:timestamp']).dt.date
).size()
absence_days = activities_per_day[activities_per_day < 5].index  # Threshold: <5 activities/day suggests absence

# Waiting time on absence days vs. normal days
waiting_time_absence = log[pd.to_datetime(log['time:timestamp']).dt.date.isin(absence_days)]['service_waiting_time']
waiting_time_normal = log[~pd.to_datetime(log['time:timestamp']).dt.date.isin(absence_days)]['service_waiting_time']
# If median(waiting_time_absence) > 2 × median(waiting_time_normal), absence has material impact
```

**Mitigation:**
- Cross-train specialists; ensure each can cover 60% of another's work.
- Maintain a surge capacity plan: who covers if one specialist is absent (e.g., hire temp, defer non-urgent cases).
- Monitor queue length daily; if queue >8 cases, trigger surge capacity plan.
- Establish "absence forecast" so local teams know who to contact for urgent cases.

# 5. Pilot design

## Pilot Scope

### Geographic/Team Scope
**Recommendation:** Pilot with Team East + partial Team South (one sub-team or one location within South).

**Rationale:**
- **East:** Already has the most stressed specialist (E_Tech 82% util.); most likely to benefit. Geographic isolation reduces contagion if pilot fails.
- **Partial South:** One caseworker sub-team (e.g., South-Location-A with S_Case1 and one intake staff) to test on a familiar team without full disruption.
- **Exclude North:** Preserve a control group; North can serve as "stable baseline" if pilot degrades.

### Process Scope
**In Pilot:**
1. Intake, Local Eligibility, Local Assessment (unchanged).
2. Technical Specialist Review: routed to centralized shared service (2 specialists: E_Tech + 1 temporary contractor or S_Tech part-time).
3. Specialist advice returns to local caseworker via shared queue (not direct handover).
4. All other activities (approval, implementation, QC) remain local.

**Out of Pilot (to narrow scope):**
- Do not pilot shared approval queue; keep one local supervisor for East + South-A.
- Do not pilot Legal/Risk centralization; keep this as-is.
- Focus on technical-specialist handover pattern only.

### Duration
- **Pre-pilot observation:** 4 weeks (baseline data collection).
- **Pilot period:** 8 weeks (steady-state behavior).
- **Post-pilot evaluation:** 2 weeks (ramp-down or decision point).
- **Total:** 14 weeks.

### Segmentation

#### By Team
- **Pilot East:** Cases routed from E_Case1, E_Case2 to TechSharedService_pilot.
- **Pilot South-A:** Cases routed from S_Case1 (sub-sample or one location) to TechSharedService_pilot.
- **Control North:** Unchanged; baseline.
- **Control South-B:** Other half of South (if splittable) unchanged.

#### By Case Complexity
- **Low:** Routed locally (no change in pilot).
- **Medium:** Routed to shared service **only if `required_specialty` in {technical}**; else local.
- **High:** All routed to shared service.

#### By Customer Segment
- **Enterprise:** Track separately; expect highest wait variance.
- **SMB:** Track separately; expect minimal impact.
- **Startup:** Track separately; usually low-complexity; expect no impact.

#### By Priority
- **High:** Ensure shared service reserves capacity for same-day turnaround (e.g., 4h max wait).
- **Normal:** Baseline (forecast 4-6h wait).
- **Low:** Can accept longer waits (forecast 6-12h acceptable).

# 6. Recommendation

## Recommendation: **PILOT FIRST, with structured monitoring and defined guardrails**

### Conditional Proceed (Post-Pilot)

**The proposed restructuring has merit** (standardization, specialist utilization) **but carries substantial risks** (bottleneck formation, local expertise loss, fairness divergence) **that are invisible in average metrics and will only emerge over weeks 3-8 of operation.**

**Action:** Do **not** roll out restructuring organization-wide immediately. Instead:

1. **Execute the 14-week pilot design above** (East + South-A teams, technical specialist centralization only, 2-specialist shared service model).

2. **Measure the seven guardrail metrics daily/weekly** (queue length, wait times, rework, SLA miss, variance, reassignment count, staff satisfaction).

3. **Prepare contingency plans:**
   - If queue forms (weeks 2-3), hire a 3rd temporary specialist immediately (do not wait for decision point).
   - If rework spikes (weeks 3-4), pause new case submissions to shared service for 48h while investigating root cause.
   - If a specialist absence occurs, trigger surge-capacity protocol (e.g., defer non-urgent cases).

4. **Conduct root-cause analysis on rework cases** (text mining and interviews) to distinguish "specialist quality issue" from "context loss" from "caseworker incompetence."

5. **Measure fairness explicitly** (cycle time by customer segment, SLA compliance by segment, NPS by segment) to ensure the restructuring does not unfairly advantage East at the expense of North/South.

6. **At week 10, make a GO/NO-GO/ADJUST decision** based on the guardrails above.

## What Management Should **Not** Infer

###  False Inference 1: "Average Cycle Time Improved, So Restructuring Works"

**Reality:** Average cycle time of 6.5d could conceal:
- 50% of cases are faster (5d).
- 40% of cases are unchanged (7d).
- 10% of cases are much slower (16d) due to queue.

If management targets SLA at 15d, the 10% in the tail miss SLA. If these are high-value customers, churn risk is high. **Do not rely on mean/median alone; measure percentiles (50th, 90th, 95th) by segment.**

###  False Inference 2: "Specialist Utilization Increased, So Efficiency Improved"

**Reality:** Specialist utilization of 90% is great on paper but operates the shared service near saturation (  0.9–1.0 in queueing terms). A small increase in arrival rate (5%) or decrease in service capacity (one absence) causes waiting times to double. **Utilization >85% is risky; target 75-80% for resilience.**

###  False Inference 3: "Rework Stayed Flat, So Quality Didn't Degrade"

**Reality:** If rework rate stayed 8-9%, but rework *causes* are shifting from "intake error" (now handled locally) to "specialist context loss" (latent in restructured model), the nature of rework is changing. Future rework may climb as cases accumulate. **Monitor rework *causes* (via text analysis or root-cause reviews), not just rates.**

###  False Inference 4: "East Improved, So Restructuring Benefited All Teams"

**Reality:** East may improve because E_Tech was the bottleneck. North/South may degrade because they lose local expertise. Overall headcount is the same, but distribution of benefit is unfair. **Measure fairness explicitly: is any team or customer segment worse off? If yes, is the restructuring still justified?**

###  False Inference 5: "Handover Count Dropped, So Coordination Improved"

**Reality:** Handovers may drop (local  specialist loops disappear), but new handovers form (local  queue  specialist  local). Handover count is the same; complexity is hidden in queue waits. **Measure both direct handovers and indirect handovers (via queues) together; measure waiting time, not just count.**

###  False Inference 6: "Staff Feedback Was Positive, So Change Is Accepted"

**Reality:** Staff may say "I understand the process" (Q1 survey) but still experience inefficiency (Q5 "prefer new process" scores are low). Or, staff from East say "Great!" (East improved) while North staff say "This is worse" (North degraded). **Segment feedback by team/role; look for divergence.**

## Key Monitoring Philosophy

**Do not assume stable, predictable behavior.** Queueing systems exhibit phase transitions. For weeks 1-2, the shared service has spare capacity and behaves like the current system. In weeks 3-4, queue builds ( approaches 1.0), and waiting times oscillate (non-linear). By week 5-6, either:
- Queue stabilizes if service capacity expands (hire more staff), or
- Queue explodes (waiting times spike 10×, cases miss SLA, customers churn).

**Intervene early.** If guardrails show queue building in week 3, do not wait until week 6 to hire a specialist. Hire immediately. If rework cause analysis (week 4) shows "context loss," redesign case summaries immediately (week 5). Do not let problems fester.

**Treat the pilot as a decision gate, not a foregone conclusion.** The pilot should be designed to *fail fast* if the restructuring is unworkable. If it fails fast (week 3-4), rollback and redesign. If it succeeds, proceed.

```python
def get_handover_waiting_time(log, handover_source_activity, handover_target_activity, source_resource=None):
    """
    Compute waiting time: time between completion of source activity and start of target activity,
    filtered by optional source resource.
    """
    log = pm4py.format_dataframe(log, case_id='case:concept:name', activity_key='concept:name', timestamp_key='time:timestamp')
    
    # Get source events
    source_events = log[log['concept:name'] == handover_source_activity].copy()
    if source_resource:
        source_events = source_events[source_events['org:resource'] == source_resource]
    
    # Get target events
    target_events = log[log['concept:name'] == handover_target_activity].copy()
    
    # Merge on case ID
    merged = pd.merge(
        source_events[['case:concept:name', 'time:timestamp']].rename(columns={'time:timestamp': 'source_time'}),
        target_events[['case:concept:name', 'time:timestamp']].rename(columns={'time:timestamp': 'target_time'}),
        on='case:concept:name',
        how='inner'
    )
    
    # Calculate waiting time (in hours)
    merged['wait_hours'] = (merged['target_time'] - merged['source_time']).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    
    # Filter for positive waits (target is after source)
    merged = merged[merged['wait_hours'] > 0]
    
    return merged['wait_hours']

    if 'local_expertise_flag' in log_baseline_team.columns and 'local_expertise_flag' in log_pilot_team.columns:
        expertise_rate_baseline = (log_baseline_team['local_expertise_flag'] == True).sum() / len(log_baseline_team)

        print(f"  Pilot expertise flag rate:    {expertise_rate_pilot:.2%}")
        print(f"  Change:                       {(expertise_rate_pilot - expertise_rate_baseline):.2%}")
```
