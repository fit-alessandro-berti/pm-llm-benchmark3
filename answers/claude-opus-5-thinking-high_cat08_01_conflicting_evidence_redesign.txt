# Warranty Claim Process: Diagnosis and Three Candidate Redesigns

## 1. Current-process diagnosis

**The headline contradiction.** The dominant path (Intake → Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval → Dispatch replacement → Quality/compliance close) carries roughly 2,300 of 5,000 cases and completes in a few hours end to end: 0.8h mean to auto-screen, 1.0h mean to fast approval, 3.0h mean to dispatch, 5.0h mean to close. Against that, the reviewed/repair path costs 22h (Manual eligibility review → Repair quote), 30h (Repair quote → Customer approval), 20h (Customer approval → Repair device) and a further 9h to quality check. On a naive DFG-performance reading, the fast path wins on both speed and cost. The evidence says this reading is wrong in three specific ways:

1. **The cost advantage is mostly accounting, not economics.** Fast approval is 31% cheaper *before* rework and only 9% cheaper *after* complaints, reshipments and service credits. Two-thirds of the apparent saving is deferred cost, not avoided cost.
2. **The failures surface after the case is closed, so they never land on the fast path's own KPI.** Dispatch replacement → Customer complaint/reopen has mean 96h / median 70h / p90 180h; Fast approval → Customer complaint/reopen has mean 72h / median 50h. The failure signal arrives 10–30× later than the fast path's own cycle time and is re-recorded as a *new* reopened case. Closing-time metrics therefore systematically flatter the fast lane.
3. **The aggregate hides opposite segment truths.** For Premium low-risk accessories the fast path is genuinely better (8h vs 46h median; 6% vs 3% complaints, with service-credit exposure kicking in above 24h — the manual path all but guarantees a credit). For Premium high-value devices it is genuinely worse (22% vs 5% complaints), and for Standard high-value devices likewise (18% vs 6%), with wrong-replacement cost of EUR 410 versus EUR 95 for accessories. One aggregated DFG averaging these two populations cannot support a single routing policy.

**Quality and rework.** Complaint/reopen affects 9.2% of all cases but 14.1% of Fast approval cases and 3.3% of the quote → approval → repair cases. That is consistent with the edge counts: Fast approval is reached 2,790 times (2,550 from auto-screen plus 240 from fraud/risk review) and generates 400 complaint edges downstream (140 direct plus 260 after dispatch) — about 14%. Notably 260 of those 400 arrive *after* dispatch, and the temporal profile attributes that long tail to **incorrect replacement part selection**. So the fast path's dominant defect mode is not "we approved a bad claim", it is "we shipped the wrong thing quickly".

**The evidence loop is the real cycle-time monster.** Request missing evidence is entered 1,430 times (650 from auto-screen, 780 from manual review); it recurs at least twice in 26% of the cases that enter it. Its exit edge to Manual eligibility review is the slowest ordinary edge in the log (mean 34h, median 26h, p90 88h), and 320 of 1,430 — over one in five — end in Claim abandoned. This is the mechanism behind the "unclear defect / missing evidence" segment: 22% of volume, 140h median, highest abandonment. Abandonment is a customer-experience failure that currently *improves* the cycle-time statistics, because abandoned claims exit the process quickly and cheaply.

**Compliance.** Fitness is 0.91 but only 68% of traces fit, and precision is low — the discovered model permits combinations of auto approval, risk review and repair that policy forbids. The alignment diagnostics name the three exposures directly: Fast approval before evidence completeness (R2), Customer complaint/reopen after close (R3), and Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices (R1). The DFG corroborates R3 exposure: complaint/reopen occurs in ~460 cases but only 310 flow back into Manual eligibility review and 120 into the escalation board, so a residual population appears to be closed or resolved without the mandatory review. Because high-value devices are almost certainly above the EUR 400 R1 threshold, the "Dispatch replacement without risk review" pattern is simultaneously the largest quality defect and the largest compliance defect — they are the same cases.

**Resources and queueing.** The delay signature is queueing, not work content: median-to-p90 ratios of 3.4× (Request missing evidence → Manual eligibility review: 26h vs 88h) and 3.5× (Manual eligibility review → Repair quote: 15h vs 60h) indicate waiting in front of a constrained pool, not slow task execution. Manual eligibility review has 7 reviewers × 36 productive hours and degrades sharply above 82% utilization; fraud/risk review has only 2 specialists with Monday and post-campaign peaks; the escalation board is a single 45-minute daily slot with high satisfaction impact and no scalability; quality inspectors are underused outside repair campaigns. Any redesign that pushes 15–35% more inflow into manual review (the two-week auto-rule update) is pushing into a pool with a known non-linear failure point.

**Two caveats on the artifacts themselves.** The DFG does not balance at several nodes — Manual eligibility review shows ~2,600 incoming and 1,400 outgoing occurrences, and only 3,625 of 5,000 cases reach a recorded end state — so it is filtered or truncated. Absolute capacity sizing and exact savings cannot be derived from it; the relative and segment-level patterns are what should drive design. Second, low model precision means "frequent" and "normative" have come apart here, so frequency alone must not be used as an argument for the fast path.

## 2. Redesign A: Value- and risk-tiered routing with a segment-aware screen and compliance guardrails

**Flow.** Replace the single auto-screen with a routing decision at intake that assigns each claim to one of three lanes using claim value, segment, product risk class and defect-clarity signals:

- **Lane 1 — Straight-through (accessories, sub-threshold value, clear defect):** Intake → Auto screen → Fast approval → Dispatch → automated close. No human touch, no pre-dispatch gate.
- **Lane 2 — Controlled decision (mid/high value, anything above EUR 400, ambiguous risk flags):** Intake → Auto screen produces a risk score that runs eligibility and fraud/risk assessment *in parallel* rather than in sequence, feeding a single Technical decision point. Fast approval is technically blocked in this lane unless an R1-qualifying review is recorded.
- **Lane 3 — Evidence-pending:** claims failing a completeness test are parked in a dedicated queue that does *not* consume manual-review capacity until evidence lands (see also Redesign B, which deepens this lane).

R1, R2 and R3 become executable preconditions in the workflow engine: no Fast approval above EUR 400 without a recorded review; no Dispatch without an evidence-completeness token, except for the Premium low-risk-accessory exemption the policy already grants; every reopened claim is forced through Manual eligibility review before close or denial.

**Targets.** The Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval edge (2,550 cases) and the Fast approval → Dispatch replacement edge (2,300), split by segment. It *expands* the fast lane for Premium and Standard low-risk accessories (43% of volume, where fast is better on both speed and complaints) and *withdraws* it from high-value devices (35% of volume, where fast triples-to-quadruples complaint rates and breaches R1/R2).

**Mechanism.** This is a reallocation, not a slowdown: today's fast path is applied on the wrong axis (whatever the outdated rules happen to catch) rather than on the value/risk axis where the outcome data actually splits. Moving high-value claims into a controlled lane attacks the 22%/18% complaint rates and the EUR 410 error cost at once; keeping accessories straight-through protects the 24h Premium service-credit threshold. Making the rules the *carrier* of the normative constraints should raise the 68% fitting-trace figure and, by narrowing permitted behaviour, improve the model's poor precision — the compliance gain is structural rather than exhortative. Parallelising eligibility and fraud/risk attacks the sequential wait chain (11h + 18h + 22h edges) without deleting any control.

**Deliberate sacrifices / unresolved.** It concedes higher manual and fraud/risk load — plausibly the stated 15–35% inflow increase — into pools that break above 82% utilization and that have only 2 fraud specialists. High-value cycle times will get worse before quality savings show up. It depends entirely on claim value, segment and product-risk attributes being correct at intake; if they are missing or gameable, misrouting silently reinstates the R1 breach. It does nothing for the evidence loop, nothing for wrong-part selection inside Lane 1, and nothing for the 12.5% high-value rework rate.

## 3. Redesign B: Evidence-first front door with one-shot completeness and an assisted channel

**Flow.** Invert the order of the front of the process. Intake is followed by a **defect-type-driven completeness check** that generates a single consolidated evidence request (serial number, purchase proof, defect photos/video, symptom questionnaire specific to the defect class) before any eligibility decision is attempted. The claim then sits in an explicit *pending-evidence* state with a paused internal SLA clock, proactive status visibility and reminder cadence, and a hard "assisted fallback": if the customer does not act through the portal within a set window, or is flagged as low-digital-literacy, an agent-guided channel (phone/inbound store/callback) collects the same items. Only completeness-confirmed claims enter eligibility; incomplete claims are resolved by a documented decision rather than being allowed to drift into abandonment.

**Targets.** The 1,430 entries into Request missing evidence, its 26% multi-occurrence rate, the 34h/26h/88h handoff back to Manual eligibility review, the 320 abandonments, and the "unclear defect" segment (22% of volume, 140h median). Secondarily it removes the R2 move-on-log deviation — "Fast approval before evidence completeness" cannot occur if completeness precedes eligibility.

**Mechanism.** Today's loop is iterative discovery: each round of questions triggers another 14h out and 34h back, so two rounds alone account for roughly 96h of the 140h median in the unclear-defect segment. Replacing iteration with a defect-specific checklist converts a multi-round conversation into one round; the portal's stated 30–45% reduction in repeat requests is the direct lever. Crucially, it also removes evidence-chasing work from the manual reviewers' queue: today the review pool is consumed by cases that are not yet decidable, which is the worst possible use of a resource that breaks above 82% utilization. Standard customers are documented as tolerating 72h completion when status visibility is good, so the pending state can be made acceptable rather than invisible.

**Deliberate sacrifices / unresolved.** It accepts an increase in front-door effort for every claim, including the accessory claims that need none — for Premium accessories that risks the 24h credit threshold, so it must be waived for the exempted class. It carries a real chance of *shifting* the failure: the portal may raise abandonment among low-digital-literacy customers, and abandonment is cheap and fast in the data, so a naive dashboard would score this as improvement. It requires paying for an assisted channel that has no line in the current cost model. And it does nothing about wrong replacement parts, high-value complaint rates, R1 breaches, or rework repair.

## 4. Redesign C: Fulfillment integrity — verified exchange, repair-first for high-value, and post-dispatch detection

**Flow.** Leave intake and eligibility largely as-is and rebuild the back half of the process around what is actually shipped:

- **Verified exchange.** Before Dispatch replacement, an automated part-match verification (model/serial/BOM/firmware-variant cross-check against the claimed device) runs in seconds, not hours; only mismatches and a sampled subset of high-value dispatches go to a human inspector. The 6–18h "lightweight quality gate" is deliberately reserved for the exception, not the norm.
- **Repair-first for high-value devices.** For high-value devices the default fulfillment flips from replacement to repair, made viable by two targeted fixes: a maintained parts-price catalog with pre-authorised quote thresholds (removing the stated main cause of quote delay and much of the 30h Repair quote → Customer approval wait, since sub-threshold quotes auto-approve), and an advance-exchange loaner for Premium so the customer is not without a device for 96h.
- **Post-dispatch detection and a compliant reopen lane.** A proactive check-in at 48–72h after dispatch converts silent failures into cheap early corrections inside the detection window implied by the 70h median complaint latency. All reopens enter a dedicated review lane with reserved capacity, satisfying R3 by construction and replacing reliance on the single 45-minute daily escalation slot.

**Targets.** The 260 Dispatch replacement → Customer complaint/reopen cases and their EUR 410 (high-value) / EUR 95 (accessory) error cost; the 22%/18% high-value complaint rates; the 12.5% Rework repair rate on high-value repairs; the underused inspector pool; and the escalation board's scalability limit.

**Mechanism.** This redesign accepts the fast path's decision speed and attacks its *execution* defect instead. The complaint data supports the fulfillment flip directly: 3.3% complaints on quote → approval → repair versus 14.1% on fast approval, and wrong-replacement cost concentrated in exactly the segment being flipped. Placing verification after the decision but before the irreversible shipment is the cheapest possible control point — it does not consume reviewer or fraud-specialist capacity at all, and it reuses inspectors who are idle outside repair campaigns. Post-dispatch outreach exploits the fact that the failure signal already exists in the log with a knowable latency distribution; today the organisation simply waits for the customer to escalate at mean 96h.

**Deliberate sacrifices / unresolved.** It does not fix R1: if high-value claims still reach Fast approval without a qualifying review, the compliance exposure survives even though the quality outcome improves — the sponsor must decide explicitly whether an automated risk score can count as an R1 control, and that is a policy question the data cannot settle. Repair-first collides with a 3-technician pool and the 12.5% high-value rework rate; done too fast it converts a shipping problem into a repair backlog. Loaner stock is working capital not in the current cost model. Proactive outreach will surface dissatisfaction that previously went unreported, so measured complaint rates may rise while real outcomes improve. It leaves the evidence loop and the 22% unclear-defect segment entirely untouched.

## 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A**
- *Attribute failure.* Routing depends on claim value, segment and product-risk data at intake. If value is self-declared or missing, high-value claims fall into Lane 1 and the R1 breach returns silently — worse than today, because everyone now believes the control exists. Mitigate with retrospective audits of routed value against invoice/serial records.
- *Queue collapse.* A 15–35% inflow increase against a pool that degrades above 82% utilization does not degrade linearly. The p90/median spread already visible (60h vs 15h on Manual eligibility review → Repair quote) will widen further, and Premium high-value cases — already flagged in the temporal profile as too slow on this edge — take the hit first.
- *Rule decay.* Today's rules are "outdated and not segment-aware" precisely because nobody owned them; a new rule set without an owner and a review cadence becomes the next legacy screen.
- *Bottleneck migration.* From the (uncapacitated) auto-screen to Manual eligibility review and then to the 2-person fraud/risk team, whose Monday and post-campaign peaks become the binding constraint on the whole high-value lane.

**Redesign B**
- *Abandonment substitution.* Repeat evidence requests fall, abandonment rises among low-digital-literacy customers, and every dashboard improves: fewer loops, shorter cycle time, lower cost. This is the single most dangerous failure mode in the whole portfolio because it looks exactly like success. Abandonment by cohort must be a first-class, pre-registered metric.
- *Over-collection.* A checklist built to cover every defect class asks for too much up front, raising customer effort and drop-off at the very first step, before any commitment has been built.
- *Front-door drag on simple claims.* If the completeness gate is applied to Premium low-risk accessories, the 24h service-credit threshold is at risk for a population where the current process is already good.
- *Bottleneck migration.* From the manual-review ↔ evidence ping-pong to intake/triage and the assisted contact channel; and, second-order, to fraud/risk review, because better evidence surfaces more risk flags into a 2-specialist team.

**Redesign C**
- *Gate over-application.* Applying a 6–18h human quality gate to all dispatches would multiply the inspectors' current load several times over and destroy the fast path's speed advantage — the reason the design restricts humans to mismatches and high-value sampling. If scope creeps back to "inspect everything", the redesign fails on both cost and speed.
- *Repair capacity collision.* Flipping high-value fulfillment to repair loads 3 technicians and a parts pipeline whose delay is already attributed to price confirmation, while 12.5% of high-value repairs need rework — cycle time and rework can both worsen, with the Repair device → Quality check → Rework repair loop becoming the new tail.
- *Metric inversion from outreach.* Proactive 48–72h contact converts silent dissatisfaction into recorded complaints; without a pre/post definition change, the programme will appear to have made quality worse.
- *Bottleneck migration.* From dispatch/logistics to repair technicians, parts sourcing and the quality-check pool, plus a new reverse-logistics load; the escalation board is relieved, but the dedicated reopen lane must be genuinely staffed or R3 compliance simply moves to a different queue.

## 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 0 — Rebuild the measurement before changing anything (2–3 weeks, no process change).**
Nothing downstream is interpretable until the log can attribute late failures to the case that caused them. Concretely: link every Customer complaint/reopen to its originating case; define a 30-day observation window per case (justified by the 96h mean / 180h p90 complaint latency); attach reshipment, service-credit, escalation-minute and wrong-part costs to that originating case; split the DFG and all KPIs by the five documented segments; reconcile the DFG's flow imbalances so capacity claims rest on real volumes; and produce per-rule alignment counts for R1, R2 and R3 by segment.
*Stop/go:* if reopens cannot be linked to origin cases for the large majority of complaints, do not start any redesign pilot — you will be measuring displacement as improvement.

**Stage 1 — Redesign A, narrow and reversible.**
Pilot two changes together: (i) confirm and widen straight-through processing for Premium and Standard low-risk accessories, and (ii) hard-block Fast approval above EUR 400 without an R1-qualifying review. Run it as a stepped-wedge by region or intake channel rather than before/after, because Monday and post-campaign seasonality would otherwise confound the read.
*Metrics:* share of Premium accessory claims closed under 24h; complaint/reopen at 30 days per 1,000 originating cases, by segment; R1 and R2 violation counts; manual reviewer utilization; fraud/risk queue wait, reported separately for Mondays; p50 and p90 cycle time per segment (never the pooled mean).
*Stop/go:* proceed if R1 violations fall to near zero, accessory complaints do not rise by more than about a percentage point, and reviewer utilization stays below the low-80s. Halt or throttle the high-value lane if utilization holds above ~82% for more than a week or high-value p90 grows by more than roughly a quarter without a visible complaint reduction.

**Stage 2 — Redesign C, cheapest component first.**
Deploy automated part-match verification on high-value dispatches only, with human inspection on mismatches and a small random sample; hold repair-first back until the parts-price catalog and pre-authorised quote thresholds are live, then pilot it on Standard high-value devices (highest wrong-replacement exposure, least service-credit sensitivity) before Premium, and only with loaners for Premium.
*Metrics:* wrong-part interceptions per 1,000 high-value dispatches; added hours at p50 and p90 from the gate; Dispatch → complaint edge frequency; Repair quote → Customer approval wait; Rework repair rate; technician and inspector utilization.
*Stop/go:* keep the gate if it adds no more than a few hours at p50 while measurably reducing post-dispatch complaints; abandon it if median added delay approaches 12h or interception rates are negligible. Keep repair-first only if quote wait falls substantially and high-value rework does not rise above its current 12.5%.

**Stage 3 — Redesign B, last and most carefully instrumented.**
Launch the evidence portal with a mandatory assisted fallback from day one, A/B by segment, starting with the unclear-defect population.
*Metrics:* repeat evidence-request rate; time from request to completeness; abandonment rate **by digital-literacy proxy cohort**; assisted-channel volume and cost; downstream fraud/risk inflow; complaint rate at 30 days for portal-completed versus agent-completed claims.
*Stop/go:* continue if repeat requests fall in the 30–45% band *and* abandonment does not rise by more than about a point in any cohort; roll back for cohorts where abandonment rises even if aggregate throughput improves.

**Sequencing logic.** A goes first because it is the cheapest to build (rules in ~2 weeks), it is the only intervention that fixes compliance structurally rather than behaviourally, and it re-segments volume so that later experiments are measured on clean strata instead of a mixed population. C follows because it protects the fast lane that A deliberately preserves for accessories, and because its first component consumes no constrained resource. B comes last because it changes customer behaviour, takes the longest to build, alters the arrival mix into manual review that A has just rebalanced, and has the benefit that is easiest to fake.

**How to tell whether improvements are real rather than displaced.** Five checks, run every cycle: (1) report complaints and cost per 1,000 *originating* cases within the 30-day window, never per closed case, so late failures cannot be laundered into new cases; (2) report the full cost envelope — labour across all pools, wrong-replacement cost, service credits, reverse logistics, escalation minutes — so a saving at the auto-screen that reappears as fraud-specialist overtime is visible as a transfer, not a gain; (3) publish a standing bottleneck panel (reviewer utilization against the 82% line, fraud-specialist wait, technician backlog, inspector load, demand on the single daily escalation slot) so migration is detected before it becomes a queue collapse; (4) re-run conformance checking each period and track both the fitting-trace percentage per rule and model precision — precision should *rise* if the lanes genuinely narrowed behaviour, and if it does not, the guardrails are being bypassed; (5) always segment cycle time by outcome, because abandoned and denied claims close fast and will otherwise make a deteriorating process look like an improving one. Throughout, judge against segment promises — 24h for Premium accessories, roughly 72h with good visibility for Standard — using medians and p90s, not a single pooled average.
