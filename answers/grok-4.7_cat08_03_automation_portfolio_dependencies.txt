## 2. Dependency and prerequisite map

The process forks after classification. KYC and financial-document review are concurrent in practice even though the system stores them in series. Committee packaging is the join: it waits for KYC status, normalized financials, collateral valuation when required, and credit-analysis notes. Customer signing is a legal join: it cannot happen before KYC clearance. Contract preparation is a data join: terms must match the committee decision, and 380 cases already jump from entity resolution straight to contract preparation.

**Upstream data quality.** A6 is only as good as the file A5 assembles. A8 and A9 are only as good as the decision record, the KYC status, and the normalized figures. A7 triggers the wrong population when product or exposure class is wrong, which is the 8% classification defect. Automating registration (A1) or activation (A11) does not improve any of these inputs. The directly-follows edge from entity resolution to contract preparation (380) is a data-quality and control defect: contract logic must key off a fresh KYC-clear event, not off “mismatch case closed.”

**Legal and compliance controls.** KYC clearance precedes signing; provisional signing already occurs in 6.5% of cases. Contract terms must match the decision, with the worst gap on credit-line renewal (14%). High-exposure credit analysis and committee packaging must be different people; violations are 5.8% in the month-end rush. A9 is preventive on the contract rule. A4 is preventive on the entity decision that feeds KYC. A12 is detective. A10 and A11 are amplifiers: they scale whichever control state they inherit. None of these modules takes compliance responsibility off the accountable officers. Each model suggestion, human override, and final decision needs a retained trail.

**Human escalation capacity.** The scarce pool is 3 compliance specialists, with KYC operations dependent on two seniors. A4 is designed to use that pool better. A3 spends it. A pre-classifier that throws borderline hits at the same two people will lengthen the 31-hour queue and the 1,160-case loop back into KYC. Credit analysts are the other capacity hinge. There are 9 of them, they sit in the middle of the working-together network, and a cleaner document inflow without A6 increases their load by the 75-minute normalization task plus the existing 110-minute judgment task. Exception paths that must stay human: low-confidence extractions, unresolved entity matches, non-standard renewal clauses, high-exposure credit judgment, and any KYC hit that is not in the narrow green set.

**External valuation.** Wait-for-external-valuation has essentially no internal service time, a 58-hour median wait, and 19% lateness. It is required in 71% of equipment finance. The current directly-follows path runs credit analysis, then valuation request, then the external wait, then committee packaging, so the 58 hours is added on top of credit rather than overlapped with KYC and document review. A7 can improve who receives the job. It cannot manufacture valuer capacity. The prerequisite is an earlier, complete collateral file and a correct product class. Quality disputes remain a bank risk.

**Core-banking integration.** Only two modules may be integrated deeply in the first six months. A9 (contract and decision data) and A6 (figures written to the credit file) are the deep pair in the recommended set. A5 (portal) and A4 (specialist workbench with human write-back through the existing KYC path) stay shallow. A1, A10, and A11 would also consume deep slots and would crowd out that pair. A8 can start as a read-only assembly over documents already stored, and does not deserve a deep slot while its inputs are inconsistent. Holding the second deep slot for a downstream bot would spend the scarce integration budget on the activity least able to use it safely.

## 3. Portfolio alternatives

Budget is four modules. One of them has to strengthen controls. The first six months allow two deep core-banking integrations.

### Quick-win portfolio: A1, A5, A9, A10

This optimizes feasibility, visible volume, and two large clocks: the document-request loop and the 44-hour signing wait. A9 satisfies the control requirement and removes a large block of legal drafting. A1 delivers the political win senior management first asked for. A5 and A10 can start outside the core; A1 and A9 take the two deep slots, so A10’s identity integration has to wait until after month six even inside this portfolio.

It sacrifices the analyst capacity buffer and the specialist bottleneck. A5 without A6 pushes cleaner files onto analysts who still hand-normalize. A1 shortens a 1.5-hour, low-rework activity performed by a pool that is already slack after 15:00, and moves that volume into classification, KYC, and document review. Entity mismatch stays at 31 hours on the critical path. Equipment-finance valuation stays sequential at 58 hours. Four-eyes is untouched.

### Bottleneck / synchronization portfolio: A4, A5, A6, A7

This optimizes the delays that dominate tails and joins. A5 and A6 shorten the document–normalize–credit chain and protect the 9 analysts. A4 attacks the 3-specialist critical path and the KYC loop. A7, paired with an earlier request, takes the 58-hour valuation off the sequential path into committee packaging for equipment finance. Integration is light: only A6 needs a deep slot, so the second slot stays available.

It sacrifices a module whose primary job is to strengthen controls. Entity assistance and valuer routing support controls indirectly; they do not close contract–decision mismatch, four-eyes, or provisional signing. It also leaves the 44-hour signing wait and the 22-hour contract wait in place, and it can worsen month-end legal overload because upstream synchronization succeeds and legal is still manual. As stated, this portfolio does not meet the compliance condition for a first wave.

### Compliance-first portfolio: A2, A3, A9, A12

This optimizes control integrity. A2 keeps high-exposure cases on the committee and four-eyes path. A3 adds an audited KYC escalation. A9 prevents clause drift, with the most value on credit-line renewal. A12 gives compliance a place to see four-eyes breaks, provisional signatures, and the contract-remediation loop, and it forces policy ambiguity into the open. A3 and A9 can be the two deep integrations; A2 and A12 are overlays.

It sacrifices cycle time. Classification and remediation are not the long poles. A3 can increase wait at the scarcest pool in the network. Signing, valuation, normalization, and credit-analysis waits stay essentially where they are. Correct classification will also slow some cases that today skip committee because they were misrouted. The portfolio is the right answer to a control audit and a weak answer to end-to-end performance.

### Synthesis used for the recommendation: A4, A5, A6, A9

This keeps the internal bottleneck logic and replaces A7 with A9 so the first wave actually strengthens a control. Valuation is handled in year 1 by an operating rule, not a module: for equipment finance, raise the valuation request at classification so it runs concurrently with KYC and financial-document review. That rule uses the concurrency the event log currently hides.

## 4. Expected process-level effects

### Quick-win (A1, A5, A9, A10)

Cycle time improves mainly at the end. Signing is the largest wait pool (44 hours, 7,300 cases, 21% reminder loop), and e-sign plus reminders can move it if customers adopt and if the KYC gate holds. Contract preparation’s 22-hour wait falls for standard terms. Registration’s 1.5 hours disappears from a path that is already stable in position 2 for 98% of cases; that change is barely visible in end-to-end time. Credit analysis (28-hour wait), normalization (20 hours), entity mismatch (31 hours), external valuation (58 hours), and the weekly committee cadence remain.

Waiting time at the request-missing-documents activity should fall, along with the 2,800 returns into financial-document review. Contract waiting falls. The entity-mismatch wait and the external-valuation wait do not. Credit-analysis waiting is likely to grow: documents arrive sooner, and analysts still spend 75 minutes normalizing before a 110-minute judgment.

Rework falls on the document loop (52% repeated requests; directly-follows 3,300 forward and 2,800 back) and on the contract loop (650 into remediation, 510 back), especially the 14% credit-line-renewal mismatch. The entity loop (1,160 returns to KYC) is unchanged. Registration rework is already only 4%.

Handovers from RMs who send incomplete instructions should fall. RM chase handovers before signing fall if e-sign is adopted. Legal handovers change from drafting to exception review. The heavy handover from KYC operations to the two senior specialists is unchanged.

Resource demand shifts the wrong way. Back-office utilization, already under 65% after 15:00, falls further. Analysts absorb an earlier wave without relief from normalization. Specialists remain the tightest pool and look worse as other queues shrink. Legal month-end load changes shape rather than disappearing. Activation volume rises with faster signing; the 16-minute activation task can absorb it. RMs feel checklist friction unless the checklist was designed with them.

Control violations improve on contract-decision match if the rule library is current. They improve on provisional signing only when A10 refuses to start without KYC clearance. If commercial pressure softens that gate, the 6.5% provisional-signing rate spreads onto a faster channel. Four-eyes stays at 5.8% in the month-end rush and can worsen if more high-exposure files arrive in the same week. The 380-case jump from entity resolution to contract preparation survives unless A9 is explicitly forbidden to read that edge as “ready.”

### Bottleneck / synchronization (A4, A5, A6, A7)

Cycle time improves in the middle of the process and in the equipment-finance variant. Overlapping valuation with credit, instead of the current sequence credit analysis → collateral request → external wait → committee packaging, takes a large share of 12 hours plus 58 hours off that product’s critical path. The mismatch variant, on the critical path in 82% of its occurrences, loses much of its 31-hour stall. Document and normalization waits compress. The residual long poles are customer signing at 44 hours and, for high-exposure cases, the weekly committee. Earlier packaging cannot make the committee meet sooner; it builds inventory in front of the meeting.

Waiting time falls at entity resolution, financial-document review, normalization, and external valuation. Contract and signing waits stay. Committee-packaging wait falls only for the portion caused by late inputs.

Rework falls on Resolve entity mismatch → KYC check (1,160), on the missing-document loop, and on the 16% normalization defect, with a residue on poor PDFs. Collateral request rework (13%) and lateness (19%) should ease if routing and file completeness improve. Contract rework at 12%, and the 8.5% / 14% term mismatch, stay.

Handovers to the two seniors become dossier reviews rather than evidence hunts; the concentration itself does not vanish. Document ping-pong among RMs, back office, and analysts shrinks. The valuer handover happens earlier, which starts the external clock, and it adds dispute handling. Packs arrive at committee with better normalized statements and earlier valuations.

Resource demand is the point of the portfolio. Specialists spend less time searching. Analysts trade transcription for judgment, so throughput into committee packaging rises. Relationship managers and analysts still assemble packs by hand. Legal, with 4 FTE and a month-end peak, receives a larger synchronized wave and has no module in this set. That is the main new bottleneck. External valuers see work earlier in the case, which is what the wait reduction depends on.

Control violations are not the design target. Faster, sloppier entity matches would create a new KYC defect. Contract mismatch and four-eyes are unchanged. Provisional signing may ease indirectly if KYC clearance arrives sooner and RMs feel less pressure to sign ahead of it. Misclassification at 8% still sends some equipment-finance cases down a path that never requests a valuation, so A7 never sees them. This set fails the requirement that one first-wave module strengthen controls.

### Compliance-first (A2, A3, A9, A12)

Cycle time barely moves, and some cases get longer for a good reason. Correct exposure classification puts high-exposure files onto committee packaging and the weekly decision instead of a shortcut. KYC hits that escalate wait on the same 3 specialists who already hold a 31-hour entity queue. Contract cycle time improves where A9 drafts cleanly and A12 speeds the 510 returns from remediation. Normalization, credit, valuation, and signing clocks are essentially unchanged.

Waiting time can rise at compliance specialists if the pre-classifier’s false-positive rate is material. That queue is already the most fragile in the social network. Remediation waiting falls. Most other waits do not.

Rework on classification (8%) falls if the recommender is calibrated and confirmed by a person, and rises if staff accept it uncritically. Contract remediation falls. KYC’s 11% exception rate is re-sorted into auto-handled greens and human escalations rather than removed. A12 will show more defects at first because four-eyes breaks and provisional signatures become cases instead of side conversations.

Handovers increase in the controlled channels: classifier confirmation, KYC escalation, remediation ownership. Side-channel handovers should fall. The four-eyes separation between credit analyst and packager stays a human handover, which is the desired state.

Resource demand concentrates on the pools least able to grow. Specialists inherit A3’s escalations on top of entity work. Legal and compliance inherit rule maintenance and the policy questions A12 flushes out. Analysts and the back office see little relief. The visible registration queue remains, which makes the portfolio harder to explain politically.

Control violations are the best of the three portfolios. A2 protects the committee path. A9 targets the 8.5% and renewal-specific 14% term gaps. A12 targets the 5.8% four-eyes pattern and gives provisional signing a tracked owner. The new exposure is a false negative on A3. Sample review of machine-green hits, and a ban on auto-clear for anything ambiguous, is part of the operating design. Compliance sign-off remains a human accountability.

### Recommended synthesis (A4, A5, A6, A9)

Cycle time should fall for working-capital and credit-line renewal through shorter document loops, less normalization delay, a shorter entity tail, and faster contract preparation. The p95 should move more than the median, because the 1,750 mismatch cases stop contributing a 31-hour critical-path stall. Equipment finance improves only modestly unless the early valuation trigger is actually adopted; otherwise the 58-hour external wait still dominates that variant. High-exposure cases still wait for the weekly committee. Across the whole book, customer signing remains the largest single wait, so end-to-end promises have to stay moderate.

Waiting time should fall at entity resolution (31 hours), financial-document review (18 hours), normalization (20 hours), and contract preparation (22 hours). Credit-analysis waiting (28 hours) is the watch item: upstream release plus removal of clerical spreading can fill the judgment queue. Committee packaging still waits on valuation for collateralized files and on the meeting cadence for high-exposure files. Activation’s 8 hours is unchanged.

Rework targets are specific. The missing-document pair is 3,300 forward and 2,800 back, with 52% repeats. The entity pair is 1,750 forward and 1,160 back to KYC. The contract pair is 650 into remediation and 510 back, against an 8.5% mismatch and 14% on renewal. Normalization rework starts at 16% and should concentrate on the low-quality-PDF exception path. Credit-analysis rework at 9% is judgment and should be expected to remain.

Handovers: document chasing among RM, back office, and analyst shrinks. Entity work arrives to the two seniors as a prepared dossier with a recorded recommendation. Legal sees exceptions and renewals rather than every draft. The analyst-to-packager handover on high-exposure cases stays mandatory. The KYC stream (A4) and the document stream (A5, A6) should run concurrently; the sequential system log is a poor reason to make one wait for the other.

Resource demand: back-office request handling falls, while registration work stays, so the most visible queue will still be there. Specialists remain 3 FTE; search time falls, hard mismatches remain, and queue age is the metric. Analysts move from 75-minute spreading toward 110-minute analysis; utilization and credit wait show whether that trade worked. Legal drafting load falls and rule-maintenance load rises; the month-end spike should ease because drafts exist before the peak. Committee inventory may grow because packs become complete earlier. RMs lose repeated document rework if the checklist is tolerable.

Control violations: A9 is aimed at term mismatch. A4 plus a KYC-clear prerequisite on A9 closes the path that lets entity resolution flow straight into contract preparation. Provisional signing is only reduced indirectly, by making clearance faster and by refusing to issue a signable contract early; the 6.5% channel can remain if wet signature sits outside the system. Four-eyes is not fixed by these four modules and needs a separate same-user block in the origination system. New control exposure sits in extracted numbers and in entity suggestions that a tired senior accepts unchanged.

## 5. Risks and compensating controls

**Auditability.** Every module in the recommended set stores the model suggestion, the confidence or rule version, the human override, the person, and the final disposition. Entity match, extracted financial fields, and contract clauses are reconstructable after the fact. A case that cannot produce that trail is treated as manually processed.

**False negatives.** A4 must not auto-clear an entity. A6 must not auto-accept low-confidence figures into the credit model. A9 must not treat “no rule fired” as “terms are correct” for non-standard renewal structures; those clauses stay on the manual legal path. If A3 is ever added, greens are sampled and anything ambiguous escalates. A false negative on KYC or exposure class is worse than a slow case.

**False positives.** Entity and document tools will flag clean cases. The escape is a short human reject with a reason code, not a second full loop. For A5, a rigid checklist that customers cannot complete becomes a new 52% loop. RMs need a documented override for items that do not apply. Specialist capacity is protected by capping how many low-confidence entity cases can sit with the two seniors at once; the third specialist takes the prepared, lower-ambiguity set.

**Model drift.** Exposure definitions, KYC typologies, statement layouts, and product terms all move. A2, A3, A6, and A9 need an owner and a review calendar. The contract library in particular drifts: a module that was a control in month three is a legal defect in month eighteen if renewal campaigns change covenants and the library does not. Gate any library change on legal sign-off, and keep the previous version executable for cases already in flight.

**Exception handling.** Low-quality PDFs, unresolved entities, non-standard collateral, and non-templated renewal terms stay with named humans. Automation routes the exception; it does not close it. The 19% late valuations and the customer portion of document delay sit outside the bank’s service time. Nudges and valuer routing reduce avoidable delay and leave genuine external wait visible, so staff do not keep reworking a case that is actually waiting on a customer or a valuer.

**Four-eyes.** Faster throughput will hit the month-end pattern that already produces 5.8% violations on high-exposure cases. The origination system should block the same user from completing credit analysis and committee packaging on those cases. A8, if built later, must record a packager identity different from the analyst. Committee packaging stays manual in year 1, which preserves a human second pair of eyes while upstream quality improves.

**Bypass variants.** Provisional signing and the 380-case entity-to-contract edge are the two shortcuts automation will cement if they are treated as normal flow. Signable output and activation both require a KYC-clear event newer than the last entity change. Contract output requires a structured decision identifier. A11 is out of scope while those checks are still soft.

**Shifted workload and accountability.** Analysts will do more judgment and less transcription. Legal will do more rule governance and fewer blank-page drafts. Specialists will decide rather than search, and will still be accountable for the match. RMs will be asked to use a checklist that must save them rework rather than add customer complaints. Compliance remains responsible for KYC policy, including every case the tools touched. A dashboard that shows “automated” as “approved” is a control failure.

**Customer friction.** A5 and, later, A10 can annoy applicants and RMs. Measure abandonment, time-to-first-complete-file, and RM override rate. If overrides become the norm, the checklist is wrong and should be changed before the module is scaled.

## 6. Recommended phased rollout

Year-1 modules are A5, A6, A4, and A9, in that dependency order. Deep integrations in the first six months are A6 and A9 only. A5 and A4 do not take a deep slot. No fifth module goes live inside the year.

**Phase 0, weeks 1–6: baseline and rules of the road.** Measure p50 and p85 cycle time by product (working-capital, equipment finance, credit-line renewal) and by exposure (micro, SME, high-exposure), with KYC and financial-document review treated as concurrent. Baseline the entity queue age, the 1,160-case KYC return loop, the 3,300 / 2,800 document loop, contract remediation by product, provisional signing, and month-end four-eyes violations. Sample PDF quality for A6. Draft the clause library with extra weight on renewal covenants. Agree the policy that a KYC-clear event, not an entity-case closure, is what contract preparation is allowed to see. Configure the same-user block for high-exposure credit analysis and packaging. Co-design the document checklist with relationship managers. Issue the operating rule that equipment-finance valuation is requested at classification, in parallel with KYC and document review, and start measuring the 58-hour wait and 19% lateness against that rule.

**Phase 1, months 2–4: shallow modules.** Release A5 by product, with reason-code overrides. Release A4 in recommendation-only mode: the specialist records the disposition, and the suggestion is stored beside it. Ramp A5 by segment so the analyst arrival rate is visible before it is universal. Do not turn on broad customer nudging for a segment until the checklist defect rate is acceptable.

Gate to continue the ramp: repeated missing-document rate moving down from 52%, no material rise in application abandonment, and analyst overtime not spiking ahead of A6. Gate for A4: queue age off the 31-hour baseline, fewer returns along Resolve entity mismatch → KYC check, and no rise in later KYC remediation. A specialist acceptance rate near 100% with no edits is a stop signal, not a success.

**Phase 2, months 4–6: first deep integration, A6.** This slot is used before A9 because A5 will otherwise move clerical work onto the analysts. Scope is tiered. High-confidence, machine-readable statements pre-fill the spreading template and an analyst still confirms. Low-quality PDFs stay fully manual, with that path measured rather than forced into the model. Extracted figures carry confidence into the credit file.

Gate to widen coverage: held-out field accuracy agreed with credit risk, analyst correction rate stable, and credit-analysis rework not rising above the current 9% for reasons traced to extraction. A failed gate keeps the manual path; it does not get “fixed” by lowering the threshold.

**Phase 3, months 5–9: second deep integration, A9.** Build can overlap Phase 2. Production integration is the second deep slot and should land at the end of the first six months only if legal has signed the library; otherwise it slips just past six months rather than going live early. Shadow mode comes first: the module drafts and lists rule breaks, and legal still issues the contract. Compare shadow output with the live 650 / 510 remediation loop.

First assisted production is standard working-capital for micro and SME, where templates are tight, plus a checker-only mode on credit-line renewal and high-exposure, where the 14% mismatch and judgment terms make silent generation unsafe. No output is releasable to a customer unless KYC is clear and a decision identifier is attached. High-exposure contracts remain human-issued even when the checker runs.

Gate to leave shadow mode: legal sign-off, complete audit trail, shadow mode catching the mismatch types that today return from remediation, and month-end legal overtime not worse than baseline. Gate to add a template family: remediation rate for that family falling, with overrides sampled.

**Phase 4, months 9–12: stabilize inside the four modules.** Extend A6 only inside its accuracy gate. Extend A9 template by template. Keep A4 on human disposition. Use the capacity that was freed to absorb judgment work, not to open a fifth automation. Review the valuation operating rule. If equipment-finance wait and lateness are unmoved, A7 becomes a year-2 candidate. A10 is a year-2 candidate only if KYC-before-sign has been systematically enforced and provisional signing is falling. A8 waits on consistent pack inputs. A1 stays off the list while back-office afternoon slack remains and downstream waits dominate. A11 is not eligible.

**Monitoring set.** Cycle time p50 and p85 by product and exposure. Waiting time for entity resolution, financial-document review, normalization, external valuation, committee packaging, contract preparation, and signing. Loop counts for the three directly-follows pairs above. Resource signals: credit-analyst utilization and the 28-hour credit wait, specialist queue age and share handled by the two seniors, legal overtime at month-end, back-office utilization after 15:00. Control signals: four-eyes violation rate on high-exposure month-end cases, provisional-signing rate, contract–decision mismatch overall and for renewal, share of A4 and A6 suggestions accepted unchanged, and sample-audit defects. Customer and RM signals: abandonment after a checklist, and checklist override rate.

**Stop rules.** Pause the A5 ramp if analyst wait rises before A6 coverage is real. Pause A6 expansion if extracted-field defects appear in credit decisions. Pull A9 back to shadow if a released contract class exceeds its baseline mismatch rate. Treat any automated KYC clearance, any signable contract without a clear event, or any same-user high-exposure pack as an incident, not a tuning item.

## 7. Final recommendation

Recommend the four-module portfolio **A5, A6, A4, and A9**, in that order, with equipment-finance valuation pulled forward to classification as an operating rule rather than as a fifth build.

A5 reduces the dominant internal rework loop and the RM-driven document defect, on a shallow integration, with the checklist designed so relationship managers are not handed a new customer complaint. A6 is the capacity keystone: it removes the 75-minute spreading task from the 9 analysts, feeds the credit model, and stops A5 from moving the bottleneck onto the people at the center of the working-together network. A4 spends a module on a low-frequency activity because that activity sits on 3 specialists, depends on two seniors, waits 31 hours, returns to KYC 1,160 times, and is on the critical path in 82% of the cases that have it. A9 is the control module the first wave is required to include: it attacks 22-hour contract waits, the remediation loop, and the 8.5% term mismatch (14% on renewal), and it gives legal a way through the month-end peak. Together they respect the two-deep-integration limit: A6 and A9 are deep; A5 and A4 are not.

Registration should not be automated first. It is the highest-frequency, lowest-complexity, most politically comfortable candidate, it already occupies position 2 in 98% of cases, and its feasibility is very high. The same evidence shows a 1.5-hour wait, 4% rework, an 8-minute service time, and a 12-person back office that is slack after 15:00. Automating it would improve a queue that is visible and not constraining, then deliver cases faster into misclassification, KYC, document review, and a specialist pool that is already the tight point in the handover network. Duplicate detection has some hygiene value later. It does not earn one of the four year-1 slots.

The quick-win set would capture signing time and a public labor saving, and it would leave the bank with overloaded analysts, an untouched 31-hour compliance tail, and a deep-integration slot spent on registration. The pure synchronization set would do more for equipment-finance cycle time and still fail the control condition, while pushing a larger wave onto unmanaged legal capacity. The compliance-first set would protect routing, KYC escalation, and remediation, and it would spend specialist capacity the network does not have, without moving the waits that dominate the case.

The deliberate residuals are customer signing and, if the early-trigger rule is weak, external valuation. Signing is the largest aggregate wait, and it stays manual for the year because automating it before KYC clearance is reliable would scale a defect that already shows up as provisional signing in 6.5% of cases. Activation stays manual for the same reason: a downstream bot with incomplete controls creates exposure faster than the process can repair it. Those are year-2 decisions, gated on the control metrics above, not gaps in the first-wave design.
