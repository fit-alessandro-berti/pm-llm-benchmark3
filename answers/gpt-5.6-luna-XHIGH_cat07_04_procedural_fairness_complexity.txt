# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness concerns whether similarly situated applicants receive a comparable opportunity to complete the process, comparable access to assistance, reasonable and predictable treatment, and a meaningful opportunity to understand, challenge, and correct a decision. It is broader than comparing final approval rates.

## Outcome parity

The raw outcomes show substantial group differences: approval is 78% for Group A, 63% for Group B, 69% for Group C, and 58% for Group D. Cycle times, appeals, and rework also differ. These are important signals, but they are not by themselves evidence of discrimination. The groups enter the process with different risk, documentation, channel, accommodation, language, household, region, and prior-case profiles. A lower approval rate can therefore reflect differences in eligibility or complexity.

Raw outcome parity is nevertheless relevant. A fair process should be able to explain the differences, and should not treat a case-mix explanation as sufficient without checking whether the inputs or the process itself are biased. The elevated appeal and rework rates are particularly important because they may indicate that an applicant was not given an equally clear or accessible route through the process, even where the final decision was substantively correct.

## Adjusted outcome disparity

The preliminary adjusted comparisons remain non-zero: relative to Group A, approval probability is lower by 5.5 percentage points for Group B, 2.2 points for Group C, and 3.1 points for Group D. Adjusted cycle time is longer by 2.6, 4.1, and 0.9 days, respectively. Group B and Group C also have higher adjusted probabilities of Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents, while Group D has a markedly higher adjusted appeal probability.

These residuals are warning signals, not causal estimates. The model may omit relevant legitimate complexity, and some controls may themselves reflect unequal treatment. Statistical uncertainty, overlap between groups, model specification, and multiple comparisons must be reported with confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals. The residual for Group C's cycle time and the residual for Group B's missing-document requests merit particular investigation, but the table cannot establish intentional or unlawful discrimination.

## Procedural burden

The process imposes burden through additional activities, document requests, handovers, queues, repeat contacts, and appeals. A support activity can be substantively justified and still be procedurally burdensome if it adds avoidable delay, requires the applicant to repeat information, or is difficult to access. Group B's language-support path and Group C's accommodation path are therefore part of the fairness assessment, not noise to be discarded. Burden should be measured conditional on need and complexity, including the number of steps, contacts, documents requested, handovers, repeat work, and applicant effort where available.

## Timeliness

Group B and Group C have much longer median and tail cycle times than Group A: 13.7 versus 8.1 days at the median, and 34.2 versus 21.4 days at the 90th percentile for Group B; 15.9 and 38.8 days for Group C. Group D also has a longer tail. Delays are not automatically unfair when a necessary review takes longer, but the process should provide a reasonable, predictable, and monitored service level for the support steps that protect access. The absence of separate timeliness targets for Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review makes it impossible to tell whether the delay is accepted, controlled, or simply hidden in the overall cycle time.

## Consistency of routing

Legitimate rules support Fraud Review for higher measured risk, Accommodation Review for an accommodation request, and Language Support Contact for a language need. Fairness requires that these rules be applied consistently to comparable cases, with documented reasons and appropriate exceptions. It also requires checking discretionary transitions: for example, whether Group B cases with the same risk and documentation profile are more likely to receive Supervisor Review or another document request than Group A cases, and whether the same accommodation need is routed in the same way across regions. A high frequency of a necessary route is not itself unfair; unexplained conditional variation is concerning.

## Quality and accessibility of support

The log records that support occurred, but not whether interpretation was accurate, whether an accommodation was effective, whether notices were understandable, or whether the applicant could participate without disclosing the same information repeatedly. Equal access is not achieved merely by counting Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review. The quality, modality, language, accessibility, and timing of the support matter.

## Appeal and rework burden

Appeal rates are 6%, 13%, 10%, and 16% for Groups A through D, and rework rates are 11%, 22%, 24%, and 20%. Appeals may reflect legitimate disagreement, higher complexity, or applicant selection rather than an unfair initial process. Rework can also be a normal response to incomplete evidence. But high rates can signal unclear requests, inconsistent decisions, poor explanations, or inaccessible communication. An appeal that is difficult to file or has a lower-quality review would be a further procedural disparity not captured by the appeal flag.

## Resource-allocation fairness

Fair allocation does not mean assigning every group to the same staff. It means providing adequate, timely, and appropriately skilled capacity for the needs that trigger the route. Group B is concentrated among seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded regional officers; Group C is concentrated among five accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. Those arrangements plausibly create queueing and handover risks. Group A's distribution across 52 caseworkers is not a directly comparable workload measure. The relevant questions are whether capacity follows expected demand, whether similarly situated cases face comparable waits, whether staff have the necessary skills, and whether workloads and escalation responsibilities are distributed sustainably.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should define the estimand before modeling. Useful estimands include the probability of each transition conditional on a legitimate need, total elapsed time, active service time, waiting time by queue, number of handovers, document requests, rework, appeal access, and outcome. The primary comparison should be among cases with comparable observed needs and complexity, while a separate analysis should estimate the burden of the support route itself. Routing variables such as Supervisor Review, Language Support Contact, and Accommodation Review should not be treated as ordinary pre-treatment controls when estimating the total process disparity: they are possible mediators and possible sources of burden.

## Matching and stratification

Use exact or coarsened exact strata, propensity or overlap weighting, and regression adjustment as complementary approaches. Initial strata should include:

- risk score band, documentation completeness, household-size band, prior-case-count band, channel, region, and application month;
- observed language need and accommodation request when comparing people with the same relevant need, while also reporting the overall group effect that includes access to those supports; and
- interactions such as risk by region, documentation by channel, and support need by region where sample size permits.

Use only information available at the relevant decision point. Do not condition on an eventual decision, appeal, or a downstream routing event when estimating its upstream causes. Check common support and report how many cases are excluded or heavily weighted. Compare Group B, C, and D with Group A, but also make within-need comparisons, for example language-support users across applicant groups or regions. Use confidence intervals, cluster-robust or bootstrap uncertainty, and prespecified outcomes rather than relying on a single adjusted point estimate.

## Path and variant comparison

Compute group-specific variant frequencies, conditional transition probabilities, and time distributions within the same complexity strata. Compare V1 with V2 and the support variants using aligned prefixes and suffixes, not just whole-trace shares. For each support need, estimate the incremental waiting time and number of handovers associated with the necessary route, and then inspect whether cases with the same need and complexity are routed to materially different paths. Use process discovery for description; use stratified models or matched comparisons for inference. Rare variants should be pooled or reported with uncertainty rather than overinterpreted.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Join each case's event history to resource, team, region, queue, and workload information. Compare, conditional on complexity and need, the probability of assignment to each resource type, the number of handovers, time to first qualified contact, queue wait, service time, and rework. Examine whether bilingual or accommodation-skilled staff are assigned in a way that reduces access delays or instead creates a bottleneck. A resource-level analysis should account for caseload, concurrency, leave, shift coverage, and task mix; simple counts of cases per worker are inadequate.

## Waiting-time decomposition

Decompose total cycle time into active service time and waiting time, and further split waiting into: submission-to-verification, completeness/document-request, language-support, accommodation-review, risk/fraud, supervisor-to-draft, notification, and appeal waits. Report medians and upper quantiles, because queues may affect the tail disproportionately. Compare both (1) route composition, such as the greater share of V4 or V5, and (2) within-route waits among comparable cases. A Oaxaca-style or counterfactual decomposition can describe how much of the group gap is associated with different paths versus different waits on the same path, but it should be labeled descriptive unless the causal assumptions are justified.

## Sensitivity to biased or endogenous covariates

Run models with and without risk score, documentation completeness, and channel. Treat documentation as both a complexity indicator and a possible consequence of language or accessibility barriers. Treat risk score as a potentially biased historical measurement, not as a neutral fact. Compare alternative specifications: coarsened bands, continuous measures where valid, missingness indicators, interaction terms, bounds for unmeasured confounding, and analyses using only more defensible pre-application attributes. Test whether conclusions change when support need is included, excluded, or modeled as a separate route. If possible, validate risk and documentation against independently reviewed evidence and use audit samples to estimate measurement error.

## Region, channel, and intersectional checks

Repeat the analysis by region and by online versus in-person channel, especially in the two regions with reported handover differences. Check group-by-region, group-by-channel, language-by-channel, and accommodation-by-region interactions. Also examine small but important intersections such as Group B applicants using in-person service and Group C applicants in regions where specialists perform supervisor review. Use shrinkage or partial pooling for sparse cells and distinguish a stable pattern from a noisy subgroup estimate.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The most visible disparity is path composition. The short V1 path accounts for 42% of Group A but only 19% of Group B, 22% of Group C, and 18% of Group D. V2, which adds a missing-document request and has a 14.9-day median, is more common for Group B (34%) and Group C (29%) than for Group A (24%). This is partly consistent with incomplete documentation: the observed shares are 46% for Group B, 36% for Group C, and 21% for Group A. It is not enough to conclude that the extra requests are improper, because the log does not show whether requests were necessary, specific, or caused by inaccessible instructions. The adjusted +9.5-point probability of a missing-document request for Group B and +4.2 points for Group C keep the issue open after measured complexity is controlled.

V4 is concentrated in Group B: 24% versus 1% for Group A, with a 19.2-day median. The Language Support Contact is legitimate for applicants who need it, and Group B has 57% recorded language-support need. The concentration therefore has a strong legitimate explanation. The additional 3.8-day median wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment, the shortage of seven bilingual caseworkers, and the repeated general-worker/bilingual-worker/general-worker handovers show a separate procedural concern: the right-preserving step appears to be a capacity bottleneck. Support should not be removed; its delay and handover design should be corrected.

V5 is concentrated in Group C: 31% versus 2% for Group A, with a 21.6-day median. The 62% accommodation-request rate makes Accommodation Review substantively justified for many Group C cases. However, the 4.7-day median wait after Accommodation Review, the five-specialist capacity, and the fact that two specialists also conduct supervisor review create a plausible avoidable delay and conflict in capacity. The adjusted +4.1-day cycle-time difference for Group C is consistent with this concern, although it could still include unmeasured accommodation complexity.

V3 is especially common for Group D (28% versus 9% for Group A), and Group D has a 51% high-risk share compared with 18% for Group A. Risk-based Fraud Review is therefore a plausible legitimate explanation. Yet Group D's risk score may encode historical disparities, and risk-based cases wait 2.8 days from Risk Screening to Fraud Review versus 2.2 days for Group A. The route should be audited for calibration, necessity, proportionality, and false-positive burden rather than accepted solely because the score is high.

V6, which includes an appeal, is 13% for Group D, compared with 4% for Group A; this corresponds to the highest raw appeal rate for Group D and an adjusted appeal difference of +5.7 points. It could reflect genuinely contested high-risk cases, but it may also reflect more adverse or less clearly explained decisions. The log does not say whether appeals are equally easy to submit or equally successful.

Across entry to verification, Group B waits 1.4 days versus 0.8 for Group A, partly because in-person applications wait longer and Group B has a 38% in-person share. At Supervisor Review to Decision Draft, Group B waits 1.9 days and Group C 1.6 days versus 1.0 for Group A, consistent with regional backlogs and specialist/supervisor overlap. Notification differences are small, so the main issue is earlier routing and queueing rather than final notification alone.

The resource pattern reinforces, but does not prove, a capacity mechanism. Concentration of Group B cases among seven bilingual workers and three overloaded officers, and Group C cases among five specialists, can produce slower service even when every assignment follows policy. The reported extra handovers in two regions add coordination cost and create more opportunities for information loss or inconsistent discretion. Because workload is observed only daily, it is not possible to determine whether the disparity reflects persistent undercapacity, temporary peaks, or different within-day queues. The adjusted residuals and support-specific waits are nonetheless sufficient to warrant corrective investigation.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. Genuine case complexity and legitimate routing. Higher risk, incomplete documents, repeated prior cases, or regional requirements can legitimately trigger Fraud Review, document requests, or Supervisor Review. Group D's high-risk share and Group B/C documentation shares make this a credible mechanism for part of the raw gap.

2. Language-access and documentation mechanism. An applicant who cannot easily understand a form may submit incomplete evidence, triggering a request and a language-support route. The resulting lower approval and longer time can arise from an access barrier upstream of the formal decision, even if downstream staff apply the written rule consistently.

3. Specialist-capacity bottlenecks. Seven bilingual caseworkers and five accommodation specialists may be insufficient for demand. Their queues, especially when some also perform other functions, can create the observed 3.8- and 4.7-day waits without any individual intentionally treating applicants differently.

4. Handover and coordination failure. General-worker to specialist to general-worker transfers can require repeated explanation, duplicate checks, or waiting for ownership to be reassigned. This can increase rework and appeals and can create region-specific disparities.

5. Regional and channel infrastructure differences. In-person applications may enter a slower intake queue, and two regional backlogs may affect particular groups more because of where they live. A nominally uniform policy can thus produce unequal practical access.

6. Discretionary scrutiny or inconsistent implementation. Staff may request documents, escalate to supervisors, or interpret completeness differently for comparable applicants. This could explain residual Group B/C routing differences after measured complexity is controlled and would be a procedural fairness concern even without proof of intent.

7. Historical or proxy bias in risk scoring. If past investigations or outcomes reflected unequal enforcement, the risk score can reproduce that pattern. Group D's greater Fraud Review exposure may then be partly an inherited measurement disparity rather than a neutral measure of present risk.

8. Explanation and appeal-selection mechanism. If notices are less clear or less accessible, applicants may be more likely to appeal or require rework. Conversely, groups with more resources or stronger advocacy may appeal more often, so an appeal flag alone cannot distinguish poor treatment from differential ability to challenge a decision.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The attributes capture some case mix but not the full reasons for a route, the quality of evidence, or the applicant's actual experience. A categorical risk band may conceal substantial variation within a band, and a high score may reflect historically biased labels. It is therefore inappropriate to regard risk_score_band as unquestionably neutral. The same concern applies to documentation_completeness: missing documents may be genuine complexity, but may also result from language barriers, inaccessible forms, disability-related barriers, unstable housing, or inconsistent staff instructions. Controlling for it can remove part of the very disparity being investigated.

Language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested are imperfect measures of need. An applicant may need an interpreter or an accessible process without requesting it, may not know that support is available, or may be recorded inconsistently across channels. The fields do not describe the type, urgency, adequacy, or completion of the support. They also do not identify other access needs such as literacy, cognitive impairment, digital access, caregiving constraints, or lack of reliable communication.

The event log does not capture the quality of an explanation, whether a request was reasonable, whether an interpreter conveyed the information correctly, whether an accommodation worked, or whether the applicant was treated with dignity. `case_outcome` records the result but not the substantive correctness of the decision or the reasons supplied. `appeal_flag` records that an appeal occurred but not ease of filing, timeliness, representation, grounds, success, or appeal-review quality. Rework similarly does not distinguish necessary evidence development from avoidable error.

Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. It omits concurrent caseload, queue position, staffing by shift, leave, task duration, complexity of other cases, interruptions, and actual availability of bilingual or accommodation expertise. The resource field also cannot by itself reveal whether a worker had the training or authority needed for a particular case. These gaps make resource-assignment and queue explanations plausible but not conclusive.

Timestamp quality may be insufficient to separate active service from waiting, especially if events are logged only at completion or in batches. There may be missing, duplicated, or unlogged contacts, informal assistance, and off-system appeals. Month and region controls may not absorb policy changes or local operational practices. The group labels do not support all relevant intersectional analyses and may conceal within-group heterogeneity. Finally, the data are observational: even a well-adjusted association does not identify the counterfactual path or prove discriminatory intent.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

## Add language-support and accommodation capacity

Increase qualified bilingual coverage and accommodation-specialist capacity, cross-train staff, use safe centralized coverage, and reserve capacity for urgent cases. This directly addresses the observed queues and reduces dependence on a few specialists.

The trade-offs are cost, recruitment and training time, possible quality variation among newly trained staff, and the risk that centralized coverage becomes less familiar with local rules. Cross-training must preserve competence, confidentiality, and the applicant's choice of appropriate support; it should not turn specialist work into unqualified generalist work.

## Set service-level targets for support steps

Create measurable targets for time to Language Support Contact, time from support to the next substantive step, Accommodation Review completion, and total time for supported cases. Publish compliance by group, need, region, and channel, with an escalation path when a target is missed.

Targets can encourage prioritization and make hidden delays visible. They can also cause staff to rush interpretation or accommodation decisions, split cases artificially, or prioritize easy cases to improve the metric. Balance speed targets with quality, completeness, applicant feedback, and outcome measures; allow documented exceptions for genuinely complex cases.

## Redesign routing to reduce handovers

Use a named case owner, warm handoffs, shared case notes, direct specialist consultation, and routing that keeps a case with one trained worker where feasible. Avoid returning a case from a bilingual worker or accommodation specialist to a general worker merely for administrative reasons. Retain the support steps and their independence while removing duplicate transfers.

Fewer handovers can reduce coordination errors and waiting, but over-centralization can create a single point of failure, reduce specialist independence, or make it harder to detect review errors. A named owner should not be allowed to bypass a required second review or make unsupported assumptions about an accommodation.

## Improve decision explanations and accessible communication

Provide plain-language, translated and accessible notices that state the evidence considered, missing items, applicable rule, next steps, expected timing, how to obtain support, and how to appeal. Test explanations with applicants and measure comprehension, correction rates, and appeal outcomes, not just whether a notice was sent.

More detailed notices require staff time and careful translation. Overly technical or overly detailed explanations can confuse applicants or expose sensitive fraud controls. Use tiered explanations that are meaningful without disclosing information that would undermine legitimate fraud prevention.

## Monitor adjusted disparities continuously

Build a dashboard for approval, transition probabilities, active and waiting time, tail latency, handovers, rework, appeal access and success, and support quality. Report raw and adjusted results with uncertainty, monitor drift, and trigger case-level review when residual disparities persist. Use a small audit sample to validate whether a statistical signal corresponds to an actual process problem.

Monitoring can encourage metric gaming, generate false alarms in small cells, or stigmatize groups if results are published without context. Predefine metrics and thresholds, use partial pooling or minimum-cell rules, protect privacy, and review both disparate outcomes and possible under-recording of support needs.

## Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness

Assess calibration and error rates of risk bands across groups, regions, and channels against independently reviewed evidence. Test whether high-risk routing is necessary and proportionate, whether equivalent evidence produces equivalent bands, and whether historical outcomes were themselves biased. Audit document requests for necessity, specificity, duplication, accessibility, and conditional rates after accounting for actual need.

Auditing may require sensitive data and independent adjudication. Replacing a score or loosening document requirements without a validated alternative could increase fraud or erroneous approvals. The safer approach is staged validation, human review of borderline cases, documented override reasons, and remediation of biased inputs while preserving legitimate safeguards.

    # PM4Py's exact signature is version-dependent; the intent is to retain
    # traces whose event attribute applicant_group equals g.

        log, "applicant_group", [g], level="event"

groups = {g: group_log(g) for g in ["A", "B", "C", "D"]}

# Variants, variant shares, and selected comparable paths.

    variants = pm4py.get_variants(glog)  # variant -> traces/events
    n = sum(len(trace_list) for trace_list in variants.values())

        {"variant": v, "cases": len(trace_list), "share": len(trace_list) / n}

variant_tables = {g: variant_table(glog) for g, glog in groups.items()}

The PM4Py outputs should be combined with a case-level analytical table. For each case, derive group, needs, baseline complexity, route/variant, every activity start/end or inter-event wait, resources and handovers, outcome, rework, and appeal fields. Then estimate stratified or weighted group contrasts for each metric, with bootstrap intervals and region/channel interactions. A DFG or declarative model describes what happened; it does not establish that a route was necessary, that an applicant was treated respectfully, or that a group difference was caused by group membership. Those questions require policy review, record audits, independent evidence review, and—where appropriate—applicant feedback.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process is not demonstrably procedurally fair on the available evidence, but it is not possible to conclude that the observed disparities are discriminatory. The evidence supports legitimate complexity-based routing for higher risk, incomplete documentation, language needs, and accommodation needs. It also shows concerning residual disparities and avoidable-looking burdens: longer support queues, concentrated specialist capacity, extra handovers, higher adjusted document-request and cycle-time measures, and higher appeal/rework rates. The most defensible conclusion is therefore **indeterminate with material fairness risks requiring audit and remediation**. Support steps should be preserved while their capacity, routing, timeliness, accessibility, explanations, and measurement bias are investigated.
