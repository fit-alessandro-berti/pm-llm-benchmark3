# 1. Mechanism map

The restructuring centralizes technical, legal/risk, and approval work into shared queues while keeping intake and simple eligibility local. The table below links each key mechanism to concrete process-mining signals.

| Mechanism | Restructuring driver | Expected process-mining effect | Key measures (from event log) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Nominal handover reduction | Fewer ad hoc cross-team transfers; standard routing from local caseworker to shared queues instead of local loops and cross-team cover | Total handovers per case may fall slightly, and cross-team handovers should drop; handover paths become more standardized (fewer unique resource-to-resource edges) | `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` edge frequencies; average and distribution of handovers per case; frequency of edges with `handover_reason="absence_cover"`; cross-team vs within-team edge volumes (`team`, `location`) |
| Central queue formation | Creation of Technical Shared Service, Specialist Review shared queue, and shared approval queue | Handover-of-work network becomes more star-shaped around shared-service resources; increased in-degree and out-degree for shared-service nodes; queueing visible as longer waits before shared-service activities | Handover network centralization indices; median and 90th percentile waiting times from local caseworker to `Technical Shared Service` / `Legal/Risk` / shared approval activities using `time:timestamp` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` / `pm4py.get_service_time` |
| Specialist utilization | Pooling N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech plus legal/risk into shared queues | Higher proportion of events executed by specialist resources; more even workload across specialists; service times per case fall if setup/idle time is reduced | Events per specialist per month; specialist share of total effort; utilization proxies from service-time vs waiting-time per specialist; `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` to see clearer specialist-role clusters |
| Rework | More systematic specialist involvement and standardized approval vs loss of local informal advice | Rework may fall if earlier specialist advice prevents errors, or rise if central queues delay feedback and degrade local-context decisions | `rework_flag` rate overall and by activity (via `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity`); frequency of loops in DFG (e.g., `Implementation → Quality Check → Rework → Specialist Review`); changes in `outcome` and SLA misses after rework |
| Local expertise loss | Removing embedded tech specialists and routing all high-complexity / special cases away from local teams | For cases with `local_expertise_flag=1` or high `case_complexity`, more work is done by central resources; rework and cycle time for those segments may increase; local caseworkers handle fewer complex variants | Share of events for `local_expertise_flag=1` cases executed by local vs shared-service resources; rework and SLA miss rates by `team`, `location`, `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`; variant analysis via `pm4py.get_variants` and `pm4py.filter_variants` comparing local vs central-heavy variants |
| Role clarity | Shared approval queue and centralized specialist advice vs new separation between decision-maker and case owner | Role boundaries for “who decides what” may become clearer (distinct approval activities and resources) but ownership of complex cases may blur, causing more reassignments and ping-pong | Number of distinct resources and activities per case; `reassignment_count` distribution, especially around Specialist Review and Approval; working-together and subcontracting networks (`pm4py.discover_working_together_network`, `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network`) becoming either simpler (clear roles) or more tangled (unclear ownership) |
| Resilience to absence | Larger shared pools vs new single points of failure at central queues | Local absence may hurt less if any caseworker can pull advice from shared queues, but specialist or approver absence can now affect all teams simultaneously | Variation in waiting times and SLA miss rates by day-of-week / holiday periods; spikes in `handover_reason="absence_cover"`; change in cross-team handovers during local vs central absence events; robustness of throughput during shocks (e.g., sudden intake spikes) |
| Fairness across teams / segments | Pooling capacity and rules while keeping local intake | East may benefit from relief of E_Tech; North/South risk longer waits; some `customer_segment` / `location` groups (e.g., needing language/regional accommodation) may see more handovers and longer tails | Differences in median and 90th percentile cycle times, rework rates, and SLA misses by `team`, `location`, `customer_segment`, `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`; outcome distributions per segment; edge weights in handover network showing whether specific segments experience more hops |

# 2. Scenario forecasts

## 2.1 Optimistic scenario

- **Handover pattern**  
  - Cross-team handovers fall toward the management estimate (≈3 per 100 cases) as shared queues replace ad hoc transfers.  
  - Typical paths become `local intake → local caseworker → Technical Shared Service / Legal/Risk / shared approval → local caseworker → implementation`, with few extra loops.  
  - Handover-of-work network shows a moderate star around shared services but still strong intra-team loops between local intake, caseworker, and implementation.

- **Queue / waiting-time pattern**  
  - Median waits to Technical Shared Service and Legal/Risk are close to the estimates (≈4 h and 10.5 h) and, crucially, 90th percentiles are not much higher than pre-change (e.g., < 1.5× current specialist waits).  
  - Waiting times for high-complexity cases improve in East and are roughly flat in North/South.  
  - Shared approval waits are stable, with low variance across teams.

- **Cycle-time distribution**  
  - Overall median cycle time drops by ~5–10%, and 90th percentile cycle time improves slightly, especially in East.  
  - The long tail for high-complexity cases shortens or at least does not lengthen, even though more of them go through specialists.  
  - Variability across teams shrinks; East converges toward North/South.

- **Rework and quality pattern**  
  - Rework after specialist and approval handovers decreases, especially for cases with `required_specialty` in {legal, risk, security}.  
  - The Quality Check → Rework loop becomes less frequent, and `outcome` distributions (e.g., successful implementations) improve.  
  - For `local_expertise_flag=1` cases, rework stays at or below current levels, indicating that centralization did not erode local effectiveness.

- **Role clarity and accountability**  
  - Activity labels and resources make it clear who gives advice and who approves; `reassignment_count` falls for approval activities.  
  - Working-together networks show cleaner clusters: local caseworkers collaborate with a defined set of specialists and approvers rather than many ad hoc contacts.  
  - Owners for exceptions are documented (e.g., explicit “Case Owner” role stays constant across the case).

- **Fairness implications**  
  - Gaps in 90th percentile cycle time and SLA miss rates between North/South/East and across `customer_segment` narrow.  
  - No segment experiences systematic worsening of tails, even when more risks are detected (more legal/risk referrals offset by better throughput).  
  - High-complexity and vulnerable segments benefit at least as much as simple, low-risk segments.

- **Early warning signs we are in this scenario**  
  - Within weeks, specialist and approval queue 90th percentile waits stabilize or decline by segment.  
  - Cross-team handovers and `handover_reason="absence_cover"` events drop without a compensating rise in `reassignment_count` or rework.  
  - Differences in queue metrics and cycle times across `team`, `location`, and `customer_segment` shrink rather than expand.

## 2.2 Central (most likely mixed) scenario

- **Handover pattern**  
  - Nominal handovers decrease slightly as cross-team transfers fall, but the number of specialist-related handovers per case rises modestly because more cases are routed to shared services.  
  - The handover network shows a pronounced central star around shared services; some local loops (e.g., caseworker ↔ supervisor) persist for edge cases.  
  - East’s paths simplify more than North/South’s; East relies heavily on shared services, while North/South retain some local-style workarounds.

- **Queue / waiting-time pattern**  
  - Median waits for shared services are acceptable (near estimates), but 90th percentile waits increase for high-complexity and legal/risk cases, especially from North/South.  
  - Waiting-time variance between teams grows slightly: East improves, North/South roughly flat or slightly worse.  
  - Queue length (as inferred from waiting times) becomes more sensitive to intake spikes.

- **Cycle-time distribution**  
  - Average and median cycle times modestly improve overall, mainly due to East and simpler cases.  
  - The right tail for complex cases is somewhat longer; 95th–99th percentiles increase for high `case_complexity`.  
  - Differences in cycle time across `customer_segment` and `location` become more visible but not extreme.

- **Rework and quality pattern**  
  - Rework falls slightly in simple cases and for East but is flat or slightly worse for high-complexity / `local_expertise_flag=1` cases in North/South.  
  - Quality Check identifies more issues that require rework in segments routed more often to shared services (better detection but more back-and-forth).  
  - Outcome quality is roughly unchanged, but more effort is spent in specialist queues.

- **Role clarity and accountability**  
  - For standard cases, roles are clearer (local handle, specialist advise, shared approver decide).  
  - For exceptions, ownership is blurred; more approvals involve multiple approvers or bounce between local supervisors and shared queue.  
  - `reassignment_count` increases in the tail of the distribution, especially for cases tagged with unusual `handover_reason` values.

- **Fairness implications**  
  - East’s fairness metrics improve (lower SLA misses), but North/South see little change or small deteriorations in high-complexity segments.  
  - Some `customer_segment` groups that require more local nuance (e.g., specific regional or language cohorts) experience slightly higher tails and rework, even as averages hold.  
  - Overall fairness picture is ambiguous and sensitive to which segment is examined.

- **Early warning signs we are in this scenario**  
  - Average cycle times and median waits look better, but 90th percentile waits to shared services and approvals creep upward for selected segments.  
  - Rework and `reassignment_count` rise only for high-complexity / `local_expertise_flag=1` cases or specific `customer_segment`s.  
  - Handover-of-work centralization indices increase markedly without proportional reductions in per-case handovers for vulnerable segments.

## 2.3 Adverse scenario

- **Handover pattern**  
  - Shared services become bottlenecks; handovers to Technical Shared Service, Legal/Risk, and shared approvals increase significantly and often repeat (ping-pong between local and central roles).  
  - Handover-of-work network shows extreme centralization: most cases traverse multiple central edges, and cross-team edges reappear as locals escalate “around” queues.  
  - `reassignment_count` spikes, and many cases show long chains of different resources touching the same approval or specialist activity.

- **Queue / waiting-time pattern**  
  - Median waits to shared services may initially align with expectations but quickly drift upward as queues saturate, especially for high-complexity and legal/risk cases.  
  - Tail waiting times (95th–99th percentiles) increase sharply, with some segments experiencing multi-day delays in shared queues.  
  - East’s previous overload is replaced by system-wide central overload; small disturbances (e.g., vacations in the shared team) produce noticeable backlogs.

- **Cycle-time distribution**  
  - Average cycle time for simple cases may still improve (fewer local loops), but the tail for complex and specialist-intensive cases extends dramatically.  
  - 90th and 95th percentiles worsen across the board, with especially severe deterioration for `local_expertise_flag=1`, high `case_complexity`, and sensitive `customer_segment`s.  
  - SLA miss rates rise in some teams/segments even while the global average cycle time is flat or slightly improved.

- **Rework and quality pattern**  
  - Reduced local informal advice leads to more incorrect or incomplete first-time decisions, driving up rework after Quality Check and customer escalations.  
  - Central specialists, lacking local context, generate advice that triggers more rework in local follow-up activities.  
  - Outcome quality for complex cases degrades (more adverse `outcome` values) despite apparently efficient handling of simple cases.

- **Role clarity and accountability**  
  - Shared approval queues make it unclear who is responsible for delays and decision quality; many approvals have multiple approvers with small touches.  
  - Staff use handover reasons and comments to “push” ownership around; approval and specialist activities show high `reassignment_count`.  
  - Working-together and subcontracting networks become dense and noisy, indicating overlapping roles and unclear boundaries.

- **Fairness implications**  
  - High-complexity and vulnerable `customer_segment`s experience much worse tail times, higher rework, and higher SLA miss rates than mainstream segments.  
  - East improves somewhat relative to its old baseline but remains disadvantaged in shared queues if routing over-corrects toward East-originated cases.  
  - Geographic or language minorities (proxied by `location` or segment tags) see disproportionate delays and more rework.

- **Early warning signs we are in this scenario**  
  - Rapid growth in 90th/95th percentile waiting times for shared services, especially for high-complexity, legal/risk, and `local_expertise_flag=1` cases.  
  - Rising rework and `reassignment_count` after shared-service and approval activities; more loops involving Rework and Specialist Review.  
  - Widening gap in cycle times, SLA misses, and adverse outcomes between teams and between `customer_segment`s, while average cycle time remains stable.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

- **Tail latency concentration**  
  Even if average cycle time and median waits improve, tail metrics (90th–99th percentile) can worsen for cases passing through overloaded shared queues. Process mining may show more cases with long idle periods in Technical Shared Service, Legal/Risk, or shared approval activities, but these can be drowned out in aggregates dominated by simple cases.

- **High-complexity case degradation**  
  Routing all high `case_complexity` cases to shared services may increase their queue time and rework, especially if central experts are also doing legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Checks. Average cycle time could still look good because low-complexity cases are faster, masking worse performance for exactly the cases where expertise matters most.

- **Local knowledge loss and context-blind decisions**  
  Reduced local specialist availability can remove informal early advice and nuanced understanding of regional conventions (`local_expertise_flag=1`), leading to more rework and misclassification. This erosion of tacit knowledge tends to show up as subtle changes in variants and rework patterns for certain `location`/`customer_segment` combinations, not in the overall average.

- **Fairness distortions across teams and customer segments**  
  Shared queues may unintentionally prioritize some teams or segments (e.g., those with better documentation or more assertive escalation cultures), leading to longer tails, higher rework, or worse outcomes for others. If only overall averages are monitored, systematic disadvantages for certain `customer_segment`s or locations will remain invisible.

- **Stalled learning and skill development in local teams**  
  When all hard cases go to shared services, local caseworkers have fewer opportunities to build technical skill and confidence. Over time, activity-based resource similarity and role-discovery analyses will show narrowing local roles and widened gaps between local and central skill sets, but average cycle time can still look stable while organizational learning capacity declines.

- **Blurred accountability in shared approvals**  
  A shared approval queue can dilute responsibility: many approvers may touch a case briefly, without anyone clearly owning the outcome or delay. The log may show increased `reassignment_count` and complex working-together patterns for approval activities, but management focusing only on cycle time might miss growing confusion over “who owns the decision.”

- **Weaker exception handling and resilience**  
  Exceptional or non-standard cases (e.g., unusual `handover_reason`s, special `customer_segment`s) may get stuck in central queues that are optimized for the majority path. During shocks (specialist absence, sudden volume spikes), the lack of local fallback expertise can cause isolated but severe failures that hardly move the average, yet matter a lot for reputation and risk.

# 5. Pilot design

## 5.1 Pilot scope

- Implement the full shared-technical and shared-approval model first for East and for a defined subset of high-/medium-complexity cases in North and South (e.g., specific `customer_segment`s or `required_specialty` values), while leaving the rest of North/South in the current model.  
- Include Legal/Risk centralization only for a subset of specialties or values of `required_specialty` to avoid overloading already near-capacity resources during the pilot.

## 5.2 Comparison group

- Use remaining North/South cases that continue under the old model as a comparison group (quasi-experimental difference-in-differences).  
- Ensure that pre-pilot `case_complexity`, `customer_segment`, and `required_specialty` profiles are comparable between pilot and comparison groups.

## 5.3 Pre/post observation window

- Collect at least 3–6 months of stable pre-pilot data and plan for at least 3–6 months of post-pilot observation, with weekly leading-indicator monitoring.  
- Freeze other major process and system changes during the core observation period, or document them explicitly to control for confounding.

## 5.4 Segmentation

- Systematically segment all analyses by:  
  - **Team / location**: North, South, East (`team`, `location`).  
  - **Case complexity**: bands of `case_complexity` (e.g., low/medium/high).  
  - **Specialty**: `required_specialty` (technical vs legal vs risk vs other).  
  - **Customer segment**: `customer_segment` (including vulnerable or priority groups).  
  - **Local expertise**: `local_expertise_flag` to track impact on cases needing strong local knowledge.

## 5.5 Guardrail metrics

- Define explicit thresholds for:  
  - 90th and 95th percentile cycle time and waits to shared services by segment.  
  - Rework rate and `reassignment_count` for high-complexity and `local_expertise_flag=1` cases.  
  - SLA miss rates and adverse `outcome` rates by `customer_segment` and `location`.  
  - Handover-of-work centralization (to avoid extreme hub formation) and the share of cases with very high handover counts.

## 5.6 Staff feedback collection

- Run regular structured feedback cycles with local caseworkers, shared-service staff, and supervisors (e.g., monthly surveys plus focus groups) focusing on perceived bottlenecks, role clarity, and learning opportunities.  
- Encourage tagging of unusual `handover_reason`s and use qualitative comments to interpret patterns seen in the event log.

## 5.7 Rollback or adjustment triggers

- Predefine triggers to pause, adjust, or roll back parts of the restructuring, such as:  
  - Sustained (e.g., 4+ weeks) increase beyond agreed thresholds in 90th percentile cycle time or waits for any protected `customer_segment` or high `case_complexity` band.  
  - Significant widening of SLA miss or adverse outcome gaps between teams or key segments.  
  - Persistent spikes in rework or `reassignment_count` around shared-service and approval activities.  
  - Strong, consistent negative staff feedback about unclear ownership or unmanageable workload in shared services.

# 6. Recommendation

The restructuring should **not** be rolled out fully in one step; instead, it should be **piloted and modified** with explicit guardrails. There is a plausible upside (better load balancing, especially for East, and higher specialist utilization), but also significant risks around tails, fairness, learning, and accountability that only detailed, segment-level monitoring can reveal. Management should interpret early drops in average cycle time cautiously, especially if they arise mainly from simple cases, and must not infer that the model is safe without checking tail metrics, segment-specific outcomes, and evidence about local capability and role clarity. Proceed with a well-designed pilot, commit to adjusting routing rules (e.g., keeping some complex local cases with strong local expertise) if the indicators signal harm, and treat the shared-service model as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a guaranteed improvement.
