# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin and horizon.** Let \(t=0\) be `Submit`, and measure all times in elapsed hours. Let:

- \(R\): elapsed time to `Resolve`, the operational success event.
- \(D\): elapsed time to the first internal non-success terminal event, either `Withdraw` or `Reject`.
- \(X\): elapsed time to `Transfer`, when internal observation stops.
- \(L=72\): the SLA horizon.

**Necessary interpretation assumption:** the supplied terminal duration is measured from `Submit`, and the supplied open-case age is elapsed time from `Submit` to the common observation cutoff. This should be verified against the data dictionary. Every case has age/duration greater than 72 hours, so the extract establishes whether each case had reached an internal terminal event by its own 72-hour deadline. It does **not** follow everyone beyond 72 hours under every transfer assumption.

There are several defensible meanings of “SLA breach.”

| Estimand | Definition | Operational question answered |
|---|---|---|
| **E1. Net probability of not achieving successful resolution by 72** | \(P(R>72)\), without conditioning on eventual resolution | Of incoming cases, what proportion will not have succeeded by the deadline? This is a net-success target and requires post-transfer outcomes to identify it fully. |
| **E2. Cumulative incidence of resolution by 72** | \(F_R(72)=P(R\le72)\), or \(P(R\le72,\ R<D)\) when competing exits are present | What proportion actually achieves `Resolve` by the deadline? Under complete outcome ascertainment, E1 is \(1-F_R(72)\). |
| **E3. Probability of remaining without an internal disposition at 72** | \(Q(72)=P(R>72,\ D>72)\) | How many cases still require internal handling at the deadline, rather than having resolved, withdrawn, rejected, or transferred out? Transfer makes this only partially observable in this extract. |
| **E4. Cause-specific late cumulative incidences** | \(F_j(72)=P(T_j\le72,\ T_j<\text{other exits})\), for \(j=R,D,X\) | How much late risk is attributable to resolution, withdrawal/rejection, or loss to follow-up/transfer rather than a single undifferentiated “breach” rate? |
| **E5. Conditional operational late-resolution risk** | \(P(72<R\le72+h\mid R>72,\ D>72,\ X>72)\), with the transfer condition replaced by the chosen observation rule | Among cases still awaiting an internal outcome at 72, how many should be expected to resolve during the next \(h\) hours? This is different from the unconditional proportion among all incoming cases. |

These estimands should not be substituted for one another. For example, a case rejected at 27 hours is a non-success and leaves internal handling, but it is not a resolution that missed the deadline. Governance must specify whether such a case counts as an SLA breach, a closed non-conforming case, or neither.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use separate fields for:

- `t_event_or_censor`: observed terminal time or end of observation.
- `event_code`: `0 = censored`, `1 = Resolve`, `2 = Withdraw/Reject`, `3 = Transfer`.
- `censor_reason`: open follow-up, transfer censoring, or administrative horizon.
- Transfer treatment: censor, internal exit, or linked external outcome.

| Observed status | Case IDs | Event indicator(s) | Competing-risk code | Censor rule | Main sensitivity alternative |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | S01–S04, S07, S13, S16–S20, S27, S28 | Resolution \(=1\) | 1 | Never censor a recorded resolution. Nine occur before 72; four occur after 72. | Preserve late resolutions rather than truncating their outcomes to ordinary censoring at 72 when assessing the realized deadline outcome. |

| `rejected` | S05, S14, S23 | Rejection \(=1\); resolution \(=0\) | 2 | Competing event at its recorded time | Same as withdrawal; adjudication-sensitive outcomes may require outcome misclassification sensitivity. |
| `open`, age \(\le72\) | S10, S15, S24, S29, S31 | No terminal event by 72 | 0 | Administrative censoring at 72 if analysis stops there; retain their observed age for longer follow-up analysis | IP censoring, pattern-mixture, or gap-based sensitivity; they must not be discarded from 72-hour risk sets. |
| `open`, age \(>72\) | S09, S11, S25, S26 | No terminal event by 72; eventual outcome unknown | 0 | Administrative censoring at 72 for the horizon question, or at observed age for a longer curve | Already past the deadline but still without a disposition; not necessarily eventual resolutions. |
| `transferred`, at/before 72 | S08 at 68; S21 at 26 | No internal resolution observed | 3 in the exit scenario | In the censoring scenario, censor at \(X\); in the linked scenario, replace observation with external outcome | Link external outcomes; otherwise show transfer share and sensitivity bounds. |
| `transferred`, after 72 | S32 at 96 | No internal resolution observed by 72; transfer occurs after the horizon | 3 if followed beyond 72 | Censor at 96, exit at 96, or attach an external outcome at its elapsed time | Its late transfer does not itself prove an internal resolution SLA breach. |

The terminal `last_event_gap_h` values are zero because withdrawal, rejection, resolution, and transfer are recorded terminal events. In particular, S08’s zero gap is **not** evidence of inactivity after transfer.

### Three explicit transfer scenarios

**T1 — Independent right censoring at transfer.**  
Set the observation end to \(X\), leave the resolution event indicator zero, and estimate resolution survival under the assumption that, conditional on measured history, transfer is independent of the unrecorded future resolution time.

This is a strong assumption: external organizations may preferentially receive difficult or expedited cases. With the present extract, censoring at 68 also removes S08 from observation before the deadline, even though it was transferred only four hours before it. The resulting resolution CIF is not a complete estimate of eventual resolution.

**T2 — Transfer as an internal-observation exit.**  
Code transfer as a competing event and estimate the probability of reaching `Resolve` before withdrawal, rejection, or transfer.

This matches a defensible **internal-workflow or net-internal-success** estimand. It does not establish that transferred cases were customer-level failures, nor does it recover their eventual resolution.

**T3 — Linked external outcome.**  
Attach an external organization identifier and obtain the external resolution, withdrawal, rejection, or continued-pending time on the original `Submit` clock. Replace the provisional censoring/exit record with that outcome.

This is the preferred scenario for E1/E2 and for conditional forecasts among unresolved cases. Continued external pending cases remain right-censored, with informative-censoring assumptions made explicit.

A further business-definition sensitivity is to treat transfer as an administratively closed non-success. That can be calculated, but it is a policy classification—not a discovered fact about the customer’s eventual outcome.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

There are **13 resolved cases**, of which four resolved after 72. Conditioning only on those 13 gives a late-resolution proportion of **4/13 = 30.8%**, compared with **9/32 = 28.1%** resolved by 72 among all submitted cases.

These are not estimates of the same quantity:

- \(4/13\) conditions on eventual successful resolution and excludes all withdrawals, rejections, transfers, and unresolved cases.
- \(9/32\) describes the fraction of the incoming population reaching resolution by the deadline.

Completed-case analysis therefore answers a selected-population question while often being presented as an operational SLA question.

### Survivor and missing-outcome bias

- S03 at 76 and S13 at 110 show that restricting the sample to resolution before 72 would remove genuine late successes.
- S06, S22, and S30 left through withdrawal, while S05 and S14 left through rejection. Excluding them removes cases that did not experience the success being modeled.
- S08 and S21 transferred before 72. Their external outcomes are missing, not known failures.
- S09, S11, S25, and S26 are open beyond 72. They are known not to have resolved by the deadline but are not known never to resolve.

If a resolve-only Kaplan–Meier curve treats every non-resolution as censoring, it estimates a hypothetical resolution-time distribution under an untestable censoring assumption. The code below illustrates this: its 72-hour non-resolution estimate is approximately **0.564**, versus the observed **23/32 = 0.719** having no observed resolution by 72. The gap is not evidence that the completed-case model is better; it is evidence that the two analyses answer different questions under strong assumptions.

### Competing events

Withdrawal and rejection are observed reasons for leaving the process without `Resolve`. Treating them as ordinary censors in a resolve-only model assumes, implausibly, that their occurrence does not alter the unobserved chance of eventual resolution. S05 was rejected at nine hours, and S06 was withdrawn at 27; both may represent systematically different case populations from successful cases.

For resolution risk, they should ordinarily be competing events. For internal workload, they are terminal workload releases. For a governance measure of successful service, they are non-successes. Those are different estimands, not interchangeable technical choices.

### Informative censoring and inactivity

Open follow-up may depend on unobserved severity, staffing, or organizational attention. Large last-event gaps make that concern concrete:

- S26: age 104, gap 72.
- S11: age 81, gap 55.
- S25: age 79, gap 48.
- S09: age 92, gap 31.
- S10: age 47, gap 19.

S10 shows that a case below the horizon can already look inactive; it should not be treated as “on track” merely because it has not reached 72.

Conversely, S15 has a two-hour gap and S31 a three-hour gap. A short gap is not evidence of imminent resolution. The extract has event times for first terminal outcomes, but not the intermediate event timestamps needed to reconstruct stage timing or historical inactivity.

Transfer may also be informative. S08 and S21 leave before the deadline, while S32 remains internally observed until 96. The assumption that their missing futures are exchangeable with those of continued cases is unsupported.

### Cohort imbalance and nonrandom policy exposure

The cohorts are small and differently composed:

| Cohort | n | Resolved by 72 | Resolved after 72 | Withdraw/reject | Transfer | Open at cutoff | No observed internal disposition by 72 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 15 | 4 | 2 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 5 |
| B | 17 | 5 | 2 | 3 | 2 | 5 | 6 |
| **Total** | **32** | **9** | **4** | **7** | **3** | **9** | **11** |

The unadjusted proportions without observed resolution by 72 are 11/15 for A and 12/17 for B. The difference is small and highly uncertain.

Cohort B followed a policy change, but assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete. It is not a pure treatment group. Differences could reflect case mix, calendar period, staffing, or implementation. A raw cohort comparison cannot establish a policy effect.

Finally, five open cases below 72—S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31—are still at risk. Four open cases above 72—S09, S11, S25, and S26—have already missed the deadline without a disposition. Neither group should be removed, and neither should be labeled an eventual failure without further observation.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

### Comparison of methods

| Method | What it estimates or provides | Assessment for these data |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaplan–Meier** | Survival under independent right censoring | Useful as a sensitivity or for a single unrestricted terminal outcome. Poor primary choice for resolution if withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are competing or informative exits. Resolve-only KM changes the estimand or requires strong assumptions. |
| **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence** | Each event type’s cumulative incidence while accounting for the event process and censoring | Preferred nonparametric summary for the internal-success estimand. If transfer is an internal exit, the resolution CIF is directly interpretable as resolution before internal termination. With uncensored external outcomes, it estimates the net-success target. |
| **Cause-specific hazards** | \(h_j(t\mid Z(t),J(t)=0)\): instantaneous event rate among cases still in the relevant state | Appropriate for understanding resolution, withdrawal/rejection, and transfer mechanisms. Can be used in a multi-state or counting-process model with time-varying stage. A cause-specific coefficient is not itself a cumulative risk and is not automatically a policy effect. |
| **Subdistribution hazards** | Fine–Gray hazard retaining cases in a weighted risk set after competing events | Useful when directly targeting net late resolution while allowing prior competing events to contribute. Less directly interpretable as an instantaneous operational hazard, especially with informative transfer censoring. |
| **Restricted mean time** | Expected time restricted to a horizon | Useful for workload-oriented summaries, such as expected time in an internal unresolved state by 72, or expected time to any internal disposition. Ordinary RMST for eventual resolution is problematic when competing outcomes and transfer censoring are present. |
| **Landmarking** | Outcome after 72 among cases event-free and uncensored at a specified landmark, such as 48 hours | Useful for a prospective “at-risk at 48” operational analysis. It answers a conditional question, excludes cases no longer under observation, and is vulnerable to selection if progression to the landmark depends on prognosis. It cannot replace the full 72-hour estimand. |
| **Multi-state model** | Stage-specific progression and terminal-state hazards, potentially with external follow-up after transfer | Best process-aware framework once exact activity timestamps and external linkage exist. Naturally represents review, rework, escalation, inactivity, internal exits, and transfer. Too little timestamp detail is available here for a credible fitted dynamic model. |

### Recommended primary and secondary analyses

**Primary estimand:** E1, net probability of not reaching `Resolve` by 72, for incoming cases.

**Primary estimation strategy:**

1. When transfer outcomes are linked, use Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence for `Resolve` versus withdrawal/rejection, with unresolved external follow-up censored. Report \(1-F_R(72)\), with uncertainty.
2. With the supplied extract alone, do not present a single model-based value for E1 as if externally ascertained. Instead, report:
   - the observed 72-hour resolution CIF;
   - the count/share transferred;
   - the probability of remaining without an internal disposition;
   - bounds or competing assumptions for net success.

For the available extract, a clearly labeled **internal-success analysis** treats transfer as an exit. Its resolution CIF through 72 is **9/32 = 28.1%**. This is an empirical horizon component, not evidence about the eventual success of transferred cases.

**Secondary analysis:** a cause-specific, counting-process multi-state model using baseline cohort and priority, plus genuinely time-updated stage, rework, escalation, and inactivity variables. This addresses both operational prediction and the mechanisms of exit.

### Horizon estimation and uncertainty

Because every case has at least 72 hours of internal observation, the 72-hour binary calculation needs no survival-library fitting:

- Administrative censoring can be imposed at 72 without losing the 72-hour status.
- S03, S13, S20, and S28 remain uncensored late-resolution observations; they are not failures to follow.
- S09, S11, S25, and S26 remain unresolved risk contributions through 72.
- A case ending administratively at 72 is distinct from a case censored earlier for lack of observation.

Report confidence intervals for population proportions and bootstrap uncertainty for the stratified/model-based summaries. There are only 13 resolutions, four known late resolutions, and nine open cases; multivariable coefficients should not be presented as stable evidence.

A production Cox model could use `lifelines.CoxTimeVaryingFitter`; scikit-survival is an option for Fine–Gray or other survival models. The supplied code does not require either. If unavailable, retain the nonparametric risk-set/Aalen–Johansen analysis and implement a counting-process partial likelihood through a time-varying risk-set constructor; a reduced stratified descriptive analysis is preferable to silently dropping open cases.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

### Available control-flow observations

- **13/32** resolved, including four late resolutions.
- **9/32** contain `Rework`: S07, S13, and S27. All three ultimately resolved, but their durations—54, 110, and 39 hours—are too few to establish a rework effect.
- **4/32** contain repeated `Review`: S03, S07, S13, and S27.
- **10/32** have `AutoCheck` before `Screen`, all in cohort B.
- **12/32** contain `Escalate`.

The fact that B has some `AutoCheck` activity does not, by itself, identify policy compliance. S17, for example, has `Screen>AutoCheck>Review`, showing that the presence of `AutoCheck` and its ordering are different features. The extract does not establish which cases actually received the intended treatment or deviated from it.

### Proposed features

| Feature | Encoding and timing | Classification and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| Cohort, priority | Recorded at case entry | Baseline. Cohort is a period/policy proxy, not a randomized treatment indicator. |
| Submission calendar period, arrival hour, queue length | Must be obtained prospectively or from timestamps | Baseline if known at `Submit`; important for secular workload and case-mix confounding. |
| Current process stage | Screen, automated check, review, rework, escalation, terminal state | Time-varying, updated at each event. A full-prefix endpoint is not a baseline stage. |
| Review count so far | Number of `Review` occurrences through the current event time | Time-varying. Full-sequence review count uses future information if entered at baseline. |
| Rework count/indicator so far | Updated to one when `Rework` occurs | Time-varying and potentially post-policy. Do not impute future rework to a baseline model. |
| Escalation indicator and timing | None at entry; becomes one at the observed `Escalate` time; include elapsed time when known | Time-varying and potentially post-treatment. The supplied `escalated` field is safe only as a description of the final observed prefix. |
| Current inactivity gap | Current time minus timestamp of latest observed event | Time-varying. The supplied final gap is usable at the cutoff, not retrospectively at 48 or 72 without event timestamps. |
| Path variant | Ordered activity pattern, preferably summarized as recurrent controlled features | Full path is post-baseline. Event order is available; durations and concurrency are not. |
| Success/rejection/transfer indicator | Updated only when the corresponding event is observed | Terminal outcome/leakage if used to predict the same outcome from the complete prefix. |

A particularly important leakage example is the supplied `escalated` field. S10 is still open at 47, but its full prefix says `Escalate`. Entering “escalated = yes” at `Submit` would give a model information that did not exist until an unknown later time. The correct counting-process representation holds this value at zero until the escalation event and changes it thereafter.

Similarly, the prefix does not establish the stage at 72. S03 resolves at 76, but the extract does not tell us whether its second `Review` occurred before or after 72. Only exact activity timestamps can reconstruct historical stage and escalation timing.

### Proposed state-transition representation

Use a semi-Markov or multi-state counting-process model, allowing repeated entry into states:

`Submit`  
 `Screening / AutoCheck`  
 `Review` or `Escalation`

From `Review` or `Escalation`:

- `Review  Rework  Review`
- `Review  Escalation  Review`
- `Review  Resolve`
- `Escalation  Resolve`
- `Escalation  Review`
- `Review  Withdraw`
- `Review  Reject`
- `Escalation  Transfer  External Resolve / Withdraw / Reject / Pending`

The supplied cases also include direct withdrawal/rejection after screening, direct resolution after automated checking, and unresolved states ending at any nonterminal stage. Those should be permitted transitions.

`Open` is not a separate observed process stage; it is continued observation in the last recorded stage. An inactivity state can be added as an operational overlay when the gap exceeds a prespecified threshold.

The extract permits variant discovery but not timestamp-based process mining, token replay, or a defensible duration model. One should not invent equal-spaced or proportional activity timestamps from the terminal duration.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analyses must change the **estimand or observation assumption**, not merely select a more favorable model.

| Analysis | Assumption varied | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Transfer censoring** | Independent censoring at transfer; withdrawal/rejection remain competing events | Estimates resolution survival under a strong exchangeability assumption. The observed resolution CIF is generally only a lower bound on full follow-up resolution. |
| **B. Transfer exit** | Transfer terminates internal observation and is a competing event | Targets successful resolution before internal termination. Appropriate for internal workload, but not a claim about customer-level eventual success. |
| **C. Linked external outcomes** | Recover external outcomes on the original elapsed-time scale | Preferred target for net SLA success. Continued pending transfers remain censored and may still be informative. |
| **D. Withdrawal/rejection handling** | Separate causes versus pooled internal terminal event; alternatively, resolve-only censoring | Pooling can be valid for the internal-disposition estimand. Resolve-only censoring changes the interpretation and can inflate apparent resolution survival. |
| **E. Informative open follow-up** | Independent censoring, inverse-probability weighting, explicit pattern-mixture departures, and gap-based selection | Tests whether stale or escalated cases have systematically different future outcomes. Weights cannot be validated reliably with only nine open cases. |
| **F. Policy attribution** | Assignment-cohort comparison versus compliance-based or per-protocol comparison | Assignment-cohort comparison is the most defensible available policy contrast. Per-protocol comparisons risk post-treatment selection; a valid causal contrast needs rollout and assignment-mechanism data. |

### Transfer bounds and possible qualitative reversal

With no external information for S08, S21, and S32, deliberately extreme bounds for the true resolution target are:

- \(F_R(72)\) between **9/32 = 28.1%** and **12/32 = 37.5%**.
- \(P(R>72)\) between **20/32 = 62.5%** and **23/32 = 71.9%**.

The lower resolution bound assigns all three transfers an eventual post-72 resolution; the upper bound assigns none an eventual resolution. These are **worst/best-case identification bounds, not confidence intervals or plausible equal-probability scenarios**.

The four known late resolutions, S03, S13, S20, and S28, contribute 4/32 under every transfer scenario. The uncertainty concerns the three transferred cases, not those observed outcomes.

Qualitative conclusions that could reverse include:

- A resolve-only KM analysis can report a much lower late-resolution burden than the observed population proportion if competing exits are assumed unrelated to future resolution.
- A transfer-exit analysis can rank cohorts or priorities differently from a censoring analysis because transfers are removed from internal resolution risk at different times.
- Excluding high-gap open cases can change late follow-up conclusions substantially. S26, S11, S25, and S09 all have gaps over 24 hours; a 24-hour gap exclusion is an explicit sensitivity choice, not a neutral cleaning rule.
- Changing the governance definition from “not resolved” to “no internal disposition” moves rejected, withdrawn, and transferred cases into a different numerator/denominator and can reverse statements about which priority or cohort is worst.
- A policy comparison could attenuate or reverse after adjustment if cohort differences are mainly case mix or calendar workload. The current data cannot resolve that possibility.

For withdrawal/rejection, pooled versus separate coding does not change resolution before the competing exit under a fixed target, but it can change workload predictions, adjudication burden, and model rankings. Misclassifying them as independent censors is not a harmless alternative.

# 7. Operational interpretation

### From estimands to decisions

- **Pre-deadline queue management:** use \(1-F_R(72)\), or a time-dependent probability of not yet resolving, to identify incoming cases likely to miss successful resolution. Do not add transfers automatically to “failures.”
- **72-hour workload reconciliation:** use the probability of remaining without an internal disposition. Cases still internally open need capacity; transferred cases need external tracking and reconciliation.
- **Post-72 resolution forecasting:** among internally unresolved cases, report conditional resolution risk over a specified additional horizon. Do not present the population late-resolution proportion as the forecast for this selected group.
- **Inactivity alerts:** use current stage and current inactivity gap. S26’s 72-hour final gap would justify a case review, but S26’s age/gap must not be used as though it were known at an earlier alert time.
- **Withdrawal/rejection governance:** distinguish policy-adjudicated closures from SLA failures. Changes in their incidence should be reviewed separately from resolution timeliness.

Risk scores are predictions conditional on observed history, not the causal effect of escalating, automating screening, or assigning priority. For example, a higher risk among escalated cases may reflect that escalation was triggered by greater complexity.

### Calibration

For each prediction time, report:

- calibration intercept and slope;
- observed versus predicted resolution and unresolved-workload risks;
- Brier score and time-dependent log loss where applicable;
- calibration by cohort, priority, current stage, and transfer status.

Calibration must use outcomes for all cases in the relevant risk population, not only completed resolutions. Transfers with unknown external outcomes cannot simply be coded as external failures for calibration.

With only 32 cases, validation should be modest. Use leave-one-out or bootstrap optimism assessment rather than claiming a validated production model. Report uncertainty prominently; four known late resolutions are too few to support fine-grained calibration.

### Fairness and governance

Priority groups contain only eight P1, fourteen P2, and ten P3 cases. Audit, at minimum:

- calibration and resolution CIF by priority and cohort;
- alert rates and alert burden at a common threshold;
- false-alert and missed-event rates where outcomes are sufficiently ascertained;
- external-transfer handling by cohort and priority;
- queue burden and staffing consequences.

A cohort difference need not itself be unfair, especially when cohort reflects a policy period. Conversely, an apparently accurate overall model can still generate disproportionate operational burdens. Governance should specify the cost of false alerts, missed late successes, and unnecessary manual reviews before setting thresholds.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Risk sets and cause-specific product-limit estimates.
#
# All cases enter at t=0. With competing causes, S_j(t-) d Lambda_j(t)
# is the Aalen-Johansen increment for cause j.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
def product_limit_cif(data, cause, horizon=H):
    t = data["t_event_or_censor"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
    code = data["event_code"].to_numpy(dtype=int)
    risk = len(data)
    survival = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    event_times = sorted(set(t[(code == cause) & (t <= horizon)]))

    for u in event_times:
        n_risk = int((t >= u).sum())  # ties are at risk just before u
        d = int(((code == cause) & (t == u)).sum())
        if n_risk == 0:
            raise RuntimeError("Invalid risk set")
        increment = survival * d / n_risk
        cif += increment
        survival -= increment
    return cif, survival


def risk_table(data, horizon=H):
    t = data["t_event_or_censor"].to_numpy(dtype=float)
    code = data["event_code"].to_numpy(dtype=int)
    times = sorted(set(t[(t > 0) & (t <= horizon)]))
    rows = []

    for u in times:
        row = {
            "hour": u,
            "at_risk_before": int((t >= u).sum())
        }
        for cause, label in [(1, "resolve"), (2, "withdraw_reject"),
                             (3, "transfer_exit")]:
            row["d_" + label] = int(((code == cause) & (t == u)).sum())
        row["censored_before_or_at"] = int(
            ((code == 0) & (t >= u) & (t <= horizon)).sum()
        )
        rows.append(row)
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def wilson_interval(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n
    den = 1 + z*z/n
    center = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / den
    half = z * math.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / den
    return center - half, center + half

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The highest-priority additional data are:

1. **Exact timestamps for every activity**, including start, completion, and event arrival, on a common clock. These are required for stage-at-72 features, escalation timing, repeated-review intervals, historical inactivity, and time-varying survival models.
2. **External outcomes after transfer:** organization identifier, transfer acceptance/dispatch time, external activity timestamps, final outcome, and continued-pending dates. These are necessary to identify eventual resolution and distinguish external latency from internal performance.
3. **Submission dates and the exact common cutoff.** The extract establishes 72-hour follow-up for these cases, but not the submission distribution, calendar exposure, or whether later submissions are absent because the extract was selected or truncated.
4. **A time-varying capacity measure:** queue size, staffing, workload, service interruptions, weekends/holidays, and elapsed-time convention. Cohort and priority effects may otherwise absorb workload and secular changes.
5. **Policy rollout details:** exact eligibility and start dates, assignment mechanism, reasons for noncompliance, and a verified treatment indicator. `AutoCheck` presence or ordering is not a complete compliance measure.
6. **Outcome adjudication and missingness:** reasons for withdrawal/rejection, duplicate or misclassified events, external linkage failures, and whether apparent inactivity is technical unavailability rather than process delay.
7. **Sampling and inclusion criteria:** whether these are all consecutive submissions, a sample, or a filtered subset. Generalization to future incoming cases is not justified without that information.
8. **A larger follow-up sample and more late events.** Four known late resolutions and nine open cases are insufficient for stable multivariable predictions, fine subgroup calibration, or strong conclusions about differential policy effects.

Until those data are available, the most defensible report is a transparent set of horizon outcomes, competing-exit cumulative incidences, explicit transfer sensitivity, and operational predictions with substantial uncertainty—not a single definitive “breach probability.”
