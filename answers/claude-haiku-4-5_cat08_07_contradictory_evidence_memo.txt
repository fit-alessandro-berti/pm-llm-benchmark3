## 1. Executive Memo

**Recommendation: Adopt Option E (Hybrid deployment with targeted measurement) rather than national scaling or rollback.**

Redesign R has delivered real but *segment-dependent* value. Median cycle time improved 24%, handovers dropped from 34% to 12%, and simple low-risk houses show 6-day faster delivery with higher satisfaction. However, the design has exposed critical weaknesses: tail duration worsened dramatically (p95 from 45 to 62 days), first-time-right installation fell from 82% to 76%, complaint rates jumped from 6.1% to 9.8%, and second visits increased from 11% to 18%. These are not minor side effects—they indicate that simplified rules are failing for complex cases and that high coordinator utilization is creating new bottlenecks.

**The core problem:** The redesign conflates two distinct improvements—coordinator ownership (beneficial) and rule-based skipping of field surveys and pre-billing activation testing (harmful for complex cases). A blunt national scale would gain handover reduction but incur unacceptable rework, customer dissatisfaction, and likely cost overruns in high-risk segments.

**Why not alternatives:**
- **National scale (A):** Risk of reputational and cost damage in apartments and old buildings, where the segment-level complaint rate jumped +7pp and first-time-right dropped 11pp.
- **Rollback (B):** Abandons demonstrated gains for simple houses and ignores the fact that coordinator ownership itself is valuable.
- **Partial rollback (C):** Restores field surveys and pre-billing activation for all cases, wasting the opportunity to optimize simple cases and losing the handover benefit.
- **Uncontrolled experiment (D):** Valuable but slow; leaves successful simple-house cases in baseline process for weeks longer than necessary.

**Option E balances speed, risk, and learning:**
1. **Immediate:** Deploy Redesign R for simple low-risk houses only (34% of cases). This captures the 6-day median gain and reduces coordinator load.
2. **Pause:** Suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation moves for apartments, old buildings, and business customers until root causes are understood.
3. **Measure:** Launch M1 (survey-skip validation), M3 (reason codes for failures), M4 (full downstream cost), M6 (predictive rule validation), and M7 (conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit-check gaps).
4. **Reintegrate:** In 8–12 weeks, decide segment-specific rules for survey skipping and activation moves based on evidence. Adjust coordinator capacity or support models if utilization hits >85%.

**Timeline and rollout gates:**
- Weeks 1–2: Deploy R for simple houses in all regions; monitor SLA and first-time-right daily.
- Weeks 3–12: Run measurement plan. Validate building-risk rule and reason codes for failures.
- Week 10: Decision gate—if survey-skip validation and permit-check analysis show safe rules for apartments and old buildings, begin staged rollout; otherwise, keep those segments in baseline.
- Week 16: Evaluate coordinator resilience and utilization; adjust team structure if needed.

**Expected value:** Preserve the ~6-day gain for simple houses (34% of volume, high NPS impact), avoid the 16pp complaint increase that national scale would likely incur, and gather evidence for a second-phase rollout that targets apartments and old buildings without the current blindness. This hedges downside risk (regional SLA breach if apartment complaints spike) while keeping upside optionality open.

**Cost and organizational implications:**
- Near-term: Maintain field-survey team capacity; redeploy activation specialists to troubleshoot manual-activation and post-billing complaint drivers.
- Medium-term: If apartment/old-building rule validation succeeds, design segment-specific coordinator roles (e.g., "apartment coordinator" with pre-survey authority).
- Critical: Measure start timestamps and reason codes immediately; current event log gaps make root-cause analysis unreliable and expose us to silent rework.

## 3. Explanation of Metric Conflicts

### 3.1 High Fitness but Low Precision

**Conflict:** The pilot discovered model achieves fitness 0.94 (excellent replay of observed cases) but precision 0.51 (model allows many unobserved sequences).

**Explanation:** The redesign introduced conditional rules (survey skip if predicted low-risk, manual activation if signal acceptable, billing start if no issues flagged). When these rules are applied, cases follow a simplified path. The discovered model learns that "these simplified paths are observed" but also encodes "the model *allows* survey skip + manual activation + billing + failed visit + complaint" as alternative branches, even if that specific combo is rare.

Under coordinator pressure (93% utilization, F8), ad-hoc exceptions multiply: some cases skip survey and fail, some activate post-billing, some take both paths. The model's large state space captures all allowed combinations, but the generalization score (0.56) is low because the actual process is drifting—not truly stable. In contrast, the baseline process, though more handover-heavy, follows a more rigid sequence (validate  schedule  survey  install  activate  bill), yielding higher precision.

**Implication:** The pilot process is less predictable and more fragile. Rules are being overridden or circumvented under load.

---

### 3.2 Improved Median but Worsened Tail

**Conflict:** Median cycle time improved 24% (14.2  10.8 days) but p95 worsened by 38% (45  62 days).

**Explanation:** For simple, low-risk cases following the intended redesign path (merged validate/schedule, skipped survey, direct install, prompt activation, billing), throughput is higher and median time is shorter. These cases are ~34% of volume and pull the median down.

However, for apartments and old buildings (~47% of cases), the predictive rule or permit-check logic fails. These cases enter the process expecting a simplified path but encounter obstacles (failed field survey, permit discovery, customer rescheduling, coordinator unavailability). They then queue for a second visit or escalation. Because the coordinator is at 93% utilization (F8), rework cases experience longer delays. Additionally, temporal profile shows "Failed visit  Second visit delay increased in regions with high coordinator utilization." These outliers (second visits, reschedules, complaints, escalations) extend the p90 and p95 tail.

**Implication:** The median gain masks a growing population of stuck cases. National scale would improve speed for fast cases but create a new "backlog of failures," worsening SLA and customer experience for complex segments.

---

### 3.3 Fewer Handovers but Lower Resilience

**Conflict:** Handovers with 3+ steps fell from 34% to 12% (strong improvement) but resilience worsened: "cases stalled when coordinator was absent" and "context was not shared."

**Explanation:** The redesign assigned each case to a single coordinator after address validation. This eliminates handoffs for simple cases but concentrates knowledge and context in one person. When that person is unavailable (sick leave, vacation, high workload), the case lacks context—no one else knows what was decided or why.

In the baseline, handovers meant multiple people touched each case, creating redundancy: the scheduler knew the address, the field surveyor knew the building, the installer knew the risks. If any one person was unavailable, another could pick up. Coordinator ownership is *efficient* but *brittle*. It is also reaching saturation (93% utilization), so unavailability is increasing.

**Implication:** Handover reduction is a real and valuable efficiency gain but must be paired with either (a) backup/cross-training to maintain resilience, or (b) capped utilization targets (e.g., 75–80%) to maintain flex capacity.

---

### 3.4 Lower Visible Cost but Higher Rework or Downstream Cost

**Conflict:** Mean cost before complaint handling improved (EUR 184  161, 12%) but estimated cost including complaint/revisit handling worsened (EUR 198  207, +4%).

**Explanation:** The visible cost metric (EUR 161) reflects labor and direct actions during the core process (validate, schedule, survey, install, activate, bill). Skipping field survey, merging tasks, and skipping pre-billing activation reduce labor during the core process.

However, the estimated cost (EUR 207) includes downstream rework: second visits, complaint handling, re-activation, and billing adjustments. The pilot data note states "Cost data excludes downstream complaint-handling after week 4," so the EUR 207 estimate for the 6-week pilot is incomplete and likely understates true cost. But the trend is clear: cost *after* the core process is rising (rework rate up, second visits up +7pp, complaints up +3.7pp). Over a full 30–60 day horizon, rework costs exceed the labor savings from task merging.

**Implication:** The redesign is front-loading efficiency (faster core process) at the cost of back-loading rework (more complaints, revisits). Full-lifecycle cost is worse, and the visible metric is misleading.

---

### 3.5 Improvement for Simple Houses but Deterioration for Apartments/Old Buildings

**Conflict:** Simple houses show median 6 days, first-time-right +3pp, and complaint 1pp, while apartments show median 2 days, first-time-right 8pp, and complaint +5pp; old buildings show median +1 day, first-time-right 11pp, complaint +7pp.

**Explanation:** The redesign embeds assumptions tailored to simple, low-risk houses:
- Merged address validation + scheduling works for standard addresses with clear routing.
- Survey skip (based on predictive rule) is safe for homes with standard construction and no permit risks.
- Post-billing activation is acceptable for low-signal-variability areas.

For apartments and old buildings:
- Addresses are complex (multi-unit, roof antenna placement, shared utilities); merged validation/scheduling misses nuance.
- Predictive rule for survey skip has false negatives: buildings with unexpected wiring, asbestos, or permit holds are flagged as low-risk.
- Post-billing activation in older buildings with signal challenges leads to billing disputes when service is not actually live.

The generalization and precision scores reflect this: the model is learning that the process works well for simple cases but is poorly specified for complex ones.

**Implication:** A one-size-fits-all rollout is ineffective. Segment-specific rules or process variants are necessary.

## 4. Decision Alternatives

### **Alternative A: Scale Redesign R Nationally**

**Advantages:**
- Rapid nationwide deployment of handover reduction (22pp) and median speedup (24%).
- Simplifies process training and documentation.
- Leverages sunk cost of pilot design and tooling.
- Fast customer-experience win for simple houses.

**Risks:**
- Complaint rate will likely increase from 6.1% to ~8–9% nationally, damaging brand and increasing contact center load.
- First-time-right will drop below 80%, increasing rework and engineer utilization.
- Tail duration (p90, p95) will worsen, causing SLA breaches and customer frustration in high-risk regions.
- Coordinator utilization will reach 100% in high-volume regions, creating cascading delays.
- Cost including rework will increase nationally (pilot shows +EUR 9 in incomplete estimate).
- Customer NPS will decline further, offsetting gains in speed.

**Conditions for reasonableness:**
- Only if the company is explicitly prioritizing speed-to-appointment over first-time-right and is willing to absorb a 3–5pp complaint increase and NPS decline as acceptable trade-offs.
- Only if regions with apartments and old buildings are ring-fenced (use baseline) and simple-house regions are scaled (use R)—but this contradicts "national scale."

**Decision weight:** **Not recommended.**

---

### **Alternative B: Roll Back to Baseline**

**Advantages:**
- Restores stability and precision (0.79 vs. 0.51) in the process model.
- Eliminates tail duration risk and post-billing activation issues.
- Maintains first-time-right rate (82%) and complaint rate (6.1%).
- Predictable cost and SLA performance.
- Resets coordinator utilization and resilience.

**Risks:**
- Abandons 24% median speed improvement for simple houses; loses competitive advantage in acquisition for fast-moving segments.
- Handover-heavy baseline is inefficient and frustrating for coordinators; re-introducing it harms internal morale.
- Wastes investment in coordinator training and tooling.
- Signals to the organization that incremental innovation is risky and fails; dampens future improvement initiatives.
- Does not address the root causes of redesign failure; if scaled differently, the redesign might succeed.

**Conditions for reasonableness:**
- If analysis of the pilot reveals that the redesign concept is fundamentally flawed (not just over-applied to wrong segments), rollback may be prudent.
- If reputational damage from complaints is imminent (e.g., public SLA breach or social media backlash), immediate rollback stabilizes brand.

**Decision weight:** **Not recommended as primary path, but may be a fallback if measurement shows fundamental design flaws.**

---

### **Alternative C: Scale Coordinator Ownership Only; Suspend Survey Skipping and Post-Billing Activation**

**Advantages:**
- Captures the handover-reduction benefit (22pp) and its efficiency gain across all segments.
- Restores field-survey discipline and pre-billing activation testing for all cases, eliminating the compliance gaps (F13).
- Reduces manual-activation rate (15%  back to baseline ~7%).
- Coordinator ownership is the likely source of the handover win and can be scaled independently.
- Process model precision should improve as skipping rules are removed.

**Risks:**
- Simple houses lose the median speedup (6 days) and revert to 2 day gain at best; competitive advantage in simple segment is halved.
- Coordinator utilization remains high (85–95%) because the role still owns the case; without task merging or survey skipping, the role may overload.
- Does not address the root causes: why did surveyors not flag permit risks? Why did post-billing activation seem attractive? Those lessons are lost.
- Conservative approach may not attract executive buy-in if competitor is scaling Redesign R nationally.

**Conditions for reasonableness:**
- If measurement (M5, M6) shows that survey skipping and post-billing activation are the failure points and coordinator ownership is safe, then this alternative isolates the safe part.
- Suitable if the company wants a "staged rollout" and can wait 8–12 weeks for clarity before full redesign relaunch.

**Decision weight:** **Viable fallback if Option E measurement shows coordinator ownership is stable and rule-skipping is the sole culprit.**

---

### **Alternative D: Run a Controlled Regional Experiment with Better Segment/Risk Measurement**

**Advantages:**
- Gold-standard evidence: randomized or matched-pair design isolates causal effects.
- Segment-specific measurement (M1: survey-skip validation, M6: rule validation) clarifies which rules are safe for which populations.
- Reduces rollout risk by answering key questions before scale.
- Supports long-term organizational capability in process optimization.
- Separates coordinator ownership from rule skipping experimentally (M5).

**Risks:**
- 8–12 weeks to run and analyze; slow decision-making.
- Existing simple-house cases continue to experience 14.2-day baseline time while experiment-region cases get faster service (fairness issue).
- Requires sustained measurement discipline and potential conflicts between experiment design and operational urgency.
- May be seen as indecisive or risk-averse by stakeholders expecting a faster decision.
- Does not guarantee that insights will generalize beyond experimental regions (A3 assumption).

**Conditions for reasonableness:**
- Best suited for a company with high cost-of-failure (reputational, financial, safety) and willingness to invest in evidence-based process design.
- Suitable if stakeholders accept 8–12 week timeline and simple-house customer experience is not a competitive urgency.

**Decision weight:** **Overcautious as standalone; best paired with immediate action on simple houses (Option E).**

---

### **Alternative E: Hybrid—Deploy R for Simple Low-Risk Houses Only; Add Controls for Apartments/Old Buildings; Run Targeted Measurement**

**Advantages:**
- **Immediate wins:** Deploy Redesign R for simple houses (34% of volume) immediately, capturing the 6-day median gain, handover reduction, and customer-satisfaction lift for the largest beneficiary segment.
- **Risk containment:** Apartments and old buildings remain on baseline or a modified process until evidence supports customization; complaint rate and first-time-right remain stable for 47% of cases.
- **Parallel learning:** Launch M1, M3, M4, M6, M7 while simple-house rollout is live; by week 10, have evidence to decide whether to extend to other segments.
- **Organizational morale:** Demonstrates commitment to innovation for the segment that most benefits; does not abandon efficiency gains.
- **Operationally pragmatic:** Simple houses have fewer exceptions (34% of volume, low permit risk, standard addresses); coordinator can safely handle them at 85% utilization; field-survey team redeployed to apartments and old buildings.
- **Path to scale:** If measurement validates survey-skip and post-billing rules for apartments, execute phase 2 rollout. If not, Redesign R remains a successful targeted optimization.

**Risks:**
- Operational complexity: maintain two process variants and two coordinator teams (or a tiered coordinator with different rule sets).
- Measurement M1–M7 requires sustained discipline; if abandoned under pressure, insights are lost.
- Simple-house process may diverge from apartment/old-building process, harming long-term generalization (two systems instead of one).
- Competitors scaling R nationally may capture market share in simple-house segments if rollout timeline slips.

**Conditions for reasonableness:**
- Suitable when segment-level benefits are clear (E clearly shows) and root causes are partially understood (survey-skip rule is suspect, permit-check failure is visible, coordinator load is measurable).
- Organizationally feasible if simple-house volume is large enough to justify separate coordinator team or tiered training.
- Requires executive buy-in that "staged rollout with measurement gates" is more profitable long-term than "fast national scale with cleanup costs."

**Decision weight:** **Recommended.**

## 6. Final Recommendation

### **Primary Decision: Adopt Option E (Hybrid Deployment with Targeted Measurement)**

---

### **Immediate Actions (Weeks 1–2)**

1. **Deploy Redesign R for simple low-risk houses (SIMPLE segment) in all regions immediately.**
   - Selection criteria: Customer type = "Residential," Building type = "Single-family," Address validation returns "standard" (no special routing, no multi-unit).
   - This is ~34% of current volume and shows the strongest positive metrics (median 6 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaint 1pp).
   - Establish daily SLA and first-time-right monitoring for this segment to catch degradation early.

2. **Keep apartments (APARTMENT segment, ~29% of volume), old buildings (OLD_BUILDING segment, ~18%), and business customers (BUSINESS segment, ~8%) on the baseline process.**
   - Explicitly communicate to field teams and customer service that these segments have NOT been redesigned; manage expectations.
   - Provide regional SLA targets (e.g., p90 < 32 days) for baseline segments; if breached, escalate immediately.

3. **Pause all survey-skipping and post-billing activation moves for non-SIMPLE segments.**
   - Field-survey team is redeployed from 52% to 70% utilization, focusing on apartments and old buildings where permit checks and complex routing are critical.
   - Activation specialists refocus on pre-billing testing for APARTMENT and OLD_BUILDING.

4. **Establish a rollout operations board with daily standups.**
   - Track first-time-right, complaint rate, SLA, and coordinator utilization for SIMPLE segment daily.
   - Identify threshold breaches early (e.g., if first-time-right drops below 75%, or if complaint jumps above 7%, trigger root-cause investigation within 24 hours).

---

### **What to Pause**

1. **Pause the application of Redesign R to apartments and old buildings** until measurement (M3, M6) clarifies root causes of the 11pp and 8pp first-time-right drops.
2. **Pause survey-skipping rule rollout** (does not start for non-SIMPLE segments).
3. **Pause post-billing activation experiments** except for SIMPLE segment (where it is already part of the minimal redesign).
4. **Pause national scale planning** until week 10 measurement gate.

---

### **What to Scale**

1. **Coordinator ownership model for SIMPLE segment:**
   - Single coordinator owns each SIMPLE case from address validation through billing start.
   - Task merging (validate + schedule) is enabled for SIMPLE only.
   - Target coordinator utilization: 80–85% (leave flex capacity for absences and escalations).

2. **SIMPLE segment Redesign R deployment:**
   - Roll out to all regions (not just pilot regions).
   - Leverage the predictive "SIMPLE" classification rules to auto-route cases.
   - Monitor daily; scale is no-pause unless first-time-right or SLA metrics breach thresholds.

3. **Handover reduction (as a standalone capability):**
   - The finding that handovers dropped from 34% to 12% is valuable, even if not scaled nationally.
   - Codify coordinator-ownership practices as a reusable pattern for future process improvements.

### **Phase 2 Decision Gate (Week 10)**

**Conditions for expanding Redesign R to apartments:**
- M3 reason codes and M6 rule validation together show that failed survey predictions are the primary failure mode (not coordinator overload or permit-check gaps).
- M4 downstream cost analysis shows the cost delta is acceptable (<+EUR 5 per case) or becomes acceptable if rule is fixed.
- M7 conformance monitoring shows billing-before-activation is rare in SIMPLE segment (< 2%) and does not correlate with complaints.
- Coordinator utilization in SIMPLE segment remains 80–85% and does not cause case stalls or absences.

**If conditions are met:**
- Design **phase 2 apartments variant:** re-enable survey-skip *only* for apartments where permit and utility risk are pre-validated; otherwise, keep field survey mandatory.
- Design **phase 2 old-building variant:** mandatory permit-check step before installation; field survey not skippable.
- Retrain coordinators on segment-specific rules.
- Begin staged rollout to apartment regions in weeks 11–14.

**If conditions are not met:**
- Sustain SIMPLE segment deployment only.
- Issue a revised memo explaining why apartments and old buildings remain on baseline.
- Document lessons learned (e.g., "survey-skipping rule is unsafe for permit-sensitive buildings; recommend future rules undergo pre-implementation field validation").
- Extend measurement timeline for phase 2 to 16 weeks if additional data is needed.

### **Success Metrics and Rollout Halt Conditions**

**Weekly monitoring (SIMPLE segment):**
- First-time-right: must stay  75%. If drops below, halt new case enrollment to SIMPLE process and conduct root-cause audit.
- Complaint rate: must stay  7.5% ( baseline 1pp). If exceeds, investigate within 24 hours.
- SLA p90: must stay  22 days. If breaches, check coordinator utilization and available capacity.
- Coordinator utilization: must stay  85%. If exceeds, add coordinator capacity or adjust case routing.

**Monthly review (all segments):**
- p95 cycle time baseline segments: must stay  46 days (slightly better than baseline 45). If worsens, field-survey team may be overloaded; reallocate.
- Cost-per-case (full lifecycle): baseline segments must stay  EUR 198. If exceeds, rework/complaints are increasing; investigate.
- NPS: track separately for SIMPLE vs. baseline; expect SIMPLE to improve 2–4 points, baseline to stay stable.

### **Organizational and Cost Implications**

**Near-term (Weeks 1–12):**
- Simple-house customers: +6 days faster median, improved NPS, no additional cost (task merging reduces labor).
- Apartment/old-building customers: no change; SLA stable; first-time-right stable at 82%.
- Coordinator team: expand by 1–2 coordinators to handle SIMPLE volume and achieve 80–85% utilization (prevents overload).
- Field-survey team: shift from 52% to 70% utilization; more stable working conditions.
- Overall cost: neutral to slightly negative (additional coordinators offset labor savings in SIMPLE merging).

**Medium-term (Weeks 12–26, post-phase-2 decision):**
- If phase 2 succeeds: expand to apartments, leverage handover-reduction and partial task-merging benefits; overall cost improves EUR 5–10 per case.
- If phase 2 is shelved: maintain SIMPLE deployment and baseline for others; establishes Redesign R as a successful niche optimization (not a universal redesign).

**Long-term:**
- Capability: organization learns to execute segment-specific process variants and measure them robustly (increases confidence in future innovation).
- Competitive positioning: fast service for simple houses (marketing advantage); reliable service for complex cases (customer retention advantage).

### **Communication and Risk Mitigation**

1. **To field teams:** "We are rolling out a faster process for single-family homes. Multi-unit and older buildings will stay on the current stable process. We will measure what works and improve everything in phase 2."
2. **To customers in SIMPLE segment:** Expect faster appointment and completion (6 days sooner).
3. **To customers in baseline segments:** No change; same reliable service.
4. **To customer service:** Prepare for shifted complaint types (will move from "slow appointment" to "fast but failed" if rework occurs, but we expect this to be small in SIMPLE segment; apartment complaints will increase if they are mistakenly routed to Redesign R—monitor escalation logs).
5. **To finance:** Expect cost to be flat or slightly negative in year 1 (due to additional coordinators and measurement overhead) and positive in year 2+ (if phase 2 succeeds and handover gains scale).
