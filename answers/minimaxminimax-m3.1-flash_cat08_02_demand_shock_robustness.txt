## 3. Robust redesign package

Principles: (i) **do not add capacity where the problem is variance or rework** — the single largest load multipliers (batch wait 4.1×, rework 2.7×, specialist hold 5.6×) are self-inflicted; (ii) **never relieve a station by moving work to a station already 95% utilised**; (iii) **protect the two contractual targets simultaneously** — they cannot be prioritised against each other; (iv) **net headcount change  0**; capacity is re-pointed, and the only genuine additions are time-bounded cover and variable partner-lab overflow.

**R1 — Guaranteed Urgent fast lane (bypass by design, not by exception).** Reserve a fixed prep slot and machine position each run for Urgent and near-Urgent (Repeat) cases, and make the bypass *default* rather than dispatcher-discretionary. **Robust:** it addresses the mechanism the data points to (bypass rate 74%58% and batch-wait entries  non-bypassing Urgent), it works in S1/S3/S4 because it protects against queueing rather than adding capacity, and it is indifferent to which case type is surging. **Assumes:** physically separable prep and machine positions exist; Urgent volume can be forecast daily; the loss of batching efficiency for Urgent (each Urgent run costs setup) is acceptable — quantify against the 36 h penalty being removed. **Fails when:** the machine pool has no reserve (S2), when reserved capacity is consumed by non-Urgent work, or when it is used to hide an SLA breach by reclassifying Urgent cases.

**R2 — Age-capped, arrival-triggered batching (replaces I2).** Keep batches at a size that matches *machine* efficiency, but bound the *wait*: (a) batches trigger on arrival volume/age, not on the clock; (b) a batch is split into an early-departure sub-batch once its oldest member passes an age threshold; (c) within a run, urgent tubes are sequenced first. **Robust:** it attacks the +36 h tail without shrinking batches, so it does not add setup load to the scarcest resource. **Assumes:** tubes can be sequenced within a run; the laboratory information system can age-order queues. **Fails when:** batches are physically inseparable by container type, or when splitting creates too many small runs (watch total machine throughput, not queue time).

**R3 — Failure-aware machine assignment (replaces I3 as stated).** Keep the *intent* of dedicating the reliable machine to time-critical work, but make it **conditional on measured failure performance**: route Urgent/Complex/repeat to M1, Routine to M2 only while M2's weekly repeat rate is within its baseline band; automatically shift 20–30% of Routine back to M1 when M2's repeat rate exceeds the band or M2's queue exceeds a threshold. **Robust:** it uses the M2 failure signal rather than assuming a static allocation, so it is safe in S1 and self-corrects in S2. **Assumes:** M1 has genuine spare capacity in off-peak windows; failure attribution per machine is measurable (needs instrumentation). **Fails when:** M1 is the failed unit, or when M2's higher failure rate is itself caused by the Routine load it was given (a self-fulfilling allocation) — hence the feedback loop and a minimum-M2-throughput floor.

**R4 — Repeat-assay containment (targets the 2.9× multiplier).** Triage repeats *before* they re-enter the machine queue: pre-allocate a protected off-peak slot on the **reliable** machine for repeats, batch repeat runs together, and separate "instrument failure" repeats from "sample failure" repeats. **Robust:** repeats are the largest single controllable load multiplier and are worst exactly when the machine is tight. **Assumes:** repeats can be delayed without breaching a target (Repeat has no stated target) and that repeat causes are diagnosable. **Fails when:** repeats are scheduled into the night-shift window where they already cluster — this would reproduce the failure pattern; and if a repeat is caused by a genuine clinical need (e.g., a borderline assay needing confirmation), deferral is not free.

**R5 — Eliminate the accession-correction queue at source (replaces I4).** Instead of cross-training technical validators (101% utilised), (a) resolve corrections at the originating station with structured forms and mandatory-field validation, (b) automate the *clerical* portion of the amendment and retain the two specialists for genuine exceptions only, (c) protect clerk schedules from the post-9-hour error penalty by scheduling amendments early. **Robust:** removes a queue instead of moving it; the accession-correction pool (2 specialists, 91% peak utilisation, 14 h queue) is a structural single-point-of-failure, and reducing its *arrival rate* (not reallocating it) is what helps in S1/S3. **Assumes:** correction causes are addressable upstream; the automated validation rules do not reject legitimate amendments. **Fails when:** a source-data system change is out of scope, or when errors are genuinely distributed (label/physician-request errors) and need cross-site policy, not workflow.

**R6 — Early pre-analytical defect triage with automated rejection notices (I6 + notification automation).** Decide acceptability within 2 h of arrival, distinguish *fixable in-house* from *must-recollect* at that moment, and issue the recollection request by automated notice to the physician/ward rather than via a clerk. **Robust:** it shortens the front end of a 42–60 h external recollection loop, prevents defective specimens occupying prep benches and machine slots, and — because 26% of peak corrections escalate to recollection (vs 14% normal) — it avoids spending a correction cycle on cases that will fail anyway. **Assumes:** objective rejection criteria (haemolysis, volume, labelling) with a human confirm; clinicians respond to automated notices. **Fails when:** it becomes an *over-rejection* engine (false positives create unnecessary recalls, patient harm and cancellations that already run at 123/wk), or when it front-loads rejections into a clerk/courier burst — the very intake window that S4 already compresses. Guardrail: never run triage faster than the notice channel can absorb.

**R7 — Two-lane medical validation with protected Urgent sign-off (structural, not extra hours).** Split the medical-validation queue into an Urgent lane with reserved daily sign-off slots (including a late-afternoon slot that catches the 17:00 intake wave) and a panel lane with twice-daily structured sign-off windows. **Robust:** the log shows one pool serving two incompatible service levels (Urgent 18 h, Complex-panel with no target but a 5.6× increase in hold demand); splitting decouples the Urgent contract from the panel surge in S1 and S3 without adding heads, because the normal-period 42% slack (58% utilisation) is exactly what peak consumes. **Assumes:** sign-off is separable by case type; there is enough validator skill mix to staff two lanes. **Fails when:** total validator capacity is insufficient (S3, S6) — lanes then differ only in which queue is longer, and the panel lane becomes an unbounded, invisible backlog that will detonate in the next wave.

**R8 — Arrival-decoupled intake (I7, coordinated).** Smooth non-urgent courier slots while leaving Urgent slots unsmoothed, and publish arrival schedules to prep so batch formation can be arrival-triggered. **Robust:** reduces variance, and variance is what converts a 1.43× load into a 54% "queue over 12 h" figure; it also protects the post-9-hour clerk error rate that seeds the correction/recollection loops. **Assumes:** courier capacity can be re-slotted; clinical urgency is genuinely known at booking. **Fails when:** smoothing is applied to urgent slots, or when outbound partner-lab transport (R10) competes for the same courier capacity — a direct **couriercourier** migration that must be scheduled separately.

**R9 — Specialist-review resilience (predictable, cheap).** Pre-book cover for the second-Friday absence 5 days ahead; triage holds into report-delta review versus full-case review; define a defined subset of holds that medical validators may clear. **Robust:** the absence is *predictable*, so it is the one disruption in the whole scenario set that can be engineered away without headcount. **Fails when:** holds rise 5.6× and a single specialist, however covered, is still the throughput ceiling — at that point R10 is required.

**R10 — Partner-lab overflow with guardrails (I10, generalised).** Use for a *defined* overflow set — Routine (72 h target absorbs +12 h transport) and Complex panels (no contractual target) — never for Urgent. Cap at ~25% of overflow-eligible volume, with a pre-agreed transport slot, chain-of-custody and result-re-entry process, and a contractual priority guarantee. **Robust:** it is the only relief valve that adds true *external* capacity to S1/S3/S5. **Fails when:** the partner lab faces the *same regional winter wave* — correlated failure — and when the 12 h transport plus re-entry work pushes a Complex panel's 111 h median past whatever clinical limit applies. Outsource the queue, never the accountability.

**Explicitly rejected / narrowed:** **I2** (batch 1224) worsens the Urgent tail and the batch-wait queue at peak and adds machine setup load — replaced by R2. **I1** (strict Urgent-first dispatch) reshuffles a queue rather than shortening it and starves Complex/Repeat, which are the classes that create the next wave's load; the mechanism it should have targeted is batching, handled in R1/R2. **I9** (hold Routine releases for batch reports) buys ~5 h of release work at the cost of pushing Routine further past a 72 h target that is already ~39% breached at peak — accept only for a small low-acuity set, never for Urgent. **I5** (evening medical shift) is retained only as a *triggered* surge valve (§4), because if the binding constraint is arrival rate rather than coverage, shifting time adds cost without removing the queue.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **A redesign-induced vulnerability: the reserved fast lane and split lanes create *invisible* backlogs in the non-priority classes.** Starving Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure does not reduce their work; it defers it. Complex-panel p90 is already 210 h and Repeat 240 h at peak, and Repeat consumes two machine slots each. Under-resourcing the panel lane to protect Urgent converts a *visible* Urgent SLA breach into a *hidden* panel/repeat backlog that detonates in the following wave as rework and machine load — exactly the mechanism that made the historical peak unstable. Mitigation: monitor panel and Repeat p90 and the repeat share explicitly (§7), and keep R11 (partner overflow) as a deliberate, capped release valve rather than letting the panel lane become a landfill.
2. **Single-person dependencies survive all of this:** one specialist (absent every second Friday), two accession-correction specialists, and a 4-week cross-training lead time that is longer than a 3-week wave. No policy in this package can create a validator or a specialist inside a wave; only pre-contracted cover and the partner lab can.
3. **Correlated external failure.** The partner lab serves the same region and faces the same winter wave, so the overflow valve can fail at the same time as the domestic system. Do not build the plan on the assumption that external capacity is available exactly when it is needed; contract for it *with* priority language.
4. **The 42–60 h recollection loop is outside lab control.** Faster internal triage shortens the front end but cannot shorten the physician/ward/courier round trip. If the external loop lengthens during a wave, the effective cycle time of a recollected case degrades regardless of internal improvements — and recollected cases are disproportionately the complex ones.
5. **M2's failure rate is an unquantified risk.** The log gives no per-machine failure attribution, so I3-style allocation is being decided with the wrong instrument. If M2's higher failure rate is load-induced rather than inherent, the allocation recommended here is self-fulfilling; if it is inherent, the feedback rule is essential. Instrumentation is a prerequisite, not an optional extra.
6. **Measurement remains the weakest link.** 13.3% of peak cases are censored at the window edge; reported rework (11.8%) and DFG-derived nonconformity (24.8%) use different definitions; edge medians cannot be summed (a straightforward sum of peak edge medians implies a ~71 h Urgent case against an observed 17 h median, so the data is heavily right-skewed and the "median" is a blend of two modes). A dashboard built on these definitions will mis-rank interventions — which is why §7 specifies which indicators must be measured directly rather than derived.
7. **Trigger flapping and gaming.** Policy P8 (deferred Routine intake) and P1 (fast-lane expansion) are both triggered on queue measurements that a functioning operator can influence; gaming (reclassifying a case as Routine to protect an Urgent metric, or splitting batches to satisfy P2) is a foreseeable failure mode and must be audited.
8. **Residual cost floor.** Fast lanes and reserved capacity reduce utilisation when demand is normal (peak design on normal volume wastes prep/machine time); peak-conditional policies (P1, P6, P11) add roster and contract cost that must be justified against the ~380 late Urgent/week exposure.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

**Leading indicators (daily, act on these):**

1. **Urgent batch-bypass rate** — baseline 74% normal / 58% peak. Warn < 80%; act < 65%. *Leading for the 18 h contract; the single most diagnostic number in the log.*
2. **Batch-wait entry rate** (share of preparation outflow entering batch wait) — baseline 4.5% / 13.8%. Warn > 6%; act > 10%. *Leading; confirms whether batch wait is a Routine artefact or an Urgent tail.*
3. **Urgent at-risk count** (cases whose age + projected remaining path > 18 h, projected from the edge medians) — warn > 10% of open Urgent; act > 20%. *Leading, not lagging; catches exposure before release.*
4. **Repeat-assay share of machine runs** — baseline 6.9% / 15.5%. Warn > 10%; act > 13%. *Leading for machine capacity and for the rework multiplier.*
5. **Repeat rate by machine (M1 vs M2)** and **repeat rate by shift** — act if M2 exceeds its baseline by > 3 pts or night-shift repeats rise. *Leading for P3 and R4.*
6. **Queue time at the three constrained stations, separately by lane:** technical validation (warn > 8 h, act > 14 h; also utilisation > 95%), medical validation Urgent lane (warn > 6 h, act > 12 h) and panel lane (warn > 12 h, act > 24 h), specialist review (warn > 12 h, act > 24 h). *Leading for P5/P6/P7.*
7. **Pre-analytical defect rate and correctionrecollection escalation** — recollection requests (baseline 4.2% / 8.0%): warn > 6%, act > 9%; escalation (14% / 26%): warn > 18%, act > 24%. *Leading for rework containment.*
8. **Cases with  1 queue > 12 h** — baseline 22% / 54%. Warn > 35%; act > 45%. *Leading for backlog formation before it reaches release.*
9. **Arrival variability (coefficient of variation of hourly intake) and courier queue** — courier queue warn > 4 h, act > 6 h; CV spike act > 0.8. *Leading for R8.*
10. **Specialist cover confirmation (binary, second Friday)** and **M2 availability/PM status** — act if either is unconfirmed 48 h before the wave window. *Leading for S2/S3.*
11. **Cancellation after recollection** — 36/wk normal, 123/wk peak. Warn > 60/wk; act > 90/wk. *Leading for service failure and rework waste.*

**Lagging outcome metrics (weekly, for validation):** Urgent median/p90 and % released  18 h (target  95%); Routine median/p90 and %  72 h (target  95%); Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure median/p90 (guard against the invisible-backlog vulnerability); overall rework on a **reconciled** definition (case-level  1 nonconformity event, currently ~13%/24.8% by DFG, not 7.5%/11.8%); % cases completed within the log window (censoring rate, currently 10.7%/13.3%).

**Data pipeline changes required to make the above measurable (treat as part of the redesign, not as an analytics nice-to-have):** stamp **case type, Urgent-bypass flag, machine ID, shift and batch ID** on every event; publish a **censor-aware** view (open-case age in addition to completed-case medians); publish **queue statistics by lane and by case type** rather than pooled averages; and restate "rework" once, so the same number is used in the dashboard, in improvement projects, and in contract discussions.
