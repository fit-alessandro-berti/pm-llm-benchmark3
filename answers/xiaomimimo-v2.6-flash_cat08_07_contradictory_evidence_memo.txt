# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### 3.1 High fitness but low precision
Fitness measures whether the model can replay the observed log; precision measures whether the model permits only what was observed. Redesign R introduced *optional* behaviour — survey skipping, merged validate/schedule, manual activation, post-billing activation — and relaxed sequencing. A model with many optional branches replays almost anything (**fitness **) while sanctioning combinations that never occurred (**precision **). Three forces compound it:

- **Structural:** fewer enforced sequences means more legal-but-unobserved traces; generalization also fell (0.70  0.56), so the model is not merely permissive, it is *less* representative of plausible behaviour.
- **Measurement:** missing risk/segment attributes before week 3 and absent reason codes mean the discovery algorithm cannot condition on case type — it must allow all orderings for all cases, conflating surveyed and skipped-survey populations.
- **Real governance weakness:** the alignment diagnostics are not modeling noise. Billing start before successful activation, permit checks absent for older apartments, and installation visit repeated without a preceding survey are *specific* forbidden sequences being executed.

So the pair is simultaneously an artifact (missing attributes inflate permissiveness) and a warning (real rules are not being enforced). Precision 0.51 says the process is now less prescribed — which is exactly what one expects when accountability is concentrated in a role rather than in enforced checkpoints.

### 3.2 Improved median but worse tail
Checks that were removed had **front-loaded, broadly distributed cost** (a field survey adds a few days to almost every case) and **back-loaded, conditional value** (it prevents failed visits, permit chases, second visits). Removing them moves time out of the majority of cases and into a minority:

- Down the middle: merged validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership remove pure wait — median 3.4 days.
- In the tail: failed visits 13%  19%, second visits 10%  17%, manual activation 8%  19%, permit omissions surfacing late. Each is low-frequency but adds weeks.

The distribution therefore **spread rather than shifted**: 3.4 days at p50, +7.5 at p90, +17.0 at p95. A single "cycle time" KPI would have hidden this; only percentiles reveal it. Two biases reinforce the concern: the pilot mix is harder (composition pushes tail up — but segment-level p90 still worsens within apartments and old buildings, so mix is not the whole story), and completion-only timestamps over a 6-week window likely drop unclosed long cases, biasing the reported tail *down*. The true tail is unlikely to be better than 38.5/62.0.

### 3.3 Fewer handovers but lower resilience
Handover count is a **structural** metric — it counts role transfers, not coordination load, context transfer, or redundancy.

- *Work absorbed, not eliminated:* the handover network simplified while the working-together network centralized on coordinators and activation specialists. Complexity concentrated instead of disappearing.
- *Handovers as checkpoints:* every handover is an informal verification point. Removing them removed error detection before irreversible steps — consistent with first-time-right falling 82%  76%.
- *No slack:* coordinator utilization 93%, second-visit team 92%. In queueing terms, wait time rises nonlinearly near saturation, so a small rise in failure rate produces a large rise in delay — exactly the observed failed-visit  second-visit delay increase in high-utilization regions.
- *Single point of failure:* when a coordinator was absent, cases stalled because context was not shared. Fewer handoffs means fewer people holding the case narrative.

Accountability improved *nominally* (one owner) while resilience, capacity and redundancy declined — the two metrics measure different things and legitimately move in opposite directions.

### 3.4 Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost
The cost boundary was drawn before the activities that deteriorated:

- Visible cost stops before complaint handling, and complaint-handling data is excluded after week 4 — the exact window into which the redesign deferred work (billing start  complaint escalation doubled its edge share, 3%  8%).
- Moving activation checks after billing start **reclassifies** rework: the fix becomes a complaint or manual activation rather than a pre-billing test.
- The estimate that *does* include downstream cost is already worse (€198  €207) and is admittedly incomplete — so the direction is established while the magnitude is understated.
- Second visits 11%  18% and manual activation 7%  15% are real resource consumption that never appeared in the €161 figure.

This is the classic pattern where an intervention improves a measured cost by moving effort outside the measurement window rather than by removing it.

### 3.5 Simple houses improve while apartments and old buildings deteriorate
The redesign's simplifications are safe only where case variance is low:

- **Simple houses:** little real survey or permit need, low schedule volatility — merging validation/scheduling and single ownership removes pure waste (6d median, 8d p90, +3pp FTR). Uniform improvement on every metric.
- **Apartments, medium risk:** the predictive skip rule acts as a classifier with costly false negatives. A wrongly skipped survey surfaces as a failed visit  second visit (+19%  second visits), destroying first-time-right (8pp) and inflating p90 (+12d). Installation visit  activation test also worsened for apartments.
- **Old, permit-sensitive buildings:** permit checks were omitted (a move-on-model deviation). Permit discovery then happens late in the case, converting a small front-end check into a +25-day p90 event and 11pp FTR.
- **Business customers:** post-billing activation creates billing disputes (manual activation  billing start edge 6%  16%), costing 4pp FTR and +4pp complaints.

Underlying mechanism: the value of a check is **conditional on risk**. A single global redesign optimizes an "average case" that does not exist — it takes value from low-variance cases and gives it to high-variance ones. Hence heterogeneity of treatment effect: aggregate median improves while conditional tails and quality collapse. This is also why aggregate comparisons are insufficient and segment-disaggregated thresholds (§6) are mandatory.
