Acceptability is judged by class-specific targets, not by the overall average cycle time: Urgent p90  18h because of contractual penalties, Routine p90  72h, Complex-panel p90  120h in normal periods and  180h in peak periods, overall rework  10%, and no case class is systematically starved.

2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

Baseline process-mining evidence:

- Because normal is 8 weeks and peak is 3 weeks, raw DFG counts must be compared as weekly rates. Technical validation  medical validation is 3,900/8 = 488/week normal versus 3,420/3 = 1,140/week peak. Medical validation  release is 521/week normal versus 1,330/week peak.
- Pre-analytical failures are a major rework source: normal 760 request recollection + 500 manual accession correction = 1,260 of 18,120 received samples, about 7.0%; peak 780 + 420 = 1,200 of 9,730, about 12.3%.
- Recollection loops are slow: request recollection  receive sample median is 42h normal and 60h peak. Manual accession correction  test preparation is 5h normal and 16h peak.
- Repeat assay loops are large: assay  repeat assay is 1,140 normal and 1,260 peak; repeat assay  assay is 990 normal and 1,080 peak. Repeat assay  assay median is 10h normal and 26h peak.
- Specialist review holds are small in count but very slow: technical validation  hold for specialist review is 290 normal and 610 peak; hold  medical validation median is 18h normal and 48h peak.
- Peak queue metrics show multiple stations near or over capacity: pre-analytical check 96%, assay machines 98%, technical validators 101%, medical validators 93%, specialist review 87% with 36h median queue.
- The performance edges that degrade most from normal to peak are: receive  pre-analytical 29h, pre-analytical  test preparation 413h, batch wait  assay 922h, assay  technical validation 515h, technical validation  medical validation 824h, hold for specialist review  medical validation 1848h.
- Variant behavior matters: Urgent cases bypass batch wait in 74% of normal cases and 58% of peak cases, but the bypass fragments machine schedules. Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter hold for specialist review. Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and cluster after night shifts and M2 usage.

S1: Winter Urgent/Complex surge

Likely bottleneck sequence: assay  technical validation  medical validation/specialist review  release.

The Urgent/Complex mix shift loads assay machines and technical validators first: peak assay utilization is 98% and technical validation is 101%. Complex-panel cases then pull medical validation and specialist review, which are already at 93% and 87% with 22h and 36h peak queues. If management applies strict Urgent-first dispatch at preparation and assay, Urgent cases improve, but Routine WIP grows and machine schedules fragment. That fragmentation can increase repeat assay and reduce effective assay capacity. When assay output eventually rises, technical validation becomes the next constraint. If technical validation is relieved by borrowing technical validators for accession correction, the accession correction queue, already 14h peak, worsens, and recollection loops consume registration clerks. If medical validation is relieved by adding an evening shift but specialist review is not addressed, the hold  medical validation edge, already 48h peak, becomes the constraint. The bottleneck migrates downstream unless the whole chain is managed by class.

S2: Assay-machine degradation/outage

Likely bottleneck sequence: assay  repeat assay  technical validation  medical validation.

M2 degradation directly reduces effective assay capacity. Because Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and cluster after M2 usage, the repeat loop amplifies the loss. Batch wait  assay median is already 22h in peak; an M2 outage makes it worse. Urgent bypass then fragments M1. If all Routine cases are routed to M1 to protect Urgent/Complex cases, M1 queue explodes. If batch sizes are increased to improve M1 setup efficiency, Urgent cases wait longer. If Urgent cases are routed to M2 to relieve M1, repeat rates may rise. When assay throughput eventually recovers, technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization, becomes the next bottleneck. Relieving assay without controlling repeat loops and class mix simply moves the queue to technical validation.

S3: Medical validator/specialist absence

Likely bottleneck sequence: medical validation/specialist review  release, with technical validation queue growing upstream.

Complex-panel and Urgent cases need medical validation. The technical validation  medical validation edge rises from 8h to 24h in peak, and hold for specialist review  medical validation rises from 18h to 48h. Specialist review queue is already 36h in peak. If a medical validator is absent and the specialist is unavailable, medical validation and specialist review become the primary constraint. Adding medical validators without addressing specialist review does not help, because medical validators wait for specialist input. Adding specialist review capacity without medical validators moves the queue back to medical validation. Outsourcing Complex-panel cases relieves specialist review but adds 12h transport delay and traceability complexity. Upstream, technical validation queue grows because cases cannot leave technical validation for medical validation. The bottleneck migrates between technical validation, medical validation, and specialist review depending on which resource is added.

S4: Courier arrival compression/delay

Likely bottleneck sequence: courier intake  receive  pre-analytical check  preparation/assay  technical validation.

Courier intake spikes at 10:00 and 17:00. Receive  pre-analytical median rises from 2h to 9h in peak, and pre-analytical queue is 9.5h peak. If pre-analytical check is relieved by adding staff or by early defect triage, WIP moves downstream in a burst and overloads preparation benches and assay machines. Assay queue is already 18h peak and technical validation 16h peak. If courier arrivals are smoothed but downstream capacity is not adjusted, the intake spike is reduced but the assay and technical validation queues still rise. If registration is automated without improving pre-analytical triage and courier smoothing, more samples arrive faster and expose the pre-analytical and assay bottlenecks. Recollection loops also consume registration clerks, so a front-end failure can migrate back to registration and courier intake.

S5: Pre-analytical defect/recollection wave

Likely bottleneck sequence: pre-analytical check  accession correction/recollection  registration/courier  receive  preparation/assay.

Pre-analytical failures create two loops: request recollection and manual accession correction. Peak manual correction queue is 14h with only 2 specialists. Request recollection  receive is 60h peak. If accession correction is relieved by cross-training technical validators, technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization, becomes the next bottleneck. If poor samples are rejected early within 2h, rework loops are reduced, but recollection requests and physician callbacks increase, consuming registration clerks and courier intake. If those callbacks are not managed, the bottleneck migrates back to registration and courier intake. Downstream, when corrected samples finally arrive, preparation and assay receive a burst of work.

General migration principle: relieving one station without controlling case mix, rework loops, and class-specific priority moves WIP to the next station. In this process, the next station is often technical validation or medical validation/specialist review, which are already at or above peak capacity.

3. Robust redesign package

Core principle: segment the flow by case class, control WIP, and add capacity only in triggered, temporary ways. The redesign is not “add capacity everywhere”; it is a class-based flow with protected lanes, front-end defect containment, adaptive batching, health-aware routing, and triggered validation flex.

Future-state flow:

Register order with digital metadata validation  courier smoothing  receive sample  early pre-analytical defect triage within 2h  class-based lanes  adaptive batching  health-aware assay routing  technical validation  early Complex-panel triage  medical validation/specialist review  release.

Strict I1 as a permanent rule, fixed I2 batch size 24, hard I3 machine dedication, and unconditional I10 outsourcing are rejected as defaults. They are either modified or made conditional.

R1. Class-based segmented flow with protected lanes and dynamic priority

What it is: create four logical lanes: Urgent, Complex-panel, Routine, and Repeat-after-failure. Each lane has protected capacity envelopes at assay and medical validation. Urgent gets priority at assay only when the Urgent queue exceeds a threshold and a Routine batch is not near completion. Complex-panel gets protected medical validation slots and early specialist review. Routine gets batched flow with predictable windows. Repeat-after-failure gets dedicated rerun slots and mandatory failure tagging.

Why it is robust: it works across S1–S5 because it prevents a mix shift toward Urgent/Complex from starving Routine, and it prevents Urgent priority from fragmenting assay schedules. It matches the variant evidence: Urgent bypass is common but harmful when uncontrolled; Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to need specialist review.

Assumptions: case class is known at registration; priority rules are enforced; WIP by class is visible in real time.

Downstream risk: if assay output rises, technical validation becomes the next bottleneck; if Complex-panel mix is high, medical validation and specialist review become the next bottleneck. If Urgent volume exceeds the protected envelope, Urgent cases spill into Routine lanes and fragment batches.

Where it may fail: if case mix changes faster than lanes can be rebalanced; if Urgent volume is so high that the protected envelope is insufficient; if Routine starvation is hidden by overall average cycle time.

R2. Front-end defect containment bundle: I6 + I7 + I8 + guarded I4

What it is: early pre-analytical defect triage within 2 hours, courier pickup smoothing, registration automation for orders with complete digital metadata, and an accession correction fast lane staffed by two cross-trained technical validators only when technical validation queue is below a threshold.

Why it is robust: it reduces the rework loops that consume downstream capacity in all scenarios. The DFG shows pre-analytical failures, recollection loops, and manual corrections are major sources of delay. Peak recollection  receive is 60h; manual correction  test preparation is 16h. Courier smoothing reduces intake spikes; registration automation reduces clerk time spent explaining recollection errors to physicians.

Assumptions: triage criteria are clinically safe; automation data quality is high; courier partners can shift pickup slots; cross-trained validators can return to technical validation quickly.

Downstream risk: I8 alone can expose pre-analytical and assay bottlenecks if not paired with I6 and I7. I6 may increase rejections and physician callbacks, shifting work to registration and courier intake. I4 can create a technical validation shortage if the guardrail is not enforced.

Where it may fail: if automation increases volume faster than pre-analytical and assay can absorb; if courier partners cannot change; if cross-trained validators are not released back to technical validation.

R3. Adaptive batching with Urgent bypass caps

What it is: replace fixed batch size 1224 with dynamic batch sizing. Routine batch size grows only when assay queue is below threshold and Urgent bypass rate is below a cap. Urgent bypass is capped, for example at 65% of assay starts, and never delays a nearly complete Routine batch beyond its maximum wait. Complex-panel samples are not held for Routine batches.

Why it is robust: it improves Routine preparation and assay efficiency without starving Urgent or Complex-panel cases. It adapts to S1, S2, and S4. It avoids the I2 failure mode where larger batches push Urgent cases into longer waits.

Assumptions: real-time batch age and bypass rate are measurable; Urgent cases can be identified; Routine p90 target is monitored.

Downstream risk: if batch size is too large, assay queue and batch wait  assay worsen; if the bypass cap is too high, machine fragmentation increases repeat assay; if the cap is too low, Urgent penalties breach.

Where it may fail: if Urgent volume is highly variable; if batch size changes too frequently and setup efficiency gains are lost.

R4. Health-aware assay routing and repeat-failure circuit breaker

What it is: do not hard-dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex and M2 to Routine. Use M1 as primary for Urgent/Complex and M2 for Routine when M2 is healthy. Circuit breakers: if M2 repeat rate exceeds a threshold or M2 queue exceeds a threshold, shift Routine to M1 and reduce batch size; if M1 queue exceeds a threshold, allow Urgent/Complex to use M2 with extra QC. Tag repeat-after-failure cases by machine and shift and feed that back into routing.

Why it is robust: it addresses S2 directly and supports S1 and S5. It avoids the I3 failure mode where M2 receives all Routine volume during high load and repeat rates rise.

Assumptions: real-time machine health and repeat data are available; M1 and M2 can run the same assays with validated settings; QC can detect M2 failures.

Downstream risk: if M2 degrades, shifting Routine to M1 can overload M1; if Urgent cases use M2, repeat rates may rise; when assay throughput recovers, technical validation becomes the next bottleneck.

Where it may fail: if both machines degrade simultaneously; if routing changes cause setup or calibration overhead; if repeat-rate data is delayed and the circuit breaker triggers too late.

R5. Medical validation/specialist flex with early Complex-panel triage: I5 + selective I10

What it is: identify Complex-panel and Urgent cases at technical validation; pre-book specialist review; use asynchronous or tele-review for selected cases; activate a temporary evening medical-validation shift when medical validation queue exceeds a threshold; use I10 outsourcing for Complex-panel overflow only when specialist review queue exceeds a threshold and transport delay is acceptable, with traceability controls.

Why it is robust: it addresses S1 and S3 without adding permanent capacity everywhere. It reduces the hold  medical validation loop, which is 48h in peak, and protects Urgent and Complex-panel cases.

Assumptions: medical validators can work an evening shift; the partner lab has capacity and a reliable interface; specialist review can be pre-booked; transport delay is clinically acceptable for selected Complex-panel cases.

Downstream risk: if specialist review is absent, adding medical validators alone does not help; outsourcing adds transport delay and traceability complexity; if early triage misclassifies Complex-panel cases, Urgent cases may wait.

Where it may fail: if specialist absence is prolonged; if the partner lab has its own bottlenecks; if interface or chain-of-custody errors create rework.

6. Residual vulnerabilities

- Single specialist review remains fragile. Even with early triage and selective outsourcing, one specialist unavailable every second Friday can still create a 36h queue. If that absence coincides with a Complex-panel surge, medical validation can stall.
- Common-mode assay failure remains possible. Health-aware routing assumes at least one machine is available. A shared power, reagent, or software failure could remove both M1 and M2. Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice, amplifying the loss.
- Redesign-induced vulnerability: priority lanes can starve Routine silently. Class-based lanes may keep Urgent p90 within 18h while Routine p90 breaches 72h. Overall average cycle time may look acceptable while one class is harmed.
- Redesign-induced vulnerability: cross-training coupling. Borrowing technical validators for accession correction can create a hidden technical validation shortage during assay surges. The guardrail may fail if queues are volatile or data is delayed.
- Redesign-induced vulnerability: adaptive batching can increase machine fragmentation. If the Urgent bypass cap is set too high, or Urgent volume is unpredictable, repeat assay and assay queue can rise.
- Front-end defect containment may create a rejection backlash. Aggressive early triage can increase recollection requests and physician callbacks, shifting work to registration and courier intake. If triage criteria are too strict, clinically important samples may be rejected.
- Outsourcing Complex-panel adds traceability and interface risk. The partner lab may have its own bottlenecks, and the 12h transport delay may be unacceptable for some Complex-panel cases. Chain-of-custody errors could create rework.
- Monitoring thresholds may be tuned to historical peak and fail under novel scenarios, such as a new pathogen, cyberattack, both machines down, or a courier strike.
- Courier smoothing depends on external courier contracts. If the courier cannot shift pickup slots, the intake queue persists.
