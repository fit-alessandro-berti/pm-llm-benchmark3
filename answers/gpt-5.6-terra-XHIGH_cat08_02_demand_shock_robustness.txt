# 1. Stress scenarios

The peak period is not simply “43% more work.” It is a different workload shape. Average weekly starts rise from about 2,300 to 3,300, but weekly Urgent volume rises from about 414 to 957 and Complex-panel volume from about 299 to 660. Routine volume is roughly flat (about 1,403 to 1,386 per week). Because Complex-panel orders require multiple assays and Medical validation, order count understates the additional downstream workload.

| Scenario | Demand/disruption profile | Why it is materially different from an average peak |
|---|---|---|
| **S1. Winter infection-wave mix shift** | Total arrivals near the observed peak, with Urgent and Complex-panel cases comprising about 49% of orders versus 31% normally. | Urgent and Complex-panel demand more Medical validation; Complex-panel orders also have multiple assays and are 2.4 times more likely to require specialist review. Routine volume is not the main driver. |
| **S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage** | M2 develops elevated failure/repeat rates, especially after night shifts, or either M1 or M2 is unavailable for several hours during a peak-like day. | M2 is faster but has a higher failure rate. A machine problem both removes capacity and creates repeat work, so it can generate a self-reinforcing assay/rework loop rather than a simple queue. |
| **S3. Medical-validator or specialist-review absence** | A Medical validator is absent for a shift, or the known every-second-Friday specialist absence coincides with a Complex-panel-heavy day. | Medical validation is needed for Urgent and Complex-panel work. Specialist review is a separate constrained route; adding Medical validation capacity does not clear cases waiting for the specialist. |
| **S4. Courier compression or weather delay** | Courier routes are delayed, then samples arrive in concentrated waves around the existing 10:00 and 17:00 spikes. Urgent samples may arrive late among a large Routine intake. | The daily volume may be unchanged, but hourly arrival intensity overwhelms Courier intake and Pre-analytical check. It is the scenario in which trying to prioritize every class simultaneously is most damaging. |

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### How to read the evidence

The DFG is strong directional evidence but should not be treated as a literal case balance. It is flattened at lab-order level, contains loops, and the reporting windows contain opening and closing work-in-process. For example, the peak window has 9,900 starts but only 8,040 releases and 540 cancellations. Likewise, repeat and recollection events are not necessarily distinct orders.

The redesign should therefore use these measures as route and congestion signals, then rebuild the event log at order, tube, and assay level. In particular, Complex-panel workload should be represented in assay-work units and expected validation workload, not just as one order.

### S1. Winter infection-wave mix shift

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Courier intake, Registration, and Pre-analytical check tighten first.**  
   Registration reaches 85% utilization and Courier intake 88% in the observed peak. More importantly, Pre-analytical check reaches 96% utilization with a 9.5-hour median queue, compared with 1.8 hours normally. The Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check transition rises from 2 to 9 hours.

2. **Exceptions create a second front-end bottleneck.**  
   The observed pre-analytical route to Request recollection rises from 760 events in the normal window to 780 in a much shorter peak window; Manual accession correction rises to 420 events. The correction queue rises from 3 to 14 hours with only two specialists and 91% utilization. Recollection is especially damaging because Request recollection -> Receive sample rises from 42 to 60 hours and clerks lose time explaining the issue to physicians.

3. **Preparation and batching become the next constraint once intake is relieved.**  
   Preparation-bench utilization rises to 89% and the median queue to 11 hours. The share of observed preparation exits entering Batch wait increases from roughly 4% in normal operations to roughly 13% in peak operations. The two batch-related transitions lengthen materially: Test preparation -> Batch wait from 6 to 14 hours and Batch wait -> Assay run from 9 to 22 hours.

4. **Assay capacity becomes critical, especially because rework rises.**  
   M1/M2 reach 98% utilization with an 18-hour median queue. The observed share of Assay run exits going to Repeat assay rises from about 7% to about 15%, and Repeat assay -> Assay run rises from 10 to 26 hours. This makes the assay queue both a capacity and a quality problem.

5. **Technical, Medical, and Specialist validation become the downstream constraint.**  
   Technical validation is already at 101% utilization in peak with a 16-hour median queue. Medical validation has a 22-hour queue despite nominal utilization of 93%, indicating variability, priority effects, and skill restrictions rather than meaningful spare capacity. The observed fraction of Technical-validation exits routed to Medical validation or Specialist review increases from about 26% normally to about 50% in peak. Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation rises from 18 to 48 hours.

**Migration risk from interventions**

- Relieving Registration with automation or overtime exposes Pre-analytical check sooner.
- Relieving Pre-analytical check exposes Preparation benches and assay machines.
- Giving all preparation and assay capacity to Urgent work can improve Urgent flow briefly but starves Routine work and can leave Complex-panel cases partially completed without downstream Medical-validation capacity.
- Clearing the assay queue without preparing Technical validation and Medical validation creates a burst into Technical validation, already above sustainable utilization.
- Adding an evening Medical-validation shift without controlling Technical validation or specialist review can simply move the queue to Technical validation, Release result, or Specialist review.

### S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **The first visible bottleneck is Assay run.**  
   With peak machine utilization already at 98%, even a short M1 or M2 outage can produce an assay queue faster than the nominal average suggests. Preparation work accumulates, then Batch wait grows because completed preparation cannot enter the machine.

2. **If M2 degrades, repeat work amplifies the loss of capacity.**  
   M2 is faster but has a higher failure rate, and Repeat-after-failure cases cluster after night shifts and M2 usage. The observed peak repeat route is already much more prevalent than normal, while the return journey Repeat assay -> Assay run is 26 hours at peak. A degradation therefore consumes capacity twice: first with the failed run, then with the repeat.

3. **A temporary downstream lull can be misleading.**  
   Technical validators and Medical validators may initially appear less loaded because machines are starved. That is not recovery. When the machine returns, accumulated samples can arrive in a burst, overwhelming Technical validation at 101% utilization and then Medical validation and specialist review.

4. **The constraint can move upstream if machines are protected poorly.**  
   A permanent version of I3—dedicating M1 to Urgent and Complex-panel work and sending all Routine work to M2—makes M2 quality problems a Routine rework factory. Conversely, if M1 fails, Urgent and Complex-panel cases lose their nominal protected path and compete on M2.

**Migration risk from interventions**

- Strict Urgent-first dispatch, I1, protects some Urgent samples but may leave Routine samples waiting so long that a later recovery wave overwhelms Technical validation.
- Sending all repeats immediately ahead of new work can consume the same scarce assay slots needed to prevent Urgent breaches. Repeats need clinically informed priority and a failure-cause check, not automatic highest priority.
- Faster repair or improved machine scheduling can produce a downstream validation surge unless Technical and Medical validation have explicit release-rate controls.

### S3. Medical-validator or specialist-review absence

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Medical-validator absence first increases the Technical validation -> Medical validation queue.**  
   That transition is already 24 hours at peak versus 8 hours normally. Since Technical validation is at 101% peak utilization, it cannot safely hold a growing downstream backlog for long.

2. **Specialist absence produces a different queue.**  
   Specialist review has an 87% peak utilization and a 36-hour median queue. The Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation transition reaches 48 hours at peak. Complex-panel cases are disproportionately represented in this route, so a specialist absence makes the Complex-panel tail much worse even if Routine and some Urgent work continue.

3. **Once the absent specialist or Medical validator returns, the bottleneck migrates again.**  
   Clearing specialist holds can flood Medical validation. Clearing Medical validation can flood Release result or reveal incomplete Technical validation. The short Medical validation -> Release result median of 5 hours does not prove release capacity is unlimited; it only indicates that the preceding queues dominate in the observed data.

**Migration risk from interventions**

- I5, a temporary evening Medical-validation shift, is useful for Medical-validator absence or a medical queue surge, but does not resolve Specialist review.
- I10, partner-lab outsourcing, can reduce local specialist-review congestion but adds 12 hours transport and traceability work. It is not a default Urgent solution because 12 hours of transport consumes most of an 18-hour contractual window before local processing and release are considered.
- I4, moving Technical validators into accession correction during this scenario, is unsafe if Technical validation is already red; it worsens the queue feeding Medical validation.

### S4. Courier compression or delay

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Courier intake and physical receipt become the first bottleneck.**  
   Courier intake rises from 60% to 88% utilization and its median queue from 1 to 7.5 hours. Register order -> Receive sample rises from 5 to 8 hours. A weather disruption can make this worse even without more daily orders.

2. **Compressed arrivals overwhelm Pre-analytical check.**  
   Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check rises from 2 to 9 hours at peak. With Pre-analytical check already at 96% utilization, an arrival wave creates sample-aging and exception risk before any assay work begins.

3. **Rushed intake produces correction and recollection work.**  
   If staff rush accessioning, manual corrections and recollection loops increase. The correction queue already reaches 14 hours in peak, and recollection return takes 60 hours. This consumes clerk time and intake capacity again.

4. **If the arrival wave is simply pushed through, the bottleneck moves to preparation and assay.**  
   Automation or fast intake alone can create a larger simultaneous release into Preparation benches, Batch wait, and machines. The apparent improvement at the front door then becomes a worse 18-hour assay queue.

**Migration risk from interventions**

- I7, smoothing courier slots, is robust only if the laboratory does not use the freed intake capacity to release an uncontrolled wave into Pre-analytical check.
- I8, registration automation, is valuable for complete digital orders but can expose Pre-analytical check as the immediate bottleneck.
- I6, early defect triage, lowers late laboratory rework but may initially increase visible recollections and physician-contact demand. It must distinguish correctable accession errors from samples that genuinely require recollection.

### S5. Compound winter Friday

This scenario produces a serial constraint chain:

1. Courier compression and a high Urgent/Complex mix overload Pre-analytical check and accession correction.
2. M2 degradation makes Assay run and Repeat assay the dominant constraint.
3. M1 protection for critical work can leave Routine work accumulating and can still be insufficient for all Urgent and Complex work.
4. Technical validation becomes overloaded when the machine backlog releases.
5. Specialist absence leaves Complex-panel work in Hold for specialist review while Medical validation is also pressured by Urgent cases.
6. A recovery burst can move the constraint to Medical validation or Release result.

The key lesson is that no single “priority rule” is robust here. The laboratory needs controlled admission to each constrained stage, protected but capped Urgent capacity, planned Complex-panel capacity, and an explicit policy for what Routine work is deferred. Otherwise all classes are nominally priority and none are actually protected.

# 3. Robust redesign package

### A. Build a workload-aware process-mining control layer

Create an operational event model with:

- Order ID, tube ID, assay ID, and parent Complex-panel ID.
- Clinical class, contractual due time, and whether an order is both urgent and complex.
- Physical receipt time, courier route and planned slot, pre-analytical defect code, correction reason, recollection request, and recollection receipt.
- Preparation ready/start times, batch membership, M1/M2 assignment, shift, assay failure code, repeat cause, and machine downtime.
- Queue entry/start/complete times for Technical validation, Medical validation, Specialist review, and Release result.
- Resource/shift identifiers, including absence and overtime status.

Use this to estimate workload in **assay and validation units**, not simply number of orders. Recompute route probabilities by case class, machine, route, and shift. For example, the apparent M2/night-shift repeat relationship should be tested after controlling for case type and assay type; it is a strong operational signal but not yet proof of causation.

Before changing rules, replay normal and peak traces and stress-test S1-S5. Evaluate:

- Urgent cases predicted to miss the 18-hour limit.
- Routine cases predicted to exceed 72 hours.
- p90 and queue-age distributions by case type.
- Recollection, repeat, and cancellation rates.
- WIP held at each constrained stage.

Do not select policies using overall median cycle time.

**Assumptions and failure mode:** this depends on timely, complete timestamps and tube/assay linkage. Flattened order-level data will otherwise hide the work created by Complex panels and repeats.

### B. Replace strict priority with downstream-feasible, capped priority

Do not implement I1 as a permanent strict Urgent-first rule.

Instead, at each control point use three queues:

1. **Urgent deadline-risk queue:** Urgent work with an 18-hour deadline that is still feasible if a protected path is reserved.
2. **Planned Complex-panel queue:** Complex work released into preparation only when assay, Technical-validation, and Medical-validation/review capacity is expected downstream.
3. **Age-protected Routine queue:** a minimum Routine flow that prevents indefinite starvation and a later recovery wave.

Use rolling, short-horizon reservations rather than dedicating an entire resource. For example, reserve defined assay and validation slots in each operating block for deadline-risk Urgent cases and planned Complex panels, while maintaining a controlled Routine batch flow. The actual number of slots should be calculated from measured service times and current WIP, not guessed from the aggregate summary.

Apply pull control:

- Do not release unlimited clean samples from Pre-analytical check into preparation if assay WIP is red.
- Do not start a non-urgent Complex panel merely because a machine is free if Medical validation or Specialist review is already blocked.
- Escalate Urgent cases that are already infeasible rather than allowing them to consume every remaining slot without a realistic path to release.

**Why robust:** it works under high mix shifts, machine outages, and validator absences because it controls the release of work into the next constrained stage.

**Where it may fail:** a severe compound event can exceed physical assay or specialist capacity. In that case the policy makes the deferral visible and controlled; it cannot guarantee that all classes meet targets simultaneously.

### C. Clean and smooth the front end: I6, I7, and selective I8

Implement a combined front-end package.

- **I6: early pre-analytical defect triage within two hours.**  
  Separate defects into: correctable accession/metadata errors, missing information that can be resolved electronically, and true sample-quality failures requiring recollection. Capture a standardized reason code.

- **I7: courier-slot smoothing.**  
  Move Routine collections away from the 10:00 and 17:00 spikes where possible. Preserve a protected late-arrival path for Urgent samples rather than allowing Urgent work to queue behind a large Routine delivery. Use route-level forecasts, not daily total volume only.

- **I8: automate registration only for complete digital metadata.**  
  Validate identifiers and order completeness before release to Pre-analytical check. Do not use automation to bypass physical sample checks.

- **Protect accession correction capacity.**  
  Use standard electronic physician queries and defect templates to reduce the time clerks spend explaining correctable errors. The two correction specialists should work on an unblock-first queue, but not at the cost of taking Technical validators away during a Technical-validation surge.

**Why robust:** it reduces avoidable late rework, makes courier variability visible early, and lowers dependence on registration overtime, which is risky after nine-hour shifts.

**Downstream risk:** a cleaner, faster intake can flood Pre-analytical check, Preparation benches, and machines. Therefore, automation and courier smoothing must be coupled to WIP limits at Pre-analytical check and preparation.

### D. Use timed batching and quality-adjusted assay routing, not blanket I2 or I3

Use a controlled preparation/assay policy.

- Keep Routine batching near the existing size by default; do not universally move from 12 to 24.
- Run timed Routine batches with a maximum hold age. Larger batches are allowed only in a stable Routine-backlog mode when Urgent and Complex-panel downstream capacity is demonstrably clear.
- Maintain small-batch or single-sample exceptions for deadline-risk Urgent work.
- Schedule Complex-panel preparation into planned blocks so its multiple assays do not create unplanned contention at the machine and Medical validation stages.
- Do not permanently dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex and M2 to Routine. Instead, reserve M1 windows for critical work and dynamically balance other work using due time, expected repeat risk, and current machine health.
- Put M2 under shift-level quality control. If its repeat rate breaches control limits, reduce its exposure to assays with high repeat consequences, investigate calibration/maintenance and night-shift conditions, and avoid routing all Routine work to it.
- Require a repeat-cause review before automatically re-entering the assay queue. Some repeats need rapid handling; others should wait for corrective action rather than consuming another scarce slot immediately.

**Why robust:** this preserves Urgent responsiveness without destroying Routine flow and avoids turning M2 into a concentrated repeat source.

**Downstream risk:** clearing preparation and assay queues more effectively can overwhelm Technical validation, which is already at 101% peak utilization. The assay dispatch board must therefore consider Technical-validation WIP before releasing a catch-up wave.

### E. Create validation and review contingencies without treating them as permanent capacity

Pre-arrange, but do not permanently staff, the following:

- **I5: temporary evening Medical-validation shift** for confirmed peak mix, Medical-validator absence, or a sustained Medical-validation queue surge.
- A scheduled specialist-review contingency for the known every-second-Friday absence, including advance case identification.
- **I10: partner-lab overflow** only for eligible non-urgent Complex panels when the predicted local specialist-review delay exceeds the end-to-end partner delay, including the 12-hour transport time.
- A formal chain-of-custody, panel-eligibility, result-reconciliation, and client-communication process before outsourcing.

Use I4 carefully:

- Cross-train the two Technical validators in accession correction during normal periods.
- Deploy them to correction only when Technical-validation WIP, utilization, and predicted Urgent risk are green.
- Maintain a minimum Technical-validation roster. At peak, with utilization already 101%, I4 is more likely to shift the bottleneck into Technical validation than to provide net benefit.

**Why robust:** the laboratory gains targeted flexibility at the actual specialist/medical constraint without staffing every station for its worst case.

**Where it may fail:** evening staff may be unavailable or fatigued; partner capacity and traceability may fail; and a specialist absence cannot be solved by Medical-validation capacity alone.

### F. Do not use I9 as a congestion remedy

Do not hold Routine results until a batch report is ready merely to reduce release work. The observed Medical validation -> Release result transition is 2 hours normal and 5 hours peak, far smaller than the 14-48 hour waits upstream. I9 adds deliberate waiting, worsens Routine cycle time, and masks rather than removes the root constraint.

If grouped reporting is clinically or commercially required, batch the reporting artifact after an individually releasable result is made available; do not hold the clinical result for the batch.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Physical capacity can still be exceeded.**  
  In a compound event with high Urgent/Complex demand, one machine degraded, and specialist absence, it may not be possible to meet every Urgent 18-hour commitment and every Routine 72-hour target. The redesign provides controlled escalation and transparent deferral; it does not create unlimited assay or specialist capacity.

- **Technical validation remains structurally fragile.**  
  It is already at 101% peak utilization. The redesign avoids using Technical validators for correction during red periods, but an assay recovery burst can still overwhelm them.

- **Accession correction has a single-point concentration.**  
  Two specialists at 91% peak utilization and a 14-hour queue are vulnerable to absence, unusual defect types, or a courier-induced error wave.

- **Specialist review remains a narrow skill bottleneck.**  
  The known every-second-Friday absence is predictable, but unplanned absence, a rare panel type, or partner refusal can still create long Complex-panel tails.

- **Machine routing can create a new failure mode.**  
  Reserving M1 windows for critical work can underuse M1 if forecasts are wrong, while excessive M2 loading can increase repeats. Dynamic routing must be reviewed frequently rather than treated as a fixed allocation.

- **Early defect triage may increase visible recollection demand.**  
  This is preferable to discovering defects after preparation or assay, but it can increase physician contacts, patient inconvenience, and clerk workload if reason codes and communication templates are poor.

- **Automation can shift rather than eliminate congestion.**  
  I8 can remove Registration work but make Pre-analytical check, preparation, and assay queues worse if release is not paced. Incorrect or incomplete metadata can also create new correction work.

- **Outsourcing introduces a redesign-created vulnerability.**  
  I10 adds transport delay, handoff errors, traceability risk, panel compatibility risk, and reconciliation work. It must not become a way to hide local WIP or export clinically urgent cases without an end-to-end feasibility check.

- **The control layer depends on data quality.**  
  Flattened order-level logs obscure tube-level and assay-level work. If the redesigned event capture is incomplete, workload forecasts and queue reservations can be wrong precisely during the disruptions they are intended to manage.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Use the leading indicators below intraday during peak periods, segmented by case class, machine, shift, courier route, and panel type. The numeric bands are starting control limits based on the observed normal/peak values and should be refined with local control charts.

### Leading indicators

| Leading indicator | Green / warning / critical bands | Why it leads | Immediate response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Forecast arrivals by workload type**: orders, assay-work units, Urgent + Complex share | Warning: Urgent + Complex above 40%; critical: at or above 45% or assay-work forecast above 120% of plan. | Predicts Medical-validation, specialist, and multi-assay pressure before queues form. | Consider P1 mixed-demand surge mode and review reservation plan. |
| **Courier and physical-receipt compression** | Warning: more than 30% of expected intake arriving within two hours or Courier queue above 4 hours; critical: route delay above 2 hours with Pre-analytical queue rising. | Detects a wave before it reaches Pre-analytical check. | Activate P4; smooth Routine deliveries, protect Urgent receipt, pace intake release. |
| **Pre-analytical and correction exception flow** | Warning: Pre-analytical utilization above 90%, median queue above 6 hours, correction queue above 10 hours, or recollection route above about 6% of observed checks; critical: near 96% utilization, 9-hour Pre-analytical queue, or 14-hour correction queue. | Defects and corrections create long loops before assay and consume intake capacity again. | Activate early triage, prioritize correctable unblockers, limit uncontrolled automation release. |
| **Preparation and batching health** | Warning: Preparation queue above 8 hours, Batch-wait route above 8%, or Urgent bypass below 65%; critical: approaching observed peak queue of 11 hours or batch waits near 14/22 hours. | Signals that batching is no longer serving flow and that Urgent exceptions are being lost. | Stop large batches, use timed batches and protected exceptions, meter work into assay. |
| **Assay availability and repeat loop** | Warning: machine utilization above 95%, assay queue age above 12 hours, any machine outage above one hour, or repeat routing above 10%; critical: M2 repeat rate above twice its shift baseline or queue approaching 18 hours. | Detects both capacity loss and the repeat-work amplification loop. | Activate P2, investigate machine/shift cause, reserve healthy capacity, throttle preparation release. |
| **Validation and specialist queue age** | Warning: Technical validation above 95% utilization or queue above 10 hours; Medical-validation queue above 12 hours; specialist hold age above 24 hours. Critical: any Urgent case predicted to miss 18 hours, Technical queue approaching 16 hours, Medical queue approaching 22 hours, or specialist path approaching 48 hours. | These are the primary downstream constraints for Urgent and Complex-panel work. | Activate P3a or P3b, protect Technical roster, add evening Medical validation if warranted, use eligible partner overflow only for specialist review. |
| **Staffing and operating-condition risk** | Warning: unfilled specialist/Medical-validator absence, registration shifts approaching 9 hours, unplanned night-shift machine quality change, or cross-trained Technical staff assigned away while Technical WIP is rising. | Identifies foreseeable skill and fatigue constraints before performance degrades. | Adjust roster, cancel unsafe flex assignment, pre-activate absence contingency. |

### Lagging outcome metrics

Do not use these alone to operate the process; they confirm whether the interventions worked after the fact.

| Lagging metric | Required segmentation |
|---|---|
| Percentage of Urgent cases released after 18 hours | By assay type, machine, shift, and whether Medical/Specialist validation was required. |
| Percentage of Routine cases released after 72 hours | By age band and whether delayed by Urgent protection, batching, machine outage, or validation queue. |
| Median and p90 cycle time | Separately for Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel, and Repeat-after-failure; never only as one overall average. |
| Recollection, manual-correction, and repeat-assay rates | By pre-analytical reason, courier route, M1/M2, assay, and shift. |
| Percentage of completed cases experiencing a queue over 12 hours | By station and case class; pair with current queue age as the leading measure. |
| Cancellation, partner reconciliation exceptions, and start-to-release WIP gap | By route and reason, to distinguish true demand loss from growing unfinished WIP. |
