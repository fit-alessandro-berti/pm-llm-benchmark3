# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

**Automated intake — R14_Nora.** Core is A1/A2 by both frequency and process position: she is the entry gate. Peripheral is effectively zero in the log, which itself is informative: downstream judgement is attributed to humans, not to Nora. Authority is procedural, not discretionary. The risk is boundary confusion — if Nora is treated as a “resource” comparable to staff, managers may understaff human validation for cases Nora mis-routes.

**Front-office routine — R01, R02.** Core A1, A2, A4, A12 defines the standard path. Peripheral A3, A5, A7, A8, A10 are occasional, lower-volume, and typically on more complex cases. Amira’s higher A3 (75), A8 (42) and A7 (18), and her higher high/critical count (67 vs Ben’s 34), suggest she is the more experienced generalist within the same formal role. Authority is to resolve and close standard cases, not to approve exceptions.

**Seasonal temporary support — R11, R12.** Core is the same A1/A2/A4/A12 set, but *restricted* core: only low-complexity cases, median complexity ~0.18, almost no high/critical. Peripheral activity is near absent, indicating constrained scope, not versatility. Temporary substitution evidence cuts against permanent competence: they sustained throughput in weeks 8–10 but generated 17 reopens linked to incomplete A2 notes. Their core is high-frequency, low-risk; errors are quality/reopen risks, not pricing/security risks.

**Technical diagnosis and implementation — R03, R04.** Core A3/A5/A10 is both frequent and complex. Peripheral A6 is the key differentiator: Chen 24 vs Dina 6, aligned with Chen’s 42 critical cases vs Dina’s 9 and median complexity 0.71 vs 0.48. Frequency alone says “same role”; complexity and A6 exposure say Chen is allocated harder, riskier work. A11/A14 small counts are likely feedback/KB contributions, not gatekeeping.

**Rare critical security — R05_Erik.** Core A6 is only 96 events, but it is core by authority and consequence, not frequency. Peripheral A10 (18) and A3 (16) are consultative/implementation around security decisions. Median complexity 0.86 and 55 critical cases — the highest critical share — mean low frequency must not be read as low importance. This is the clearest case where frequency-weighted clustering fails.

**Commercial, formal — R06_Fatima.** Core A7/A8 (122/90) is pricing exception and negotiation. Peripheral A2 (22), A9 involvement (16) and A12 (18) support end-to-end commercial handling. Complexity is high (median 0.67). Authority is formal commercial authority; Giorgio’s substitution shows a substitute can perform A7/A8 but at roughly double duration, so core competence is not interchangeable at equal speed/quality.

**Senior hybrid shadow — R07_Giorgio.** This role has two cores: a front-office core (A1/A2/A4/A12) and a commercial bridging core (A7/A8). Which is “core” depends on the case: on front-office cases he is a senior generalist; on pricing cases he is a shadow commercial specialist. Peripheral A3 (40) and A9 (20) suggest senior escalation handling within front-office scope. Treating A7/A8 as merely peripheral because they are < A2/A4 would miss his organisational function.

**Quality gatekeeping — R09, R10.** Core A11/A14 (about 300/100 each) is gatekeeping and knowledge capture. Peripheral A10 (38/35) and A12 (32/30) are likely verification-driven touches, not primary implementation. Their authority is to stop or send back work, which frequency under-rates. Ivan (late) and Julia (day) give shift coverage; similarity 0.97 plus similar complexity supports one role with two shifts, not two roles.

**Supervisory approval — R08_Hana.** Core A9 (138) is small in count but maximal in authority. Peripheral A13 (24) and A8 (22) relate to escalations/reopens. Complexity is high (median 0.79, 192 high/critical of 274). In week 5, Mateo’s 21 preliminary A9 events, 13 later rechecked by Hana, confirm that performing A9-like work does not equal holding A9 authority.

**Cross-functional brokerage — R13_Mateo.** Paradoxically, Mateo has no dominant activity; coordination is the core, and the breadth is the signal. His peripheral-looking touches on A6, A7, A5, A11, A14 are routing/filtering decisions on high/critical cases (median 0.77, 224 high/critical). Frequency clustering reads breadth as “generalist”; organisationally, breadth plus high complexity plus handover position reads as broker/gatekeeper.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

**R07_Giorgio — commercial shadow (benefits and risks).** Giorgio is not a rogue actor: he holds entitlement *and* pricing certifications, is Senior Service Agent, and is explicitly noted as informal substitute for commercial review. Benefits: resilience during Fatima’s 6-day absence (31 A7, 18 A8 performed), continuity for front-office cases that turn commercial, and local knowledge that fragments 4 shows in flow. Risks: duration doubling (38  74 minutes) suggests slower or less consistent pricing decisions; informal authority can create inconsistent discounts, unclear audit trail, and single-person dependency. The appropriate conclusion is not “improper”, but “formalise or bound”: delegate limits, second checker, and outcome audit.

**R13_Mateo — cross-functional escalation broker.** Mateo bridges Technical, Commercial, Security, Quality and Supervisory work. Benefits: he coordinates reopens (A13 42), filters security referrals, and in weeks 7–8 reduced false escalation complaints. Risks: brokerage becomes gatekeeping. When he filtered low-risk security referrals, two critical cases waited >2 days. In week 5 his preliminary approvals blurred accountability, with 13 of 21 rechecked by Hana. He is a shadow *decision* role, not just a messenger: his filtering rules need explicit risk criteria and escalation SLAs.

**R05_Erik — rare but critical specialist.** Erik is a bridging specialist between Technical and Risk: Security Review sits between Diagnose and Implement (fragment 3). Benefits: deep expertise for critical cases, 55 critical handled, only security-certified resource. Risks: single point of failure, queueing delay, and invisibility in volume metrics. Mateo’s filtering reduced his load but created waiting risk. Shadow aspect: Erik’s consultative A3/A10/A11 touches mean his influence extends beyond the 96 A6 events. Resilience requires a trained deputy and clear criteria for what must reach Erik vs what can be safely filtered.

**R14_Nora — automation boundary role.** Nora is a shadow actor in the sense that she performs A1/A2 work previously human, shaping case flow before any human sees it. Benefits: scale (760/610), 24/7 coverage, low-complexity pre-check. Risks: automation bias, misclassification of entitlement, and an accountability gap if managers treat her output as a human decision. She should be governed as a system role with human override metrics, not clustered with staff.

**Temporary workforce and quality gatekeepers as shadow capacity.** Kai/Lina provide shadow *capacity* for front-office demand — valuable in seasonal peaks, risky when “closed” is confused with “correctly validated”. Ivan/Julia are shadow *control* points: their A11 gate can stop poor technical/front-office work before Close, but if quality is seen as a downstream cluster rather than a gate, rework (A13) will be misattributed to front-office alone.

# 6. Operational interpretation

**Staffing.** Keep the formal structure close to the proposed roles, but make shadow capacity explicit: a named deputy for Erik’s A6, a named deputy for Hana’s A9, and a bounded delegation for Giorgio’s A7/A8 with pricing limits and second-check thresholds. Protect late-shift quality coverage (Ivan) so gatekeeping does not become a day-shift-only control. Treat Kai/Lina capacity as peak-load, low-complexity capacity with a complexity ceiling, not as general front-office cover.

**Training.** Build pathways that reflect complexity, not just activity: Dina  Chen via supervised high/critical and A6-adjacent cases; Ben  Amira via A3/A8 exposure; temps via A2 note-quality training (the 17 reopens are a training signal, not just a staffing signal); Giorgio via formal commercial certification/assessment if his pricing role is to be permanent; Mateo via explicit security-filtering and preliminary-approval criteria so brokerage does not become unauthorised gatekeeping.

**Risk control.** Pricing exceptions and security reviews need auditable authority: who approved, under what limit, with what outcome. The Fatima-absence duration increase and the Hana recheck of 13/21 preliminary approvals show that substitution without authority creates delay and rework. Reopen analysis (A13) should be fed back to the originating role (front-office validation, technical implementation, or quality gate), not averaged away.

**Resilience.** Run absence simulations for Fatima, Erik and Hana — the three lowest-volume, highest-authority roles. Cross-train at least one backup per critical activity, document Mateo’s filtering rules, and monitor queue time for critical cases when filtering is applied (the >2-day waits in weeks 7–8 are the warning metric).

**Cautions about changing staffing based only on mined roles.**

1. **Clusters confound allocation with competence.** Mined roles reflect who was *given* low, medium or critical cases, who was absent, shift patterns and product-line mix over 10 weeks — not who *could* do the work. Reassigning staff to match clusters could strip Erik, Hana and Mateo of precisely the rare work that defines their value, and could promote temps on volume while ignoring reopen quality.

2. **Volume and service-time metrics punish critical/complex work.** Erik, Hana, Chen and Mateo handle disproportionately high/critical cases; Fatima’s substitute took twice as long. Judging individuals or redesigning teams by event counts, average duration, or frequency clusters alone will penalise gatekeeping, approvals and rework handling, and may increase pricing, security and quality risk while appearing to improve throughput.

A mined role structure is therefore best used as a hypothesis for process governance — to clarify authority, deputies, training pathways and audit points — and only converted into staffing changes after complexity-stratified, outcome-based validation.
