# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee
**Subject:** Fiber-installation redesign—retain targeted benefits, restore controls, and condition further rollout on evidence
**Decision requested:** Approve a guarded version of **Alternative E**, supported by controlled experiments rather than national rollout of R unchanged.

**Recommendation.** Retain coordinator ownership and combined validation/scheduling for independently verified simple, low-risk houses. Expand this pathway gradually, with a comparison group and capacity limits. Suspend predictive survey skipping for medium-risk and uncertain addresses, restore applicable permit checks, and require verified successful activation before billing in **every** segment. Give coordinators shared case records, named deputies, and clear activation-specialist support. Do not expand single-person dependency.

The pilot shows valuable simplification alongside serious deterioration. Median duration fell by 3.4 days, and cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%. Simple low-risk houses—34% of pilot cases—improved in median, p90, first-time-right installation, and complaints. A complete rollback would discard those benefits.

However, the overall p95 increased by 17 days, first-time-right installation declined by six percentage points, and complaints increased from 6.1% to 9.8%. Estimated downstream-inclusive cost is €207 rather than €198, despite lower visible cost. Apartments and permit-sensitive old buildings have particularly adverse outcomes. These patterns do not support national scaling of the bundled redesign.

**The leading hypothesis is that R removes unnecessary coordination for easy cases but removes useful preparation or defers unresolved work for harder cases.** More failed visits, manual activation, and repeat visits then burden already heavily utilized coordinators and specialists. The temporal profiles, exception edges, resource loads, and customer complaint themes support this explanation, but the observational pilot does not establish each component’s causal contribution.

Several measurement issues could materially change the estimated effect. Pilot case mix differs from baseline; early risk attributes are incomplete; downstream cost coverage is incomplete; and cycle-time endpoints need verification. A 62-day p95 in a six-week pilot requires reconciliation of carry-in cases, follow-up, or estimation methods. If cycle time ends at billing, moving activation checks after billing may also create apparent speed without earlier working service. These uncertainties justify measurement—not continued exposure to avoidable control failures.

**Authorize immediate containment and a bounded learning programme.** Operations should triage billed-but-unactivated cases, review disputed charges, restore required preparation, and relieve coordinator and second-visit queues. Preserve survey capacity rather than treating its lower utilization as an immediate staffing saving. Analytics should prioritize control monitoring, representative validation of the building-risk rule, full 60-day downstream costing, and ownership-resilience testing.

Review safety and queue indicators weekly. Make expansion decisions only after comparable cohorts have sufficient duration, complaint, and cost follow-up. Require segment-specific evidence of retained speed benefits, acceptable tails and quality, credible full-cost economics, and functioning absence cover. Neither improved median duration nor higher discovered-model fitness should override these gates.

**Trade-off:** this approach accepts some additional upfront work, staffing reserve, and routing complexity to preserve demonstrated benefits while reducing the risk of scaling failures. It is reversible and informative. It does not assume that the modified pathway will retain all of R’s observed gains; that must be tested.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## High fitness but low precision

**Fitness asks whether a model can reproduce observed behavior; precision penalizes allowing behavior unsupported by the log.** A model can therefore replay the pilot well while permitting too many combinations of survey omission, repeat visits, manual activation, and billing. High fitness is not a measure of service quality or control effectiveness. 

The process-mining team’s interpretation is plausible: a common simplified pathway supports high replay fitness, while loosely constrained exception branches reduce precision. But the precision decline does **not** prove that every extra combination permitted by the discovered model actually occurred. Operational concerns come from observed exceptions and outcomes, not merely the model’s additional behavior.

The generalization decline is a separate warning. Depending on its implementation, a generalization score may reflect how well-supported model elements are, rather than direct performance on future cases. Sample size and rare paths can matter. The supplied score does not establish a measured probability of successful national transfer. 

Rediscover and evaluate segment-specific models using harmonized activity definitions, consistent settings, and held-out cohorts. Keep the **descriptive discovered model** separate from an **approved control specification**. The building-risk predictor also needs its own validation: process-model precision of 0.51 says nothing directly about the predictive rule’s classification precision.

Alignment mismatches are relative to a reference model. They can reveal problematic execution, but also an outdated or incomplete model; they are not automatically compliance violations. ([Process Mining][1]) Here:

* **Billing before successful activation** is the highest-priority control concern, reinforced by billing-before-activation complaints. Validate against operational service status.
* **Expected permit checks not observed** require checking applicability and source records. Missing events are not proof that required checks were never performed.
* **Repeated installation without survey** may indicate inadequate preparation, but a second visit does not universally require a new survey.
* **Complaint escalation after billing** is generally a recovery response, not itself misconduct. Excluding it from the model would hide rather than fix the problem.

## Improved median but worse tail duration

The supplied figures describe a distribution becoming faster near its center and slower at its upper end. There is no arithmetic contradiction. The low-risk segment improves strongly, while several other segments have modest median improvements but markedly worse p90 outcomes. Thus, the conflict exists **within segments as well as across the portfolio**.

A plausible mechanism is:

**Less upfront preparation → more installation exceptions → more recovery demand → longer exception queues.**

This is supported by the survey decline, increased failed/repeat visits, rising manual activation, and longer repeat-visit delays in highly utilized regions. It remains a hypothesis until these events are linked within cases and compared under similar risk and workload conditions.

DFG edges describe observed adjacency, not causal dependencies. Their meaning also depends on activity abstraction. The decline in validation → scheduling is partly explained by the new combined task; it should not be counted as deterioration. ([Process Mining][2]) Nor should edge shares be multiplied to estimate a failed-installation-to-complaint probability: repeated activities, different denominators, and intervening events prevent that inference.

**Duration maturity needs immediate reconciliation.** Six weeks is approximately 42 days. A 62-day p95 could reflect orders started before the pilot, follow-up beyond it, or an estimated rather than directly observed quantile. Carry-in cases may also have received only part of R. None of these possibilities is specified.

Use order-admission cohorts, retain unfinished orders, distinguish treatment exposure, and report follow-up and censoring. Right-censored cases provide a lower bound on their eventual duration, not an observed completion time; appropriate time-to-event methods can incorporate that information. ([NIST][3]) Do not publish an unsupported tail quantile as mature evidence. Separately report backlog age and the share still lacking working service at fixed elapsed times.

Finally, verify the endpoint. **Order-to-billing and order-to-working-service are no longer interchangeable under R.** A shorter billing-based cycle could partly result from moving the finish line.

## Fewer handovers but lower resilience

The handover measure counts transfers; it does not measure whether another employee can take over effectively.

Coordinator ownership can reduce repeated explanations and ambiguous responsibility. Yet the centralized working-together network, high coordinator utilization, and absence-related stalls suggest dependence on a few people. A simpler handover network can therefore coexist with more concentrated operational risk.

The appropriate response is not to maximize or minimize handovers indiscriminately. Preserve a named accountable owner while making case context, next actions, deadlines, and exception status accessible to a deputy or team. Evaluate recovery during absence, backlog aging, and dependence on key individuals alongside transfer counts.

The resource evidence also argues against immediately removing survey staff. Restoring preparation will consume some of the apparently spare survey capacity. Redeployment into activation or revisit work may help, but skill compatibility and the resulting survey queue must be checked rather than assumed.

## Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

The visible saving is **€23 per case**. On the supplied estimates:

$$
\text{Downstream increment: baseline}=198-184=€14
$$

$$
\text{Downstream increment: pilot}=207-161=€46
$$

Thus, the estimated downstream increment rises by **€32**, exceeding the visible saving and producing the estimated **€9 net increase**.

This is an accounting reconciliation, not a causal cost estimate. It assumes comparable cost categories and denominators. Nevertheless, it establishes a useful decision boundary: **additional downstream costs above €23 per case would erase the visible saving**, before allowing for other benefits or harms.

Moving checks past billing can shift work outside the measured process boundary without eliminating it. First-time-right deterioration, repeat visits, and complaint themes make that explanation credible. Finance should include recovery work, customer credits, coordination overhead, backup capacity, and relevant implementation costs. Count costs for open and unbilled orders too; costing only successfully billed cases would leave part of the process out.

The 60-day cost horizon is a minimum evaluation window, not evidence that all costs end there. Report unresolved obligations beyond it separately.

## Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings

The strongest interpretation is **different treatment effects by segment**, not a single universal verdict on simplification.

For simple houses, merging administrative work and reducing coordination friction may remove genuine waste. For apartments and older buildings, a survey or permit check may supply information essential to successful installation. Omitting useful work can save time initially while increasing recovery later.

Composition also matters: pilot regions contain more difficult buildings than baseline. However, case mix alone cannot dismiss the reported adverse changes within apartment and old-building segments. Those estimates still require comparable cohorts and risk adjustment, but they provide an independent reason for caution.

Do not compute national impact by weighting segment medians or p90s: quantiles do not aggregate that way. Estimate segment-level outcome distributions and rate/cost effects first, then evaluate them using the intended rollout population. Treat unknown risk conservatively rather than excluding it from analysis or assigning it to the fast path.

Business and rural cases need separate designs. Restoring apartment surveys will not necessarily resolve business billing disputes or long-distance rescheduling constraints.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative                                                                                  | Advantages                                                                                                                            | Risks and limitations                                                                                                                                                               | When reasonable                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **A. Scale R nationally**                                                                    | Rapidly extends the observed median and handover improvements; avoids maintaining multiple pathways.                                  | Exposes more customers to worsened tails, rework, premature billing, and concentrated resource demand. National composition and capacity differ from the pilot.                     | **Not justified now.** Requires mature, representative, component-specific evidence and functioning controls. A materially corrected design would no longer be unchanged R.                                                          |
| **B. Roll back to baseline**                                                                 | Restores familiar preparation and sequencing; can contain exposure when selective routing or controls cannot be implemented reliably. | Discards low-risk benefits, incurs transition costs, and assumes baseline ownership is preferable without isolating its effect. Baseline performance was not flawless.              | A temporary local fallback when required controls fail, risk classification is unusable, or the hybrid cannot be operated safely. Broader rollback becomes reasonable if controlled evidence shows harm persists despite safeguards. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation** | Separates accountability from risky shortcuts and restores a more defensible sequence.                                                | Ownership itself is not causally isolated and may reproduce absence dependence and overload. As stated, it also forgoes the promising combined task.                                | A useful containment design, with deputies and capacity limits. Broader ownership rollout should depend on resilience testing, not just fewer handovers.                                                                             |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement**                                | Can separate component effects, account for risk, and test capacity and organizational alternatives.                                  | Delays benefits and may expose customers to harm if experiments simply repeat unsafe practices. Four regional assignment units provide limited independent organizational evidence. | Appropriate immediately as the **learning strategy**, while protective changes proceed. Use genuine uncertainty to define eligible experiments; do not randomize required controls away.                                             |
| **E. Segment-specific hybrid with controls and measurement**                                 | Preserves the strongest observed benefits, limits exposure, and allows reversible learning.                                           | Adds routing complexity; missing attributes can misroute cases; the modified package remains untested. Fast-path work can also displace capacity from harder cases.                 | **Preferred**, provided eligibility is verified, unknown-risk cases use the safeguarded pathway, activation precedes billing everywhere, and segment/capacity gates govern expansion.                                                |

The recommended choice is therefore **E as the operating policy, D as the evaluation method, and B as a targeted fallback**. C supplies useful safeguards but is not, by itself, evidence for nationally scaling coordinator ownership.

**Sequence and dependencies.** Start M7, M3, M4, and basic M8 collection together. Run M6 promptly and design M5 in parallel. Add M2 before committing to structural staffing changes. Release M1 only after reliable eligibility, permit safeguards, outcome definitions, and stopping rules exist. Ranking is not an instruction to wait until each preceding study finishes.

For M6, survey outcomes from only the currently surveyed population are insufficient: the disputed decisions concern addresses the rule would skip. Conduct representative field verification among predicted-safe addresses, preserve the rule version and inputs available at routing time, and inspect performance by building type and region.

For M1, keep ownership arrangements, activation-before-billing controls, and relevant staffing comparable. Exclude cases where surveys or permit checks are required or where the evidence already makes skipping unacceptable. Analyze assignment as well as actual adherence, and stop affected strata for predefined harm.

For organizational tests, randomize suitable operational teams or pods when shared queues make case-level assignments interfere. Record spillovers onto comparison and non-fast-path cases. Nine thousand cases do not provide nine thousand independent tests of an organizational policy.

Before relying on any study, reconcile case identifiers, admission dates, treatment versions, merged-task semantics, billing and service-status timestamps, and follow-up. Recover early attributes where defensible; otherwise retain an explicit unknown category and show sensitivity analyses. Missingness should not silently remove the cases most relevant to the decision.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Approve a guarded, phased Alternative E. Do not nationally scale R unchanged and do not perform a blanket rollback.**

**Immediate action.** The COO should establish an exception-recovery operation for billed-but-unactivated orders, missing applicable permit checks, and aging repeat visits. Assign clear ownership through successful activation and unresolved customer recovery—not merely billing. Introduce shared case records, deputies, and workload limits. Finance and customer service should jointly review disputed charges.

**Pause.** Suspend predictive survey skipping for medium-risk and uncertain addresses, all post-billing deferral of required activation verification, survey-capacity reductions, and volume expansion in overloaded teams.

**Scale.** Gradually extend combined validation/scheduling and supported coordinator ownership for independently verified simple, low-risk houses. Keep higher-risk, business, rural, and unknown-risk cases on appropriately safeguarded pathways. This is a modified design, not an assumption that all observed pilot benefits survive unchanged.

**Measure next.** Prioritize M7, M6, M4, and M5, while adding reason codes and lifecycle timestamps. Review controls and queues weekly; do not declare success before complaint, duration, and downstream-cost cohorts mature.

The following are **proposed management gates**, not thresholds established by the supplied data or universal industry standards. Approve them before examining the next experiment’s results.

| Gate                                | Evidence required for broader rollout                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Control integrity**               | Every billing start has verified preceding successful activation for the relevant connection; every permit-sensitive case has completed applicable checks or a documented valid exemption before affected work. Zero confirmed unauthorized bypasses in the evaluation cohort, with source-record audits to validate logging.                                                                         |
| **Tail and customer quality**       | Against comparable contemporaneous controls, each expansion segment has non-worsening point estimates for p90, p95, complaints, SLA breaches, and first-time-right installation. Pre-specified one-sided 95% confidence bounds should exclude deterioration greater than **2 days** for p90/p95 and **1 percentage point** for complaint, SLA, and first-time-right rates, accounting for clustering. |
| **Retained benefit**                | Demonstrated improvement in **order-to-working-service**, not merely billing time. A proposed practical target is at least a **2-day median reduction**, without violating the tail or quality gates.                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| **Full economics**                  | Mature downstream-inclusive costing supports a saving after controls, backup, and relevant implementation costs. The default expansion gate is an upper one-sided 95% bound on incremental mean cost of **€0 or less**; any premium requires a separately approved business case rather than being hidden by visible-cost savings.                                                                    |
| **Capacity and resilience**         | Stable backlog and repeat-visit aging for at least four consecutive weeks, tested absence coverage, and no deterioration imposed on non-fast-path cases. Use **85% planned utilization** for coordinators and second-visit teams as an initial capacity-review trigger, to be revised from measured workload variability—not treated as a guarantee.                                                  |
| **Transferability and measurement** | Complete eligibility evidence for every fast-path assignment; reconciled cohorts and mature follow-up; retained benefits in controlled replication beyond the original pilot footprint. Harmonized held-out process models must support diagnosis, while explicit rule tests—not fitness alone—verify controls.                                                                                       |

Widen rollout **one segment and capacity envelope at a time**. A failed gate should trigger a pause or targeted fallback for the affected pathway, not automatically cancel improvements elsewhere.

**The governing decision is to preserve simplification where it improves working service, while refusing to count deferred checks, downstream recovery, or customer harm as efficiency.**
