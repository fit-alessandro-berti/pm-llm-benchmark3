## 1. Analytical assumptions

I treat a flattened “case notion” as a trace-grouping decision: for a chosen object type, each object instance becomes one case, and each event is copied into that case if the event has a direct E2O relation to the object. Thus, flattening on `Order` creates cases `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, `O4`; flattening on `Item` creates cases `I11`, `I12`, `I21`, `I31`, `I32`, `I41`; and so on. I do not assume that the chosen object type is the “true” process case; it is only a lens.

I define **convergence** as the merging of distinct business episodes or objects into one flattened case because they share an event or object participation. Examples: `e16`/`e24` converge `O1`, `O2`, and `O3` through `D2`; `e19`/`e22`/`e25` converge `O2` and `O3` through `V2`. I define **divergence** as the splitting of one coherent business episode across multiple flattened cases. Examples: package `P3` is split across `O2` and `O3`; delivery `D2` is split across `O1`, `O2`, and `O3`; invoice `V2` is split across `O2` and `O3`; settlement of `V2` is split across payments `M2` and `M3`.

I treat copied shared events as **both evidence and duplication**. The copy is evidence that the event touched multiple objects: `e16` is evidence that `D2` simultaneously involved `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, `P2`, `P3`, and several items. But the same event should not be counted as three independent dispatch executions when the question is “how many dispatches occurred?” Therefore, frequency, DFG edge counts, and performance metrics must be interpreted with awareness of copy multiplicity.

For the baseline comparison, I use **direct E2O relations only**, as supplied. I permit relation propagation through O2O links only as an explicitly labelled analytical transformation, not silently. Propagation would be useful for sensitivity checks, for example to ask whether `P3` should be considered part of the lifecycle of `O2` and `O3` through `contains`/`packs`, but it would also create severe artificial convergence: an `Order`-related event could be pulled into unrelated `Item`, `Package`, `Delivery`, `Invoice`, and `Payment` cases through the connected object graph.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order flattening of cross-order package `P3`**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. Event `e13` is related to `O2`, `O3`, `I21`, `I31`, and `P3`. In order cases, `e13` is copied into both `O2` and `O3`. This creates apparent `Pack Package` activity twice for one physical package event. If an analyst counts order-trace events, `P3` appears as two packing executions. The distortion is harmful for package-level frequency, but informative if the question is “which orders were involved in `P3`?”

2. **Order flattening of cross-order package `P4`**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. Event `e14` is related to `O3`, `O4`, `I32`, `I41`, and `P4`, and is copied into both `O3` and `O4`. `O3` then also contains `e17`/`e27` for `D3`, while `O4` contains the same events. The package `P4` lifecycle is split across two order cases. This can make `O3` look as if it owns `I41` and `O4` look as if it owns `I32`, although the O2O graph says `P4` packs both items from different orders. Harmful for package ownership inference; context-dependent for order exposure.

3. **Package flattening of `P3` contaminated by consolidated delivery `D2`**  
   Affected flattening: `Package`. `P3` has `e13`, `e16`, and `e24`. But `e16` and `e24` also include `O1`, `I12`, and `P2` because `D2` carries both `P2` and `P3`. The `P3` case therefore shows `O1`/`I12`/`P2` participation, even though `P3` packs only `I21` and `I31`. A DFG or trace view may suggest package `P3` is linked to `O1`’s item `I12`. This is harmful if interpreted as package composition, but informative if interpreted as “delivery `D2` affected orders and packages beyond `P3`’s contents.”

4. **Package flattening of `P2` contaminated by consolidated delivery `D2`**  
   Affected flattening: `Package`. `P2` packs `I12` for `O1`, but `e16`/`e24` include `O2`, `O3`, `I21`, `I31`, and `P3`. The `P2` case therefore appears to involve `O2`/`O3` items. This is a cross-order package distortion created by the delivery event, not by the packing relation. It is harmful for package-level attribution and useful only for understanding consolidated delivery impact.

5. **Order flattening of consolidated delivery `D2`**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. Events `e16` and `e24` are copied into `O1`, `O2`, and `O3`. This inflates `Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery` frequencies by a factor of three for `D2`. It also imposes the same dispatch timestamp `2026-02-03 11:05` and confirmation timestamp `2026-02-03 16:00` on all three orders, masking that `P2` was packed at `e12` 10:10 and `P3` at `e13` 10:12. Harmful for order-level delivery performance; informative for identifying all orders affected by `D2`.

6. **Invoice flattening of consolidated invoicing `V2`**  
   Affected flattening: `Invoice`. `V2` contains `e19`, `e22`, and `e25`, all of which also mention `O2` and `O3`. The invoice case converges two orders into one financial case. If the same events are flattened on `Order`, `e19`, `e22`, and `e25` are copied into both `O2` and `O3`, inflating invoice and payment frequencies. Harmful for order-level financial attribution; informative for invoice settlement analysis.

7. **Partial payments `M2` and `M3` are ambiguous in activity-only views**  
   Affected flattening: `Invoice`, `Order`, and `Payment`. Events `e22` and `e25` are both activity `Record Payment`. The distinction that `M2` `partially_settles` `V2` and `M3` `completes_settlement` of `V2` is only in O2O qualifiers. A flattened `Invoice` DFG may show a generic `Issue Invoice`  `Record Payment`  `Record Payment` loop, not a partial-then-complete settlement pattern. In `Payment` flattening, `M2` and `M3` become separate singleton cases, so the relation to the same `V2` disappears unless propagation or O2O inspection is used. Harmful for settlement semantics; informative only if qualifiers are explicitly incorporated.

8. **Near-singleton lifecycle trap for Payment**  
   Affected flattening: `Payment`. Each payment object has exactly one event: `M1` with `e21`, `M2` with `e22`, `M3` with `e25`, `M4` with `e26`. The resulting log has no directly-follows edges and no meaningful lifecycle duration. If used as a lead object, the process appears as isolated payment transactions. This is harmful for end-to-end analysis, but acceptable for counting payment transactions.

9. **DFG edge orientation trap from shared event timing**  
   Affected flattening: `Order`. In `O2` and `O3`, `e24` (`Confirm Delivery`) occurs before `e25` (`Record Payment`), creating a `Confirm Delivery`  `Record Payment` edge. In `O1`, `e21` (`Record Payment`) occurs before `e23`/`e24` (`Confirm Delivery`), creating `Record Payment`  `Confirm Delivery`. This suggests different variants, but the difference is partly caused by `D2` being shared across `O1`, `O2`, and `O3`, and by `V2` being settled after the consolidated delivery confirmation. Context-dependent: useful for spotting financial-versus-physical timing differences, harmful if interpreted as causal order-specific behaviour.

10. **Relation-propagation trap through the O2O graph**  
   Affected flattening: any type if O2O propagation is silently applied. For example, propagating undirected through `contains`, `packs`, `carries`, `bills`, and `settles` can connect `O1` to `O2` and `O3` through `I12`  `P2`  `D2`  `P3`  `I21`/`I31`. An `Order` event such as `e01` could then appear in unrelated item, package, delivery, invoice, and payment cases. This produces artificially long traces and false convergence. Harmful unless explicitly labelled as a sensitivity transformation.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would still create selected flattened logs, but only as purpose-specific views.

- **Order flattening** should be created as the primary traditional log for order fulfilment. It can safely answer: order throughput, create-to-close duration, order variant counts, and which orders were affected by shared events. It should not be used for unique package, delivery, invoice, or payment counts without de-duplication.
- **Invoice flattening** should be created for financial settlement. It can safely answer: invoice issue-to-settlement duration, number of settlement events per invoice, and consolidated invoicing of `O2` and `O3` through `V2`. It cannot answer physical delivery or order closure questions.
- **Package flattening** should be created for logistics package movement. It can safely answer: pack-to-confirm duration for `P1`–`P4`. It must be interpreted with the ET–OT graph to avoid treating `e16`/`e24` objects such as `O1`/`I12` in the `P3` case as package composition.
- **Delivery flattening** should be created only for delivery SLA. It can safely answer: dispatch-to-confirm duration for `D1`, `D2`, and `D3`. It cannot attribute that duration to individual orders or packages without object-centric participation analysis.
- **Item flattening** can be created for line-level physical flow, but it is not an end-to-end log because it omits `e01`–`e04`, `e18`–`e26`, and `e28`–`e31`.
- **Payment flattening** should not be treated as a process lifecycle log. It is useful only for transaction counts, because `M1`–`M4` are singleton cases.

Questions requiring object-centric views:

- **Cross-order packages**: `P3` and `P4` require an OC-DFG, ET–OT graph, and O2O inspection. The order log shows `e13` in `O2` and `O3`, and `e14` in `O3` and `O4`; the package log shows `P3` and `P4` as single objects. Only object-centric views reconcile the one-package, many-orders relation.
- **Consolidated delivery**: `D2` involves `P2`, `P3`, `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, `I12`, `I21`, and `I31` in `e16`/`e24`. This requires an object interaction summary and ET–OT graph to distinguish direct event participation from package composition.
- **Consolidated invoicing and partial payments**: `V2` bills `O2` and `O3`, and `M2`/`M3` settle it. This requires OTG or O2O qualifier inspection; flattened activity logs alone cannot reliably distinguish partial settlement from completion.
- **Contradictory performance findings**: If order flattening says three orders had a dispatch at `e16`, while delivery flattening says one delivery occurred, the reconciliation is by event ID: `e16` is one dispatch event with three order perspectives.

Reconciliation method: compare unique event IDs, case copies, object interaction counts, and OC-DFG weights. If an edge or duration appears in a flattened order log but disappears or changes weight in the OC-DFG, inspect whether the source event is shared. If a package case contains an order not packed by that package, inspect the event’s direct object set rather than assuming silent propagation.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**Decision: B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

**Decision criteria:** coverage of all major activities, presence of start and end lifecycle events, alignment with the business question “order fulfilment,” and manageability of convergence/divergence.

**Strongest supporting evidence:** `Order` is the only object type directly related to every event in the snapshot. It has explicit creation events (`e01`–`e04`) and closure events (`e28`–`e31`). It also participates directly in add-item, pack, dispatch, invoice, payment, and confirm events. Other object types truncate the lifecycle: `Item` lacks order creation, closure, invoicing, and payment; `Package` lacks creation, invoicing, payment, and closure; `Delivery` has only two events per case; `Invoice` lacks physical flow and closure; `Payment` is singleton.

**Strongest counterargument:** `Order` flattening badly duplicates and converges shared episodes. `e16`/`e24` make `D2` appear in three order cases; `e19`/`e22`/`e25` make `V2` and its settlement appear in two order cases; `e13`/`e14` split `P3`/`P4` across multiple orders. Therefore, no single flattened order log can truthfully represent package, delivery, invoice, and payment lifecycles.

**Residual risk:** Analysts may treat order-case copies as independent executions, inflating dispatch, confirm, invoice, and payment frequencies. They may also attribute `D2` or `V2` durations to individual orders as if those episodes were order-specific. Package-level performance may be contaminated by consolidated delivery events.

**Conditions to change the decision:** If the primary KPI is package dwell time, `Package` should become primary with order and delivery as secondary. If the primary KPI is delivery SLA, `Delivery` should become primary. If the primary KPI is financial settlement, `Invoice` should become primary. If many-to-many sharing increases further, or if relation propagation is required, I would move toward rejecting a single lead object and using an explicitly multi-lifecycle object-centric analysis.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Remove or down-weight copied shared events.**  
   In the order flattening, weight each event copy by `1 / number_of_related_order_cases`. For example, `e16` and `e24` should each contribute one total dispatch/confirm execution rather than three; `e13`, `e14`, `e17`, `e27`, `e19`, `e22`, and `e25` should each contribute one total execution rather than two. Recompute activity frequencies, DFG edge counts, and create-to-close durations. If the order DFG changes substantially, the original view was dominated by copied shared events rather than order-specific behaviour.

2. **Compare direct E2O relations with an explicitly propagated-relation variant.**  
   Build a labelled variant where event participation is extended through O2O links such as `contains`, `packs`, `carries`, `bills`, and `settles`. Then compare, for example, whether `e01` (`Create Order O1`) remains limited to `O1` or propagates through `I12`  `P2`  `D2`  `P3`  `I21`/`I31`  `O2`/`O3`. If propagation creates all-orders convergence, report it as a sensitivity artifact, not as the baseline case notion.

3. **Change the lead object and compare conclusions.**  
   Compare order-based create-to-close durations with invoice-based issue-to-settlement durations and package-based pack-to-confirm durations. For example, `O2` and `O3` close at `e29` and `e30`, but `V2` settlement completes at `e25`, and `D2` confirmation occurs at `e24`. If a conclusion such as “orders close after settlement” changes when `Invoice` is primary, that indicates the conclusion is lead-object-dependent, not process-intrinsic.

4. **Compare frequency metrics based on unique events, unique objects, and total object occurrences.**  
   For `Dispatch`, unique events are `e15`, `e16`, and `e17`; unique deliveries are `D1`, `D2`, and `D3`; order-event copies are six; package-event copies are four; item-event copies are six. For `Pack Package`, unique events are `e11`–`e14`; unique packages are four; order-event copies are six; item-event copies are six. Use these comparisons to avoid interpreting order-log copies as independent executions.

5. **Validate shared-object claims with object interaction summaries and OTG qualifiers.**  
   Use the object interaction summary to verify that `D2` interacts with `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, `P2`, `P3`, `I12`, `I21`, and `I31` through `e16`/`e24`. Use the OTG or O2O qualifiers to verify that `M2` `partially_settles` `V2` and `M3` `completes_settlement` of `V2`. If a flattened log suggests a generic repeated `Record Payment` pattern, the qualifier-aware object view should be used to decide whether the pattern is truly partial-then-complete settlement.
