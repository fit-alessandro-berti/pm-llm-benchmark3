## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation

The massive gap between raw model fitness (0.55) and mapped model fitness (0.90) in March implies that the event labels or logging granularity changed significantly, causing the old normative model to reject the new traces. It does not imply that the underlying business process execution fundamentally broke down or changed its core sequence. 

A label mapping can recover conformance by acting as a translation layer, aligning new semantic representations (like `Eligibility Screen`) with the old model (`Validate Eligibility`). However, this recovery does not prove the business process was entirely unchanged. Label mapping only addresses semantic alignment; it ignores other critical dimensions such as performance, rework loops, or case mix, which might have drifted simultaneously.

Duplicated technical events, such as the `Open Case` anomalies, artificially inflate activity counts and create false loops or parallelisms in raw DFGs, severely distorting control-flow analysis. Meanwhile, missing resources disrupt organizational mining, resource utilization metrics, and bottleneck analysis, making it much harder to attribute performance degradation to specific teams or staffing changes.

The post-May DFG and performance patterns cannot be dismissed as logging drift alone because they reflect substantive changes in operational reality. We observe a structural increase in rework (e.g., `Assess -> Request Info` doubling) and a severe deterioration in cycle times (p90 increasing from 71 to 104 hours). These are physical delays and actual rework loops directly correlating with the Supplier Z disruption and staffing cuts, representing true behavioral and performance drift rather than mere changes in how events are recorded.

## 6. Segmentation plan

| Variant | `trace_variant` (Fast vs Rework) | Analyze performance of specific paths | >5% of traces | Service times for happy path vs rework | Ignores rare but costly edge cases |

**Primary monitoring population:** Standard complexity, non-Supplier Z cases. 
**Why:** This segment represents the baseline operational health, isolated from the specific shocks of Supplier Z and the priority rule distortions. Monitoring this segment reveals underlying systemic performance and staffing adequacy without the noise of known external disruptions.

## 9. Provisional attribution

The observed drift is a complex mixture of logging, behavioral, performance, and population shifts. 

**Strongest conclusions:** 
1. The March drift is overwhelmingly a logging/semantic issue (High confidence). The sudden drop in raw fitness alongside stable mapped fitness, new labels, and missing resources perfectly aligns with a system migration rather than a business process breakdown. 
2. The May drift is supplier-driven behavioral drift (High confidence). The jump in Supplier Z volume coincides exactly with the spike in rework variants and `Request Info` loops, indicating incomplete document bundles causing structural rework.
3. The October drift is seasonal population drift (High confidence). The increase in volume and high-complexity cases matches the historical annual renewal season pattern, driving up Senior Assessment routing.

**Plausible conclusions:**
The June/July performance degradation is primarily driven by staffing cuts (Medium-High confidence). The departure of 4 assessors reduced capacity, causing queue waiting times to explode (seen in the p90 spike), especially since the system was already burdened by the May rework backlog. The December improvement is plausibly attributed to the overtime and quality campaign (Medium confidence), though it is confounded by the natural end of the seasonal peak.

**Mediation and Interaction:**
Clean attribution is prevented by the severe interaction between the May rework backlog and the June staffing cuts. The reduced workforce had to process a swollen queue of complex, rework-heavy cases, leading to non-linear performance degradation. Furthermore, the July priority rule interacted with the staffing shortage: it likely redistributed delay, starving standard cases to push high-complexity cases through, but aggregate metrics obscure this effect.

**Conditions for revision:**
I would revise this account if case-level queue mining revealed that waiting times did not actually increase for standard cases after the priority rule, or if segmenting the log showed that Supplier Z cases did not experience higher rework rates than other suppliers during the May-August window.
