# 1. Fairness dimensions

Assessing a public-benefits process requires moving beyond simple outcome comparisons to evaluate how the administrative machinery treats applicants across the entire case lifecycle. A rigorous evaluation encompasses eight interrelated fairness dimensions:

* **Outcome parity (raw outcome comparison):**
  Outcome parity examines whether final approval rates are equal across groups (`78%` for Group A vs. `69%` for Group C, `63%` for Group B, and `58%` for Group D). While a 15 to 20 percentage-point gap is a critical diagnostic signal, raw outcome parity cannot be equated with discrimination because baseline eligibility and case complexity differ substantially across groups (e.g., Group D has a `51%` high `risk_score_band` share compared to `18%` in Group A, and Group B has a `46%` incomplete documentation share compared to `21%` in Group A).
* **Adjusted outcome disparity:**
  Adjusted outcome disparity evaluates approval differences among similarly situated applicants after controlling for observed case-mix factors (`risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and `month`). In the preliminary model, statistically meaningful penalties persist relative to Group A: Group B faces a `-5.5 percentage point` adjusted approval gap, Group D faces `-3.1 percentage points`, and Group C faces `-2.2 percentage points`. This indicates that observed case-mix differences do not fully explain why minority-language, accommodation-seeking, and high-prior-case applicants are approved less often.
* **Procedural burden:**
  Procedural burden captures the administrative friction, extra verification loops, and compliance hurdles imposed on applicants. Even after controlling for baseline `documentation_completeness` and risk, Group B has a `+9.5 percentage point` higher adjusted probability of receiving `Request Missing Documents` and a `+8.0 percentage point` higher adjusted probability of `Supervisor Review`. Group C similarly faces `+4.2 percentage points` for missing-document requests and `+5.5 percentage points` for supervisor review. Furthermore, multi-hop handovers (`general caseworker → bilingual caseworker → general caseworker` for Group B; `general caseworker → accommodation specialist → supervisor` for Group C) increase cognitive and compliance burdens on vulnerable applicants.
* **Timeliness:**
  Timeliness measures both typical speed and tail-risk delays. Raw median and 90th-percentile cycle times show severe divergence: Group A finishes in `8.1 days` (`21.4 days` at p90), whereas Group B requires `13.7 days` (`34.2 days` at p90), Group C requires `15.9 days` (`38.8 days` at p90), and Group D requires `12.1 days` (`29.5 days` at p90). Crucially, after controlling for documented covariates, Group C still waits `+4.1 days` longer and Group B waits `+2.6 days` longer than Group A, whereas Group D's adjusted delay shrinks to `+0.9 days`. This shows that timeliness disparities for Groups B and C are heavily driven by internal process bottlenecks rather than applicant attributes alone.
* **Consistency of routing:**
  Procedural consistency requires that cases with identical documented needs and risk profiles follow predictable, rule-governed pathways. While policy mandates specific routing for high risk (`Fraud Review`), disability accommodation (`Accommodation Review`), and language needs (`Language Support Contact`), the elevated adjusted rates of `Supervisor Review` (`+8.0 pp` for B, `+5.5 pp` for C) and `Request Missing Documents` (`+9.5 pp` for B, `+4.2 pp` for C) reveal discretionary routing inconsistency. Noticeably, Variant V5 routes Group C cases through `Supervisor Review` after `Accommodation Review` (`31%` of Group C), even when risk is not elevated.
* **Quality and accessibility of support:**
  Rights-preserving steps (`Language Support Contact` and `Accommodation Review`) exist to guarantee equal access to public benefits. Procedural fairness requires that invoking a legally protected right does not degrade service quality or subject the applicant to an unmonitored penalty queue. Currently, policy excludes these support steps from separate service-level timeliness thresholds, resulting in `3.8 days` of median waiting after `Language Support Contact` and `4.7 days` after `Accommodation Review` before `Eligibility Assessment` even begins.
* **Appeal and rework burden:**
  High rates of rework and appeals indicate upstream procedural failure—such as unclear document requests, premature denials, translation errors, or inadequate accommodation—that shifts the burden of error correction onto the applicant. Reopen/rework rates for Groups B (`22%`), C (`24%`), and D (`20%`) are roughly double that of Group A (`11%`). Raw appeal rates are `13%` (Group B), `10%` (Group C), and `16%` (Group D) vs. `6%` (Group A), and adjusted appeal probabilities remain elevated (`+4.4 pp` for B, `+1.1 pp` for C, `+5.7 pp` for D). Taking the appeal path (Variant V6) extends median cycle time to `30.7 days`.
* **Resource-allocation fairness:**
  Fairness in resource allocation requires provisioning staffing capacity and role structures proportional to case volume and complexity across all applicant groups. Here, capacity is starkly imbalanced: Group A has access to a broad pool of `52 caseworkers`, whereas Group B (`1,250 cases`, `57%` needing language support) is bottlenecked into just `7 bilingual caseworkers` and `3 overloaded regional officers`. Group C (`820 cases`, `62%` requesting accommodation) relies on only `5 accommodation specialists`, two of whom must split their capacity with `Supervisor Review`.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To separate **raw disparity** (unadjusted group differences), **legitimate routing** (necessary workflow adaptations to genuine case needs), **procedural discrimination / structural disparate impact** (unjustified differential treatment or under-resourced pathways for protected needs), and **uncertainty** (unobserved confounding and measurement bias), the analysis should follow a six-part strategy:

### 1. Matching and stratification design
Construct tiered propensity-score or Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM) strata to compare apples to apples without blindly conditioning on potentially biased variables:
* **Tier 1 (Exogenous / baseline demographic & structural controls):** Stratify on `region`, application `month`, and `household_size_band`.
* **Tier 2 (Access & intake controls):** Add `channel` (online vs. in-person) and explicit rights-triggering indicators (`language_preference`, `disability_accommodation_requested`).
* **Tier 3 (Full observed complexity controls):** Add `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, and `prior_case_count`.
Comparing estimators across Tiers 1, 2, and 3 reveals how much of the raw disparity is absorbed by intake mode versus measured complexity/risk.

### 2. Path and variant comparison within strata
Within matched strata of identical documented attributes:
* Partition variants into **normatively mandated paths** (e.g., V3 for high `risk_score_band`, V4 when `language_preference` requires support, V5 when `disability_accommodation_requested` is true) versus **discretionary path divergences** (e.g., routing into V2 via `Request Missing Documents` instead of V1 fast-track when `documentation_completeness` is already marked complete, or inserting `Supervisor Review` for low/medium-risk cases).
* Test conditional independence: given `documentation_completeness = complete` and identical `risk_score_band`, does `applicant_group` still predict transition into `Request Missing Documents` or `Supervisor Review`? The preliminary adjusted gaps (`+9.5 pp` and `+8.0 pp` for Group B; `+4.2 pp` and `+5.5 pp` for Group C) confirm significant unexplained routing divergence.

### 3. Resource-assignment and handover comparison
For cases within the same matched stratum and variant:
* Compare **effective queue ratio** (active cases per available qualified caseworker at the timestamp of step arrival).
* Measure **handover depth and ping-pong rate**: count distinct `org:resource` transitions per case and identify ping-pong loops (`R1 → R2 → R1`).
* Evaluate **role-contention exposure**: flag whether a case is assigned to hybrid resources (such as the 2 accommodation specialists who also handle `Supervisor Review` or the 3 overloaded regional officers) and estimate the delay penalty attributable specifically to resource congestion vs. applicant group.

### 4. Waiting-time vs. service-time decomposition
Total cycle time $T_{\text{total}}$ must be decomposed into active processing time and queue delay:
$$T_{\text{total}} = \sum_{k \in \text{shared steps}} \left(W_k + S_k\right) + \sum_{m \in \text{support/risk steps}} \left(W_m + S_m\right)$$
where $W$ is waiting time before resource pick-up and $S$ is active service time:
* **Shared-step queue penalty:** Compare waiting times $W_k$ on universal transitions (`Submit → Identity Verification`, `Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents`, `Supervisor Review → Decision Draft`, `Decision Draft → Applicant Notification`). If Group B waits `1.6d` vs. Group A's `0.4d` between `Completeness Check` and `Request Missing Documents`, that `1.2d` gap is pure queueing disparity caused by waiting for bilingual staff.
* **Support-step structural penalty:** Isolate the wait time $W_m$ inside `Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment` (`3.8d`) and `Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment` (`4.7d`). While the *existence* of the support step is legitimate routing, multi-day *queue waiting* due to understaffing (7 bilingual workers, 5 accommodation specialists) is an avoidable structural burden, not inherent case complexity.

### 5. Sensitivity analysis for tainted / endogenous covariates
Because `risk_score_band` may encode historical bias and `documentation_completeness` may be degraded by unstranslated instructions or inaccessible forms:
* **Post-treatment / mediator bounds:** Treat `documentation_completeness` as partially mediated by language and disability barriers rather than purely exogenous applicant non-compliance. Re-estimate approval and delay gaps omitting `documentation_completeness` and modeling it as an intermediate procedural outcome.
* **Misclassification sensitivity:** Simulate reclassifying $10\%\text{–}25\%$ of Group D and Group B cases from high to medium `risk_score_band` (to test historical over-scoring) and re-compute adjusted differences in approval probability and `Fraud Review` routing.
* **Oster's $\delta$ / Rosenbaum bounds (E-values):** Quantify how strong an unobserved legitimate complexity factor would need to be to reduce the `-5.5 pp` (Group B), `-3.1 pp` (Group D), and `-2.2 pp` (Group C) adjusted approval penalties to zero.

### 6. Subgroup checks by region and channel
Disaggregate all adjusted metrics across `region × channel` cells:
* **Regional isolation:** Compare the two regions documented as having supervisor backlogs, ping-pong handovers, and 3 overloaded officers against the remaining regions. This establishes whether Group B and C disparities are systemic across the agency or concentrated in specific regional bottlenecks.
* **Channel isolation:** Separate online vs. in-person applications within each group to verify whether the `Submit → Identity Verification` gap (`1.4d` for B and `1.2d` for C vs. `0.8d` for A) disappears within channel or persists for minority-language/disabled applicants even when applying online.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least six concrete operational mechanisms—spanning legitimate policy design, structural capacity mismatches, and discretionary bias—can explain the observed patterns:

1. **Legitimate complexity-driven specialization (Legitimate mechanism):**
   Cases involving high fraud risk (`51%` in Group D), missing verification records (`46%` in Group B, `36%` in Group C), disability accommodations (`62%` in Group C), or non-majority languages (`57%` in Group B) genuinely require specialized verification steps (`Fraud Review`, `Request Missing Documents`, `Accommodation Review`, `Language Support Contact`). Under this mechanism, longer unadjusted cycle times (`12.1d–15.9d` vs. `8.1d`) partly reflect thorough, policy-compliant execution of protective and fiduciary checks rather than animus.
2. **Capacity starvation and absence of SLA governance on rights-preserving steps (Concerning structural mechanism):**
   While Group A is served by a well-provisioned pool of `52 caseworkers`, Group B (`~712` language-need cases) is funneled to just `7 bilingual caseworkers` (`~102` target cases per bilingual worker), and Group C (`~508` accommodation cases) is funneled to `5 accommodation specialists` (`~102` target cases per specialist). Because agency SLAs omit separate timeliness targets for support steps, management does not rebalance queues when `Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment` (`3.8d`) and `Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment` (`4.7d`) become starved for capacity.
3. **Role contention and dual-hatting feedback loops (Concerning organizational mechanism):**
   Two of the five accommodation specialists also serve as supervisor reviewers, and Group B relies heavily on three overloaded regional officers. When `Supervisor Review` demand spikes in the two backlogged regions, those dual-hatted specialists are pulled away from `Accommodation Review`, inflating the `4.7d` accommodation queue while simultaneously slowing `Supervisor Review → Decision Draft` (`1.6d` for C, `1.9d` for B vs. `1.0d` for A).
4. **Handover fragmentation and context loss ("ping-pong" routing) (Concerning workflow mechanism):**
   In two regions, general caseworkers do not relinquish end-to-end ownership smoothly; instead, cases bounce `general caseworker → bilingual caseworker → general caseworker` (Group B) or `general caseworker → accommodation specialist → supervisor` (Group C). Each handover incurs a queue re-entry delay and strips tacit case context. When the receiving generalist resumes the case without full notes from the bilingual contact or accommodation review, they are more likely to defensively trigger `Request Missing Documents` (`+9.5 pp` adjusted for B, `+4.2 pp` for C) or escalate to `Supervisor Review` (`+8.0 pp` for B, `+5.5 pp` for C).
5. **Communication friction, premature denial for non-response, and defensive review (Concerning discretionary mechanism):**
   Even when `documentation_completeness` is nominally rated at the same level, general caseworkers reviewing unfamiliar foreign-language documents or non-standard medical/accommodation paperwork may apply stricter scrutiny, request redundant documents (`+9.5 pp` for B), or issue poorly explained denials (`-5.5 pp` adjusted approval for B). Applicants who misunderstand poorly translated document requests or notification letters miss deadlines, driving high reopen/rework rates (`22%` for B, `24%` for C) and higher adjusted appeals (`+4.4 pp` for B).
6. **Endogenous risk scoring and historical feedback loops (Concerning algorithmic/institutional mechanism):**
   Group D has both high `prior_case_count` (`49%`) and high `risk_score_band` (`51%`). If prior administrative denials or churn (applicants re-applying after procedural knockouts) feed into `prior_case_count`, and `prior_case_count` is weighted heavily in calculating `risk_score_band`, then past procedural barriers mechanically inflate current risk scores. This pushes Group D into `Fraud Review` (Variant V3, `28%`) and lowers approval rates (`58%` raw, `-3.1 pp` adjusted), prompting high appeal rates (`16%` raw, `+5.7 pp` adjusted) when erroneous risk flags are contested.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

Relying solely on the columns in the event log and the preliminary regression model leaves critical blind spots that prevent definitive causal attribution:

* **Measurement bias in `documentation_completeness`:**
  `documentation_completeness` is recorded by caseworkers during `Completeness Check`, not measured as an objective ground truth. If application instructions are poorly translated or inaccessible, Group B and C applicants will submit forms deemed "incomplete" due to agency design flaws rather than applicant negligence. Furthermore, if general caseworkers mark unfamiliar minority-language documents as "incomplete" pending bilingual review, controlling for `documentation_completeness` absorbs and masks procedural bias (a "tainted control").
* **Historical bias and proxy confounding in `risk_score_band` and `prior_case_count`:**
  `risk_score_band` is treated in the regression as an objective complexity control, yet the qualitative context notes it may encode historical disparities. If the scoring model uses `prior_case_count`, neighborhood/region proxies, or historical fraud referrals that disproportionately targeted Group D or Group B, controlling for `risk_score_band` understates unfairness by treating biased risk inflation as legitimate case difficulty. Conversely, if `risk_score_band` is too coarse (only bands rather than continuous scores), residual within-band risk variation between Group D (`51%` high band) and Group A (`18%` high band) could remain unadjusted.
* **Missing granular applicant needs and complexity indicators:**
  The dataset lacks crucial determinants of genuine assessment complexity and statutory eligibility: exact income/asset verification complexity (e.g., gig/informal work vs. salaried W-2 income), type and severity of disability accommodation needed, digital literacy, housing stability (affecting receipt of mail notifications), and third-party verification responsiveness (e.g., employers or medical providers delaying paperwork).
* **Coarse and incomplete observation of `org:resource` workload:**
  Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. Daily counts hide intraday queue arrival spikes, context-switching overhead from multi-hop handovers, non-logged administrative or translation duties performed by the `7 bilingual caseworkers`, and priority preemption rules between `Accommodation Review` and `Supervisor Review` among the dual-hatted specialists. Nor does the log record start and end timestamps for every activity (if only completion timestamps exist, separating service time from waiting time requires imputation assumptions).
* **Absence of appeal outcome, error direction, and interaction quality:**
  `appeal_flag` and `case_outcome` do not reveal whether appeals resulted in *reversals* (proving the initial decision was erroneous) or *upheld denials*. Additionally, `appeal_flag` suffers from selection bias: vulnerable applicants exhausted by `34–39 day` waits or unclear notifications may abandon valid claims rather than appeal. Finally, the log captures no measure of explanation clarity, translation accuracy, or dignity of treatment during in-person and support interactions.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Crucially, **removing rights-preserving steps (`Language Support Contact` or `Accommodation Review`) merely to compress cycle time is unacceptable**, as it would violate statutory rights and reduce substantive access. Instead, targeted operational, analytical, and governance interventions should be deployed, with explicit attention to their trade-offs:

| Mitigation option | Operational design | Potential side effects and trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Add dedicated capacity for language support and accommodation review** | Expand the bilingual specialist pool beyond `7` workers, hire or cross-train additional accommodation specialists beyond `5`, relieved of dual-hatted `Supervisor Review` duties, and rebalance caseloads for the `3 overloaded regional officers`. | **Trade-offs:** Higher personnel and training costs; temporary productivity dip while existing specialists train new hires; risk of lower utilization during low-volume weeks if specialists cannot flex into general `Eligibility Assessment` when support queues are empty. |
| **2. Establish explicit service-level targets (SLAs) for support steps** | Define binding timeliness thresholds specifically for `Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment` (currently `3.8d`) and `Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment` (currently `4.7d`), alongside overall parity SLAs for end-to-end cycle time across groups. | **Trade-offs:** "Goodhart's Law" risk—staff under strict timer pressure may rush consultations, provide superficial language or accommodation support, or prematurely close complex accommodation requests to avoid SLA breaches unless paired with quality audits. |
| **3. Redesign routing to eliminate ping-pong handovers ("case ownership")** | Replace `general → bilingual → general` and `general → accommodation specialist → supervisor` chains with **end-to-end specialized case ownership** at intake (routing cases with `language_preference` directly to bilingual caseworkers who complete `Completeness Check` and `Eligibility Assessment`), and decouple routine `Accommodation Review` from mandatory `Supervisor Review` (V5). | **Trade-offs:** Requires cross-training specialists in full eligibility rules; may create siloed sub-units where workload spikes cannot easily spill over to the 52 generalists unless a structured overflow protocol is maintained. |
| **4. Improve decision explanations and intake document guidance** | Standardize multilingual, plain-language, accessible templates for `Request Missing Documents` and `Applicant Notification`; provide interactive checklists at intake (`Submit Application`) tailored to language and disability needs to prevent avoidable V2 loops. | **Trade-offs:** Slightly increases drafting service time at `Decision Draft` and upfront IT/translation maintenance costs; clearer explanations of appeal rights may initially *increase* observed `appeal_flag` rates among previously deterred applicants before lowering erroneous denials long-term. |
| **5. Continuous monitoring of adjusted procedural disparities** | Implement automated monthly dashboards tracking adjusted gaps in approval probability, cycle time, step-level waits, handover depth, `Request Missing Documents` rates, and `Supervisor Review` rates stratified by `region` and `channel`. | **Trade-offs:** Risk of "metric gaming" (e.g., caseworkers avoiding logging a formal `Request Missing Documents` event by asking informally, or downgrading accommodation flags) and false alarms in small region-by-group cells if confidence intervals are not reported. |
| **6. Audit and recalibrate `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness`** | Audit `risk_score_band` features to strip or discount `prior_case_count` caused by administrative churn, calibrate false-positive `Fraud Review` rates across groups, and standardize objective checklists for `documentation_completeness` audited independently of applicant language. | **Trade-offs:** Lowering risk sensitivity on `prior_case_count` could slightly increase undetected fraud if alternative behavioral indicators are not validated; stricter checklists for `Request Missing Documents` reduce caseworker discretion in edge cases. |

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

The following Python implementation demonstrates how to operationalize the fairness audit using the specified `pm4py` functions across data preparation, variant comparison, performance DFG waiting-time decomposition, organizational handover/role mining, and declarative/log-skeleton conformance checking:

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# ==============================================================================
# 1. FORMAT DATAFRAME & AUGMENT WITH SERVICE / WAITING TIMES AND DURATIONS
# ==============================================================================
raw_df = pd.read_csv("public_benefits_event_log.csv")

# Standardize XES columns (case:concept:name, concept:name, time:timestamp)
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw_df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    timest_format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S"
)

# Insert step-level service and waiting times into the event log
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)

# Extract activity-level mean/median service times and overall case durations
activity_service_times = pm4py.get_service_time(df, aggregation_measure="median")
all_case_durations = pm4py.get_all_case_durations(df)

# ==============================================================================
# 2. SUBGROUP & STRATIFIED FILTERING BY GROUP, REGION, CHANNEL, AND COVARIATES
# ==============================================================================
GROUPS = ["Group A", "Group B", "Group C", "Group D"]
group_logs = {
    g: pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        df, attribute_key="applicant_group", values=[g], level="case", retain=True
    )
    for g in GROUPS
}

# Example matched stratum: complete documentation, low/medium risk, online channel
matched_stratum_df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    df, attribute_key="documentation_completeness", values=["complete"], level="case", retain=True
)
matched_stratum_df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    matched_stratum_df, attribute_key="risk_score_band", values=["low", "medium"], level="case", retain=True
)
matched_stratum_df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    matched_stratum_df, attribute_key="channel", values=["online"], level="case", retain=True
)

# ==============================================================================
# 3. VARIANT ANALYSIS & DISCRETIONARY ROUTING CHECKS WITHIN MATCHED STRATA
# ==============================================================================
FAST_TRACK_V1 = (
    "Submit Application", "Identity Verification", "Completeness Check",
    "Eligibility Assessment", "Decision Draft", "Applicant Notification", "Benefit Activation"
)

for g in GROUPS:
    sub_df = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        matched_stratum_df, attribute_key="applicant_group", values=[g], level="case", retain=True
    )
    variants_dict = pm4py.get_variants(sub_df)
    
    # Filter solely to Fast-Track V1 to compare matched fast-track access rates
    v1_df = pm4py.filter_variants(sub_df, variants=[FAST_TRACK_V1], retain=True)
    total_cases = sub_df["case:concept:name"].nunique()
    v1_cases = v1_df["case:concept:name"].nunique() if not v1_df.empty else 0
    print(f"{g} (Matched Stratum) -> Total: {total_cases}, V1 Share: {v1_cases / max(total_cases, 1):.2%}")

# ==============================================================================
# 4. FREQUENCY DFG, PERFORMANCE DFG, AND BOTTLENECK PATH FILTERING
# ==============================================================================
CRITICAL_TRANSITIONS = [
    ("Submit Application", "Identity Verification"),
    ("Completeness Check", "Request Missing Documents"),
    ("Language Support Contact", "Eligibility Assessment"),
    ("Accommodation Review", "Eligibility Assessment"),
    ("Risk Screening", "Fraud Review"),
    ("Supervisor Review", "Decision Draft"),
    ("Decision Draft", "Applicant Notification")
]

for g, g_df in group_logs.items():
    dfg_freq, start_acts, end_acts = pm4py.discover_dfg(g_df)
    perf_dfg, _, _ = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(g_df, perf_aggregation_key="median")
```

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

Based on the combined process-mining and statistical evidence, the case-management process is **procedurally unfair with respect to administrative burden, waiting-time allocation, and routing consistency for Groups B and C, while the exact causal breakdown of the remaining adjusted approval-rate gaps (`-2.2` to `-5.5 percentage points`) is indeterminate pending an audit of covariate bias and unobserved case complexity.**

Specifically:
1. **What can be inferred with high confidence (Demonstrable procedural unfairness):**
   The process fails procedural fairness standards on timeliness, resource parity, and administrative friction. While routing Group B to `Language Support Contact`, Group C to `Accommodation Review`, and Group D to `Fraud Review` is policy-mandated and legitimate in principle, the operational execution penalizes applicants who invoke protected support rights. Funneling Group B and Group C into severely understaffed specialist queues (`7` bilingual staff and `5` accommodation specialists vs. `52` general caseworkers) without SLA timeliness targets imposes `3.8-day` and `4.7-day` queue waits. Coupled with multi-hop handovers, dual-hatted specialist contention, and unexplained adjusted spikes in `Request Missing Documents` (`+9.5 pp` for B, `+4.2 pp` for C) and `Supervisor Review` (`+8.0 pp` for B, `+5.5 pp` for C), the system imposes an adjusted cycle-time penalty of `+2.6 days` on Group B and `+4.1 days` on Group C and doubles their rework rates (`22%–24%` vs. `11%`).
2. **What cannot be definitively inferred from current data (Indeterminate outcome & risk-score fairness):**
   Raw approval differences (`58%–69%` vs. `78%`) cannot be interpreted as direct discrimination because baseline risk and documentation completeness vary widely. Conversely, the remaining adjusted approval penalties (`-5.5 pp` for B, `-3.1 pp` for D, `-2.2 pp` for C) and adjusted appeal gaps (`+4.4 pp` for B, `+5.7 pp` for D) cannot be dismissed as benign residual complexity: they may reflect either unobserved legitimate eligibility differences (e.g., complex income structures) *or* downward bias caused by tainted controls (`risk_score_band` encoding historical churn and `documentation_completeness` reflecting language/accessibility barriers). Resolving this requires auditing the risk score, capturing appeal reversal rates, and reforming support-step capacity and handover rules.
