# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency for high-complexity cases:** Simple, automated cases will lower the average cycle time, hiding severe delays in the 90th percentile where high-complexity cases sit in central queues.
2. **Local knowledge loss:** Central specialists lack regional context. The resulting rework may only manifest weeks later at the Quality Check stage, not immediately in the intake phase.
3. **Fairness disparities:** Uniform routing rules may inadvertently penalize customer segments requiring language accommodations or specific regional knowledge, an effect invisible in unsegmented averages.
4. **Learning degradation:** By routing all hard cases away from local teams, local caseworkers stop developing technical skills. This manifests as a gradual decrease in local technical activities in the event log, weakening future resilience.
5. **Accountability ambiguity:** Shared approval queues obscure who made the final decision. The log may show a generic "Shared Approval" resource, making it hard to trace accountability for poor outcomes.
6. **Exception handling failures:** An increase in `handover_reason` categorized as "escalation" rather than "absence cover" indicates that the new system is struggling with exceptions, even if overall throughput looks fine.
7. **Hidden Triage Bottlenecks:** Tech specialists in the central pool may spend significant time triaging Legal/Risk cases. This undocumented internal workload won't appear as a handover in the log but will severely delay the visible Tech Review activity.

# 5. Pilot design

- **Pilot scope:** Route all Team East cases (currently overloaded) and 30% of Team North cases (currently stable) to the new Technical Shared Service and shared approval queue.
- **Comparison group:** Team South remains fully local and embedded, serving as the control group.
- **Pre/post observation window:** 4 weeks of pre-pilot baseline logging, followed by an 8-week pilot implementation period.
- **Segmentation:** Analyze results disaggregated by `team` (East vs. North vs. South), `case_complexity` (low/medium/high), `required_specialty` (tech, legal, risk), and `customer_segment` (to monitor language/regional needs).
- **Guardrail metrics:**
  - 90th percentile cycle time for high-complexity cases must not increase by more than 10%.
  - Overall `rework_flag` rate must not exceed 12%.
  - Median wait time in the central Tech Shared Service queue must not exceed 5.0h.
- **Staff feedback collection:** Weekly structured standups with local caseworkers and central specialists to qualitatively assess role clarity, perceived fairness, and the loss of informal advice loops.
- **Rollback or adjustment triggers:** If guardrail metrics are breached for two consecutive weeks, or if qualitative feedback indicates severe accountability loss, pause central routing and revert to local embedded specialists for the affected teams.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: Pilot First (Conditional).**

Management should not proceed with a full organizational rollout based on the proposed routing model. While the theoretical benefits of higher specialist utilization and standardized approvals are compelling—particularly for relieving the overloaded Team East—the risks of central queue bottlenecks and local expertise loss are substantial. 

A phased pilot is necessary to determine if the central queue can handle the combined demand of technical, legal, and risk triage without degrading service. Management should strictly monitor the 90th percentile cycle time and `reassignment_count` for high-complexity cases. 

**Crucially, management must not infer success from early improvements in average cycle time.** The centralization of simple, automated cases will naturally pull the average down, creating a false sense of security. The true test of this restructuring lies in the tail latency of complex cases, the rework rate for cases requiring local context, and the retention of technical skill among local caseworkers.
