# 1. Executive memo

Approve E. Expand the combined validation and scheduling step, with a named coordinator and a deputy, in capped waves for verified low-risk houses in the four pilot regions. Restore risk-based surveys elsewhere and require successful activation before billing for every order. Use a concurrent comparison before releasing the next wave.

The low-risk house segment accounts for 34% of pilot orders. Its median fell six days, its p90 fell eight days, and first-time-right installation rose three percentage points. A full rollback would give up a credible service improvement. National rollout would expose customers to a different experience. Across the pilot, the median fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, but p95 rose from 45 to 62 days and 30-day complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%. Customers increasingly reported being billed for service that did not work. The €23 visible saving per order becomes an estimated €9 premium once complaint and revisit costs are included.

Give operations authority today to hold billing until activation is verified, review billed orders awaiting confirmation, and correct service and charges where needed. Give regional leads responsibility for permit evidence, deputy coverage, and the revisit backlog. The pilot bundled four changes, served a harder building mix, and has incomplete follow-up. It supports protecting customers and retaining the low-risk benefit now. Expansion into each additional segment should depend on its own service, tail, and cost results.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **Fitness and precision measure different things.** Fitness measures how well a model can replay observed traces. Precision falls when it also permits many unobserved sequences. A common coordinator path helps replay, while flexible combinations of survey skips, repeated visits, manual activation and billing admit extra sequences. Lower generalization gives less confidence in performance on unseen cases. For operational controls, inspect alignments against the intended workflow. Repeated installation without a preceding survey is a move on the log. An expected permit check absent from the log is a move on the model. Each flags a case for verification.
- **The median and tail reflect different orders.** The temporal profile shows faster validation-to-scheduling for simple houses and slower installation-to-activation for apartments. Revisit delays grew in high-utilization regions. Enough routine orders can finish sooner to move the midpoint while repeated visits and queues stretch the upper percentiles. Segment quantiles cannot be averaged to recover the overall quantile. Completion-to-completion intervals combine waiting and work. Track order-to-working-service alongside order-to-billing, since earlier billing can move a recorded endpoint ahead of service.
- **Ownership and resilience depend on different network properties.** The handover network became simpler, while the working-together network became more concentrated around coordinators and activation specialists. A customer can have one owner and still wait on that person's availability or specialist capacity. Stalls during coordinator absence make shared case notes and deputy access worth testing even where routine ownership works well.
- **Visible savings and downstream costs accrue at different stages.** The implied addition for complaint and revisit handling rose from €14 to €46 per order. That €32 increase exceeds the €23 visible saving. At €161 visible cost, the pilot could absorb €37 per order in additional costs before reaching the €198 baseline estimate. The billing-to-complaint edge rose from 3% to 8% and became a long-tail relation. Late complaints make the observation window material to that break-even calculation.
- **The redesign removed different work in different segments.** Combining validation and scheduling removes a delay for simple houses. For an apartment, a survey can uncover access or technical issues before a crew travels. For an older building, a permit check may determine whether work can proceed at all. The DFG shift from installation → activation test (78% to 60%) toward installation → manual activation (8% to 19%) is consistent with problems surfacing later. Edge shares describe adjacent events and cannot establish that a particular skipped survey caused a downstream fault. The harder pilot mix can amplify the aggregate deterioration, while the within-segment declines still require action.

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranks reflect the decisions each result could change and the cost of waiting. Start long follow-up and inexpensive logging immediately, even where the eventual readout ranks lower.

| Rank | Measurement | Uncertainty resolved and decision consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | M7. Monitor activation and permit conformance | Link flagged orders to activation evidence, billing transactions and applicable permit records. A verified breach triggers correction and a hold on expansion. A completed check missing from the log calls for a recording fix. This protects current orders immediately. |
| 2 | M4. Capture full 60-day downstream cost | Link revisit labor and travel, activation support, complaint handling and billing corrections to orders in both arms for 60 days after billing. If recovery consumes the visible saving in a segment, drop the cost-saving rationale for expanding its fast path. A sustained saving strengthens expansion if service gates also pass. |
| 3 | M6. Validate the building-risk rule | Independently survey a sample on both sides of the rule’s cutoff, including representative apartments it would skip. Estimate how often the rule misses a material field issue by stratum. Frequent or poorly bounded misses keep that group on the survey route. A well-performing group becomes eligible for M1. |
| 4 | M1. Randomize survey skipping within eligible risk strata | Among orders for which either choice is acceptable, compare survey versus skip within region and risk stratum, holding ownership and billing controls fixed. This estimates the causal trade-off in visits, working-service time and cost that M6 alone cannot establish. Expand skipping only in a stratum that passes the gates. |
| 5 | M5. Test ownership during absences | Compare coordinator-led and shared-team coverage across matched teams or randomized team periods, recording absences, backlog and failed-to-second-visit time. If pooled coverage shortens recovery without losing routine speed, extend the shared arrangement. Persistent stalls argue against scaling ownership alone. |
| 6 | M3. Code exception reasons | Distinguish site access, permits, customer rescheduling, signal problems and billing disputes. Audit a sample against case notes. Concentrated access failures favor surveys, while concentrated activation failures favor specialist coverage. Codes are quick to collect but do not establish intervention effects. |
| 7 | M2. Record start times | Pair starts and completions for installation, activation testing and manual activation to distinguish time waiting for a specialist from time performing the work. The result directs staffing versus technical changes and helps explain the tail. |
| 8 | M8. Separate customer experience measures | Ask about speed to appointment and whether service worked after installation, including customers whose activation remains open. Divergence would change the service measures used to judge the fast path. The aggregate post-close NPS fell from +21 to +17, and reported billing complaints already justify the control. |

For the low-risk rollout, randomize rollout timing among comparable teams within each region and retain contemporaneous teams on the standard route. Apply billing and permit controls in both arms. Enroll by order-entry date, define working service by confirmed successful activation, and record building risk and segment before routing. Treat missing early labels as unknown, repeat the historical comparison using weeks 3–6 of the pilot, and standardize to a common region and segment mix. Track open orders with censoring and allow the 30-day complaint and 60-day cost windows to mature. A reported 62-day p95 in a six-week pilot makes the inclusion of carry-in orders and the follow-up cutoff especially important to verify.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Authorize E now.** Scale combined validation and scheduling with backed-up coordinator ownership only for verified simple, low-risk houses. Pause predictive survey skipping pending validation and suspend post-billing activation in every segment. Route uncertain addresses for assessment, verify applicable permits before dispatch, and assign an activation owner through confirmed working service. Review business billing disputes and protect revisit slots for rural orders, whose p90 rose nine days despite a faster median.

**Release the next wave by segment.** Use audited billing and permit controls, 60-day cost, rule validation and the controlled comparisons above. Proposed management tolerances, to agree before enrollment, are a median working-service gain of at least two days and no increase in mean fully loaded cost versus the concurrent standard route. Require one-sided 95% confidence bounds to rule out p90 and p95 delays exceeding two days, a first-time-right decline exceeding one percentage point, or increases exceeding one point in second visits, 30-day complaints or SLA breaches. Size each segment comparison to assess its own tail, account for team-level assignment, and allow follow-up to mature. Keep volume capped if results remain inconclusive or revisit backlogs grow during absences.

**Hold expansion on any confirmed early bill or missed required permit check.** Correct the affected orders, verify the control on subsequent orders, and resume only when the segment clears the release criteria. A segment that repeatedly fails returns to the standard route while qualifying segments retain the faster path.
