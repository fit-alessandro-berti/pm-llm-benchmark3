# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

For each case $c$ and branch $i \in \{A,B,C\}$ (with start $S_{i,c}$ and end $E_{i,c}$):

- **Upstream elapsed**: $\text{up}(c) = \text{fork\_release}_c - \text{arrival}_c$
- **Branch queue**: $\text{q}_{i,c} = \max(0,\; S_{i,c} - \text{fork\_release}_c)$.  
  The branch is permitted to start only after $\text{fork\_release}$; any later start is queue.
- **Branch service**: $\text{sv}_{i,c} = E_{i,c} - S_{i,c}$
- **Branch completion**: $\text{comp}_{i,c} = E_{i,c}$
- **Join-ready** (not logged): $\text{join}_c = \max_i \text{comp}_{i,c}$
- **Critical branch**: $\text{crit}_c = \arg\max_i \text{comp}_{i,c}$ (alphabetical tie-break: $A \succ B \succ C$)
- **Synchronization wait** (branch-specific): $\text{syn}_{i,c} = \max(0,\; \text{join}_c - \text{comp}_{i,c})$.  
  For the critical branch $\text{syn}=0$.
- **Fork-join stage** (elapsed, parallel): $\text{fj}_c = \text{join}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c$
- **Downstream queue**: $\text{dq}_c = \max(0,\; \text{final\_start}_c - \text{join}_c)$
- **Final service**: $\text{fs}_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{final\_start}_c$
- **Total flow time**: $\text{tot}_c = \text{final\_end}_c - \text{arrival}_c = \text{up}(c) + \text{fj}_c + \text{dq}_c + \text{fs}_c$

**Why summing synchronization waits double-counts.**  
The waits $\text{syn}_{A,c}, \text{syn}_{B,c}, \text{syn}_{C,c}$ occur *in parallel* during the single elapsed window $\text{fj}_c = \text{join}_c - \text{fork\_release}_c$. Adding them sequentially counts the same minutes multiple times (once for each non-critical branch). The correct accounting is to measure $\text{fj}_c$ once, then allocate its internal composition by the critical path ($\text{q}_{\text{crit}}+\text{sv}_{\text{crit}}$) and note that the remaining branches finish earlier within that same window.

| F01, F04, F08, F11 | No | — | 0 | — | 0 |

Aggregate direct $\text{join}$ reduction across B-critical cases: $10+10+12.5+15+15+12.5+10+15 = 100$ min. However, because $\text{dq} \ge \Delta$ in 6 of those 8 cases, the **propagated total-flow savings** are small (only F05 and F12 show probable net gains). This demonstrates that **downstream congestion masks branch-level improvements**.

Secondary effect: In F11, B queue falls from 10 to 5, so B ends at 11:15 while A (unchanged) ends at 11:30; no tie or shift in criticality.

### 3. Add final-station capacity / reduce downstream queue by 50%

Assume $\text{dq}_c \to 0.5 \cdot \text{dq}_c$ (e.g., a flexible third resource or policy change).

- Direct benefit for every case: $\text{tot}_c$ reduced by exactly $0.5 \cdot \text{dq}_c$.
- Aggregate direct saving: $\frac{1}{2}\sum \text{dq}_c = \frac{176}{2}=88$ min over 12 cases (mean $\approx 7.3$ min/case).
- **Unmasking interaction:** With lower $\text{dq}$, the masked gains from Counterfactuals 1 and 2 become visible. For example, F02’s $\text{dq}$ drops to 7.5; a 10-min B improvement then exceeds the queue, yielding $\approx 2.5$ min net flow reduction. Thus downstream capacity is a **precondition** for branch redesigns to realize their full value.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

### Redesign 1: Target B-branch queue reduction
*Rationale:* B controls 67% of cases and has the highest mean critical-path time (65 min). Its queue ranges widely (5–30 min), indicating unstable supply or resource contention.

- **Throughput:** Arrival rate is $\sim$4/hr (15-min spacing); final station can process 6/hr (2 resources $\times$ 20 min). The bottleneck is therefore branch-level, not final-station throughput. Reducing B queue/service lowers WIP and flow-time tail (F07, F12) but will not raise completed-throughput until arrival rate exceeds $\sim$6/hr.
- **Flow-time tail:** Cutting B queue by half could reduce the worst-case $\text{fj}$ by up to 15 min (F07, F12).
- **Resource utilization:** B resources appear under-utilized or poorly scheduled (high queue despite moderate service). A pilot should measure actual B-resource occupancy.
- **New bottleneck / synchronization effect:** If B is accelerated, A (fixed 50 min) becomes critical in more cases (e.g., F02: B reduced by 10 $\to$ B ends 09:15, tying A). The synchronization wait of C/A would shrink, but $\text{join}$ might not fall if A remains.
- **Pilot / measure:** Run a 2-week pilot tracking $\text{B\_start}-\text{fork\_release}$ and B-resource idle vs. busy minutes. Verify whether queue is resource-driven or administrative.

### Redesign 2: Add flexible final-station capacity or eliminate release-policy delay
*Rationale:* Downstream queue totals 176 min (mean $\sim$14.7 min/case) and masks branch gains.

- **Throughput:** A third flexible resource (F3) raises max capacity to 9/hr, accommodating future arrival growth.
- **Flow-time tail:** F10 and F11 suffer 20-min queues; reducing these by half saves 10 min each.
- **Resource utilization:** F1 and F2 currently have gaps (e.g., F1 idle 09:28–10:00). A flexible resource could absorb peaks without over-utilizing existing stations.
- **Synchronization / masking effect:** Lower $\text{dq}$ makes Counterfactuals 1 and 2 visible. Without this redesign, accelerating B or A yields near-zero flow-time improvement in most cases.
- **Risk:** The current data cannot distinguish resource queuing from a fixed release policy (see Section 7). Before investing in capital, measure whether $\text{final\_start}-\text{join}$ correlates with resource occupancy or with an administrative hold.
- **Pilot / measure:** Temporarily allow cross-assignment to a backup station for 2 weeks; compare $\text{final\_start}-\text{join}$ distributions.

    ends = {"A": row["A_end"], "B": row["B_end"], "C": row["C_end"]}
    mx = max(ends.values())
    cand = sorted([k for k, v in ends.items() if v == mx])

# 7. Limits of inference

1. **Causality:** We only observe completed-event timestamps. We cannot claim that B’s long queue *causes* the delay; the queue may reflect upstream resource contention, scheduling rules, or case-specific complexity.
2. **Branch resource capacity:** The data do not log which technicians or machines serve A, B, or C. We therefore cannot determine whether B’s queue is due to a single shared resource or independent sub-queues.
3. **Preemption / interruption:** There is no evidence of work interruption within a branch; we assume continuous service from start to end.
4. **Downstream queue vs. release policy:** $\text{final\_start}-\text{join\_ready}$ is consistent with queuing (e.g., F08 waits exactly for F06 on F2), but it could also include an administrative release hold. Without resource-occupancy logs for F1/F2, we cannot fully separate resource congestion from a fixed release delay.
5. **Sample size:** Only 12 cases on one day. Tail estimates (e.g., 90th percentile) are unstable; the counterfactual effects should be treated as point estimates with wide uncertainty.
6. **No hidden variability:** Timestamps are accurate to one minute, but inter-arrival and service variability across days is unknown. Any redesign recommendation should be piloted before full rollout.
