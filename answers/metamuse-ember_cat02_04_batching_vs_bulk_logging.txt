# 1. Cluster diagnoses

Key discrimination rules:

- Identical points are necessary for a point-equality batch, but not sufficient for physical simultaneity. The same equality is produced by a fixture cycle, a group button, a folder submission, bulk entry, or rounding.
- Adjacency is the signature of serial work, not of a batch. PM4Py can falsely convert adjacency into a batch if `merge_distance` is larger than the service interval.
- Overlap is the signature of concurrency or of a false start/end anchor. It cannot distinguish multitasking from shared identity without badge data.
- Recording lag is an independent check. G4 and G8 have second-level lags consistent with real-time scanner capture. G6 has one common recording minute for seven cases. G7 has recording times increasing by one minute per sequence number inside a rounded 15-min window.
- Uniform minute precision cannot manufacture equality across gaps of 6–27 min in G2, G3 or G6. Fifteen-minute precision can manufacture equality in G7.

# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

Definitions used here: waiting time `W = start - queue_arrival`, service time `S = complete - start`, utilisation approximated by summed service time divided by the elapsed active window for a single resource. These are point-estimate definitions only. Throughput is completions per unit time; conformance concerns whether recorded order and timing support the assumed execution semantics.

### G1 — genuine simultaneous batch

- **Waiting:** Measured waiting is 6, 5, 4 and 2 min. This is real batch-accumulation waiting if items waited for the fixture cycle. It should not be interpreted as waiting for a serial labeller.
- **Service:** Each item shows 3 min, but the fixture service is one 3-min cycle producing four items. Summing per-item service gives 12 min of apparent work in a 3-min window, i.e. 400% utilisation for a single “resource”.
- **Utilisation and bottleneck:** The correct capacity statement is four items per 3-min fixture cycle. A bottleneck at the labeller would be missed if utilisation is capped or averaged naively; the real constraint is fixture cycle capacity and upstream release timing.
- **Throughput/conformance:** Throughput is bursty by design. Conformance to a serial model would falsely flag three of the four cases as overlapping violations.

### G2 — start batching with probable sequential work

- **Waiting:** Measured waiting falls from 18 to 6 min as queue arrival gets later, but this is an artefact of fixing every start at 10:00. If the work was sequential, S04 probably started near 10:21, so its true waiting was about 27 min, not 6 min. Waiting is therefore underestimated for later items.
- **Service:** Measured service rises from 8 to 27 min. The completion gaps are only 6–7 min, suggesting a per-item processing interval of roughly that size if serial. Later service times are inflated by up to about fourfold under that interpretation.
- **Utilisation:** Summed measured service is 70 min over a 27-min window, about 259% for one tablet/user. That impossibility is itself diagnostic of a false start anchor or multitasking.
- **Bottleneck:** Surface inspection looks like a long-service bottleneck. Under the sequential interpretation, the bottleneck is real but its service time is the 6–7-min completion interval, and the queue waiting is larger than recorded.
- **Throughput/conformance:** Completions show a plausible serial throughput of one per 6–7 min. Conformance to a no-overlap rule fails only because of the group start.

### G3 — end batching/bulk submission

- **Waiting:** Starts are staggered, so waiting estimates of 22, 18, 19 and 19 min may be approximately usable if the starts are real. They remain subject to A-Clock and to whether “start” means first touch or formal case opening.
- **Service:** Measured service is 28, 20, 13 and 6 min because every completion is forced to 11:30. Early starters are inflated; only the last starter’s service could be close to a true duration. The start gaps of 7–8 min suggest a much more uniform touch time or interleaving pattern.
- **Utilisation:** Summed service is 67 min over a 28-min window, about 239% for one user. Either the user interleaved work or the completions are submission times. The folder-submission context favours the latter.
- **Bottleneck:** Release review appears to have highly variable, long service. The true bottleneck may instead be folder assembly or submission discipline at 11:30. Throughput appears as a four-case spike at 11:30 rather than flow-through.
- **Conformance:** A model expecting completion shortly after each review would be falsely violated by the common end time.

### G4 — sequential batching/serial flow

- **Waiting:** Waiting rises systematically from 7 to 19 min because arrivals are 1–2 min apart while service is 4 min. This is genuine queueing under a saturated serial resource.
- **Service:** Four minutes per case is consistent across all five cases and supported by second-level recording lag.
- **Utilisation:** Summed service is 20 min over the 20-min window from 12:05 to 12:25, i.e. about 100%. This is the control case: utilisation greater than 100% does not occur.
- **Bottleneck:** Pack item at T-D is correctly identified as the bottleneck. Additional capacity or faster service would reduce the linearly increasing waiting time.
- **Throughput/conformance:** Throughput is one completion every 4 min. No conformance correction is needed.

### G5 — shared-account artifact

- **Waiting:** Waiting to the pool is about 15–16 min for all four cases. That can be interpreted as waiting for analyst-pool capacity, but not as waiting for a named analyst.
- **Service:** Service times are 25, 15, 20 and 10 min. Summed service is 70 min over the 30-min span from 13:00 to 13:30, about 233% for one ID. If three analysts were continuously available, pool utilisation would be about 78%; that figure depends on the unverified assumption of three available analysts for the whole window.
- **Utilisation:** Per-person utilisation is unidentifiable. Pool utilisation is only bounded by the unknown number of analysts actually on shift. Four-way overlap also exceeds the stated maximum of three analysts, so at least one of multitasking, interval padding, or an understated pool size must be present.
- **Bottleneck:** The bottleneck is the analyst pool as a group, not an individual. Individual workload, handover and social-network analyses under `analyst_pool` are invalid.
- **Throughput/conformance:** Pool throughput can be counted, but assignment-level conformance cannot be checked.

### G6 — end-of-shift bulk entry: why inflated waiting and near-zero service co-occur

- **Waiting:** Measured waiting is 175, 148, 122, 110, 76, 58 and 35 min, median 110 min. Because the recorded start is 17:00 for every ticket, measured waiting is simply `17:00 - queue_arrival`. It is an upper bound on true waiting and mechanically decreases for later arrivals.
- **Service:** Measured service is zero for every ticket because start and complete are both set to 17:00. A human does not perform seven ticket closures in zero time; the zero is a logging artefact.
- **Why both occur together:** Let `A` be arrival, `s` true start, `c` true completion and `t` submission time. True waiting is `s-A` and true service is `c-s`. If the form stamps both start and complete with `t`, measured waiting becomes `t-A = (s-A) + (c-s) + (t-c)`, i.e. true waiting plus true service plus submission delay, while measured service becomes `t-t = 0`. Total measured flow time `t-A` is preserved as an upper bound, but its decomposition is shifted entirely into waiting.
- **Utilisation:** T-F appears to have 0% utilisation despite presumably doing the work earlier. Bottleneck detection is inverted: the analysis sees a huge queue and an idle resource, and may recommend capacity at 17:00 instead of investigating daytime work recording.
- **Throughput/conformance:** Throughput appears as seven completions at 17:00. Any model of daytime flow, late work or conformance to an expected ticket-closure sequence is meaningless for execution timing.

### G7 — timestamp coarsening

- **Waiting:** Measured waiting is 2, 1, 4, 7 and 9 min. Negative waiting is physically impossible under A-Clock, proving that at least the rounded start times cannot be exact. Waiting is therefore not merely noisy; the point estimates are invalid.
- **Service:** Every case shows exactly 15 min. Summed service is 75 min in a 15-min window, about 500% utilisation for one resource, which is impossible for serial manual measurement.
- **Utilisation and bottleneck:** Manual measure appears catastrophically overloaded. In reality, five measurements could have been performed sequentially inside the window, for example at roughly 3 min each, but the data cannot establish that.
- **Throughput/conformance:** All completions collapse to 14:15. The steadily increasing `recorded_at` values from 14:16 to 14:20 and increasing `sequence_no` preserve insertion order, not execution durations. Conformance analysis on point times would flag impossible overlaps and negative waits.

### G8 — queue accumulation after capacity loss

- **Waiting:** Waiting is 38–58 min. If the queue and scanner clocks are aligned, this is genuine backlog waiting: five arrivals in 8 min could not be served by a 7-min serial activity even without an outage, and the first service did not begin until 15:38.
- **Service:** Service is consistently 7 min after restart, with 3–6 sec recording lag. Post-restart utilisation is about 100%.
- **Utilisation and bottleneck:** T-H is correctly identified as unavailable before restart and saturated afterwards. However, attributing the entire backlog to the safety check requires synchronised clocks. The different-clock warning means the safety interval is contextual evidence, not a measured capacity-loss interval on the queue clock.
- **Throughput/conformance:** Throughput is zero before 15:38 in the repair clock and one per 7 min thereafter. That pattern is consistent with capacity loss followed by serial recovery, but also with an arrival burst into a serial activity.

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

## If resource IDs are trusted versus treated as shared accounts

Under the base rule that the same ID normally means the same worker:

- G2 and G3 each imply about 240–260% utilisation for one person. That forces either multitasking, multiple unrecorded workers, or false start/end anchors. The group-button and folder-submission context makes false anchors more parsimonious than unrecorded parallelism.
- G5 implies four-way concurrency for one ID. Since the pool is stated to have up to three analysts, even a shared-account reading leaves an unexplained fourth concurrent interval. Therefore G5 requires at least one additional mechanism: multitasking by one analyst, padded start/end times, or more than three analysts in practice.
- G1 is not a shared-account problem: RBT-7 is an automated cell with a stated four-place fixture, so device-level concurrency is physically expected.
- G4 and G8 remain serial under either reading because adjacency and non-overlap are consistent with one worker.

If every ID is treated as potentially shared, overlap never proves multitasking and utilisation above 100% never proves a logging artefact. That sensitivity destroys the ability to diagnose G2, G3 and G5 from intervals alone. It is therefore a robustness bound, not the preferred base case: the prompt states that shared accounts are possible and explicitly identifies only `analyst_pool` as shared. The defensible position is to trust T-B, T-C, T-D, T-F, T-G and T-H provisionally, while flagging G5 as identity-unreliable.

## If timestamps are exact versus interval-censored by stated precision

Under exact timestamps, G1, G2, G3, G6 and G7 show literal start or end equality, and G4/G8 show literal adjacency. Under interval censoring, the conclusions change in strength but not uniformly:

- For 1-sec sources G1, G4 and G8, censoring width is negligible relative to 3–7-min service times and 4–7-min spacing. Batch equality in G1 and adjacency in G4/G8 are robust.
- For 1-min sources G2, G3, G5 and G6, equality spans are 19–140 min in queue time and 6–27 min in completion spacing. A 1-min censoring interval cannot explain those equalities. The start-batch and end-batch diagnoses are robust.
- For 15-min G7, censoring is decisive. If times were floored, a displayed 14:00 start means a true start in `[14:00, 14:15)` and a displayed 14:15 complete means `[14:15, 14:30)`. If times were rounded to the nearest quarter hour, the intervals are roughly `[13:52:30, 14:07:30)` and `[14:07:30, 14:22:30)`. The prompt says the rule is undocumented and involves down/up rounding, so a conservative union is wider still. Service could range from near zero to several tens of minutes. Only the window, the ordering information in `sequence_no`, and the rising `recorded_at` are usable.
- Queue-arrival precision is not supplied. Under A-Clock and an assumed displayed-minute granularity for queue arrivals, every waiting time carries at least about a minute of censoring uncertainty, plus any unknown clock offset. That uncertainty does not affect the large G6/G8 waiting patterns, but it does affect exact medians.

A useful bound is: censored service time lies between `(earliest possible complete - latest possible start)` and `(latest possible complete - earliest possible start)`. For G7 that bound is so wide that point service time should be abandoned. For G2/G3 the bound still excludes the hypothesis that 1-min rounding created the equality.

## Cases where the same temporal pattern supports multiple physical explanations

1. **G2, start equality with staggered ends.** Story A: one operator performed a common setup at 10:00 and then inspected items concurrently or in an interleaved way. Story B: the operator inspected items sequentially, but the tablet stamped every item with the group start. The completion gaps of 6–7 min favour Story B for timing, but only direct observation or per-item start confirmation can distinguish interleaved inspection from serial inspection.
2. **G3, end equality with staggered starts.** Story A: four reviews were genuinely completed or approved together when the folder was released. Story B: reviews finished at different times and the form was submitted once at 11:30. The reported practice favours Story B, but the timestamps alone cannot distinguish a release event from a submission event.
3. **G5, four-way overlap under one ID.** Story A: three analysts worked in parallel and one also multitasked or had padded times. Story B: fewer analysts multitasked heavily. Story C: the pool size was larger than stated. Badge or VDI session data are required; interval overlap alone cannot allocate work.
4. **G7, identical 14:00–14:15 intervals.** Story A: a genuine 15-min batch measurement cycle. Story B: five sequential manual measurements inside one rounded window. The rising recorded times favour sequential recording, but they do not prove sequential execution.
5. **G8, long wait followed by a serial chain.** Story A: the safety check removed T-H from service and the queue accumulated. Story B: five arrivals in 8 min overloaded a 7-min serial activity without any capacity loss. The safety context favours Story A, but different clocks prevent a causal timing proof.
6. **G1, identical fixture intervals.** Story A: four items were physically processed together on the fixture. Story B: the PLC wrote one batch cycle start and end for four items whose individual handling differed inside the cycle. For throughput and capacity, the distinction matters less; for per-item service time, it matters greatly.

# 7. Final adjudication

**High-confidence interpretations**

- **G4:** ordinary serial flow. Waiting, service, utilisation and bottleneck conclusions can be used with only minor sub-minute uncertainty.
- **G6:** the recorded start and complete are bulk-entry artefacts. The conclusion that execution times are invalid is high confidence, even though the true work times are unknown.
- **G1:** a genuine four-item fixture batch is high confidence as an operational batch, provided analysis uses fixture capacity rather than per-item serial service.
- **G8:** serial triage after a long delay is high confidence within the scanner clock. The specific causal attribution to the safety check is only medium confidence because of the different clocks.

**Exclude from point-estimate queue analysis or interval-censor**

- **G6:** exclude start and complete from waiting, service and utilisation point estimates. Retain queue arrivals and case counts; execution time is censored at or before 17:00, and measured flow time to submission is an upper bound.
- **G7:** exclude all point waiting and service estimates; negative waiting proves invalidity. Use only the 15-min window and sequence order.
- **G2:** exclude per-item starts for S02–S04 from service and waiting estimates. The group start is usable as an anchor; completions are provisionally usable.
- **G3:** exclude completions from service and throughput timing. Starts are provisionally usable; completions are upper bounds at 11:30.
- **G5:** exclude per-person utilisation, handover and workload analysis. Pool-level waiting and throughput may be used only with an explicit unknown capacity.
- **G8:** waiting estimates should carry a clock-offset caveat; do not subtract the safety interval from waiting until clocks are synchronised.

**Require operational validation before any performance conclusion**

- G2: whether inspection was serial, interleaved or concurrent, and what the group button actually stamps.
- G3: whether 11:30 is a release, completion or submission event, and whether reviews are serial or interleaved.
- G5: the actual number of analysts on shift, their badge-level assignments, and whether one analyst multitasked to explain four-way overlap.
- G7: the legacy MES rounding rule and the true order and durations inside the 14:00–14:15 window.
- G8: the clock offset between the safety system and the repair queue/scanner, and whether T-H was actually unavailable.
- G1: whether capacity planning should use the fixture cycle as the unit of service, and whether per-item handling inside the cycle matters for quality or conformance.

Overall adjudication: only G4 supports uncorrected point estimates of waiting, service and utilisation. G1 supports batch-level estimates. G8 supports serial post-restart estimates with a clock caveat. G2, G3, G5, G6 and G7 contain logging or identity artefacts that must be censored, modelled at group/pool level, or validated operationally before they are used for bottleneck, staffing or conformance conclusions.
