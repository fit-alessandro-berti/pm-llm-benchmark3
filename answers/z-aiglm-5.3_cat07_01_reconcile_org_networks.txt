# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk — low for the mainstream, moderate-to-high for the exception path.** The mainstream is demonstrably independent of her (median unchanged in her absence). The exception path fails without her (p90 +65%, queue accumulation). The dependency is structural (single point of passage), not volumetric (~1 logged service hour/day).
- **Coordination risk — moderate.** Two parallel coordination structures exist: formal teams (Alice/Bruno  adjusters) and the exception network (Bot_Rules  Mira  specialists/adjusters), which barely intersect (4–5% co-occurrence). Routing rules for hard cases may be inconsistent between the two systems. "Request Missing Information" is fragmented across four resources of mixed seniority, including temporaries. Vendor latencies (Vendor_Verify 2.1 h, Vendor_Pay 1.3 h, both flagged as high waiting-time contributors) shape what eventually arrives at the exception desk — coordination risk extends beyond internal resources.
- **Key-person risk — high.** Singleton behavioral role, no observed backup, demonstrated outage effect, exclusive gatekeeping of specialist access. Parallel but smaller exposures exist for Omar, Priya, and each bot (Bot_Rules itself has betweenness 0.41 — the automation is the *other* single point of coordination).
- **Compliance/control risk — elevated as a *design* exposure; no evidence of misuse.** Mira combines problem detection, specialist selection, reopen authority, complaint escalation, and (in 62 observed loops) fraud-screen updates. In a regulated claims process this concentrates unreviewed discretion in one role — a segregation-of-duties concern. The log shows the *structural possibility* of uncontrolled discretion, not wrongdoing; nothing here supports a misconduct claim. Two governance questions need answers before judging: who sets exception_flag (could the flag that routes cases to her desk be influenced by her?), and is the split reopen authority between Alice and Mira intentional?
- **Workload fairness risk — moderate, and not only for Mira.** Her logged hours are light but interrupt-driven (many 4-minute switches; cognitive load is not measurable from this log, so this should not be overstated). The specialists carry the objectively hardest work (62–71% flagged, 37–51 min events). Adjuster referrals are balanced in count (350/330/290/280), but count balance does not establish difficulty balance — amount_band mix per adjuster should be checked. Temporaries handle low-exception routine work (13–14%), which is prudent risk containment but worth monitoring for development and quality.
- **Data-quality risk — material.** "Assign Specialist" is not in the official activity catalog; exception_flag semantics are ambiguous (the 19%  31% rise between Bot_Ingest and Bot_Rules is only explicable if the flag accrues during the case, not a static case attribute — this must be verified, since it changes every exception-rate interpretation); the handover edge-frequency arithmetic (Section 1) suggests a relaxed counting definition that inflates relay centralities; lifecycle:transition is unused in the reported statistics, so start/complete pairs may double-count handovers; 4100 claims received vs. 2840 paid requires handling denials/right-censoring before cycle-time claims; resource labels mix persons and roles; 12 weeks of data with a 6-day absence is a small sample with unknown seasonality.

# 5. Recommendation

**Change immediately (low-risk, reversible):**
- Appoint and *log* a named deputy for the exception desk, even before full cross-training, so that absence no longer equals outage.
- Start recording exception_flag provenance (who/what sets it, when) and make "Assign Specialist" a governed activity in the catalog.
- Instrument the queue: alert on waiting time before Mira's events and on flagged-case p90 cycle time.
- Begin codifying her actual routing decisions as an explicit shadow matrix — documentation only, no behavior change yet — and fix the lifecycle/handover counting hygiene before the next reporting cycle.

**Monitor:**
- Flagged-case queue depth and p90 cycle time, especially during any absence; deputy performance once piloted.
- Assignment balance *by case difficulty* (amount_band, case_type mix per adjuster), not just counts.
- Specialist load (Omar, Priya are singleton roles too) and vendor latency trends.
- The ratio of Bot_RulesMira referrals to automation flag rate — if flagging rises, her queue grows, and capacity planning is needed.

**Do not change until more evidence is available:**
- Do **not** remove or "de-layer" the role: the absence experiment previews the failure mode (p90 +65% on flagged cases, queue accumulation), and no replacement coordination function currently exists. Any restructuring must first specify how specialist routing, reopen handling, and complaint triage would be performed — by rule, by team leads, or by a trained second coordinator.
- Do not hard-code her routing into Bot_Rules until the discretion-vs-rules question is tested with a controlled pilot and fallback.
- Do not reorganize specialist or vendor arrangements before the cycle-time decomposition shows where exception time is actually spent.
- Do not treat the compliance concern as a disciplinary matter — the evidence shows a control-design gap, not misconduct.
- Do not interpret her working-together peripherality as evidence of dispensability; it is largely a measurement property of a scoped relay role.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira is the process's exception handler. About one in five claims needs special attention — missing documents, fraud flags, medical or legal questions, complaints — and the difficult ones mostly pass through her hands. She spends only minutes per case, deciding who should handle it, yet when she was absent for six days, difficult claims piled up and their slowest turnaround times rose by about two-thirds, while routine claims were unaffected. Network analysis shows she is the central connector for hard cases but almost never a co-worker on ordinary ones; both findings are true and describe the same role from two angles. Her expertise about where hard cases should go seems to live mainly in her head, and she currently combines problem detection, specialist selection and claim reopening — a concentration of discretion that auditors may question, though nothing indicates misuse. We recommend appointing and training a backup, documenting her routing decisions as rules, and monitoring exception queues closely. We do not recommend removing the role: the data show the process depends on it.
