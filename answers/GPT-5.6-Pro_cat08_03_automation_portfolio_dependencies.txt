# 2. Dependency and prerequisite map

The practical dependency structure is not a single serial chain. After classification, the KYC branch and financial-document branch are often concurrent even though the information system records many events sequentially. Portfolio evaluation should therefore focus on reducing the duration and variability of the **slower branch at the synchronization point**, rather than adding recorded waiting times as though every activity were serial. 

A simplified dependency map is:

`A1/A2 → {KYC branch: A3 → A4 when needed || document branch: A5 → A6} → A7 when collateral is required → A8 → Committee decision → A9 → A10 → A11`

`A12` operates across the chain as an exception, evidence, and control-resolution layer.

## Upstream data-quality dependencies

**A1 and A2 establish case identity and routing context.** Duplicate detection, product classification, and exposure classification affect almost every later control. An A1 error can combine two customers or applications; an A2 error can send a High-exposure SME through the wrong KYC, collateral, or committee route. A2 should therefore be a recommender with mandatory confirmation for high-risk or low-confidence classifications.

**A5 is an upstream enabler for A6.** Financial extraction cannot perform reliably when statements are missing, wrong-period, unreadable, or inconsistent. A5 should collect not only files but also document metadata such as statement period, entity, currency, and whether the file is consolidated or standalone.

**A6 is an upstream enabler for A8 and A9.** Committee packs and contracts should consume reconciled, versioned facts rather than copied values. Every generated value should link back to its source document and any human correction.

**A8 is not a substitute for upstream reconciliation.** Auto-assembling an incomplete pack can make the process faster while increasing committee risk. Completeness checks must distinguish “artifact present” from “artifact approved and current.”

## Legal and compliance controls

**A3 and A4 must preserve human compliance responsibility.** The system may prioritize hits, assemble evidence, suggest candidate entities, and explain similarity. It should not autonomously clear an unresolved fuzzy match merely because a model score is low.

**A4 must produce a definitive, versioned KYC status.** That status must be consumed by A9, A10, and A11. A free-text note saying “probably cleared” is not a sufficient machine prerequisite.

**A8 must enforce four-eyes separation.** For High-exposure SME cases, the person completing Credit analysis cannot also complete Committee packaging. The observed 5.8% violation rate during month-end means this cannot remain a passive dashboard warning. The workflow should block completion, require reassignment, and log any formally authorized exception.

**A9 requires two independent gates:** KYC must be clear, and generated terms must match the final Committee decision. The rule engine must use the decision version that is actually approved, not a draft or cached field.

**A10 must not send a signable contract before KYC clearance.** A provisional draft may be shared only if it is technically non-executable and clearly differentiated from a legal signing invitation.

**A11 should consume control attestations, not infer them.** Activation is safe only when the system can verify signing, KYC, decision-contract consistency, and remediation completion.

## Human escalation capacity

A3 and A4 are jointly constrained by the three compliance specialists and the observed dependency on two senior specialists. A conservative pre-classifier may improve control quality while increasing specialist demand. Deployment therefore needs:

* an explicit abstention category rather than forced classification;
* separate queues for routine mismatches and material ownership/sanctions ambiguity;
* priority rules based on exposure and deadline;
* specialist workload and queue-age monitoring;
* a fallback process when the assistant or external data source is unavailable.

A5 changes RM and customer behavior rather than simply removing back-office work. Relationship managers should see and confirm customer-facing instructions for unusual cases. Their corrections should update the checklist rules rather than becoming undocumented workarounds.

A6 changes analyst work from data entry toward validation, anomaly investigation, and credit judgment. It should reduce total normalization labor, but the remaining cases will be disproportionately difficult. Staffing plans must therefore consider complexity, not just the number of automated files.

A9 changes Legal ops from document production toward rule governance, exception approval, and complex-contract review. It may reduce average effort while concentrating legal work in the most difficult cases and around rule changes.

## External valuation dependency

A7 cannot remove the external valuation activity or guarantee a shorter external turnaround. It can:

* identify valuation requirements earlier;
* route by asset type, geography, capacity, and quality history;
* send complete information packages;
* parallelize the request with other work where legally and operationally permitted;
* escalate late valuations before they block Committee packaging.

Routing must not optimize turnaround time alone. Quality disputes, revaluation frequency, geographic coverage, partner concentration, and conflicts of interest must be monitored alongside speed. The long external wait remains a residual risk even after A7.

## Core-banking integration constraints

For the recommended portfolio, the first-six-month integration allocation should be:

1. **Deep integration slot 1: A4**, connecting the KYC case state, entity master data, evidence record, and final clearance status.
2. **Deep integration slot 2: A6**, connecting the document repository, normalized financial data, analyst corrections, and credit-analysis workbench.

A5 can initially use a lighter portal/workflow integration and should not require broad core-banking write access. A9 should begin after the first six months, first in shadow mode and then with deep contract/core integration after the upstream gates have demonstrated reliability.

This sequencing avoids consuming scarce integration capacity on A1 merely because it is easy, and avoids connecting A9 or A11 deeply before the data and control states they depend on are trustworthy.

## Quick-win portfolio

This portfolio is easiest to explain in terms of transactions automated and staff minutes removed. It provides a visible early success through A1, reduces routing and document friction through A2 and A5, and gives Legal ops a high-feasibility productivity tool through A9.

Its weakness is that it treats the most visible queue as though it were the system constraint. Back office has 12 FTE and falls below 65% utilization after 15:00. Accelerating registration can therefore release cases into KYC, document review, normalization, and credit analysis faster without increasing the capacity of the three compliance specialists or nine central credit analysts.

A9 must not be treated as straight-through contract generation until KYC and Committee-decision prerequisites are proven. Otherwise the “quick win” would automate a downstream artifact while its upstream control state remains unreliable.

## Bottleneck/synchronization portfolio

This portfolio targets the completion time of the parallel prerequisite branches feeding Committee packaging:

* A4 addresses the rare but severe KYC mismatch path.
* A5 reduces document-request loops and customer ambiguity.
* A6 reduces the normalization prerequisite and protects analyst capacity.
* A7 reduces avoidable delay around external valuation.

It is the strongest pure lead-time portfolio, especially for Equipment finance and cases with KYC ambiguity. Its main sacrifice is that contract preparation and contract remediation remain manual. A4 must be explicitly treated as the control-strengthening module: all suggestions, evidence, overrides, and final KYC decisions must be recorded, and no ambiguous result may be automatically cleared.

## Compliance-first portfolio

This portfolio concentrates spending on controls that can create asymmetric harm:

* false KYC clearance;
* unresolved entity identity;
* divergence between Committee decision and contract;
* undocumented or repeatedly handled control exceptions.

It is the strongest choice where risk appetite is very low or where regulatory remediation is the primary objective. It may not produce the largest immediate decrease in overall cycle time because missing documents, statement normalization, and external valuation remain unchanged.

It also requires additional specialist-capacity planning. A conservative A3 is likely to send more uncertain cases to A4 and the existing specialist pool before process learning and better evidence reduce that workload.

## Balanced process-and-control portfolio

This portfolio selects A4, A5, A6, and A9. It deliberately combines:

* one low-frequency, high-criticality KYC exception;
* one high-rework customer/document loop;
* one analyst-capacity and committee-prerequisite activity;
* one high-feasibility downstream module with explicit control checks.

It omits A7 in year one because the external service remains partner-dependent and segment-specific. The bank should still implement non-module measures such as earlier valuation requests, partner scorecards, complete request packages, and late-case escalation. A7 can become a year-two module if those operational measures demonstrate that routing, rather than partner capacity, is the addressable cause.

# 5. Risks and compensating controls

| automation risk                                                       | affected modules                       | compensating controls                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **False negatives allow a risky case to proceed**                     | A3, A4, A6, A9                         | Use conservative thresholds and an explicit abstain outcome; prohibit autonomous KYC clearance for ambiguous or High-exposure cases; independently sample cleared cases; require source-evidence links; provide a kill switch and rollback procedure.                                          |
| **False positives overload scarce specialists**                       | A3, A4, A2                             | Monitor escalations per case and specialist queue age; cap or rate-limit low-priority escalations; separate policy-required escalation from model uncertainty; tune thresholds against actual three-FTE capacity rather than model accuracy alone.                                             |
| **Automation bias causes staff to accept suggestions without review** | All recommendation modules             | Show evidence and uncertainty, not only a recommendation; require meaningful confirmation for material decisions; train staff that overrides are legitimate; review acceptance rates that are implausibly high or vary sharply by person.                                                      |
| **Model or data drift**                                               | A2, A3, A4, A6, A7                     | Monitor performance separately for Micro, SME, High-exposure SME, each product, document-quality bands, and month-end periods; compare current override, abstention, and error patterns with validated baselines; retrain or suspend when drift is unexplained.                                |
| **Legal-rule drift**                                                  | A9, A11                                | Assign a named Legal/Compliance owner; version every rule with effective dates and approvals; test against historical and synthetic boundary cases; prevent use of expired rules; retain the exact rule version used for every contract.                                                       |

## Portfolio-level monitoring

The steering dashboard should combine local module measures with process-level and control measures:

**Flow and synchronization**

* End-to-end median and tail cycle time by Micro, SME, High-exposure SME, and product.
* Completion-time difference between the KYC and financial-document branches.
* Waiting before Committee packaging after the last required prerequisite becomes available.
* Equipment-finance cycle time separately, because the deferred external-valuation bottleneck may dominate.

**Rework and exception paths**

* `KYC check → Resolve entity mismatch → KYC check`.
* `Financial document review → Request missing documents → Financial document review`.
* `Contract preparation → Remediate control issue → Contract preparation`.
* Number and age of automation exceptions, abstentions, and human overrides.

**Changed resource demand**

* Compliance-specialist queue age and dependency on the two senior specialists.
* Analyst time divided among normalization, validation, credit judgment, and rework.
* Back-office workload by time of day, rather than assuming released time represents scarce capacity.
* Legal-operations workload and month-end concentration.
* Committee-packaging and Customer signing queues after upstream acceleration.

**Control outcomes**

* High-exposure four-eyes violation rate, with the existing 5.8% month-end rate as the baseline to eliminate.
* Signing attempts before KYC clearance, with provisional signing and remediation separately tracked.
* Contract-decision discrepancies overall and for Credit-line renewal.
* Percentage of automated actions with complete model suggestion, human override, and final-decision records.
* False-negative, false-positive, abstention, and override outcomes by exposure and product.

# 7. Final recommendation

I recommend the **balanced process-and-control portfolio: A4, A5, A6, and A9**.

This portfolio is preferable to the pure quick-win option because it addresses both root causes and a material downstream control:

* **A4** attacks a low-frequency but high-impact KYC exception that waits 31 hours, is on the critical path in 82% of affected cases, and depends on a very small specialist pool.
* **A5** attacks the repeated missing-document loop rather than merely processing each request faster.
* **A6** removes a major analyst-capacity burden and improves the shared input needed by Credit analysis and Committee packaging.
* **A9**, deployed only after A4–A6 are stable, reduces contract effort while strengthening the requirement that contract terms match the final Committee decision.

The portfolio also produces a plausible distribution of benefits. It reduces back-office and analyst rework, protects compliance specialists from unstructured investigation, and shifts Legal ops from routine drafting toward rule ownership and complex exceptions. It does not assume that automation removes human responsibility: Compliance retains KYC accountability, analysts validate uncertain financial data, and Legal owns the contract-rule library.

**A1 should not be automated first merely because Register application has the highest frequency.** Its eight-minute service time and 1.5-hour median wait are modest relative to the 31-hour entity-mismatch wait, the 18–20-hour document/normalization waits, the 28-hour wait before Credit analysis, the 58-hour external-valuation wait, the 22-hour contract wait, and the 44-hour signing wait. Back office also has spare capacity later in the day, whereas compliance specialists and credit analysts are structurally central and constrained. Automating A1 first would therefore be likely to improve a local metric while increasing clean inflow and waiting elsewhere. 

A1 remains a reasonable later automation once downstream queues are stable, particularly if management needs a visible transaction-volume win. It should then be introduced with reversible duplicate decisions and admission controls, not used as evidence that the end-to-end process has improved.

The principal sacrifice in the recommended portfolio is deferring A7. The 58-hour valuation wait is important, especially for Equipment finance, but much of it is external and cannot be removed by an internal bot. During year one, the bank should request valuations earlier where permitted, improve request completeness, monitor partner performance, and test whether routing is genuinely the controllable cause. A7 should receive priority in the next portfolio if those measures show that better triage and routing can materially reduce the synchronization delay.

The recommendation therefore rejects both extremes: it does not automate only the most frequent clerical activity, and it does not automate the final downstream action before prerequisites are reliable. It prioritizes modules that reduce repeated work, shorten the slow branches feeding synchronization points, relieve scarce resources, and strengthen rather than bypass mandatory controls.
