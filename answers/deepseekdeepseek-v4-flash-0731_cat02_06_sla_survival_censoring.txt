# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Time origin is the `Submit` event. The SLA horizon is \(\tau = 72\) h. The outcome of interest is internal `Resolve`, but other events can happen first and remove the case from internal observation.

Estimands:

- **E1: On-time internal resolution**, \(F_R(72) = P(\text{observed internal Resolve by 72 h})\).  
  This is the cumulative incidence of `Resolve`, not a simple Kaplan-Meier survival probability, because `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are competing terminal outcomes.

- **E2: No internal Resolve by 72 h**, \(1 - F_R(72)\).  
  This is the broad “SLA-breach-like” tail probability: not reaching `Resolve` by the horizon. It includes late-resolved cases, cases that withdrew/rejected, cases transferred out, and cases still open at 72 h.

- **E3: Still active at 72 h**, \(S_A(72) = P(\text{no terminal disposition by 72 h})\).  
  Here terminal disposition means `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`. This is useful for workload and backlog estimation.

- **E4: Resolution risk after the SLA horizon**, \(P(\text{Resolve within } h \text{ hours} \mid \text{active at 72})\).  
  This is a landmark estimate for operational follow-up after a potential breach.

Example contributions:

- **S03, resolved at 76 h**:  
  E1: not counted as resolved by 72; counted as a late resolved event.  
  E2: contributes to “no internal Resolve by 72”.  
  E3: active at 72, terminal at 76.  
  E4: included in the 72-h landmark set and observed to resolve 4 h later.

- **S09, open at 92 h**:  
  E1: right-censored at 92; not resolved by 72.  
  E2: contributes to “no Resolve by 72”.  
  E3: active at 72.  
  E4: included in the 72-h landmark set, then censored at 92.

- **S06, withdrawn at 27 h**:  
  E1: contributes as a competing event before 72; not an internal Resolve.  
  E2: contributes to “no Resolve by 72”.  
  E3: not active at 72.  
  E4: not in the 72-h landmark risk set.

- **S08, transferred at 68 h**:  
  E1: competing terminal event at 68; no internal Resolve by 72.  
  E2: contributes to “no Resolve by 72”.  
  E3: not active at 72.  
  E4: not in the 72-h landmark risk set.  
  For an external-resolution estimand, S08 would instead be right-censored at 68 because the internal extract stops observing the case.

Open cases are right-censored at the cutoff age, not dropped. Already-observed breaches such as S03, S13, S28, S32, and open overdue cases such as S09, S11, S25, S26 must remain in the analysis as evidence of tail risk.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

| observed_status | Time used | Resolve event | Other-cause code | Censoring rule primary | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | `duration_or_age_h` | 1 | 0 | Not censored | If duration > 72, treat as late resolved, not as censored. |
| `rejected` | `duration_or_age_h` | 0 | 2 | Terminal competing event, not censored | Sensitivity: treat as ordinary censoring to test assumption. This is usually wrong because the case can no longer resolve. |
| `withdrawn` | `duration_or_age_h` | 0 | 2 | Terminal competing event, not censored | Sensitivity: treat as ordinary censoring. Usually not justifiable. |
| `transferred` | `duration_or_age_h` | 0 | 2 | Primary: terminal competing event for internal process | Alternatives: (1) right-censor at transfer, (2) treat transfer as external “success” at the transfer time, (3) drop transfers, (4) treat transfer as a non-resolve failure. The choice changes interpretation. |
| `open` | `duration_or_age_h` | 0 | 0 | Administrative right-censoring at cutoff | Sensitivity: censor at last observed gap time instead of cutoff, or treat stale cases as informative losses. |

For the primary competing-risk encoding, `Transfer` is not ordinary non-informative censoring if the estimand is “internal Resolve by 72”. The internal process stops observing the case at transfer, but the case has also not achieved internal `Resolve`. Three defensible transfer assumptions are:

1. **Transfer as competing terminal event**: internal process no longer can produce `Resolve`; useful for operational SLA of the inspected system.
2. **Transfer as right-censoring**: the case may resolve externally, but we do not observe it; useful for external-outcome questions, but requires unavailable external data and an independence assumption.
3. **Transfer as success at handoff**: assumes that transfer is an acceptable operational outcome at the transfer time; this is an optimistic scenario and is likely not valid without evidence about the external organization.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

If we analyze only observed resolved cases, we exclude 19 non-resolved rows: 9 open, 4 withdrawn, 3 rejected, and 3 transferred. That is a severe survivor bias.

- **Survivor bias**: S09, S11, S25, and S26 are open at 79–104 h. They are already past the 72-h horizon and unresolved. Dropping them falsely improves apparent SLA performance.
- **Late resolved cases**: S03, S13, and S28 resolve at 76, 110, and 88 h. They are not failures at cutoff, but they are SLA breaches if SLA requires resolution by 72. A resolution/no-resolution binary at cutoff would misclassify them.
- **Informative censoring**: S26 is open at 104 h with a 72-h final gap; S11 has a 55-h gap; S25 has a 48-h gap. These may be stale or neglected, not randomly missing. Treating them simply as administrative censor at cutoff can be optimistic.
- **Competing risks**: S06 withdrew at 27 h, S05 rejected at 9 h, S08 transferred at 68 h. Treating these as ordinary censor would keep them in the risk set after they can no longer reach internal `Resolve`, distorting both the survival curve and the resolution cumulative incidence.
- **Cohort imbalance**: Cohort B contains 5 P1 cases out of 17, compared with 3 P1 out of 15 in cohort A. The policy change was non-randomized and incompletely adopted, so any cohort effect is confounded with priority and case mix.
- **Open below vs above 72 h**: S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31 are open at 23–67 h and are censored before the horizon. They still contribute to early risk sets. Open cases above 72 h, such as S09 and S26, prove that a nontrivial backlog exists. Removing them biases both the numerator and denominator of tail-risk estimates.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

- **Kaplan-Meier** is appropriate only for a composite active-state endpoint such as “no terminal disposition”. It is not appropriate for `Resolve` when competing events exist, unless competing events are genuinely equivalent to censoring, which they are not for `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`.
- **Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence** is the primary estimator. It handles right censoring and competing outcomes, providing \(F_R(72)\), \(1 - F_R(72)\), and cumulative incidence of other terminal states. This directly answers the SLA estimands E1 and E2.
- **Cause-specific hazards** are useful secondarily if we want to know which operational actions reduce `Resolve` versus `Withdraw`/`Reject`/`Transfer`. With only 32 cases, a fully adjusted cause-specific Cox model would overfit.
- **Subdistribution hazards / Fine-Gray** can model the effect of covariates directly on the cumulative incidence, but with this sample size and no exact intermediate timestamps, it is not preferred as primary.
- **Restricted mean active time** up to 72 h gives the expected number of hours a case spends in the active process state, useful for capacity planning.
- **Landmarking at 72 h** is the right way to model post-SLA risk among cases still active at 72. It avoids using events after 72 as predictors before 72.
- **Multi-state model** would be ideal if exact transition times were available. The prefix gives order but not timestamps of intermediate states, so a full Markov model cannot be reliably estimated from these data.

**Primary recommendation**: Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence estimates by priority and cohort, with `Transfer` as a competing terminal event for the internal process.

**Secondary recommendation**: a 72-h landmark analysis for cases active at the horizon, plus active-state restricted mean time. These are more interpretable for workload and follow-up actions than a single binary classification.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Features derivable from prefixes:

- **Current stage**: last activity in the observed prefix, for example `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`. This is a time-varying state, not a baseline covariate.
- **Event count / prefix length**: number of activities so far, time-varying.
- **Path variant**: full observed prefix, e.g. `Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Review>Escalate>Resolve`. This is only known at the terminal event or cutoff, so it is not baseline.
- **Rework count**: final rework count is supplied. It is useful only if evaluated as a time-varying occurrence; using the final value from the start is leakage.
- **Escalation event**: `Escalate` happens after case start. Treating “ever escalated” as a baseline predictor creates immortal-time bias, because cases must survive until escalation to be labeled escalated.
- **AutoCheck presence**: `AutoCheck` occurs after `Submit`, usually very early. It is not exactly baseline because it is a process event, but it can be treated as a near-baseline path feature only if the decision point is immediately after submission.
- **Last activity gap**: `last_event_gap_h` is available for open cases. It is not a baseline covariate; it is a snapshot at cutoff. It is useful for sensitivity analyses for stale cases.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

At least three sensitivity axes are needed.

1. **Transfer treatment**
   - Primary: transfer is a competing terminal event.
   - Sensitivity 1: transfer is right-censoring, assuming external resolution is the same endpoint but unobserved.
   - Sensitivity 2: transfer is an immediate operational success at the transfer time.
   - Sensitivity 3: transfer is grouped with withdrawal/rejection as “non-resolve”.
   With only three transferred cases, conclusions can reverse. S08 transfers at 68 h and S21 at 26 h affect the 72-h estimates; S32 transfers at 96 h mainly affects later tail estimates.

2. **Censoring of stale open cases**
   - Primary: open cases are censored at their cutoff age.
   - Sensitivity: censor open cases at `duration_or_age_h - last_event_gap_h`, treating the inactive gap as loss to follow-up.
   S26, S11, S25, and S09 have large gaps. If those cases are considered effectively lost before 72 h, the evidence that they are active at 72 is weakened, and the estimated active backlog may decrease.

3. **Withdrawal/rejection as censoring**
   - Primary: `Withdraw` and `Reject` are competing terminal events.
   - Sensitivity: treat them as ordinary censoring.
   This is not operationally meaningful for internal resolution, but it demonstrates how much estimates change if the competing-risk assumption is ignored. The direction is not guaranteed because censoring keeps cases in the risk set without adding resolution events.

4. **Cohort and priority stratification**
   Because Cohort B is not randomized, any difference between A and B is associational. Small cell counts mean the estimated differences could reverse with a few changed outcomes.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Survival outputs should be translated into decisions without claiming causal effects.

- **Alerts**: For a case active at, say, 48 h, use the conditional active-state survival and process state to flag high backlog risk. The output is a probability or risk percentile, not a guaranteed outcome.
- **Workload actions**: Expected active time up to 72 h and cumulative incidence of `Withdraw`/`Reject`/`Transfer` can be used to route cases to reviewers, prioritize P1 work, and manage stale cases.
- **SLA governance**: Report \(F_R(72)\), \(1 - F_R(72)\), and active survival by priority and cohort. Separate “already observed late resolution” from “still open after 72” from “terminated non-resolve”.
- **Calibration checks**: Compare predicted risk groups with observed outcomes in rolling windows. With 32 cases, calibration is very rough; formal calibration should wait for more data.
- **Fairness checks**: Monitor calibration by priority and cohort. For example, if P1 cases are predicted to have lower resolution risk but also have more transfers, that may reflect triage behavior or case complexity, not just priority.
- **Causal caution**: The Cohort B policy change was non-randomized and incompletely adopted. Do not present a cohort survival difference as the causal effect of AutoCheck or escalation-policy change.

HORIZON = 72

def encode_case(row, transfer_mode):
    status = row["observed_status"]
    time = float(row["duration_or_age_h"])
    if status == "open":
        return time, 0, True
    if status in ("withdrawn", "rejected"):
        return time, 2, False
    if status == "resolved":
        return time, 1, False
    if status == "transferred":
        if transfer_mode == "compete":
            return time, 2, False
        elif transfer_mode == "censor":
            return time, 0, True
        elif transfer_mode == "success":
            return time, 1, False
    raise ValueError(f"unknown status {status}")

def cif_resolve_estimate(df, transfer_mode):
    encoded = df.apply(lambda r: encode_case(r, transfer_mode), axis=1, result_type="expand")
    df2 = df.copy()
    df2["time"] = encoded[0]
    df2["cause"] = encoded[1]
    df2["cens"] = encoded[2]
    df2 = df2[df2["time"] >= 0]

    survival = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in sorted(df2["time"].unique()):
        if t <= 0 or t > HORIZON:
            continue
        at_risk = df2[df2["time"] >= t]
        n = len(at_risk)
        if n == 0:
            continue
        e_resolve = int(((at_risk["time"] == t) & (at_risk["cause"] == 1) & ~at_risk["cens"]).sum())
        e_other = int(((at_risk["time"] == t) & (at_risk["cause"] == 2) & ~at_risk["cens"]).sum())
        cif += survival * e_resolve / n
        survival *= 1 - (e_resolve + e_other) / n
    return {
        "cif_resolve_72": cif,
        "no_resolve_72": 1 - cif,
        "active_72": survival,
    }

def print_summary(df, transfer_mode, label):
    est = cif_resolve_estimate(df, transfer_mode)
    print(f"[{label}] CIF Resolve<=72: {est['cif_resolve_72']:.3f}; "
          f"no internal Resolve<=72: {est['no_resolve_72']:.3f}; "
          f"active at 72: {est['active_72']:.3f}")

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The main limitation is the absence of timestamps for individual prefix events. The dataset provides only the total duration or cutoff age and the final gap, not exact transition times. Without those timestamps, time-varying state hazards, landmark states at 72 h, and multi-state transition intensities cannot be estimated precisely.

Priority additional data:

- Exact event timestamps for every transition in the prefix.
- External outcomes after each `Transfer`, or at least a transfer-agreement outcome.
- Calendar dates and cutoff dates for all cases, to distinguish administrative censoring from calendar-period effects.
- Assignment and rollout compliance data for Cohort B, including which cases actually received the automated screening and escalation changes.
- Case complexity, assignee, workload, and queue-state variables over time.
- More observations, especially P1 and transferred cases, to support subgroup analyses without overfitting.
- Event attributes such as reviewer, department, and reason for `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`.
