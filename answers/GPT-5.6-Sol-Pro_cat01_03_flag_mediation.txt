## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

Represent the policy bundle as (A=(A_D,A_P,A_M,A_E,C_M,C_E)): binary-flag display, priority-lane routing, automatic manual-review routing, escalation rule, manual capacity, and senior capacity. I define **P1** as hiding only the binary flag from workers while backend routing remains unchanged and the risk score stays visible. I define **P3** as genuinely incremental protected capacity; reserving existing capacity would be a different, potentially harmful intervention. I split **P4** into **P4a**, changing escalation criteria, and **P4b**, adding senior capacity. These distinctions follow the supplied process, in which the flag is part of a policy bundle rather than the underlying risk itself. 

Natural direct and indirect effects are not my primary framework. They require cross-world mediator assumptions that are particularly implausible here because priority changes subsequent queue state and resource assignment, cases interfere through shared queues, and manual review and escalation are selectively defined. I instead use total cluster-policy effects, operationally separable component effects, controlled direct effects only for feasible fixed regimes, and stochastic interventional indirect effects. Manual review is treated as a two-part mediator: occurrence (M_o), followed by waiting (M_w) only when (M_o=1); an absent stage is not a zero-duration stage.

Interference is intrinsic: routing one flagged case into manual review or escalation changes the queue faced by later cases, including unflagged cases, so an individual treatment effect depends on peer policy saturation. `arrival_load` is only a coarse baseline proxy; the realized queue state after triage is partly caused by prior flagged routing and therefore can be both mediator and exposure-induced confounder of later mediator–outcome relations. For example, priority changes triage completion time, which changes the manual-queue state encountered; that queue state may influence reviewer assignment, review duration, escalation, and cycle time. Human judgment and reviewer expectations may also respond to the displayed flag while using unrecorded evidence, creating treatment–confounder feedback. Conditioning naively on manual review, queue wait, escalation, or resource can therefore induce selection or block part of the policy effect. Valid analysis needs time-indexed queue exposures, peer-treatment summaries, and either a cluster/saturation intervention or a clearly stated partial-interference model.

## 3. Stage decomposition

Values are **mean clock hours**. Parentheses give the number of cases with an observed interval for that stage. Review and escalation intervals remain missing when the stage did not occur; they are not encoded as zero. `residual-to-close` is measured from the completion of the last realized stage—escalation, manual review, or triage—to `close`. Consequently, conditional stage means do not necessarily sum to the subgroup’s total-cycle mean.

Descriptively, the flag is associated with a front-loaded acceleration and a larger downstream burden. Flagged cases reach triage start about 2.24 hours sooner on average, 0.24 versus 2.48, and have slightly shorter triage service, 0.60 versus 0.95. Yet every flagged case enters manual review, versus 4/10 unflagged cases, and the conditional wait to manual review is 6.50 versus 1.88 hours. Escalation occurs in 6/10 flagged versus 2/10 unflagged cases; flagged escalated cases average 21.42 hours total, compared with 9.15 hours for flagged non-escalated cases. High-load flagged cases also wait longer for review and finish later than normal-load flagged cases. These contrasts fit a mechanism in which priority merely moves flagged cases quickly into scarce downstream queues. They are not causal estimates: flagged cases have higher scores and complexity, resources are segregated by flag, queue states are only coarsely measured, stage occurrence is selective, and each case changes other cases’ waits. The tiny subgroups make all estimates unstable.

**Limited pilot — P2.** Pilot P2 because it preserves the observed triage benefit while experimentally varying the review-occurrence mechanism. **Eligibility:** flagged, Medium-complexity cases in a prespecified narrow score band around the operational threshold, with no independent legal, safety, fraud, or senior-review trigger; exclude High-complexity cases. **Assignment:** use randomized saturation: shifts are assigned a 25% or 50% P2 saturation, then eligible cases are randomized within shift to P0 or P2, stratified by score band, arrival load, and time block. In P2, review is optional only under documented criteria; priority triage remains unchanged.

**Queue guardrail:** cap P2 saturation at 50%, do not remove capacity from unflagged work, and pause enrollment if senior-team WIP breaches its safe limit or the unflagged/control p90 review wait rises more than 20% for two consecutive blocks. **Primary outcomes:** total cycle time and a non-inferiority safety endpoint based on blinded independent decision adjudication. **Secondary outcomes:** triage completion, review occurrence and reasons, manual and escalation waits/services, escalation, reopens/overturns, complaints, resource utilization, and direct/spillover effects on unflagged cases.

**Minimum data:** score and flag timestamps, display state, exact routing rule and override reason, queue entry/exit and WIP at each decision, staffing/skills, resources, peer treatment saturation, complete stage start/end times, and adjudicated quality. **Stopping conditions:** any missed mandatory control, breach of the quality boundary, material increase in severe downstream errors or reopens, sustained queue-guardrail violation, or treatment contamination that destroys contrast.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk:** The P2 recommendation reverses toward P0 or P3 if independently audited data show that unmeasured underlying risk, beyond score and complexity, strongly drives both longer processing and material harms that manual review reliably prevents.

2. **Queue interference:** The downstream-congestion interpretation reverses if a saturation experiment shows that removing eligible reviews does not reduce manual waiting, or if flagged gains are offset by longer unflagged, senior-team, or next-shift waits.

3. **Decision quality:** P2 should be abandoned if bypassing automatic review breaches the prespecified non-inferiority boundary for false approvals, false rejections, severe reopens, regulatory errors, or expected decision harm—even when cycle time improves.

4. **Timestamp semantics:** The queue interpretation reverses if audit logs show that stage starts are retrospectively batch-entered, service intervals include off-duty periods, local clocks are inconsistent, or recorded waiting primarily represents documentation delay rather than queueing.

5. **Positivity and overlap:** A P2 effect is not supportable if essentially every flagged case in the relevant risk or complexity region has an independent mandatory-review reason, or if no comparable cases exist near the assignment threshold; a capacity intervention would then be more defensible.

6. **Reviewer behavior change:** P1 or P2 conclusions reverse if workers infer hidden status, escalate defensively when the mandate is removed, delay optional cases, or shift substantive review into unrecorded activities, thereby recreating the same workload or worsening quality through another pathway.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed.** In these 20 cases, flagged cases begin triage much sooner, with a mean wait of 0.24 versus 2.48 hours, receive slightly shorter triage service, and then spend much longer before manual review. All flagged cases are reviewed, 60% are escalated, and their mean cycle time is 16.51 hours versus 8.17 for unflagged cases. High complexity, escalation, high arrival load, and particular resources coincide with longer durations. 

**Plausible mechanism.** The policy bundle appears to pull flagged work rapidly through triage and deposit it into shared, scarcer review and senior queues. Automatic review occurrence increases workload; queue waiting, rather than review service alone, contributes materially. Escalation adds another long wait. Visible labels may also alter caution and resource assignment.

**Unidentified.** The data cannot separate true risk and complexity from the flag’s effects, distinguish display salience from automated routing, or identify natural direct and indirect effects. Queue interference violates independent-case reasoning, mediator–outcome confounders are treatment-induced, resources have little overlap across flag groups, and approve/reject is not a correctness label. Timestamp semantics and the small sample further limit inference.

**Next evidence.** A guarded randomized P2 pilot in borderline, Medium-complexity flagged cases is most informative: preserve priority triage, randomize removal of automatic review, record exact queue states and peer exposure, and require independent outcome adjudication. It directly tests whether review occurrence drives delay while monitoring quality and spillovers. P3 is the safer operational fallback when review is mandatory, but it learns less about whether the review mandate itself is necessary.
