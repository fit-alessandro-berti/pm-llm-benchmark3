# 1. Data audit and estimands

The data support several descriptive operating regimes, but do not support one stationary queueing model or a validated estimate of mean waiting time. Two audit findings particularly limit interpretation: the reported morning-peak percentages disagree with the hourly vectors, and later WIP values are incompatible with a conserved queue under the assumptions stated below.

Use the following definitions:

- **Arrival intensity:** observed entries divided by opening hours, in cases/open hour. A day contributes 12 hours, from 07:00 to 19:00; an hourly bin is interpreted as a half-open interval, such as [08:00, 09:00).
- **Completion intensity:** observed completions divided by opening hours, also in cases/open hour. This is realized output, not a server's service rate or maximum capacity.
- **Service time, S:** active handling minutes per case, excluding waiting. Only daily medians and p90s are supplied; neither the mean nor the full distribution is identified. The service-statistic cohort and sample sizes are unspecified.
- **WIP, L:** cases in the system, including waiting and handling. A daily snapshot mean approximates the opening-hours time average if snapshots cover the day uniformly. Across equal-length days, the average of daily WIP means has the same weighting.
- **Capacity, m:** mean concurrently available FTE. Nominal staff-hours are 12m per day. FTE is not necessarily an integer number of interchangeable servers.
- **Flow time, T:** elapsed time from entry to exit, including waiting and handling. Calendar flow time includes overnight closure; opening-time flow time counts only intervals when the center is open. Neither is directly observed.
- **Recorded imbalance:** A−C. It measures a backlog change only if entries, exits, and the WIP population have compatible boundaries and all population changes are captured.

**Assumption A1, used only for a conservation audit:** arrivals are all entries, completions are all exits, there are no transfers, cancellations, or other population changes, including overnight, and the initial backlog is nonnegative. This assumption is needed to translate counts into a queue trajectory; the prompt does not establish all its conditions.

Under A1, the cumulative recorded imbalance is +88 through April 24 and +110 through May 1. Therefore, opening backlog on April 25 is at least 88. With only 43 completions that day, WIP cannot fall below 45 at any instant, even if every completion precedes every arrival. The supplied mean is 23. The same calculation gives:

| Date | Conditional opening-backlog lower bound | Daily completions | Conditional WIP lower bound throughout day | Reported mean WIP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| April 25 | 88 | 43 | 45 | 23 |
| April 26 | 96 | 39 | 57 | 20 |
| May 2 | 110 | 51 | 59 | 22 |
| May 3 | 112 | 47 | 65 | 19 |

These are **conditional inconsistencies**, not estimates of the actual backlog. A positive initial backlog makes the bounds larger. Ordinary snapshot sampling cannot explain a mean below a bound that holds throughout the day. At least one A1 condition or measurement definition must fail. Possible explanations include unrecorded exits, transfers, a WIP definition that excludes retained cases, or mismatched reporting populations; none is observed here.

The hourly vectors all reproduce their daily arrival and completion totals. However, bins 08–09 and 09–10 give different morning shares:

| Representative date | Reported 08:00–09:59 share | Share calculated from hourly arrivals |
|---|---:|---:|
| April 7 | 31% | 17/101 = 16.8% |
| April 15 | 32% | 19/105 = 18.1% |
| April 21 | 47% | 11/115 = 9.6% |
| April 25 | 22% | 6/51 = 11.8% |

The differences exceed rounding error. A timing or reporting-definition discrepancy is a hypothesis, not an established correction. If the vectors are illustrative profiles rather than actual records for those dates, that distinction also needs clarification. Analyze each source separately until reconciled.

Other threats to naive inference are:

1. **Calendar composition:** weekends have fewer arrivals and substantially fewer staff; pooled rates describe neither schedule well.
2. **Cohort mismatch and censoring:** completed cases can be earlier arrivals. Arrival-based complexity percentages need not describe cases whose service times were recorded that day.
3. **Quantile aggregation:** daily medians cannot be averaged into a pooled case median; daily p90s cannot recover the mean, variance, or pooled p90. The upper tail prevents a finite upper bound on mean service from these quantiles alone.
4. **Intraday heterogeneity:** equal daily totals can conceal isolated arrival bursts, smooth demand, or several hours of blocked output.
5. **Carry-over and dependence:** missing opening/closing backlogs prevent independent daily queue reconstructions. The same backlog can influence several days.
6. **Confounding:** staffing reacts to congestion; case mix, arrival timing, and the intervention change together. Associations do not identify staffing effects.
7. **Measurement changes:** the complex-variant increase might reflect route changes, coding changes, or both. A variant label alone does not establish added staff effort.
8. **Small, selected samples:** some cells contain one or two days, and four representative profiles are not random replication. IID confidence intervals or fitted M/M/c parameters would be unjustified.

Rates below are empirical estimates over stated windows. Service ranges are observed ranges of **daily quantiles**. Weighted complexity and reported-peak percentages use arrivals as weights and remain approximate because the input percentages are rounded.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A serious alternative uses **contiguous queue episodes**, with weekday and time of day represented as periodic covariates rather than independent regimes. It emphasizes a persistent queue state across overnight closures and weekends.

| Episode | Dates | Days | Arrivals/open hour | Completions/open hour | Reported mean WIP | Recorded A−C | Operational story |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Reference episode | April 6–14 | 9 | 7.23 | 7.19 | 18.7 | +5 | Approximately balanced periodic operation |
| Disruption and incomplete recovery | April 15–19 | 5 | 6.72 | 6.43 | 32.6 | +17 | One shock episode extending through the weekend |
| Pressure accumulation | April 20–28 | 9 | 8.26 | 7.28 | 39.2 | +106 | Persistent positive imbalance under high recorded complexity |
| Intervention and drainage candidate | April 29–May 3 | 5 | 7.38 | 7.68 | 30.4 | −18 | Transition followed by excess completions, with a weekend schedule |

This segmentation avoids implying that Saturday begins a new queue with no inherited cases. It also treats April 18–19 as potentially affected by recovery, rather than assigning them to a clean reference regime. Its weakness is that episode averages mix very different staffing and arrival schedules. They require explicit weekday/hour effects and cannot themselves be stationary queue parameters. April 29 also remains partially exposed to the intervention.

The primary segmentation better describes staffing schedules and comparisons within recorded complexity levels, but creates sparse, sometimes noncontiguous cells and can obscure queue memory. The alternative preserves chronology and recovery context, but sacrifices a direct interpretation of each episode's average rate. Neither resolves the WIP audit problem.

With raw events, a further state-based model could distinguish blocked service, net accumulation, and backlog drainage at 15-minute resolution, while allowing complexity and capacity to vary. That would represent congestion state rather than a permanent calendar regime. The present data provide neither reliable absolute backlog states nor enough intraday replication to identify that model; zero initial backlog or fixed backlog thresholds should not be invented.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

For equal-duration count bins x, define the descriptive **Fano factor** as sample variance s² divided by the sample mean, and **count CV** as s divided by that mean. Calculations use sample variance with denominator n−1. The peak/mean ratio provides a third measure. Count CV is not the CV of interarrival times and should not be inserted into a queueing approximation requiring interarrival variability.

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| April 7 | 8.42 | 3.23 | 0.62 | 2.38 | +0.68 |
| April 15 | 8.75 | 4.14 | 0.69 | 2.51 | +0.71 |
| April 21 | 9.58 | 10.12 | 1.03 | 3.55 | −0.14 |
| April 25 | 4.25 | 1.29 | 0.55 | 1.88 | +0.80 |

The ordinary candidate has a broad daily peak rather than uniform arrivals. The post-mix candidate has isolated bursts: 34 arrivals at 10:00–11:00, 24 at 13:00–14:00, and 14 at 16:00–17:00. Those three hours contain 72/115, or 62.6%, of daily arrivals. At 10:00–11:00, arrivals exceed completions by 21; at 13:00–14:00, by 12. The completion vector is smoother, with Fano about 0.98 and CV 0.34, while the daily recorded imbalance is +14. Smoother departures can coexist with substantial accumulation.

On April 15, zero completions in the 11:00–14:00 bins accompany 37 arrivals. Cumulative A−C reaches +55 relative to the opening state and ends at +47. These are increments, not absolute queue lengths. Even April 7 requires an opening backlog of at least three cases under A1 because early cumulative completions exceed arrivals by three. Empty opening queues cannot be assumed.

Hourly Fano factors above one are not by themselves evidence against an inhomogeneous Poisson process. Variation across clock hours combines stochastic variation with different expected intensities. To test additional overdispersion, compare replicated counts within the same hour, weekday, and regime, or fit time-varying intensities and inspect residual dispersion. The adjacent-bin correlations above primarily describe one profile's shape; April 21's isolated spikes produce a slightly negative adjacent correlation despite its high dispersion.

Daily arrival-count Fano is 10.03 when all 28 days are pooled, but 0.66 for weekdays and 0.73 for weekends. It falls to 0.26 and 0.29 for reference and pre-intervention high-mix weekdays. Their count CVs are both about 0.05, versus 0.33 pooled. This demonstrates how calendar and regime mixing create apparent overdispersion. The small within-cell values do not prove Poisson arrivals, stochastic underdispersion, or constant intraday demand.

On the complete chronological daily sequence, descriptive lag-one correlations are 0.41 for arrivals, 0.61 for WIP, and 0.93 for recorded complexity. Arrival lag-seven correlation is 0.99, and staffing lag-seven correlation 0.94. Weekly schedules and persistent level shifts account for much of these associations. Only 21 lag-seven pairs exist; these are neither independent replications nor validated autoregressive coefficients. Removing weekdays or regime gaps changes the time spacing, so filtered-row autocorrelation must not be called a daily lag.

Three candidate changes have different interpretations:

- April 15 is a logged capacity disruption, with an approximate intraday boundary supplied by the hourly zeros.
- April 20 begins a sustained recorded-complexity level change: all earlier percentages are 18–27%, and all later percentages are 39–47%. Actual routing changes and coding changes remain competing explanations.
- April 29 begins a recorded staffing increase and a reversal of completion imbalance, but its intraday implementation time and causal effect are unidentified.

The code illustrates a heuristic: confirm a mix candidate after two dates at or above 35%, and a high-mix staffing candidate after two successive weekdays whose FTE exceeds the same weekday one week earlier by at least 0.75. It flags April 20 and April 29, respectively, after next-day confirmation. Thresholds exploit the visible contrasts and are descriptive, not significance tests or independent validation.

Regime labels describe operating conditions, not guaranteed stationary queue states. For example, high-mix weekday WIP rises from 37 on April 20 to 50 on April 28, with positive recorded imbalance throughout the interval. The post-intervention observations still show drainage and have too short a follow-up to demonstrate equilibrium. Four selected hourly profiles cannot establish a Poisson/Cox/renewal arrival model, a service distribution, independence, scheduling discipline, or stable resource pooling. Four weeks likewise provide little basis for reliable dependence-adjusted uncertainty intervals.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law relates compatible long-run averages, L=λE[T]. It does not require Poisson arrivals, exponential service, or FCFS. Strict stochastic stationarity is not necessary for its sample-path foundations, but the desired mean must refer to the same population and clock, and finite-window boundary contributions must be controlled. A stable periodic system can satisfy the relationship over complete cycles; isolated weekday/weekend cells need not form separate conserved queues.

For a window of H opening hours, the observed area is approximately Q=Σd 12Ld case-hours. Dividing Q by its arrival or completion count gives an **implied opening-time residence**, in hours/case. Multiplying by 60 converts it to opening minutes. These ratios approximate mean flow only when throughput is representative, boundary censoring is small, and WIP counts the same population.

The exact finite-window area counts the portion of each case's residence inside the window, including initially present and unfinished cases. It is not automatically the sum of complete durations for cases arriving or finishing inside the window. Thus even A=C does not eliminate censoring bias or demonstrate stationarity.

# 7. Operational implications

**Reconcile the population and clock first.** Confirm whether WIP includes all carried-over cases, and account explicitly for transfers, cancellations, archiving, and other exits. Reconstruct opening and closing balances for each day. Align the peak percentage definition with clock bins before making a permanent morning staffing decision. The audits identify concrete incompatibilities, not merely missing precision.

**Use capacity plans conditional on workload and schedule.** High-mix weekdays show increased input and service quantiles at essentially unchanged pre-intervention FTE. Retain the temporary uplift provisionally while measuring total staff effort by actual variant, resource, and hour; use those means and observed variability to estimate offered workload. The observed post-intervention improvement supports follow-up, but does not establish that 7 FTE, or any other fixed level, achieves a desired waiting-time target. Weekends require separate coverage: low absolute demand is accompanied by low staffing and similar reference demand per FTE-hour.

**Manage recovery as a distinct objective.** Record backlog size, age, urgency, and remaining work. Set a recovery target using expected arrivals plus the desired backlog reduction over a chosen horizon, subject to skills and available staff effort. The observed extra 6–7 completions/day on April 30–May 1 is a short-run drainage signal, not a sustainable service-rate estimate. April 29–May 3 offsets only 18 of the preceding episode's 106 recorded excess arrivals; these counts cannot establish actual clearance given the WIP inconsistencies. Restore outage resilience and log exact outage and intervention times so lost capacity and recovery exposure can be distinguished.

**Smooth confirmed bursts and place flexible cover around them.** The vectors suggest testing staggered intake, appointment slots, or temporary skilled cover for 10:00–11:00 and 13:00–14:00 bursts, with monitoring before permanent roster changes. Do not infer the same pattern for every high-mix day. Verify the conflicting reported 08:00–10:00 peak measure. Prioritization and separate handling of complex work should also be checked for downstream bottlenecks and unfair delay to other cases.

**Avoid policies that look attractive only after pooling.** A flat roster near the pooled 5.14 FTE and a single 7.50/hour demand forecast may appear adequate, but would reduce coverage during weekdays with roughly 9–9.5 arrivals/hour and longer high-mix handling. Likewise, apparent low weekend WIP is not a sound basis for shifting recovery work to weekends with 2.5–3.0 FTE. Treating the recovery rate near 10 completions/hour as normal capacity could encourage staffing cuts while older cases are still being drained. The pooled positive FTE/WIP correlation provides no causal justification for such cuts.

Collect persistent case IDs; true external entry and final exit; activity start/complete and pause/resume times; resource and skill assignments; actual routing, rework, and handoffs; timestamped staffing and outages; coding-version changes; and all non-completion population movements. Preserve cases across midnight, and obtain opening/closing backlogs plus finer WIP measurements around peaks. Process mining can then compare actual complex variants and conformance, separate handling from inter-activity waiting, and locate bottlenecks rather than infer them from a single percentage.

Evaluate the intervention over additional weekly cycles, matching weekday, hour, verified variant, and opening backlog. Use dependence-aware comparisons and report uncertainty rather than treating 28 rows as independent cases. A contemporaneous comparator or prospective operational trial would strengthen causal evidence if available; neither is supplied here.

def primary_regime(row):
    date, weekend = row["date"], row["weekend"]
    if date == pd.Timestamp("2026-04-15"):
        return "outage"
    if pd.Timestamp("2026-04-16") <= date <= pd.Timestamp("2026-04-17"):
        return "recovery"
    if date == pd.Timestamp("2026-04-29"):
        return "staff_transition"
    if date >= pd.Timestamp("2026-04-30"):
        return "post_weekend" if weekend else "post_weekday"
    if date >= pd.Timestamp("2026-04-20"):

def summary(label, frame):
    hours = frame["open_h"].sum()
    staff_hours = (frame["open_h"] * frame["staff_fte"]).sum()
    a, c = frame["arrivals"].sum(), frame["completed"].sum()
    return {
        "window": label, "days": len(frame), "A": a, "C": c,
        "A/open_h": a / hours, "C/open_h": c / hours,
        "L": np.average(frame["mean_wip"], weights=frame["open_h"]),
        "FTE": np.average(frame["staff_fte"], weights=frame["open_h"]),
        "complex_Aw_pct": np.average(frame["complex_pct"],
                                      weights=frame["arrivals"]),
        "peak_Aw_reported_pct": np.average(frame["peak_08_10_pct"],
                                            weights=frame["arrivals"]),
        "daily_median_range": (f"{frame['median_service_min'].min()}-"
                                f"{frame['median_service_min'].max()}"),
        "daily_p90_range": (f"{frame['p90_service_min'].min()}-"
                             f"{frame['p90_service_min'].max()}"),
        "net_A-C": a - c,
        "A/FTE_h": a / staff_hours, "C/FTE_h": c / staff_hours,
        # Conditional on one FTE providing 60 staff-minutes per hour,
        # and handling time representing total staff effort per case:
        "critical_mean_service_min": 60 * staff_hours / a,
    }

def dispersion(values):
    values = pd.Series(values, dtype=float)
    mean = values.mean()
    variance = values.var(ddof=1) if len(values) >= 2 else np.nan
    return {"n": len(values), "mean": mean,
            "Fano": variance / mean if mean > 0 else np.nan,
            "CV_count": np.sqrt(variance) / mean if mean > 0 else np.nan,
            "max/mean": values.max() / mean if mean > 0 else np.nan}

def confirmed_run_start(flag):
    flag = flag.fillna(False).astype(bool)
    return flag & ~flag.shift(1, fill_value=False) & flag.shift(-1, fill_value=False)
