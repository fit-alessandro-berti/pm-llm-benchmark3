# 1. Stress scenarios

Historical averages are not a safe design basis. Normal volume is 2,300 cases/week versus 3,300/week in peak, but peak harm comes from the *combination* of +43% volume, a worse mix, more loops, and queues over 12h rising from 22% to 54% of cases. Design for tails — Urgent p90 33h against an 18h penalty threshold and Routine p90 120h against a 72h target in peak — not medians.

| Scenario | Definition | Why it is plausible from the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **S1: Winter mix shift** | 3+ weeks at peak volume; Urgent 18%29%+, Complex-panel 13%20%+, Routine falls as share but rises in absolute WIP. Rework drifts 7.5%11.8%. | Exactly the observed normalpeak change. Complex-panel cases are 3.1x more likely to need Medical validation and 2.4x more likely to need Specialist review, so mix shift loads the scarcest downstream resources disproportionately. |
| **S2: Assay degradation / M2 outage** | M2 failure rate rises or M2 is down 1–3 days; effective assay capacity falls 30–50% while Repeat assay demand rises. No two scenarios stress the same first bottleneck, and they compound: S4 worsens S5, S5 consumes capacity needed in S1, S2's repeats consume capacity needed by everyone.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

## S1 — Mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel
1. **First bottleneck: Pre-analytical check and Preparation.** Utilisation 96%/89% in peak; `Pre-analytical -> Test preparation` degrades 4h13h. More Complex-panel work means more preparation complexity per case.
2. **Migration to Assay.** Preparation releases in batches; `Test preparation -> Batch wait` cases rise from 760 to 1,180 and `Batch wait -> Assay run` from 9h22h. Urgent bypass of Batch wait falls from 74% to 58% in peak — the bypass mechanism itself saturates, fragmenting machine schedules without protecting Urgent cases.
3. **Migration to validation.** Technical validators exceed capacity (101%). Because the mix is richer, `Technical validation -> Medical validation` rises from ~25% of technical-validation exits normally to ~42% in peak, hitting Medical validators at 93% utilisation with a 22h queue, plus Specialist hold at 36h queue / 48h edge time.
4. **Feedback:** Queues age samples, rework rises to 11.8%, and Repeat-after-failure cases (median 145h) re-enter Assay, stealing capacity from first-time cases. Relieving Assay alone (e.g. bigger batches, I2) simply delivers more work faster into a saturated Technical/Medical validation chain — cycle time moves, it does not fall.
5. **Priority trap:** Strict Urgent-first (I1) protects 29% of cases by delaying the other 71%; Routine starvation inflates WIP, and Complex-panel cases — which cannot bypass Medical validation — wait behind repeated Urgent pre-emptions at Assay, then hit the same Medical queue anyway.

## S2 — Assay degradation / M2 outage
1. **First bottleneck: Assay machines.** At 98% peak utilisation there is no slack; losing M2 or slowing it queues everything at `Batch wait -> Assay run` (already 22h).
2. **Migration upstream:** Preparation benches block because they cannot release into a full assay queue; Batch wait becomes the de facto buffer and batching discipline collapses into ad-hoc expediting.
3. **Migration downstream, then back:** When the machine recovers, a bolus hits Technical validation (already 101%). Meanwhile hasty reruns raise `Repeat assay -> Assay run` (10h26h) loops; Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice, so the outage effectively lasts longer than the repair.
4. **Intervention trap:** Dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex and all Routine to M2 (I3) is precisely wrong here — it concentrates all Routine volume on the least reliable machine, raising repeats, which then re-enter and displace the critical work I3 was meant to protect.

## S3 — Medical validator / specialist absence
1. **First bottleneck: Specialist review and Medical validation.** `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation` is 18h normal  48h peak; with the specialist absent this edge effectively stops. Medical queue (22h median) grows even though Medical utilisation is "only" 93% — queueing is driven by variability and the upstream Hold dependency.
2. **Migration upstream:** Technical validators complete work that cannot be released; WIP accumulates between Technical and Medical validation. Technicians may slow or re-sequence toward directly-releasable cases (Technical -> Release is 11,700 normally but only 4,050 in peak versus 3,420+610 routed onward), distorting case order.
3. **Migration to Assay by feedback:** Held Complex-panel cases age; clinicians chase results; some orders are re-registered or repeated, adding duplicate assay demand.
4. **Intervention trap:** Adding assay or preparation capacity in this scenario is actively harmful — it manufactures more unreleasable WIP. Conversely, outsourcing (I10) only helps if the partner absorbs specialist review too; otherwise cases return to the same absent specialist after a 12h transport delay.

## S4 — Courier compression / delay
1. **First bottleneck: Courier intake and Registration at spike times.** Average utilisation (88%/85%) hides the 10:00/17:00 spikes; queues form in bursts and registration overtime raises error rates after 9h shifts.
2. **Migration to Pre-analytical check and Accession correction.** Rushed, poorly documented arrivals raise Manual accession correction demand on only 2 specialists (91% utilisation, 14h peak queue) and raise recollection requests. `Manual accession correction -> Test preparation` degrades 5h16h.
3. **Feedback loop:** Each recollection costs a 60h peak return leg plus clerk time explaining errors to physicians, reducing the intake capacity that caused the problem. Cancellations after recollection rise in peak (370 vs 290 requested-then-cancelled, on half the volume).
4. **Intervention trap:** Automating registration (I8) without defect triage accelerates bad-metadata orders into Pre-analytical check and Assay — the bottleneck moves downstream and rework loops multiply.

# 3. Robust redesign package

These interventions help in several scenarios and do not depend on correctly forecasting which scenario occurs. None is "add capacity everywhere"; each is paired with the downstream load it creates.

**R1. Early pre-analytical defect triage within 2h (I6), with a protected correction path.**
Reject or flag poor samples at receipt rather than after full check. Every avoided late recollection saves a 42–60h loop and the clerk explanation time that erodes intake. *Downstream load:* faster, cleaner flow arrives at Preparation/Assay sooner — acceptable only combined with R3's release discipline so Assay is not flooded in bursts. *Assumption:* defects are detectable early; *failure mode:* over-rejection raises recollection/cancellation, so rejection criteria need weekly audit against downstream failure data.

**R2. Courier smoothing (I7): staggered pickup slots, cut-off discipline, and arrival-time visibility to Registration.**
Cheap, helps S1/S4/S5, reduces the spike-driven overtimeerrorcorrection chain. *Downstream load:* smoother arrivals raise *average* utilisation of Pre-analytical check even as peaks fall — monitor that the queue does not simply become constant; pair with R1 so smoother arrivals are also cleaner. *Failure mode:* referrers may not comply; keep two anchor slots but add one midday slot rather than redesigning all routes at once.

**R3. Capped-priority dispatch instead of strict Urgent-first (reject I1, modify I2).**
Urgent cases get priority but with a cap — e.g. at most ~50–60% of preparation/assay starts in any rolling 4h window may be priority pre-emptions — and a guaranteed minimum flow for Complex-panel (whose real constraint is downstream validation, so early assay completion has value only if validation is booked) and Routine. Increase Routine batch size toward 24 (I2) *only* with a maximum batch-wait cap (e.g. release a partial batch after 6h) so efficiency does not convert into Routine starvation. *Downstream load:* steadier, less fragmented machine schedules reduce setup losses and repeats for everyone; the risk is Technical validation receiving a smoother but higher sustained flow — mitigate by sequencing batch release against validator availability (see R5). I9 (holding Routine releases for batch reports) is rejected: it adds waiting to the case type already breaching its 72h target at p90 120h.

**R4. Flexible, ring-fenced accession-correction capacity (modify I4).**
Cross-train two technical validators for accession correction, but as a *surge pool with a floor*: they move only when correction queue exceeds a trigger (Section 4) and at least minimum technical-validation staffing is preserved. This addresses the 2-specialist fragility (14h peak queue unblocks many cases). *Downstream load / risk:* the known trap — draining Technical validation, already at 101% in peak. The floor and trigger are what make this robust rather than bottleneck-shuffling. *Assumption:* 4-week cross-training lead time, so training must happen *before* winter, not during it.

**R5. Validation-aware release sequencing and protected Medical/Specialist capacity.**
Reserve daily medical-validation slots proportional to the Complex-panel/Urgent share of cases *entering* Technical validation (leading signal), and publish a specialist-review schedule that covers the every-second-Friday gap with a named deputy or pre-booked partner cover. Modest, targeted capacity at the true scarce resources — not everywhere. *Downstream load:* faster Medical validation increases Release workload slightly; Release itself shows no queue problem in the data, so this is the safest downstream in the chain. *Failure mode:* reserved slots waste capacity in normal mix — scale the reserve with mix, don't fix it.

**R6. Registration automation for complete-metadata orders only (I8, gated by R1).**
Automate the clean majority, keep human registration for incomplete orders, and never let automation bypass defect triage. This cuts clerk load without the S4 trap of feeding defects downstream faster. *Downstream load:* Pre-analytical check sees volume sooner; R1+R2 are prerequisites, and automation go-live should be staged outside peak. *Assumption:* metadata completeness is honestly measurable at order entry.

**R7. Flexible machine assignment instead of rigid dedication (reject I3 as stated).**
Keep both machines pooled, but apply *conditional* protection: when M2's repeat rate rises, shift complex/first-time-critical work toward M1 and give M2 simpler, repeat-tolerant work with extra QC — the opposite rigidity of I3. Reduces the Repeat-after-failure clustering after M2/night shifts. *Downstream load:* M1 queue must be watched; pooling preserves an escape route that dedication removes.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **Single-specialist dependency remains.** R5 mitigates with deputy/partner cover, but if the specialist and deputy are simultaneously unavailable and the partner cannot supply equivalent review, Hold cases (48h peak edge) stop entirely. There is no internal fix at acceptable cost; this is accepted fragility with a contractual, not operational, mitigation.
2. **Technical validation has no true slack.** At 101% peak utilisation it is the system's shock absorber of last resort, and R4 deliberately borrows from it. If S1 and S5 coincide (heavy mix *and* correction surge), the ring-fence floor forces a choice between correction queues and validation queues — the redesign makes the trade-off explicit and triggered, but cannot abolish it.
3. **Redesign-caused vulnerability — triage over-rejection and automation bias.** R1+R6 create a new failure mode: borderline samples rejected early, or referrers learning to game metadata completeness, could raise recollections/cancellations or push defects past a triage step staffed for speed. The system becomes more dependent on triage quality than the old process was on the full pre-analytical check; audit of rejection accuracy is a permanent new control cost, and a triage staffing absence becomes a new single point of failure at intake.
4. **Redesign-caused vulnerability — priority-cap gaming and WIP ageing.** Any explicit cap (R3) invites referrers to over-label orders Urgent. Urgent share creep would silently consume the protected capacity and recreate I1's starvation dynamics inside the new rules. Urgency-label auditing is required.
5. **Outsourcing traceability (P3).** When activated, split custody of Complex-panel samples adds transport delay, sample-integrity and result-matching risk; used too long it also erodes internal specialist caseload and skills.
6. **M2 reliability is managed, not fixed.** R7/P2 route around M2; a simultaneous M1 failure still collapses assay capacity. No redesign here survives a two-machine outage — contingency is demand-side (deferral communication), not capacity.
7. **Behavioural:** batch caps and WIP limits depend on supervisors not expediting informally under pressure; the peak data (Urgent bypass falling to 58%) suggest informal expediting already occurs and will erode formal rules.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Reviewed daily in peak; leading indicators drive the Section 4 triggers, lagging indicators only confirm outcomes.

**Leading indicators (act on these)**

| Indicator | Warning band | Critical / trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Arrivals by case type, rolling 3 days — Urgent+Complex share | >35% | >40%  P1 |
| Arrivals per courier slot vs plan | >115% | >130%  P4 |
| Queue *age* (oldest case + median) at Pre-analytical, Preparation, Assay, Technical validation, Medical validation, Hold — by case type | Any median >8h | Assay >12h; Medical >12h; Correction >8h  P2/P3/P5 |
| `Assay run -> Repeat assay` rate, rolling 24h, split by machine and shift | >8% | >10%  P2 |
| Recollection request rate (% of receipts, rolling 3 days) and correction queue | >6% | >8%  tighten triage; deploy P5 |
| Medical/Hold inflow forecast: Complex+Urgent cases entering Technical validation today (tomorrow's Medical demand) | Rising 2 days | Hold queue >10 cases  P3 pre-activation |
| Specialist/medical-validator roster cover, next 7 days | Any uncovered day | Absence + queue >12h  P3/I5/I10 |
| Urgency-label share trend (gaming check) | Urgent share +3 pts/week without case-mix explanation | Audit referrers; review R3 cap |
| Registration overtime hours and auto-registration defect rate | Shifts >9h; auto defect rate > manual | Cap overtime (P4); suspend automation expansion |

**Lagging outcome metrics (review weekly, do not manage by these)**

- Median and p90 cycle time by case type vs targets (Urgent 18h penalty line, Routine 72h) — p90 mandatory, medians alone hid the peak failure (Urgent median 17h "acceptable", p90 33h).
- Overall rework rate (7.5% normal vs 11.8% peak baseline) and Repeat-after-failure cycle time.
- % cases with any queue >12h (22% normal vs 54% peak baseline).
- Cancellations after recollection, penalty payments, outsourcing turnaround.

The governing principle: queues, mix, repeat rates, and roster cover move 24–72h before cycle-time outcomes do. Manage the redesign on the leading panel; use the lagging panel only to verify that bottleneck relief is real rather than migrated.
