# 1. Executive memo

**Recommendation: Alternative E.** Do not scale full Redesign R nationally and do not roll it back wholesale. Separate the components that work from those creating tail risk and downstream harm.

Redesign R improved the center of the distribution: median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, cases with 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%, and visible cost fell from EUR184 to EUR161. Simple low-risk houses improved strongly: median -6.0 days, p90 -8.0 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints -1pp.

But the pilot worsened reliability and downstream customer outcomes: p90 rose from 31.0 to 38.5 days, p95 from 45.0 to 62.0 days, first-time-right fell from 82% to 76%, complaints from 6.1% to 9.8%, second visits from 11% to 18%, manual activation from 7% to 15%, and SLA breaches from 13% to 16%. The pilot process model has high fitness (0.94) but low precision (0.51), and alignments show billing before activation, repeated visits, and missing permit checks.

The best interpretation is that coordinator ownership and merged front-end steps help predictable, simple cases. Survey skipping and activation-after-billing are risky in apartments, older buildings, permit-sensitive cases, and business accounts. High coordinator utilization and centralized ownership also reduce resilience when coordinators are absent. Therefore, aggregate median gains mask segment-specific deterioration and a longer exception tail.

I recommend a hybrid controlled deployment:

1. Scale coordinator ownership and merged address validation/appointment scheduling only for simple low-risk houses, where benefits are clear, with coordinator backup and capacity limits.
2. Suspend survey skipping and billing-before-activation for apartments, old/permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, and any segment without validated risk data. Require activation before billing and hard permit checks.
3. Run targeted measurement and experiments before extending survey skipping or the full redesign to other segments.

Trade-off: this gives up some potential speed in complex segments in the short term, but protects service reliability, cost, revenue assurance, and customer trust. It preserves the strong simple-case gains and the handover benefits while fixing the parts of R that created rework and complaints. National scaling is premature because tail duration, rework, and compliance-like activation issues are unresolved. Full rollback is unnecessary because the simple-house segment and reduced handovers show real value.

Decision gates for broader rollout should require segment-level p90/p95, first-time-right, complaints, SLA, and 60-day full cost no worse than baseline; zero billing-before-activation and required permit checks; and coordinator resilience metrics. Until then, manage R as a targeted, monitored operating model, not a universal redesign.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision.**  
  Fitness measures whether the discovered model can replay observed behavior. The pilot’s simplified coordinator path covers many observed cases, producing high fitness (0.94). Precision measures whether the model restricts behavior to a coherent process. The pilot’s low precision (0.51) indicates many possible combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, repeated visits, and billing start. Alignment diagnostics support this: billing before activation, repeated installation without survey, and missing permit checks are frequent. Sparse start timestamps and incomplete risk attributes may further reduce model reliability. High fitness therefore does not prove the pilot is stable; it may describe a highly variable process.

- **Improved median but worse tail duration.**  
  Median improvement reflects the typical case, especially simple low-risk houses where merged validation/scheduling and coordinator ownership reduce waiting. Tail metrics reflect exceptional cases: wrong survey skips, missing permits, failed visits, second visits, manual activation, coordinator bottlenecks, and complaint escalation. The pilot case mix includes more apartments and older buildings, which can worsen aggregate tail metrics, but segment-level p90 deterioration shows the problem is not only case mix. The temporal profile also shows new long-tail behavior from billing start to complaint escalation.

- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience.**  
  The redesign reduced handovers dramatically: 3+ handovers fell from 34% to 12%. The handover network became simpler. However, the working-together network became centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. Coordinator utilization reached 93% in some regions, and cases stalled when coordinators were absent because context was not shared. Fewer handovers can reduce coordination cost, but they can also remove redundancy. Accountability improved only where the coordinator was available and not overloaded.

- **Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.**  
  Visible cost before complaint handling improved from EUR184 to EUR161. But this measure excludes downstream complaint handling after week 4. At the same time, complaints, second visits, manual activation, and billing-related complaint escalation increased. The estimated full cost including complaint/revisit handling is worse, EUR207 versus EUR198, though incomplete. The redesign may have shifted cost from installation to customer service, revisits, billing disputes, and activation exceptions.

- **Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings.**  
  Simple low-risk houses have predictable technical paths, so merged steps and coordinator ownership reduce delay. Apartments, older buildings, permit-sensitive cases, and some business cases require surveys, permits, careful activation, and more coordination. The skip rule appears to have been applied too broadly, permit checks were missed, and activation exceptions increased. DFG evidence shows higher shares of failed visits, second visits, manual activation, and billing-to-complaint paths. SLA performance improved for simple houses but worsened overall because complex segments deteriorated.

# 4. Decision alternatives

## A. Scale Redesign R nationally

**Advantages**
- Captures median cycle-time improvement and handover reduction.
- Simplifies the operating model around one coordinator.
- May reduce visible installation cost in simple cases.

**Risks**
- Scales worsened tail duration, rework, complaints, second visits, and manual activation.
- Scales compliance-like issues: billing before activation and missing permit checks.
- Low model precision suggests the pilot is not a stable repeatable process.
- Coordinator overload and absence-related stalls may worsen nationally.
- Incomplete risk attributes could cause misrouting of complex cases.

**When reasonable**  
Only after tail metrics, rework, downstream cost, permit conformance, activation controls, and coordinator resilience are demonstrably fixed. Not reasonable now.

## B. Roll back to the baseline process

**Advantages**
- Stops risky survey skipping and billing-before-activation paths.
- Restores known controls for permits and activation.
- Reduces short-term operational and compliance risk.

**Risks**
- Loses clear gains for simple low-risk houses.
- Loses handover reduction and accountability benefits.
- Does not solve baseline waiting problems.
- May discard useful coordinator ownership and front-end simplification.

**When reasonable**  
If downstream cost is materially worse, compliance violations persist, or coordinator model cannot be made resilient. Not as the first response because segment-specific benefits are real.

## C. Scale only coordinator ownership, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation

**Advantages**
- Keeps accountability and handover reduction.
- Removes two of the riskiest mechanisms.
- Simpler to implement than full segment-specific hybrid.

**Risks**
- Coordinator overload and resilience problems remain.
- May still underperform in complex segments if coordinator ownership centralizes context too much.
- Does not fully address segment-specific survey, permit, and activation needs.

**When reasonable**  
As an interim risk-reduction step if the organization cannot yet operate segment-specific rules, provided backup coverage and conformance monitoring are added.

## D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement

**Advantages**
- Produces causal evidence on redesign components.
- Fixes measurement gaps in segment, risk, and timestamps.
- Avoids premature national scaling.

**Risks**
- Delays realization of proven simple-case benefits.
- Experimental regions may differ from other regions.
- If harmful paths continue during the experiment, customer harm may persist.

**When reasonable**  
As part of the next step, especially for survey skipping and coordinator resilience, but not sufficient alone if simple-house gains can be safely captured now.

## E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses, add controls for apartments/old buildings, and run measurement before wider scale

**Advantages**
- Matches the evidence: strong gains in simple cases, deterioration in complex cases.
- Preserves benefits while pausing risky mechanisms.
- Allows learning through targeted experiments and measurement.
- Addresses compliance-like activation and permit issues before broader rollout.

**Risks**
- Operationally more complex: requires routing, segment classification, and exception controls.
- Incomplete risk attributes may cause misclassification.
- Requires monitoring and governance overhead.

**When reasonable**  
Now. This is the most evidence-proportionate option because it neither scales unresolved harm nor discards proven segment-specific gains.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E — targeted hybrid deployment with controlled measurement.**

## Immediate action

- Stop further national or full-pilot expansion of Redesign R.
- Suspend the survey-skipping rule for apartments, medium/high-risk addresses, old or permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, and any case without complete risk attributes.
- Suspend billing-before-activation as the default. Require successful activation test before billing start unless in a tightly monitored experiment.
- Implement hard permit-check controls for older apartment buildings and permit-sensitive cases.
- Deploy real-time conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and missing permit checks, with stop-work alerts.
- Add coordinator backup, shared case context, and utilization caps before expanding coordinator ownership.
- Default to baseline controls when segment or building-risk attributes are incomplete.

## What to pause

- National scaling of full Redesign R.
- Survey skipping outside validated low-risk strata.
- Post-billing activation outside tightly controlled experiments.
- Expansion into apartments, old buildings, permit-sensitive cases, business accounts with billing-dispute risk, and complex rural installs until controls and evidence are in place.
- Management decisions based only on aggregate median duration or visible cost.

## What to scale

- Coordinator ownership and merged address validation/appointment scheduling for simple low-risk houses, in controlled waves.
- Activation-before-billing and permit-check controls as standard governance.
- Conformance monitoring and downstream cost tracking.
- Coordinator backup and shared ownership where utilization is high.

## What to measure next

Priority order:

1. **M7**: Conformance monitoring for billing before activation and missing permit checks.
2. **M6**: Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.
3. **M1**: Randomized pilot of survey skipping by building-risk stratum.
4. **M4**: Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing start.
5. **M5**: Coordinator ownership versus shared-team ownership under coordinator absence.
6. **M3**: Structured reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, complaints, and second visits.
7. **M2**: Start timestamps for installation visit, activation test, and manual activation.
8. **M8**: Separate customer-experience measurement for appointment speed and working service after installation.

## Thresholds for broader rollout

Broader rollout should be justified only when the following evidence is observed for the target segment:

- Segment-level p90 and p95 cycle time are no worse than baseline, ideally within +5%.
- Median improvement is retained without harming the tail.
- First-time-right, complaint rate, SLA breach rate, second-visit rate, and manual-activation rate are no worse than segment baseline.
- Full 60-day cost per case, including complaint handling and revisits, is no worse than baseline.
- Billing-before-activation is 0% outside approved experiments.
- Required permit checks occur in 100% of applicable cases.
- Survey skipping is supported by validated rule accuracy and randomized evidence showing no increase in failed visits, second visits, complaints, or tail duration.
- Coordinator utilization is below about 85%, absence-related stalls are rare, and shared context is available.
- Process-model precision for the target segment improves materially, with alignment moves for risky paths near zero.

If these thresholds are not met, pause or roll back the affected components. If survey skipping is proven safe only in selected strata, expand it only there. If downstream cost remains worse, do not scale billing and activation changes.
