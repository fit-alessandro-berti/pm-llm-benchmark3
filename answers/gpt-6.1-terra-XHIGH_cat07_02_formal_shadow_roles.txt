A resource may have a primary role and a secondary or temporary role; event labels do not establish decision rights. Case-complexity counts describe cases a resource touched, not necessarily the complexity of each activity they performed.

# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

- **Automated intake and precheck:** A1 and A2 are Nora's entire recorded profile. Its high volume and low case complexity make it an intake boundary, but an A2 event labelled Validate may be a precheck rather than a completed human entitlement decision. Handoffs and policy documentation are needed to establish where human responsibility begins.
- **Front-office generalists:** Registration, entitlement validation, standard response and closure dominate Amira and Ben's work. Their diagnosis, configuration and negotiation events are useful extensions, not enough on their own to establish technical or commercial decision rights. Amira has more medium/high exposure than Ben. Giorgio also performs these core front-office activities, but his higher-complexity work and commercial activity justify an overlapping role.
- **Seasonal support:** Kai and Lina's frequent intake, response and closure events occur predominantly in simple cases. Their sparse diagnosis and negotiation events are peripheral. Their temporary contracts and absence of listed certifications matter when interpreting autonomy; the reopened cases suggest a need to examine entitlement documentation, without proving that every temp closure was defective.
- **Technical diagnosis and delivery:** Diagnosis, configuration and implementation are recurrent for both Chen and Dina; quality and knowledge updates are peripheral. Chen handles substantially more high/critical cases and more A6 events. An A6 event signals security-adjacent work, but certification and delegated authority must be checked before treating either analyst as a replacement for Erik.
- **Security:** Erik's A6 work is central even though its volume is smaller than the routine activities of larger teams. Diagnosis, implementation and other events support interventions but do not define the role. His concentration in high/critical cases and security certification make risk exposure and specialist judgment important alongside frequency.
- **Commercial:** Fatima's A7 and A8 dominate her specialist work. Giorgio's A7 and A8 form a substantial secondary pattern alongside his front-office work; 31 A7 and 18 A8 events occurred during Fatima's six-day absence. That episode demonstrates coverage in observed circumstances, not permanent equivalence of skill, scope or authority. The 16 and 20 A9 events attributed to Fatima and Giorgio respectively need review before interpreting them as final supervisory decisions.
- **Supervisory decision:** Hana's A9 work is the main observed decision activity, with involvement in reopened and escalated cases. Mateo's 21 preliminary A9 events during the workshop, 13 later rechecked by Hana, illustrate a temporary boundary between preliminary action and final authority. The data do not establish the disposition of the other eight.
- **Quality and knowledge:** Ivan and Julia's A11 and A14 are recurrent core work. Implementation, diagnosis, configuration and closure are occasional contributions that may support investigation or correction. Their late/day shifts provide complementary coverage, but their participation in delivery does not establish responsibility for all technical decisions.
- **Coordination:** Mateo's core routing function is inferred from the fragments, escalation certification and broad involvement, rather than from a dedicated matrix column. Reopen, approval-labelled, security and other events are evidence of involvement in cross-functional cases, not proof of specialist or supervisory authority. His filtering of referrals and temporary preliminary approvals show useful flexibility with boundaries that require explicit confirmation.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

- **Giorgio — commercial shadow and front-office bridge.** His pricing certification, 72 A7 events, 88 negotiations, complex case mix and substitution episode support a real secondary commercial capability. He connects customer context to pricing decisions and increases absence coverage. The longer median pricing-exception duration during Fatima's absence, 74 versus 38 minutes, suggests a capacity or process difference worth investigating; case mix and other conditions may also explain it. Check decision scope, outcomes and review requirements before assigning him as an independent replacement. Shadow work is valuable and is not inherently improper.
- **Mateo — escalation broker.** His broad activity footprint, high/critical case involvement and the coordination fragments indicate that he routes work among technical, commercial, security and management functions. Referral filtering coincided with fewer false-escalation complaints, but two critical cases waited more than two days during the incident-load episode; the evidence does not establish that his filtering caused either outcome. Preliminary A9 entries and later rechecks show why routing, preliminary assessment and final approval must be distinguished. His bridging role can reduce noise and improve coordination while creating a queue or an unclear authority boundary if overloaded.
- **Erik — rare but critical security specialist.** Erik's 96 reviews and 55 critical cases make him a specialist whose importance is not proportional to total event volume. His work bridges security assessment and technical implementation. The incident episode raises a coverage and triage question: filtering may protect his capacity, while delayed critical cases make timely escalation and backup essential. Confirm which A6 events require his certified judgment and who may deputize.
- **Nora — automation boundary.** The bot connects registration and precheck to human validation or response. Its high volume can obscure the human accountability needed for exceptions, overrides and downstream errors. Review how prechecks are labelled and transferred.
- **Ivan and Julia — quality gatekeepers.** Their checks and knowledge updates connect delivery to learning and closure. Their occasional implementation work may reflect rework or assistance; sequence-level inspection is needed to distinguish those from independent delivery ownership.

# 4. Ambiguous assignments and alternative interpretations

- **Giorgio:** A senior front-office generalist with occasional commercial support, or a durable dual front-office/commercial role with delegated pricing scope. Decide using pricing decision rights and thresholds, the distribution of A7/A8 outside absence periods, case outcomes and evidence of independent review.
- **Mateo:** A coordinator who records preliminary activities on behalf of decision makers, or a formally delegated incident manager with some independent approval or triage powers. Inspect delegation records, event lifecycle and actor semantics, final sign-offs, referral decisions, and timestamps for the two delayed critical cases.
- **Erik:** A distinct security decision specialist, or a technical delivery specialist who happens to handle most security reviews. Determine whether security certification and policy require an independent review, what decisions his A6 events represent, and which other resources can perform them under what conditions.
- **Chen and Dina:** One technical role with different case assignments, or separate senior/high-risk and standard technical scopes. Compare assignment rules, product line and escalation level, independent decision rights, outcomes and review requirements after controlling for case mix and shift.

Inspect approval thresholds, event definitions, audit trails and subsequent sign-offs. Hana's own occasional A7 events likewise could be commercial decisions or supervisory reviews of them.
- **Kai and Lina:** Temporary agents performing a bounded routine role, or interchangeable front-office capacity for an explicitly defined set of cases. Review entitlement-note completeness and reopen rates by case type, training, certification and supervision, comparing like cases and accounting for the weeks of seasonal load.

- **Ivan and Julia:** Independent quality gatekeepers or combined quality/rework practitioners. Examine whether A11 is an independent sign-off, whether they fix the same cases they check, and how their occasional A10 events relate to rework and closure.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

The following is illustrative: PM4Py return representations and accepted options can vary by version, so inspect the raw results before adapting them. It uses the default activity and resource columns after formatting; it deliberately does not assume a particular organizational-role output schema. The input must contain individual events, not just the aggregate tables above.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# One row per event. Preserve original attributes when formatting.
df = pd.read_csv("events.csv")
df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

# Keep an explicit row order for reproducible sequence inspection. If timestamps
# tie, use a source event ID or other real sequence field when one is available.
df = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"], kind="stable")

roles_raw = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df)
similarity_raw = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df)
handover_raw = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df)
together_raw = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df)

print("Roles:", roles_raw)
print("Activity similarity:", similarity_raw)
print("Handover:", handover_raw)
print("Working together:", together_raw)

# normalization and filtering before comparing network values. Handover reflects
# observed succession, not necessarily a deliberate transfer; co-working means
# shared cases, not necessarily direct collaboration.

# Resource-case exposure: count each case once per resource, not once per event.
# Check that case_complexity is case-level and constant within each case before
# using the first value. Stop rather than silently resolve inconsistent values.
case_col = "case:concept:name"
resource_col = "org:resource"
complexity_col = "case_complexity"
sub_col = "temporary_substitution"
if (df.groupby(case_col)[complexity_col].nunique(dropna=False) > 1).any():
    raise ValueError("case_complexity varies within a case; define its semantics first")

resource_cases = df[[resource_col, case_col, complexity_col]].drop_duplicates()
complexity_exposure = (
    resource_cases.groupby([resource_col, complexity_col], dropna=False)
    .size().rename("cases_touched").reset_index()
)

# Parse the actual source representation explicitly; do not cast strings such as
# 'False' to bool (nonempty strings would all become True). Reject unknown codes.
flag_map = {
    "true": True, "1": True, "yes": True,
    "false": False, "0": False, "no": False,
}
flag_text = df[sub_col].astype("string").str.strip().str.lower()
unknown = flag_text.notna() & ~flag_text.isin(flag_map)
if unknown.any():
    raise ValueError("Unknown temporary_substitution values; define their meaning")
df["substitution_flag"] = flag_text.map(flag_map).astype("boolean")

# Event-level exposure; a flag may describe an event or an entire case, so
# confirm its provenance before interpreting this as actual substitution work.
substitution_exposure = (
    df.groupby([resource_col, "concept:name", "substitution_flag"], dropna=False)
    .size().rename("events").reset_index()
```

Role discovery and similarity provide candidate activity-based groupings, not formal titles, competence assessments or permission to approve. Examine network edges alongside case-level traces, especially reopens, specialist referrals and approval rechecks. A case may involve several resources, so exposure counts across resources are not additive unique-case totals. Compare substitutions separately from routine periods and account for product, complexity, time and workload before attributing duration or outcomes to a person.

# 6. Operational interpretation

Use the overlapping structure to plan capacity by both volume and risk: staff routine intake and late-shift technical and quality coverage, preserve security specialist availability for critical work, and monitor the coordination queue and time to specialist review. Define escalation thresholds, including a fast route for critical security cases, and investigate the two long waits. Measure queue age and outcomes as well as event counts.

Support resilience by documenting pricing and approval delegations, review and escalation paths, and named, trained backups. Giorgio's observed coverage is a starting point for supervised commercial cross-training and assessment; Mateo's preliminary approvals need an explicit final-sign-off process. Test absence scenarios and track pricing-review duration with comparable case mixes. Review the entitlement-note defects behind the 17 reopens, then improve training, templates and quality checks for temporary staff. Verify whether bot prechecks and human validations are distinguishable and provide a clear exception handoff. Use quality and knowledge-base work to feed lessons back into intake and delivery.

Do not reduce security or coordination capacity simply because these roles generate fewer events: criticality, waiting time and indirect work may be poorly represented by counts. Do not treat high similarity or a temporary substitution as proof that staff are interchangeable or hold the same authority. The ten-week window, overlapping case participation, selection into complex cases, shift and product differences, missing activity semantics and possible recording errors limit causal conclusions. Validate the proposed roles with the people doing the work and with policies and outcomes before changing staffing or decision rights.
