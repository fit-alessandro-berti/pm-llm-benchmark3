## 1. Stress scenarios

I define four scenarios that differ in case mix and disruption type. Volume is secondary; the mix and the disrupted station drive the process response.

- **S1 — Viral surge with mix shift.** Volume is 1.5× normal. Case mix moves to Routine 35%, Urgent 32%, Complex-panel 23%, Repeat 10%. This pushes Urgent + Complex-panel from 31% of normal cases to 55%, with no equipment failure. The likely signature is high pre-analytical and assay pressure, plus a large jump in specialist-review holds.

- **S2 — M2 assay degradation/outage.** Volume is 1.3× peak. M2 develops intermittent failures: two failures per week, slower run cycles, and elevated repeat rates. Case mix is Routine 40%, Urgent 30%, Complex-panel 20%, Repeat 10%. The likely signature is a growing Repeat assay loop and saturation of M1.

- **S3 — Medical validator/specialist absence.** Volume is 1.2× peak. One of the two medical validators is out for 10 days; the specialist reviewer is unavailable for 48 hours. Case mix is Routine 41%, Urgent 28%, Complex-panel 22%, Repeat 9%. The likely signature is a large medical-validation backlog and a long Hold for specialist review queue.

- **S4 — Courier compression/delay.** Volume is near normal, but 70% of daily tubes arrive in a 3-hour window because of a snow delay and consolidated courier routes. Case mix is Routine 45%, Urgent 25%, Complex-panel 18%, Repeat 12%. The likely signature is an intake/pre-analytical spike, rushed accession work, and more recollection loops.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

The normal and peak summaries hide the real problem: the tail. Normal p90 urgent time is 19h; peak p90 is 33h, against an 18h contractual deadline. Peak median urgent time is 17h, so half of urgent cases are already close to deadline and the p90 tail is severe. I therefore evaluate bottlenecks by queue age, WIP, and p90, not by average cycle time.

### S1 — Viral surge with mix shift

**Initial bottleneck: Pre-analytical check.**

In the peak DFG, the Pre-analytical check  Request recollection edge rises from around 95 per week in normal periods to 260 per week in peak periods. The pre-analytical median queue rises from 1.8h to 9.5h. This station is the first substantial queue.

**Migration sequence:**

Pre-analytical check  Test preparation/Assay machines  Technical validation  Medical validation/Specialist review.

If we relieve pre-analytical check with early triage or automation, more clean cases flow to preparation and assay. Assay machines are already at 98% utilization in the peak period, with a median queue of 18h. Urgent cases bypass Batch wait in only 58% of peak cases, so 42% of urgent cases are being delayed by batching. If we then relieve assay by dedicating M1 to urgent/complex, the routine volume moves to M2. If M2 has the higher failure rate, repeats increase and consume more assay capacity.

Once assay output grows, Technical validation is the next constraint. In peak data its utilization is 101%, with a median queue of 16h. If technical validation is relieved, the queue moves to Medical validation, whose peak median queue is 22h. Complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to need medical validation and 2.4× more likely to enter Hold for specialist review. The Technical validation  Hold for specialist review edge rises from about 36 per week normal to 203 per week peak. So the final bottleneck is the small specialist-review pool.

### S2 — M2 assay degradation/outage

**Initial bottleneck: Assay capacity and assay repeat loops.**

The peak DFG already shows Assay run  Repeat assay rising from about 143 per week normal to 420 per week peak. When M2 degrades, the repeat loop expands further. A repeat consumes assay capacity twice and also sends work back into the queue.

**Migration sequence:**

M2 failures  M1 saturation  Technical validation  Medical validation.

If we respond by routing all routine cases to M2 and reserving M1 for urgent/complex, M2 becomes overloaded and its repeat rate rises. If we instead route all routine to M1, urgent and complex cases compete with routine for M1. Either way, the downstream effect is the same: more completed assay runs reach technical validation. Technical validation is already at 101% peak utilization, so that queue grows. If we later add technical validation capacity, medical validation and specialist review become the next bottlenecks.

### S3 — Medical validator/specialist absence

**Initial bottleneck: Medical validation.**

Peak medical-validation queue is already 22h. The Technical validation  Medical validation performance edge has a 24h median in peak. Removing one of two medical validators halves that capacity. Urgent cases and complex panels are both affected.

**Migration sequence:**

Medical validation  Hold for specialist review  Result release/transport.

If we add an evening medical-validation shift, more cases are signed off. But complex-panel cases are much more likely to be waiting for specialist review. The Hold for specialist review  Medical validation edge has a median of 48h in peak when the specialist is not the constraint. If the specialist is also unavailable, the hold queue grows. If we respond by outsourcing complex-panel overflow to a partner lab, we relieve the specialist queue but add a 12h transport delay and traceability complexity. The bottleneck migrates to transport and result integration. Urgent cases cannot use this path because 12h transport would consume most of the 18h deadline.

### S4 — Courier compression/delay

**Initial bottleneck: Courier intake and pre-analytical check.**

Peak courier utilization is 88%, and the queue median is 7.5h. The arrival spikes at 10:00 and 17:00 are amplified when a delay compresses all arrivals into one late-afternoon block.

**Migration sequence:**

Courier intake  Pre-analytical check  Test preparation/Assay  Technical validation.

Smoothing courier pickup slots will reduce the intake spike. However, the same volume will then hit pre-analytical check more quickly. If pre-analytical check is still at 96% peak utilization, that queue becomes the bottleneck. If we also add early defect triage, we remove many bad samples sooner, but the clean samples then reach preparation and assay in a larger block. If routine batch sizes are increased to absorb the block, urgent cases may wait. The bottleneck moves downstream to preparation and assay, then to technical validation.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The robust package is not “add capacity everywhere.” It is a set of process controls that reduce loops, smooth arrivals, and shift staff only when a specific queue crosses a threshold.

### R1. Front-door automation and early triage

Use I8 and I6 together.

- Automate registration for orders with complete digital metadata.
- Add a 2-hour pre-analytical triage decision that rejects clearly inadequate samples or starts a correction immediately.

This is robust because it reduces the highest-volume loop in the process. Recollection requests per week rise from about 104 in normal periods to about 297 in peak periods. Every avoided recollection saves a full pass through intake, pre-analytical check, and often assay capacity.

Assumption: referring clinics and EMRs can send complete structured metadata; laboratory staff and physicians agree on rejection criteria.

Failure mode: if triage is too strict, it creates false rejections and a new physician callback loop. If it is too lenient, poor samples still reach assay and cause repeats.

### R2. Cross-trained surge pool, gated by queue thresholds

Use I4, but not as a permanent transfer.

- Train two technical validators in accession correction.
- Train one or two accession-correction specialists in pre-analytical triage.
- Deploy them only when the target queue is high and their primary queue is not already critical.

This is robust because it lets the process respond to whichever bottleneck appears. The risk with I4 is that it takes technical validators away from a station that is already at 101% peak utilization. Therefore the rule must be explicit: pull a technical validator into accession correction only when the accession queue is above 12h and the technical validation queue is below 8h. If technical validation becomes the constraint, the redeployment is reversed.

### R3. Dynamic dispatch and batching control

Replace strict Urgent-first priority with deadline-slack priority.

- Use remaining time to deadline, not case class alone, as the priority key.
- Urgent cases with less than 6h remaining jump the queue.
- Urgent cases with more slack can wait for a batch, but never beyond a maximum batch-wait time.
- Increase routine batch size from 12 to 24 only when urgent and complex WIP is below trigger thresholds.
- Add a break-out rule: if an urgent case reaches 70% of its remaining allowable time, split the current batch and run it.

This is robust because it prevents the strict Urgent-first policy from starving Routine and Complex-panel cases. A strict rule helps urgent cases initially but creates large WIP behind them, which then creates new urgent cases when they arrive later. The hybrid rule protects the 18h deadline without destroying the total flow.

At the assay machines, use a conditional lane policy instead of a hard I3 rule:

- M1 is the preferred machine for Urgent and Complex-panel cases.
- M2 handles Routine cases by default.
- If M2 repeat rate exceeds 10%, automatically split routine volume across M1 and M2 and reduce routine batch size.

This is robust because it preserves the faster M2 for routine work when it is reliable, but does not let a failing M2 create a repeat loop that consumes the whole assay capacity.

### R4. Validation buffers with escalation

Use I5 as a triggered evening shift, not a permanent shift.

- Add a 4–5 hour evening medical-validation shift when medical-validation queue age exceeds 8h or urgent backlog is high.
- Create a validation triage step so that cases that do not truly need medical validation are not sent to medical validators.
- For complex-panel cases held for specialist review, create an escalation rule: if hold time exceeds 24h, escalate to a second reviewer or to the partner-lab overflow path.

This is robust because it adds capacity only at the station that is most likely to become the final bottleneck for urgent and complex cases. It does not add capacity everywhere.

### R5. Courier slot smoothing

Use I7.

- Spread courier pickup slots across the day instead of creating two large spikes.
- Accept pre-registered digital manifests so clerks do not have to enter the same data twice.
- Maintain a weather-delay protocol using temporary extra staff at intake.

This is robust because it reduces the intake rush that creates errors and recollection loops. It does not solve all downstream bottlenecks, but it protects the front end from being the cause of later rework.

### Explicitly rejected: I9

I would not add “Hold Routine releases until a batch report is ready.” This reduces release work but adds waiting. Routine p90 is already 120h in peak, above the 72h target. Adding waiting would make the tail worse and increase WIP. It might be useful only in an extreme release-staff shortage, and even then only as a temporary, bounded policy.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

| Policy | Trigger ON | Main actions | Trigger OFF |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 — M2 failover | M2 repeat rate >10% over 24h OR M2 downtime >2h OR assay queue median >14h | Route routine volume away from M2; reduce routine batch size to 12; keep M1 as priority for urgent/complex; start M2 root-cause check | M2 repeat rate <6% for 48h and assay queue <8h |
| P2 — Evening medical-validation shift | Forecast Urgent + Complex-panel share 45% OR medical-validation queue age >8h OR >10 active urgent cases with <6h remaining | Add a 4–5h evening medical-validation shift; prioritize urgent cases by remaining deadline slack; prepare complex summaries early | Medical-validation queue age <4h for 72h and urgent breach risk low |
| P3 — Partner-lab overflow | Specialist review queue >24h OR specialist unavailable >1 day | Send only non-urgent complex-panel cases with hold time >48h to the partner lab; confirm traceability and transport chain; inform clinicians of extended TAT | Specialist queue <8h for 48h and internal specialist available |
| P4 — Courier compression contingency | Arrival-spike index >60% of daily tubes in any 3h window OR courier delay >4h | Open temporary receiving lanes; deploy extra pre-analytical triage staff; use digital pre-registration; align an evening prep/assay block with the late arrival | Arrival-spike index <45% for 24h and intake queue cleared |
| P5 — Bounded urgent-emergency mode | >15% of active urgent cases have <6h remaining to the 18h deadline | Raise urgent share of assay capacity to at most 50%; defer some routine entries to later slots; keep a reserved minimum share for complex panels | <10% of active urgent cases with <6h remaining for 48h |

These policies are designed to be switched off when the trigger clears. If they are left on, they create a permanent bias against Routine cases and increase total WIP.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Specialist review remains the hardest constraint.** Even with the redesign, complex-panel cases still need a scarce specialist. If the specialist is absent, the Hold for specialist review queue can still grow. Partner-lab overflow helps but adds transport delay and traceability risk.

- **The redesign itself can create false rejections.** Early triage is meant to reject poor samples within 2h. If it is too strict, it will reject samples that could have been corrected quickly. The result is a new recollection loop, using the same intake and courier capacity that the redesign was meant to save.

- **The redesign itself can also drain technical validation.** The cross-trained surge pool is safe only if the trigger rules are enforced. If managers pull technical validators into accession correction while technical validation is already overloaded, the technical validation queue will grow. This is a self-created bottleneck if the policy is not disciplined.

- **Urgent cases remain vulnerable to logistics.** No internal scheduling rule can guarantee the 18h deadline if a courier delay means the sample has not arrived. The process redesign can only manage internal queues; it cannot fix a 6h delay in the physical supply chain.

- **Routine p90 is likely to suffer in severe scenarios.** The priority hierarchy will protect Urgent and Complex-panel cases, so Routine cases carry the residual service-level risk. In a severe combined scenario, Routine p90 may still exceed 72h.

- **The automated front door depends on external data quality.** If a major hospital system sends incomplete metadata, registration falls back to manual work. The front-end bottleneck then returns despite the automation.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

The dashboard should be daily during peak periods. The key is to monitor leading indicators before the queue becomes a service-level failure. Lagging outcome metrics, like p90 cycle time and breach rate, should be reviewed weekly.

| Indicator | Type | Warning bands |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival-spike index: share of daily tubes arriving in any 3h window | Leading | Green <40%; Amber 40–60%; Red >60% |
| Pre-analytical queue age: median wait of cases in pre-check | Leading | Amber >6h; Red >9h |
| Accession-correction queue age: oldest case waiting | Leading | Amber >8h; Red >14h |
| M2 repeat rate: repeat-assay starts divided by assay starts over 24h | Leading | Amber >10%; Red >15% |
| Assay queue age: oldest case waiting for M1/M2 | Leading | Amber >10h; Red >18h |
| Urgent remaining-time share: % of active urgent cases with <6h to the 18h deadline | Leading | Amber >15%; Red >25% |
| Medical-validation queue age: oldest case waiting for medical validation | Leading | Amber >8h; Red >22h |
| Specialist hold queue: number of complex-panel cases in hold >24h | Leading | Amber >20; Red >50 or any hold >48h |
| Event-log completeness: % of process steps with complete timestamps | Infrastructure | Amber <95%; Red <90% |
| p90 cycle time by case type; 18h breach rate; 72h breach rate; overall rework | Lagging | Review weekly to validate the daily leading indicators |

The daily control discipline should be simple: if an indicator is amber for 24h, prepare the scenario policy; if it is red, activate the policy. If the leading indicators are managed, the lagging service-level outcomes will not need to be used as the primary steering signal.
