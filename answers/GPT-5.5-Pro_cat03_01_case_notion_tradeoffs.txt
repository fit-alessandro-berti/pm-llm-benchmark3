## 1. Analytical assumptions

A **case notion** is the rule that turns the object-centric event log into traditional traces. Here, flattening on object type `T` means: for each object of type `T`, create one trace containing the events that are **directly** related to that object in the E2O table, ordered by timestamp. I do not silently infer that an item-related event is order-related, or that a payment-related event is item-related, unless I explicitly define a propagated-relation variant.

I use **convergence** to mean that one real event is related to multiple objects of the flattened type and is therefore copied into multiple traces. For example, `e16 Dispatch` is directly related to orders `O1`, `O2`, and `O3`; in an order-flattened log it becomes three case events although operationally it happened once.

I use **divergence** to mean that one flattened trace serializes several secondary lifecycles as if they were one linear lifecycle. For example, order `O1` has package branches `P1` and `P2`, so the order trace contains `Pack Package -> Pack Package`, `Dispatch -> Dispatch`, and `Confirm Delivery -> Confirm Delivery`. That may be a split shipment, not rework.

Copied shared events are both **evidence** and **duplication**. They are evidence that an object participated in a real shared operation. They are duplication if later interpreted as independent work, independent delays, or independent DFG edges.

The baseline comparison below uses only direct E2O relations. I would permit explicit analytical transformations, but only as labelled sensitivity variants: for example, propagating from `Order -> Item -> Package -> Delivery`, or from `Order <- Invoice <- Payment`, to study indirect exposure. Such propagation may be useful, but it increases duplication and must not be mixed with the direct-E2O baseline.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order flattening, cross-order package `P3`.**
   `e13 Pack Package` relates to `O2`, `O3`, `I21`, `I31`, and `P3`. In an order-flattened log, the same packing event is copied into both `O2` and `O3`. This inflates `Pack Package` frequency and creates separate apparent order-level pack actions, even though one package physically mixed items from two orders. Harmful for workload counting; informative as evidence of cross-order consolidation.

2. **Order flattening, cross-order package `P4`.**
   `e14 Pack Package` relates to `O3`, `O4`, `I32`, `I41`, and `P4`. In `O3`, it follows `e13`, so the order trace shows `Pack Package -> Pack Package`. That can be misread as repeated packing or rework for one order. It is really two item branches of `O3`, one shared with `O2` and one shared with `O4`. Context-dependent: harmful for simple control-flow mining, informative for identifying split/mixed fulfilment.

3. **Package flattening hides commercial mixing.**
   In a package-flattened DFG, `P3` and `P4` both look like ordinary `Pack Package -> Dispatch -> Confirm Delivery` cases. The DFG does not by itself reveal that `P3` packs `I21` from `O2` and `I31` from `O3`, while `P4` packs `I32` from `O3` and `I41` from `O4`. Harmful if the question is “were orders kept separate?”; acceptable if the question is only package movement.

4. **Consolidated delivery `D2` creates replicated delivery performance.**
   `e16 Dispatch` and `e24 Confirm Delivery` relate to `D2`, packages `P2/P3`, items `I12/I21/I31`, and orders `O1/O2/O3`. In order flattening, the `Dispatch -> Confirm Delivery` interval for `D2` is copied into three order cases. In item flattening it is copied into three item cases. In package flattening it is copied into two package cases. Harmful for estimating independent delivery observations; informative for estimating how many objects were exposed to the same delivery.

5. **Consolidated invoice `V2` duplicates financial milestones over orders.**
   `e19 Issue Invoice`, `e22 Record Payment`, and `e25 Record Payment` are directly related to `O2`, `O3`, and `V2`. In order flattening, both `O2` and `O3` appear to have their own invoice and two payment events. That doubles invoice/payment activity counts if interpreted as per-order work. The correct financial unit is one invoice, `V2`, with two orders attached.

6. **Partial payments can look like payment rework.**
   Invoice `V2` is settled by `M2` at `e22` and completed by `M3` at `e25`. In invoice flattening, `V2` has variant `Issue Invoice -> Record Payment -> Record Payment`. Without O2O qualifiers `partially_settles` and `completes_settlement`, this may look like duplicate payment entry, correction, or rework. It is informative for receivables analysis but harmful if interpreted as process nonconformance.

7. **Order `O2` creates a misleading edge from delivery confirmation to payment.**
   `O2` has `e22 Record Payment` at 2026-02-03 12:40, `e24 Confirm Delivery` at 16:00, then `e25 Record Payment` on 2026-02-04 09:00. A DFG from order flattening can contain `Confirm Delivery -> Record Payment`. That does not necessarily mean delivery confirmation triggers payment; it reflects partial settlement of consolidated invoice `V2` after delivery confirmation. Context-dependent and risky for causal interpretation.

8. **Order `O1` serializes split shipment branches.**
   `O1` has item `I11` in package `P1` and item `I12` in package `P2`. Its flattened trace includes `e11 Pack Package`, `e12 Pack Package`, `e15 Dispatch`, `e16 Dispatch`, `e23 Confirm Delivery`, and `e24 Confirm Delivery`. Edges such as `Dispatch -> Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery -> Confirm Delivery` may look like duplicate work. They are actually split fulfilment milestones. Harmful for loop discovery; useful for detecting split shipments.

9. **Delivery flattening creates artificially short lifecycles.**
   `D1`, `D2`, and `D3` only contain `Dispatch` and `Confirm Delivery`. `D3`, for example, runs from `e17` on 2026-02-03 11:15 to `e27` on 2026-02-04 10:00. That is valid carrier-leg performance, but if used as end-to-end fulfilment time it ignores `Create Order`, `Add Item`, `Pack Package`, invoice, payment, and close events. Harmful for order lead-time analysis; informative for transport SLA.

10. **Payment flattening is a near-singleton lifecycle.**
    `M1`, `M2`, `M3`, and `M4` each appear only in one `Record Payment` event: `e21`, `e22`, `e25`, and `e26`. A payment-flattened log has no meaningful DFG edges and cannot measure issue-to-payment delay. It is harmful for process discovery but useful as an audit list of payment objects.

11. **Direct E2O versus propagated relations changes the story.**
    Item `I21` is directly related to add, pack, dispatch, and confirm events, but not to invoice `V2` or payments `M2/M3`. A direct item flattening therefore suggests the item lifecycle ends at delivery confirmation. A propagated variant through `I21 -> O2 -> V2 -> M2/M3` would add finance events, but would also copy the same invoice/payment to every related item and order. Context-dependent and must be labelled.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would still create several flattened logs, but only with narrow interpretations.

The **order-flattened log** should be created for customer-facing lifecycle questions: order creation-to-close time, which orders were split, which orders experienced post-delivery payment completion, and which orders participated in shared logistics or finance. It should not be used alone for counting package work, delivery work, invoice work, or payment work.

The **item-flattened log** is safe for physical item progress: whether each item was added, packed, dispatched, and delivery-confirmed. It is unsafe for cash collection, order closure, or invoice compliance.

The **package-flattened log** is safe for package logistics: pack-to-dispatch and dispatch-to-confirm timing for `P1` to `P4`. It is unsafe for judging whether an order was fulfilled as one unit, because `P3` and `P4` mix orders.

The **delivery-flattened log** is safe for carrier-leg SLA: `D1`, `D2`, `D3` dispatch-to-confirm performance. It is unsafe for claiming that three independent deliveries occurred for `O1/O2/O3`; `D2` is one delivery object with many attached commercial objects.

The **invoice-flattened log** is safe for billing and settlement questions, especially the two-instalment settlement of `V2`. It is unsafe for operational fulfilment.

The **payment-flattened log** should not be used for process discovery. It can be used only for payment-object inventory and reconciliation.

Object-centric views are required for the central questions in this snapshot: which orders share packages, which packages share deliveries, which invoices consolidate orders, and how split order fulfilment interacts with partial payment. An **OC-DFG** can show activity relations without forcing one case notion. An **OTG** can expose object-type interactions such as Order–Item, Item–Package, Package–Delivery, Order–Invoice, and Invoice–Payment. An **ET–OT graph** can show which activities touch which object types. An **object interaction summary** is essential for identifying `P3`, `P4`, `D2`, and `V2` as consolidation points. Equivalent-execution clustering is useful if order remains the primary perspective, because `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, and `O4` have materially different interaction structures.

Contradictory findings should be reconciled by changing the denominator, not by declaring one view correct. For example, `D2` is one delivery event pair, two package cases, three item cases, and three order exposures. All four statements can be true. Reports should therefore distinguish event counts, object counts, copied case-event counts, and interaction counts.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

The decision criteria are: end-to-end business coverage, presence of meaningful start and end events, relevance to customer obligation, ability to connect operations and finance, and severity of flattening distortion.

The strongest supporting evidence for `Order` as primary lead is that orders `O1` to `O4` are the only objects with a broad commercial lifecycle from `Create Order` to `Close Order`. They are directly related to item addition, packing, dispatch, invoice issue, payment recording, delivery confirmation, and closure. If the analysis question is “what happened in order fulfilment?”, the order perspective is the only flattened view that even approximates the end-to-end story.

The strongest counterargument is that order flattening has severe convergence and divergence. `O3` participates in `P3`, `P4`, `D2`, `D3`, and `V2`, producing a trace that mixes split fulfilment, cross-order packaging, consolidated delivery, consolidated invoicing, and partial payment. The order DFG may contain edges that are true in timestamp order but misleading as business causality.

The residual risk is that stakeholders will overread the order-flattened log as if every copied event were independent order work. This would inflate dispatch, confirmation, invoice, and payment frequencies for shared events such as `e16`, `e24`, `e19`, `e22`, and `e25`.

I would change the decision if the primary question shifted. For warehouse optimization, `Package` or `Item` would become primary. For carrier SLA, `Delivery` would become primary. For receivables, `Invoice` would become primary. If the organization’s main concern were cross-order consolidation itself, I would reject a lead object entirely and use an object-centric portfolio as the main analysis layer.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

# Assumption: an OCEL object named `ocel` already exists.
print(ocel)

OBJECT_TYPES = ["Order", "Item", "Package", "Delivery", "Invoice", "Payment"]

def first_existing(df, candidates):
    for col in candidates:
        if col in df.columns:
            return col
    raise KeyError(f"None of these columns were found: {candidates}")

# --- Basic OCEL inspection -------------------------------------------------

events_df = ocel.events.copy()
objects_df = ocel.objects.copy()
relations_df = ocel.relations.copy()

event_id_col = first_existing(events_df, ["ocel:eid", "event_id", "eid"])
activity_col = first_existing(events_df, ["ocel:activity", "concept:name", "activity"])
timestamp_col = first_existing(events_df, ["ocel:timestamp", "time:timestamp", "timestamp"])

object_id_col = first_existing(objects_df, ["ocel:oid", "object_id", "oid"])
object_type_col = first_existing(objects_df, ["ocel:type", "object_type", "type"])

rel_event_col = first_existing(relations_df, ["ocel:eid", "event_id", "eid"])
rel_object_col = first_existing(relations_df, ["ocel:oid", "object_id", "oid"])

activity_frequency = events_df[activity_col].value_counts().rename("event_count")
object_type_frequency = objects_df[object_type_col].value_counts().rename("object_count")

relations_typed = relations_df.merge(
    objects_df[[object_id_col, object_type_col]].drop_duplicates(),
    left_on=rel_object_col,
    right_on=object_id_col,
    how="left"
)

e2o_frequency_by_object_type = (
    relations_typed[object_type_col]
    .value_counts()
    .rename("e2o_relation_count")
)

activity_by_object_type = (
    relations_typed
    .merge(events_df[[event_id_col, activity_col]], left_on=rel_event_col, right_on=event_id_col, how="left")
    .groupby([object_type_col, activity_col])
    .size()
    .sort_values(ascending=False)
    .rename("direct_e2o_count")
)

# Copy factors: if an event has k objects of the flattened type,
# it will appear in k traces under that flattening.
copy_factors_by_type = {}
for ot in OBJECT_TYPES:
    subset = relations_typed[relations_typed[object_type_col] == ot]
    per_event = subset.groupby(rel_event_col)[rel_object_col].nunique()
    copy_factors_by_type[ot] = (
        pd.DataFrame({
            "objects_of_type_on_event": per_event,
            "extra_copies_if_flattened": (per_event - 1).clip(lower=0)
        })
        .sort_values(["extra_copies_if_flattened", "objects_of_type_on_event"], ascending=False)
    )

# --- Traditional flattened logs -------------------------------------------

flattened_logs = {}
dfgs = {}
log_skeletons = {}

for ot in OBJECT_TYPES:
    flattened_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, ot)
    flattened_logs[ot] = flattened_log

    # DFGs are useful, but their edge frequencies must be interpreted
    # with the copy factors above.
    dfgs[ot] = pm4py.discover_dfg(flattened_log)

    # Skeletons can reveal apparent ordering constraints, but in this OCEL
    # repeated shared events may create constraints that are artefacts
    # of the selected case notion.
    try:
        log_skeletons[ot] = pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(flattened_log)
    except Exception as exc:
        log_skeletons[ot] = {"not_computed": repr(exc)}

def extract_dfg_edges(dfg_result):
    """
    PM4Py versions may return either a DFG dictionary or a tuple whose first
    element is the DFG. This helper keeps the downstream comparison explicit.
    """
    dfg = dfg_result[0] if isinstance(dfg_result, tuple) else dfg_result
    return pd.Series(dfg, name="edge_frequency").sort_values(ascending=False)

dfg_edge_frequencies = {
    ot: extract_dfg_edges(dfgs[ot])
    for ot in OBJECT_TYPES
}

primary_order_log = flattened_logs["Order"]
secondary_logs = {
    "Item": flattened_logs["Item"],
    "Package": flattened_logs["Package"],
    "Delivery": flattened_logs["Delivery"],
    "Invoice": flattened_logs["Invoice"],
    # Payment is retained for audit, not for control-flow discovery.
    "Payment": flattened_logs["Payment"],
}

# --- Object-centric discovery ---------------------------------------------

ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
objects_interactions_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)

# Because the chosen position is "primary Order plus secondary lifecycles",
# equivalent-execution clustering can be useful for finding structurally
# similar order executions, such as split orders versus consolidated orders.
try:
    equivalent_ocel_clusters = pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel(ocel)
except Exception as exc:
    equivalent_ocel_clusters = {"not_computed": repr(exc)}

# --- Non-visual comparison outputs ----------------------------------------

analysis_outputs = {
    "activity_frequency": activity_frequency,
    "object_type_frequency": object_type_frequency,
    "e2o_frequency_by_object_type": e2o_frequency_by_object_type,
    "activity_by_object_type": activity_by_object_type,
    "copy_factors_by_type": copy_factors_by_type,
    "flattened_logs": flattened_logs,
    "dfgs": dfgs,
    "dfg_edge_frequencies": dfg_edge_frequencies,
    "log_skeletons": log_skeletons,
    "ocdfg": ocdfg,
    "otg": otg,
    "etot": etot,
    "objects_interactions_summary": objects_interactions_summary,
    "equivalent_ocel_clusters": equivalent_ocel_clusters,
}

# The analyst should now compare these outputs against the business questions.
# Visual inspection alone must not be used to decide that one flattening is
# semantically correct.
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

First, compare the baseline flattened logs with a version where copied shared events are removed, down-weighted, or counted once per original event. For example, `e16` and `e24` should be counted once at event level, three times at order-exposure level, three times at item-exposure level, and two times at package-exposure level. The difference quantifies convergence inflation.

Second, compare direct E2O flattening with an explicitly propagated-relation variant. For example, propagate from orders to packages and deliveries through items, or from orders to payments through invoices. Then report which new edges appear and which counts grow. This is especially important for `I21/I31`, which directly lack invoice/payment events but indirectly connect to `V2`, `M2`, and `M3`.

Third, change the lead object and observe which conclusions survive. If order flattening says `O3` is complex, check whether package, delivery, and invoice views explain why: `O3` touches `P3/P4`, `D2/D3`, and `V2`. If delivery flattening says `D2` is a normal delivery, check order and package views to see that it consolidates `O1/O2/O3` and `P2/P3`.

Fourth, compare OC-DFG metrics based on original events, unique objects, and total object references. `e19` is one invoice-issue event, one invoice object, but two order exposures. `e16` is one dispatch event, one delivery, two packages, three items, and three orders. These are different denominators, not competing truths.

Fifth, validate performance metrics by unit of analysis. Delivery duration for `D2` should be reported once for carrier performance, twice for package exposure, and three times for order or item exposure. Aggregating all as independent observations would bias averages and confidence intervals.

Sixth, inspect equivalent-execution clusters or manually compare interaction signatures. `O1` is split across `P1/P2`; `O2` shares `P3`, `D2`, and `V2`; `O3` is the most entangled order, sharing `P3`, `P4`, `D2`, `D3`, and `V2`; `O4` shares `P4` and uses `V3`. Any “standard variant” claim should be checked against these object-interaction patterns.
