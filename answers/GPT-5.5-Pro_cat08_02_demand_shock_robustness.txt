## 1. Stress scenarios

The redesign should be evaluated against scenarios that change **case mix, arrival pattern, resource availability, and rework**, not just total volume. The evidence base is the uploaded diagnostic-lab process log and manager intervention list. 

| Scenario                                               | What changes                                                                                                                                         | Why it matters                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **S1. Winter demand-mix surge**                        | Total weekly volume rises, but the bigger change is mix: Urgent rises from 18% to 29%, Complex-panel from 13% to 20%, Routine falls from 61% to 42%. | Historical averages understate stress because Urgent and Complex-panel cases consume scarce assay, validation, medical-validation, and specialist-review capacity.                                                |
| **S2. Assay-machine degradation or partial outage**    | M1 or M2 availability falls; M2 is faster but has a higher failure rate. Repeat-after-failure cases cluster after night shifts and M2 usage.         | The assay station is already at 98% peak utilization with an 18h median queue. Extra repeats consume capacity twice and then overload validation.                                                                 |
| **S3. Medical-validator or specialist-review absence** | Evening, weekend, or every-second-Friday specialist absence coincides with a high Complex-panel share.                                               | Complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to need Medical validation and 2.4× more likely to enter Hold for specialist review; peak specialist-review queue reaches 36h and Hold → Medical validation reaches 48h. |
| **S4. Courier arrival compression or weather delay**   | Samples arrive in large waves around 10:00 and 17:00, or delayed couriers compress arrivals into the evening.                                        | Courier intake, registration, and pre-analytical check become bursty. A delayed arrival wave can miss preparation, assay, or validation windows even if daily capacity looks sufficient.                          |
| **S5. Front-end defect / metadata deterioration**      | More incomplete digital orders, poor samples, recollections, and manual accession corrections.                                                       | Pre-analytical failures create loops: Request recollection → Receive sample takes 60h at peak, and clerks lose intake capacity explaining errors to physicians.                                                   |
| **S6. Compound late-week peak**                        | Urgent/Complex demand surge + courier compression + specialist absence + M2 degradation.                                                             | This is the fragile realistic case: fixing one station simply moves WIP to the next constrained station unless flow is actively capped and segmented.                                                             |

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

| Scenario                                               | Likely bottleneck sequence                                                                                                                                        | Bottleneck migration and spillovers                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **S1. Winter demand-mix surge**                        | Courier intake / Registration → Pre-analytical check → Preparation / Batch wait → Assay machines → Technical validation → Medical validation / Specialist review. | Peak utilization is already high across several stations: Pre-analytical check 96%, assay machines 98%, technical validators 101%, medical validators 93%. Improving only registration through automation would push more cases into Pre-analytical check, where the median queue rises from 1.8h to 9.5h. Improving preparation alone would push WIP into assay, where the peak median queue is 18h. Improving assay throughput would then expose Technical validation and Medical validation, whose peak queues are 16h and 22h. |
| **S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage**            | Preparation → Assay machines → Repeat assay loop → Technical validation → Medical validation.                                                                     | The DFG shows Assay run → Repeat assay increases materially in peak conditions, and Repeat assay → Assay run has a 26h peak median edge. If M2 handles too much Routine volume because M1 is protected for Urgent/Complex cases, M2’s higher failure rate can create more repeats, raising total assay demand and increasing Repeat-after-failure p90 cycle time, already 240h at peak. Once the machine backlog clears, validation can receive a pulse of cases and become the next bottleneck.                                   |
| **S3. Medical-validator or specialist-review absence** | Technical validation → Medical validation → Hold for specialist review → Medical validation → Release result.                                                     | A strict Urgent-first rule at preparation and assay may improve early Urgent flow but then dump Urgent and Complex-panel cases into Medical validation, whose peak queue is 22h. Complex-panel cases are especially vulnerable because Hold for specialist review → Medical validation is 48h at peak. Cross-training technical validators into accession correction helps the front end but can worsen Technical validation, which is already above 100% peak utilization.                                                        |
| **S4. Courier arrival compression or delay**           | Courier intake → Registration → Pre-analytical check → Preparation cut-off / Batch wait → Assay run.                                                              | Arrival spikes at 10:00 and 17:00 create queues even when daily capacity appears adequate. If courier smoothing is not done, registration overtime may clear the intake wave but increase errors after 9h shifts, feeding Manual accession correction and recollection loops. If late samples miss assay windows, Urgent cases lose the benefit of bypassing Batch wait; the Urgent bypass rate already falls from 74% normal to 58% peak.                                                                                         |
| **S5. Front-end defect / metadata deterioration**      | Registration / Pre-analytical check → Manual accession correction or Request recollection → Receive sample → Pre-analytical check again → Test preparation.       | Front-end quality defects amplify demand. At peak, recollection and correction paths consume a larger share of Pre-analytical check exits, while Request recollection → Receive sample takes 60h. If clerks spend time explaining errors, intake slows, which can look like a courier or registration bottleneck even though the root problem is defective orders or samples. Cross-training can unblock corrections, but only if Technical validation has slack, which it does not during peak.                                   |
| **S6. Compound late-week peak**                        | All front-end stations queue first, then assay becomes the hard capacity constraint, then validation and specialist review dominate late-cycle aging.             | This is where average-cycle-time optimization fails. Urgent, Complex-panel, Routine, and Repeat-after-failure cases cannot all be prioritized. A policy that accelerates Urgent cases may starve Routine cases and increase Routine p90 beyond the 72h target. A policy that increases Routine batching may improve bench efficiency but can push borderline Urgent cases into Batch wait. A policy that outsources Complex-panel overflow reduces specialist-review WIP but adds 12h transport delay and traceability complexity. |

## 3. Robust redesign package

**A. Replace strict priority with segmented lanes and WIP caps.**
Do not implement I1 as a blanket “Urgent-first everywhere” rule. Use three operational lanes:

* **Urgent lane:** protected preparation, assay, technical-validation, and medical-validation capacity, with aging alerts against the 18h deadline.
* **Complex-panel lane:** planned assay and medical/specialist-review slots, because Complex-panel cases are not merely “less urgent”; they consume different scarce resources.
* **Routine / Repeat lane:** batching and aging rules, with protection against indefinite starvation.

This is robust because it prevents one case type from consuming all scarce capacity. It depends on reliable case classification and visible WIP by lane. It may fail when Urgent plus Complex-panel volume exceeds the protected-capacity envelope; in that case, scenario-specific surge rules must activate.

**B. Add early pre-analytical defect triage and source feedback, using I6.**
Reject or request recollection for poor samples within 2h, rather than letting bad samples sit in the general queue. Track defects by physician, site, courier route, sample type, and metadata-completion status.

This is robust because it reduces avoidable loops before they consume preparation, assay, and validation capacity. It also reduces clerk time spent explaining errors after the queue has already grown. It may fail if physicians cannot recollect quickly; Request recollection → Receive sample is already 60h at peak, so early rejection must be paired with clear instructions and source-level prevention.

**C. Automate registration only for complete digital orders, using I8 with a quality gate.**
Automation should not mean “push all orders faster into the lab.” It should auto-register only orders with complete digital metadata and route incomplete orders into a correction queue with visible aging.

This is robust because it frees clerks while avoiding hidden accession defects. Its main risk is bottleneck migration: if automation succeeds, Pre-analytical check, assay machines, and validation will see more cases sooner. Therefore automation must be monitored together with downstream queues, not as a standalone productivity metric.

**D. Smooth courier intake, using I7.**
Negotiate staggered pickups and delivery windows, especially around 10:00 and 17:00. Separate true Urgent pickups from bulk Routine pickups where possible.

This is robust because it reduces burst-driven queues without adding permanent capacity. It also reduces overtime pressure at registration, where error rates rise after 9h shifts. It may fail during weather disruptions or regional infection waves when external clinics cannot comply with slots.

**E. Use dynamic Routine batching, not a blanket batch-size increase.**
I2 should be conditional. Increasing Routine batch size from 12 to 24 can improve preparation efficiency, but only when Urgent and Complex-panel lane waits are below thresholds. Routine batches should have a maximum waiting time, not just a target batch size.

This is robust because Routine cases tolerate batching better than Urgent cases, but it avoids turning batching into hidden starvation. It may fail if managers chase bench efficiency while ignoring downstream assay and validation queues.

**F. Use health-based assay routing instead of rigid M1/M2 dedication.**
Do not implement I3 as “M1 for Urgent/Complex-panel, M2 for Routine” without safeguards. Reserve M1 capacity for time-critical and complex work, but keep some Routine flow on M1 and restrict M2 when its repeat rate rises.

This is robust because it avoids concentrating all Routine cases on a higher-failure machine, which could create repeat loops and consume assay capacity twice. It depends on near-real-time repeat-rate monitoring by machine and shift. It may fail if M1 has an outage and M2 becomes the only available machine.

**G. Shape validation capacity selectively, using I4 and I5 with guardrails.**
Use I5, a temporary evening medical-validation shift, during peak periods or when Urgent/Complex-panel WIP is high. Use I4, cross-training two technical validators for accession correction, only in time-boxed windows and only when Technical validation is not already the dominant queue.

This is robust because validation is a recurrent late-stage bottleneck in peaks. The risk is serious: Technical validation is already at 101% peak utilization, so pulling validators upstream can worsen late-cycle queues.

**H. Use Complex-panel outsourcing selectively, using I10.**
Outsource only Complex-panel cases that are predicted to wait longer internally than the partner-lab transport penalty plus processing time. The 12h transport delay is acceptable only when the internal specialist-review queue is worse.

This is robust under specialist-review scarcity but not under ordinary flow. It may fail if the partner lab faces the same regional infection wave, if traceability controls are weak, or if transport delays erase the queue benefit.

**I. Avoid Routine release holding as a default.**
I9 should not be a main redesign lever. Medical validation and specialist review are much larger peak problems than Release result, whose selected edge is 5h at peak. Holding Routine releases may reduce administrative work but adds waiting and can make Routine SLA breaches worse.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

| Scenario                               | Activate when                                                                                                                                                                   | Policy                                                                                                                                                                                                             | Deactivate when                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Urgent / Complex surge mode**        | Urgent + Complex-panel share exceeds roughly 45%, Urgent p80 projected cycle time approaches 18h, or Medical validation queue exceeds 10–12h.                                   | Activate segmented lanes, protected Urgent assay/validation slots, evening medical-validation shift, Complex-panel slot planning, and Routine aging protection.                                                    | Mix and WIP remain below thresholds for 48h, and no Urgent cohort is projected to breach 18h.                |
| **Assay degradation mode**             | Machine downtime exceeds 1h, assay median queue exceeds 12h, M2 repeat rate rises above warning level, or Repeat assay share approaches peak levels.                            | Reserve reliable-machine capacity for Urgent/Complex cases, throttle non-critical Routine assay starts, add QC checks before M2 night-shift runs, and avoid routing all Routine cases to M2.                       | Machine availability and repeat rate stabilize for at least one full operating day.                          |
| **Medical / specialist scarcity mode** | Medical-validation queue exceeds 12h, specialist-review queue exceeds 18–24h, scheduled Friday absence is within 24h, or oldest Complex-panel hold is aging toward SLA risk.    | Start evening medical-validation shift, pre-book specialist-review windows, outsource selected Complex-panel overflow, and stop pushing additional Complex panels through assay unless validation capacity exists. | Medical-validation and specialist-review queues fall below warning bands and next-day coverage is confirmed. |
| **Courier compression mode**           | More than one-third of daily arrivals are expected within a 2h window, courier intake queue exceeds 4h, or weather/route disruption compresses deliveries.                      | Stagger pickup slots, split Urgent from bulk Routine routes, add temporary front-end triage immediately after arrival waves, and hold Routine batch starts until Urgent arrivals are screened.                     | Arrival pattern is smoothed and courier/pre-analytical queues return below amber thresholds.                 |
| **Front-end defect mode**              | Pre-analytical defect rate exceeds normal levels, recollection/correction exits approach peak proportions, or a specific source site produces repeated metadata/sample defects. | Enforce 2h defect triage, send source-specific feedback, temporarily require complete digital metadata before auto-registration, and use correction specialists only on high-unblocking-value cases.               | Defect rate returns near normal and correction queue age falls below target.                                 |
| **Routine starvation protection mode** | Routine WIP older than 48h grows, Routine p90 projection exceeds 72h, or Urgent-first rules have consumed protected Routine capacity for more than one shift.                   | Add aging priority for Routine cases, cap Urgent queue-jumping after protected Urgent slots are used, and run Routine batches even if not perfectly efficient.                                                     | Routine aging normalizes without increasing Urgent breach risk.                                              |

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

Even after redesign, several weaknesses remain.

First, **assay capacity remains structurally fragile**. At peak, assay machines are already near saturation, and repeat loops can consume capacity twice. If M2 degradation worsens or M1 has an outage, routing policies can reduce damage but not eliminate the capacity loss.

Second, **medical validation and specialist review remain scarce expert constraints**. Evening medical validation helps only if suitable validators are available. It does not fully solve specialist-review absence, especially for Complex-panel cases.

Third, **recollection delays are partly outside the lab’s control**. Early rejection within 2h is useful, but if the physician or collection site takes 60h to return a sample, the lab cannot recover the elapsed time internally.

Fourth, **automation can create a new failure mode**. If registration automation is judged only by throughput, it may push bad or incomplete orders downstream faster. The redesign therefore needs exception-rate and downstream-defect monitoring, not just auto-registration volume.

Fifth, **segmented priority creates fairness and starvation risk**. Protecting Urgent cases is necessary because of the 18h contractual target, but it can cause Routine p90 breaches unless Routine aging limits are enforced.

Sixth, **cross-training can cannibalize Technical validation**. I4 looks attractive because accession correction has a 14h peak median queue, but Technical validation is already at 101% peak utilization. Using technical validators upstream without guardrails may simply move the delay later.

Seventh, **outsourcing Complex-panel overflow creates traceability and dependency risk**. I10 reduces internal specialist-review WIP only if the partner lab is not facing the same infection wave and if transport plus integration does not erase the queue benefit.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Daily peak-period monitoring should emphasize **leading indicators** that warn before p90 cycle time deteriorates. Lagging outcomes should still be tracked, but they should not drive the policy alone.

| Indicator type | Metric                                                         | Green / amber / red guidance                                                                                        | Why it matters                                                                                                |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Leading**    | Forecast share of Urgent + Complex-panel cases                 | Green <40%; amber 40–45%; red >45%                                                                                  | Mix shift is more important than total volume because these cases stress validation and specialist resources. |
| **Leading**    | WIP by lane and oldest case age                                | Red if Urgent cases have <6h SLA margin before assay/validation; red if Routine WIP >48h before assay or validation | Prevents both Urgent breaches and Routine starvation.                                                         |
| **Leading**    | Pre-analytical defect rate: recollection + manual correction   | Green near normal ~7%; amber >9%; red around or above peak-like 12%                                                 | Early signal of loops that will later consume intake, correction, and recollection capacity.                  |
| **Leading**    | Accession-correction queue age                                 | Amber >8h; red >14h                                                                                                 | Corrections unblock many cases, but overusing technical validators here can hurt validation.                  |
| **Leading**    | Courier arrival compression                                    | Amber if >25–30% of daily samples arrive in 2h; red if >35% or queue >7h                                            | Arrival spikes create queues even when daily capacity is adequate.                                            |
| **Leading**    | Urgent bypass rate around Batch wait                           | Green near normal 74%; amber <65%; red near peak 58% or lower                                                       | A falling bypass rate signals that Urgent cases are being trapped by batching or machine scheduling.          |
| **Leading**    | Assay queue and machine utilization                            | Amber if assay median queue >12h; red near 18h or utilization >95%                                                  | Assay saturation quickly creates repeats, validation pulses, and SLA breaches.                                |
| **Leading**    | Repeat rate by machine and shift                               | Green near normal; amber >10%; red near peak-like 14–15% or concentrated on M2/night shift                          | Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and drive long p90 times.                             |
| **Leading**    | Technical-validation queue                                     | Amber >10h; red near 16h or utilization ≥100%                                                                       | Warns against pulling validators into accession correction during peak.                                       |
| **Leading**    | Medical-validation queue                                       | Amber >10–12h; red near 22h                                                                                         | Critical for Urgent and Complex-panel cases.                                                                  |
| **Leading**    | Specialist-review queue and next-48h coverage                  | Amber >18h; red >24–36h or scheduled absence without cover                                                          | Complex-panel cases are disproportionately exposed here.                                                      |
| **Leading**    | Partner-lab effective turnaround for outsourced Complex panels | Red if transport + partner queue is not at least meaningfully better than internal specialist-review wait           | Prevents outsourcing from becoming a slower, less traceable detour.                                           |
