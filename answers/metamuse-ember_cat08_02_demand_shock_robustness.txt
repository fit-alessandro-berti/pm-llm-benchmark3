## 1. Stress scenarios

“Acceptable” means the tail, not the average, is controlled: Urgent p90 should remain around the 18h contractual limit, Routine p90 around 72h, and Complex-panel/Repeat-after-failure should not silently accumulate unbounded work-in-process. Peak-period evidence already breaches these tails: Urgent median 17h but p90 33h; Routine median 62h but p90 120h.

**S1 — Winter mix-shift surge.** Volume rises from 2,300 to 3,300 cases/week, but the mix changes more than the total. Weekly Routine volume is almost flat, about 1,403 normal vs 1,386 peak, while Urgent rises 414 to 957/week and Complex-panel 299 to 660/week. Assay repeats rise from about 142 to 420/week, Technical-to-Medical transitions from 488 to roughly 1,140/week, and specialist holds from 36 to 203/week. This is a demand-composition disruption, not simply “more of everything.”

**S2 — Assay-machine degradation or outage.** M2 failure rate increases, or one of M1/M2 is unavailable for one to three days. Peak evidence makes this plausible: assay utilisation is 98%, AssayRepeat Assay is 14.8% of assay transitions versus 6.7% normal, RepeatAssay median is 26h, and repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 use. A repeat consumes assay capacity twice.

**S3 — Medical-validator or specialist absence.** A medical validator is sick, or the specialist who is unavailable every second Friday is absent for a longer period. Medical validation is already at 93% peak utilisation with a 22h median queue; specialist review is at 87% with a 36h queue. Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist hold.

**S4 — Courier arrival compression or delay.** Courier arrivals normally spike at 10:00 and 17:00. In this scenario, arrivals are compressed into those spikes or a delayed route returns after the next spike. Courier-intake utilisation reaches 88%, its queue rises from 1h to 7.5h, and ReceivePre-analytical check rises from 2h to 9h.

**S5 — Pre-analytical quality and workforce-fatigue surge.** Defective or incorrectly documented samples increase, while registration staff work repeated shifts longer than nine hours. Pre-analyticalRequest recollection is 780 of 9,730 peak cases, and including Manual correctionRecollection, recollection demand is 9.1% versus 4.6% normal. RecollectionReceive takes a median 60h, and 41.6% of recollection cases are then cancelled.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

At peak utilisation above roughly 90%—Pre-analytical 96%, Assay 98%, Technical validation 101% and Medical validation 93%—small increases in arrivals or repeats produce disproportionate queue growth. Relieving one station therefore transfers work-in-process to the next scarce station unless the whole chain is paced.

**S1: Mix-shift surge.** The likely sequence is Registration/Courier  Pre-analytical  Preparation/Batch wait  Assay/repeats  Technical validation  Medical validation  Specialist review. Evidence: Test preparationBatch wait rises from 4.4% to 12.7% of preparation transitions; TechMedical rises from 24.5% to 42.3%; specialist holds rise from 1.8% to 7.5%. Automating registration alone would release orders faster into a 96%-utilised pre-analytical station and a 98%-utilised assay stage. Larger batches would free preparation capacity but create a larger assay and medical-validation wave. Prioritising Urgent cases at assay without also protecting their medical validation is ineffective because the 22h medical queue alone exceeds the 18h Urgent target.

**S2: Assay degradation/outage.** Sequence: M2 fault  AssayRepeat Assay  RepeatAssay  Batch wait and assay queue  Technical validation, with some RepeatMedical cases bypassing the normal technical path. Dedicating all Routine cases to M2 would increase exposure to the failure-prone machine and could turn a machine problem into twice as much assay work. When the machine recovers, accumulated batches can create a release wave into Technical validation, which is already above 100% peak utilisation. Protecting M1 without a cap merely moves congestion from a failing M2 to M1.

**S3: Validator/specialist absence.** Sequence: Technical validation  Medical validation  Specialist hold  Medical validation  Release. TechnicalMedical has a 24h peak median and HoldMedical 48h. Accelerating registration, preparation or assay in this scenario mainly increases cases waiting for validation and the proportion with a queue over 12h. An evening medical shift clears ordinary medical cases but leaves the specialist-hold subset blocked. Outsourcing Complex-panel work can relieve the specialist queue, but only if the partner also has validation capacity; otherwise the bottleneck moves to transport and partner traceability.

**S4: Courier compression/delay.** Sequence: Courier intake  ReceivePre-analytical  Pre-analyticalPreparation  Accession correction  Preparation. Peak queues are 7.5h at courier intake, 9h ReceivePre-analytical, 13h Pre-analyticalPreparation, and 14h for accession correction. Automation can worsen a compressed arrival if it pushes a burst into pre-analytical check. Recollection returns create a second wave 42–60h later, potentially colliding with the next courier spike. Urgent bypass of Batch wait also falls from 74% normal to 58% peak, so urgent cases lose part of their normal protection precisely when arrivals become bursty.

**S5: Pre-analytical quality/fatigue surge.** Sequence: Registration error  Pre-analytical defect  Manual accession correction or Request recollection  Receive sample again  Preparation/Assay. Only two accession specialists support this stage, with peak utilisation of 91% and a 14h queue. Clerks explaining recollection errors also consume intake capacity, creating a feedback loop. Early defect rejection prevents poor samples from consuming preparation and assay capacity, but excessively aggressive rejection increases the 60h recollection loop and the 41.6% recollection cancellation rate. Moving technical validators permanently into correction would relieve accession but starve Technical validation, already at 101%.

Because the log is flattened by lab order, multiple tubes or assays for Complex-panel orders are hidden. Order counts therefore underestimate true assay-machine load; monitoring must count assay runs and repeats, not only orders.

## 3. Robust redesign package

The robust core avoids permanent capacity everywhere. It combines variability reduction, defect prevention, bounded priority, machine reliability, and flexible validation.

### R1. Paced, gated front end — modified I7 and I8

Smooth courier slots into smaller, staggered windows rather than allowing two large daily waves. Automate registration only when digital metadata is complete and validated; incomplete orders use a short manual checklist and automated feedback to the ordering source. Release work from registration under a downstream work-in-process cap: if Pre-analytical queue is red, non-urgent registration release is paused or paced.

**Why robust:** This attacks arrival variability and overtime-related errors in S1, S4 and S5 without permanently adding staff.  
**Assumptions:** Carriers can adjust slots; enough orders have complete metadata; case type is known accurately at registration.  
**Failure/downstream risk:** Unpaced automation would overload Pre-analytical, preparation and assay. Courier smoothing can move the peak to a shift boundary. If metadata is often incomplete, automation gains are small and manual work remains.

### R2. Early triage plus a flexible accession pool — modified I6 and I4

Within two hours at Pre-analytical, classify samples as accept, simple auto-correctable defect, specialist correction, or immediate recollection with a standard reason code and return slot. Cross-train registration/preparation staff first for simple corrections. Technical validators are a secondary flex pool only when their own queue is low; they are not permanently reassigned. Standard recollection explanations reduce clerk time spent explaining errors.

**Why robust:** Preventing poor samples from entering preparation and assay saves scarce downstream capacity in every scenario and reduces repeats.  
**Assumptions:** Major defects are detectable at triage and reason codes are reliable.  
**Failure/downstream risk:** Overly strict rejection increases recollection, courier returns and cancellations. A larger correction queue can starve Preparation, while permanent use of technical validators creates a Technical-validation shortage.

### R3. Bounded end-to-end priority lanes and dynamic batching — modified I1 and I2

Create a capped Urgent fast lane across preparation, assay, technical validation and medical validation, including a reserved medical-validation slot because Urgent cases needing validation cannot meet 18h if they join a 22h queue. Any Routine or Complex-panel case waiting beyond an aging limit is promoted. Routine batches may increase to 24 only in dedicated Routine windows and must close after a maximum waiting time, for example six hours. Complex panels are scheduled as panels with medical capacity reserved at booking.

**Why robust:** It protects Urgent SLA without strict Urgent-first starvation, retains some batch efficiency, and recognises that Complex-panel demand for medical validation rises sharply during peaks.  
**Assumptions:** Dispatch can reserve capacity and enforce aging; priority class is reliable.  
**Failure/downstream risk:** Too large a reserve fragments assay schedules and starves Routine/Complex cases. Mega-batches can overload assay and medical validation unless time-capped. Priority at preparation alone shifts the queue to medical/specialist review.

### R4. Reliability-centred assay routing — modified I3

Do not statically send all Routine work to M2. Prefer M1 for Urgent and Complex-panel work, cap M2 utilisation and monitor repeats by machine and shift; when M2 repeat rate rises, move suitable Routine work back to M1 and perform enhanced night-shift quality checks. Schedule preventive maintenance before winter peaks. After a repeat, investigate machine/shift cause before simply re-running under the same conditions.

**Why robust:** It attacks the repeat loop, which doubles assay consumption, and works in S1, S2 and S5.  
**Assumptions:** Machine/shift repeat data are available and maintenance can be scheduled.  
**Failure/downstream risk:** Capping M2 moves load to an already congested M1. Excessive preference for M1 can fragment schedules and delay Routine preparation batches.

### R5. Flexible, not permanent, validation capacity — modified I5

Use a conditional evening medical-validation shift during forecast peaks, with explicit prioritisation by SLA risk rather than FIFO. Maintain an on-call specialist backup for hold cases and avoid relying on a single specialist on the second Friday. Cross-trained technical validators work either technical validation or the evening medical shift, not both in a long double shift.

**Why robust:** Medical/specialist capacity is scarce mainly in peak or absence scenarios; flexibility avoids permanent expansion for normal periods when medical utilisation is 58%.  
**Assumptions:** Evening and backup staff are available and fatigue rules are enforced.  
**Failure/downstream risk:** The bottleneck migrates to specialist holds; fatigue may increase validation errors or repeats. Additional evening medical capacity is wasted if preparation/assay have not released suitable cases.

### Deliberately rejected: I9

Holding Routine releases for a batch report adds waiting and work-in-process but does not relieve assay, technical or medical constraints. Routine cases often move directly from Technical validation to Release—11,700 of 15,890 normal technical transitions—so this would worsen Routine p90, already 120h at peak. Administrative reporting should occur after release or off the critical path.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

**P1. Strengthened Urgent lane.** Activate when Urgent share exceeds 25% for two days, assay queue exceeds 8h, or forecast Urgent p90 approaches 18h. It reserves capped assay and medical capacity and bypasses Batch wait. Deactivate after three days below 20% Urgent share and below 4h assay queue, with aging protection retained.

**P2. Routine mega-batch 24.** Activate only when preparation queue exceeds 8h but assay queue is below 6h, medical queue below 10h, and Urgent share is below 25%. Batches close after six hours even if not full. Deactivate immediately if assay queue exceeds 8h or Batch wait median exceeds 14h.

**P3. M1 protection and M2 cap.** Activate when rolling 24h M2 repeats exceed 8%, M2 utilisation exceeds 90%, or a night-shift repeat cluster is detected. Route Urgent/Complex preferentially to M1 and cap M2 Routine load. Deactivate after 48h below 5% repeats and stable machine health. During an M1 outage, reverse the policy and suspend mega-batches and non-critical outsourcing promises.

**P4. Evening medical shift and specialist on-call.** Activate when medical queue exceeds 10h, specialist queue exceeds 18h, or specialist absence is forecast within 48h. Deactivate after two consecutive days with medical queue below 5h and specialist queue below 12h. Cases in specialist hold require specialist coverage, not merely more medical-validator hours.

**P5. Complex-panel outsourcing — I10.** Activate only if specialist queue exceeds 24h for two days, Complex-panel WIP is growing, and the partner’s 12h transport plus partner queue is materially shorter than the internal projected wait. Cap partner share at roughly 20–30% and use only for eligible Complex-panel cases. Deactivate when specialist queue falls below 12h. Partner turnaround and traceability must be monitored as a new downstream stage.

**P6. Courier/registration surge pacing.** Activate when one arrival slot exceeds 1.5 times its rolling average, courier-intake queue exceeds 4h, or registration staff approach nine-hour shifts. Reroute slots, pace non-urgent registration release and use standard recollection templates. Deactivate when intake queue is below 2h and arrival slots return to normal.

Trigger use should include hysteresis—two or three stable days before deactivation—to prevent policy oscillation.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Correlated winter disruption:** Simultaneously high Urgent/Complex demand, machine failure and specialist absence can overwhelm flex capacity. The redesign reduces, but does not eliminate, the need for clinical prioritisation beyond the four recorded case types.
- **Small expert pools:** Two accession specialists and limited specialist cover remain single points of failure. Cross-training helps simple work but cannot reproduce specialist judgement.
- **Only two assay machines:** Redundancy is inherently fragile. A major outage at 98% utilisation will create a queue that cannot be recovered quickly without deferring lower-priority work.
- **Metadata and classification dependency:** Automation and priority lanes fail if orders are incomplete or Urgent/Complex classification is wrong at registration. Errors then propagate into triage, preparation and assay.
- **Courier dependency:** External carriers may not honour smoothed slots. A delayed route can still create a secondary recollection-return wave.
- **Partner-lab risk:** Outsourcing introduces transport delay, sample-chain-of-custody complexity and a hidden partner queue. It relieves the internal specialist queue but may worsen Complex-panel p90 if partner capacity deteriorates.
- **[Redesign-induced] Priority fragmentation:** More lanes and protected slots can fragment machine schedules, increase setups and make batch efficiency harder. Aging rules mitigate but do not remove this effect.
- **[Redesign-induced] Flex-work fatigue:** Evening validation and night quality checks can increase errors, especially where repeats already cluster after night shifts. Without rest rules, the cure can recreate the repeat problem.
- **[Redesign-induced] WIP caps and triage rejection:** Pacing registration or rejecting samples earlier can shift delay and cancellation burden to referring clinicians and patients, particularly when recollection takes 60h.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

### Leading indicators — checked at shift start and midday during peaks

| Indicator | Amber warning | Red warning / action |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival volume and case mix by courier slot | Urgent share >25%, Complex >18%, or slot >1.5× rolling average | Urgent >30% or intake queue >4h: activate pacing and Urgent lane review |
| Queue and oldest-case age at each station | Pre-analytical >5h, Preparation >6h, Assay >8h, Technical >8h, Medical >10h, Specialist >18h | Approach peak values—9.5h, 11h, 18h, 16h, 22h, 36h: stop pushing WIP into red station |
| Pre-analytical defect and accession health | Defect/recollection >6%; correction queue >8h | >9% defect or correction queue >14h: activate triage/flex pool |
| Batch and assay health | Batch-wait cases >8% or repeat rate >8% | Batch wait >12.7%, repeat rate >14%, M1/M2 utilisation >95%: activate M2 cap and suspend mega-batch |
| Validation backlog by case type | Oldest urgent medical wait >6h; medical queue >10h | Urgent medical wait >12h or specialist queue >24h: activate evening shift/on-call/outsourcing review |
| Workforce risk | Any repeated >9h registration shifts; unfilled evening/specialist cover | Multiple overtime breaches or no specialist cover for next 48h: rebalance work and activate backup |

### Lagging outcome metrics — reviewed daily, interpreted by case type

- Median and especially p90 cycle time by Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure; red if Urgent p90 exceeds 18h or Routine p90 exceeds 72h.
- Overall rework/repeat rate; red above the 11.8% peak level.
- Percentage of cases with at least one queue over 12h; red approaching 54%.
- RecollectionCancel rate and Urgent SLA penalty count.
- Batch-wait bypass rate for Urgent cases, which fell from 74% normal to 58% peak.

The daily control decision should be based primarily on the leading indicators: which station is red, which case type is ageing, and whether repeats are rising. Lagging cycle-time metrics confirm harm only after cases have already waited.
