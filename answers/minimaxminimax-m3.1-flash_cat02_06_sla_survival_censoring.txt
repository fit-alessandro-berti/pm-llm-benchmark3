# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin.** All durations are measured in elapsed hours from `Submit` (\(t=0\)). The SLA horizon is \(\tau=72\) hours, with “resolved by SLA” defined as reaching `Resolve` at \(t\leq72\).

**Observed outcomes.**

- \(R\): time to internal `Resolve`.
- \(W\): time to internal `Withdraw`.
- \(J\): time to internal `Reject`.
- \(H\): time to `Transfer` to an external organization. This is an observed handoff time, not an observed eventual resolution time.
- For open cases, `duration_or_age_h` is age at the extract cutoff, not an event time.

**Censoring.** For time to an internal terminal disposition, an open case is naturally right-censored administratively at its cutoff age. Its inactivity gap does not by itself end observation: silence is not a terminal event. If terminal-event ascertainment is assumed to stop when activity stops, the alternative censoring time is the last recorded event, approximately `duration_or_age_h - last_event_gap_h`. That stronger assumption should be treated as a sensitivity analysis, not as the default.

**Transfer semantics.** Transfer is neither a normal non-informative censoring event nor an observed external failure without an additional decision. It can be handled as censoring for internal resolution, as a competing handoff, or through a two-stage external-outcome model.

### Useful estimands

1. **Internal resolution attainment by 72 hours**
   \[
   F_R(72)=P(R\leq72).
   \]
   This is the clearest operational SLA-attainment measure. Under competing-event coding, cases that withdraw, reject, or are handed off cannot subsequently contribute an observed internal resolution.

2. **Internal non-resolution at 72 hours**
   \[
   B_R(72)=P(R>72)=1-F_R(72),
   \]
   where \(R=\infty\) after a competing outcome. This is a defensible meaning of “breach” if the commitment is specifically to resolve internally. It should not be conflated with “case still open”: a withdrawal at 27 hours is non-resolution but not an overdue unresolved case.

3. **Late internal resolution**
   \[
   L_R=P(72<R<\infty)=F_R(\infty)-F_R(72).
   \]
   This distinguishes a case resolved at 76 or 110 hours from a case with no known later outcome.

4. **No internal disposition by 72 hours**
   \[
   S_D(72)=P\{\min(R,W,J)>72\}.
   \]
   Under permanent-handoff semantics, a transferred case has no observed internal disposition. A more directly observable governance endpoint is
   \[
   S_{\mathrm{obs}}(72)=P\{\min(R,W,J,H)>72\},
   \]
   meaning no observed resolution, withdrawal, rejection, or handoff by 72 hours.

5. **Cause-specific cumulative incidence**
   \[
   F_k(72)=P(T_k\leq72,\ T_k<T_{\text{other observed event}}),
   \]
   separately for resolution, withdrawal, rejection, and, under the competing-handoff scenario, transfer. These quantities describe actual outcome probabilities rather than the hypothetical “what if every competing event had resolved?” interpretation.

The primary “breach” report should therefore use **internal non-resolution**, accompanied by separate withdrawal, rejection, transfer, and late-resolution components. A separate closure KPI should use **no observed terminal status by 72 hours**.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The following coding is specific to the endpoint being analyzed. Withdrawal and rejection are competing events for resolution, not ordinary censoring. Transfer requires an explicit semantic choice.

| Observed status | Event indicator(s) | Competing-risk code | Default censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | \(E_R=1\), \(E_W=E_J=E_H=0\), at `duration_or_age_h` | `R` | None | For a composite “any observed closure” endpoint, encode resolution as part of that composite event |
| `withdrawn` | \(E_W=1\), \(E_R=E_J=E_H=0\), at duration | `W` | None for resolution endpoint; it is a competing event | As a censoring reason in a sensitivity analysis; as part of a composite closure endpoint |
| `rejected` | \(E_J=1\), \(E_R=E_W=E_H=0\), at duration | `J` | None for resolution endpoint; it is a competing event | As a censoring reason in a sensitivity analysis; as part of a composite closure endpoint |
| `transferred`: **T-A, independent censoring** | No internal event; right-censored at transfer | `CENSOR_TRANSFER` | Censor at 26, 68, or 96 hours for S21, S08, S32 respectively | Requires external resolution to be unrelated to observed covariates conditional on the censoring model; usually implausible without external data |
| `transferred`: **T-B, competing handoff** | \(E_H=1\) at transfer; no later internal resolution | `H` | None for the internal-resolution endpoint | Counts handoff as non-resolution for internal SLA purposes; may be inappropriate if the business counts eventual external success |
| `transferred`: **T-C, external-outcome extension** | Internal follow-up ends at transfer; external resolution remains missing | `H` plus an unobserved external outcome | Censor internal resolution at transfer and model/link the external outcome separately | Current extract cannot identify the external component; report a lower bound with no external success and an optimistic bound in which transfers before 72 externally resolve by 72 |
| `open` | All event indicators are 0 | `CENSOR_ADMIN` | Right-censor at cutoff age | Censor at age minus inactivity gap if inactivity implies unascertained outcomes; joint/IPCW models if censoring depends on inactivity, workload, or cohort |

Three distinct transfer assumptions are therefore essential:

1. **T-A: censoring.** The internal-resolution CIF estimates a projection conditional on transferred cases behaving like comparable untransferred cases. This is strong and probably false because transfer routing is likely related to complexity, priority, or external receiving capability.
2. **T-B: competing handoff.** Transfer permanently removes the case from observed internal-resolution risk. This is coherent for “internal resolution by 72,” but it deliberately gives no credit for later external resolution.
3. **T-C: external-outcome augmentation.** Transfer starts a second observation process. The current data support only bounds or a missing-outcome model, not a point estimate of total resolution.

For S08 at 68 hours, T-A censors it four hours before the horizon, T-B records a competing handoff, and T-C treats external resolution by 72 as unknown. S21 at 26 hours is similarly exposed to all three assumptions; S32 at 96 hours cannot affect a 72-hour event probability but matters for longer-horizon analyses.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Completed-case selection changes the population.** Only 12 of 32 cases are resolved. A resolved-only analysis discards nine open cases, four withdrawals, three rejections, and three transfers. Those cases are not missing at random: they are competing or unfinished outcomes.

The direction of bias in this extract is plausibly optimistic because slow resolutions are less likely to be complete. S03 resolved at 76 and S13 at 110, while S09 remained open at 92, S11 at 81, and S26 at 104. These are precisely the cases that expose tail risk but disappear from a completed-resolved analysis.

**Open cases above and below 72 hours are not equivalent.**

- S09, S11, S25, and S26 are definitely unresolved at 72. They contribute to \(B_R(72)\), although their eventual outcomes remain unknown.
- S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31 are unresolved when observation ends before 72. They are not observed 72-hour failures. They enter the risk set until censoring and then leave it; survival estimators project beyond them only under their censoring assumptions.

Treating both groups as 72-hour failures would conflate an observed deadline breach with insufficient follow-up.

**Withdrawal and rejection are competing risks.** S06 withdraws at 27, S12 at 15, S22 at 44, S30 at 70, S05 rejects at 9, S23 at 11, and S14 at 63. Once these events occur, internal resolution is no longer possible. Treating them as if they were randomly censored and would have resolved at the same rate as other cases can distort the resolution CIF.

**Transfer selection is plausibly informative.** All three transfers have an `Escalate` prefix, and two are P1. Transfer may reflect priority, complexity, eligibility, or external capacity. An independent-censoring assumption therefore needs explicit justification. S32 at 96 cannot affect the 72-hour risk set, but it demonstrates that transfer is not confined to early failures.

**Inactivity may indicate a different future risk.** S26 has a 72-hour gap and is still open at age 104; S11 and S25 have 48- and 55-hour gaps. Long inactivity may predict withdrawal, delayed work, or an unrecorded disposition. Importantly, inactivity is not automatically informative censoring: if the system continues to observe terminal status, silence simply means no event. It becomes informative censoring only if the probability of detecting or recording a terminal event depends on inactivity or other unrecorded factors.

**Cohort composition and policy implementation differ.** Cohort B has 11 of 17 cases starting with `AutoCheck`, compared with 2 of 15 cohort A cases containing `AutoCheck`. P1 represents 3/15 of A versus 5/17 of B. Escalation appears in 6/15 A and 6/17 B cases, but at different positions and for different reasons. Thus cohort is a bundle of calendar time, case mix, routing, and possibly incomplete policy compliance.

The crude administrative summaries illustrate endpoint fragility:

- Resolved by 72 among all starts: A = 4/15 = 26.7%; B = 5/17 = 29.4%, only a one-case difference.
- Any observed terminal status by 72: A = 9/15 = 60.0%; B = 9/17 = 52.9%.

These are not censoring-adjusted CIFs, but they show that a conclusion about which cohort “looks better” can change simply by changing the SLA endpoint.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

### Method comparison

| Method | Appropriate use here | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Kaplan–Meier** | Descriptive analysis of a composite endpoint such as time to any observed terminal status or handoff; cause-specific KM can describe a particular hazard | \(1-\mathrm{KM}\) for resolution is not the actual resolution probability when withdrawal, rejection, or handoff compete. Treating competitors as censors creates a hypothetical net-resolution interpretation |
| **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence** | Primary estimation of \(F_R(72)\), \(F_W(72)\), \(F_J(72)\), and \(F_H(72)\) under explicit competing-event and transfer rules | Transfer assumptions remain consequential; small-sample CIs will be wide |
| **Cause-specific hazards** | Model how priority, cohort, current process stage, inactivity, and other time-varying features relate to each hazard; combine hazards with the Aalen–Johansen estimator to obtain CIFs | A predictor may be a post-treatment marker or an indicator of complexity; coefficients are associations, not intervention effects |
| **Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard** | Secondary model directly related to a cause-specific CIF, useful for compact adjusted risk summaries | Proportional subdistribution-hazards assumptions are restrictive; ordinary time-varying predictors and post-treatment mechanisms are awkward |
| **Restricted mean time** | Express operational exposure in hours, e.g. expected time to an observed closure within 72 hours; a net restricted mean time to resolution can be obtained from cause-specific hazards | A resolution-only KM RMST that treats competitors as censors is not the observed time to resolution. Use either a clearly defined composite endpoint or a net-survival formulation |
| **Landmarking** | Conditional analysis among cases still active at 24 or 48 hours, using only process history available by the landmark | Discards early failures and estimates a conditional, not population-wide, outcome. Under a composite observed endpoint there are 27 cases alive at 24 hours and 23 at 48 hours |
| **Multi-state model** | Analyze movement through screening, review, rework, escalation, handoff, and absorbing outcomes; support stage-specific workload actions | Current data contain ordered prefixes but no event timestamps, so only transitions and coarse endpoint timing—not state durations—are presently estimable |

### Primary analysis

Use the **Aalen–Johansen estimator** for mutually exclusive observed outcomes under the competing-handoff transfer scenario:

- Primary SLA KPI: \(F_R(72)\), with \(B_R(72)=1-F_R(72)\) under that same coding.
- Companion outcomes: \(F_W(72)\), \(F_J(72)\), \(F_H(72)\), and late internal resolution.
- Secondary governance KPI: no observed terminal status or handoff by 72, using a composite endpoint that explicitly includes transfer.
- Report overall and cohort/priority-stratified estimates, preferably with case-bootstrap or Greenwood intervals and explicit small-cell counts.

Use administrative censoring at cutoff age for open cases in the primary analysis. Treat transfer as a competing handoff for the internal-resolution KPI, while repeating the entire analysis under censoring and external-outcome bounds.

### Secondary analysis

Fit a **penalized cause-specific time-varying hazard model** or a discrete-time pooled hazard model. Include baseline priority and calendar/cohort information, then add time-updated stage, review/rework counts, escalation status, and inactivity gap. Derive prediction-at-risk-set curves and CIFs from the fitted hazards.

The multi-state model should be exploratory rather than primary. With 32 cases, 12 resolutions, four withdrawals, and three rejections, unrestricted multivariable modeling would overfit. Use penalized likelihood, Firth-type bias reduction, or Bayesian partial pooling, and avoid fine priority-by-cohort interaction models without substantially more data.

A Fine–Gray model can be reported as a secondary association analysis, but its coefficients should not be used as causal effects. A landmark analysis at 24 or 48 hours can support operational decisions about cases still active at those times.

### Cohort-policy analysis

The cohort comparison must be labeled **observational and associational**. Adjustment for post-assignment variables such as escalation or the final path can remove mediated effects or introduce bias. A stronger policy analysis would require:

- exact submission dates and rollout dates;
- assignment probabilities or a credible propensity model;
- policy-compliance measures at each case;
- baseline workload and case-mix variables;
- a causal design, such as randomized phased rollout or a defensible quasi-experimental design.

A start–stop data representation should contain one row per interval during which a case remains in a given process state. Each row should carry the current stage, counts accumulated so far, inactivity measured as of the interval start, baseline priority/cohort, and the event cause at the interval end. Open cases must remain in this representation until administrative censoring.

### Proposed state-transition structure

The observed prefixes support the following schematic structure:

- `Submit  Screen` and `Submit  AutoCheck`.
- `Screen  AutoCheck`, `Screen  Review`, and `Screen  Reject`.
- `AutoCheck  Screen`, `AutoCheck  Review`, `AutoCheck  Escalate`, and `AutoCheck  Resolve`.
- `Review  Review` through `Review  Rework  Review`.
- `Review  Escalate`, `Review  Resolve`, `Review  Withdraw`, and `Review  Reject`.
- `Escalate  Review`, `Escalate  Resolve`, and `Escalate  Transfer`.
- Any nonabsorbing state  administrative censoring at cutoff.
- `Transfer  external process`, whose `Resolved`, `Rejected`, or still-pending state is not observed in this extract.

This is a process topology, not yet a timed multi-state model. Exact event timestamps are required to estimate time spent in `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, and `Rework`.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

| Sensitivity analysis | Alternative specification | Expected effect | Conclusion that could change |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Transfer treatment** | T-A censor; T-B competing handoff; T-C external-outcome bounds | Changes both the resolution CIF and the apparent prevalence of unresolved work; transfers before 72 are S21 at 26 and S08 at 68 | Absolute breach probability and any ranking that relies on handoffs can change |
| **Withdrawal/rejection** | Competing events in the primary analysis; censoring in a sensitivity analysis; part of a composite “closed within 72” endpoint | Censoring competitors tends to make eventual resolution look more likely than it is; closure endpoint makes early withdrawals/rejections look favorable | A statement about resolution improvement can become a statement about closure with no operational improvement |
| **Open-case censoring** | Administrative censoring at cutoff age versus censoring at the last event | Last-event censoring removes cases earlier, including S09 at 61, S11 at 26, S25 at 31, and S26 at 32, and materially changes the risk set | Cohort ordering and absolute 72-hour risk may reverse, especially with sparse data |
| **Informative censoring** | Add an inactivity or workload covariate; joint model; IPCW; pattern-mixture tipping-point analysis | Tests whether long gaps or workload predict both remaining uncensored and eventual resolution | A conclusion that inactivity is harmless can reverse |
| **Cohort-policy confounding** | Unadjusted; priority-adjusted; route/compliance-stratified; propensity-weighted once assignment data exist | Separates or attempts to separate policy-era effects from case mix and compliance | Any claimed causal improvement for cohort B may attenuate, disappear, or reverse |
| **Endpoint definition** | Internal resolution, any observed closure, no observed handoff, or total internal-plus-external resolution | Changes which cases count as successful | The crude cohort ordering already illustrates this: resolution slightly favors B, while observed closure by 72 favors A |
| **Late resolution treatment** | Report late resolutions separately versus excluding them from SLA numerators | Separates delayed service from permanent non-resolution | A “no breach” conclusion can hide substantial delay, while a strict deadline conclusion can obscure the size of the late-resolution population |

For the external-transfer bound, the pessimistic assumption is that no transferred case externally resolves by 72. The optimistic bound assumes that transfers occurring before 72—S21 and S08—resolve externally by 72. These are stress-test bounds, not empirical estimates; the extract cannot identify external resolution timing or probability.

# 7. Operational interpretation

### Converting outputs into decisions

1. **Case alerts.** At time \(t\), predict \(P(R\leq72\mid\text{still active},X(t))\) and its complement. Refresh the prediction when a stage, escalation, rework, or inactivity update occurs. Do not use the final prefix or final risk score for earlier alerts.
2. **Queue management.** Combine the predicted resolution CIF with cause-specific probabilities. A high no-resolution risk accompanied by high withdrawal/rejection incidence suggests a different intervention from a high risk accompanied by long inactivity in `Review`.
3. **Capacity planning.** Use late-resolution CIFs and restricted mean time within 72 to estimate unresolved workload and reviewer hours. Report withdrawal/rejection separately so that early closure is not mistaken for successful resolution.
4. **SLA governance.** Maintain a dashboard with:
   - internal resolution by 72;
   - resolution after 72;
   - withdrawal/rejection before 72;
   - transfer before 72 and eventual external outcome;
   - no observed terminal status by 72;
   - open age and current inactivity.
   
   Open cases below 72 belong in a follow-up-pending category, not the breach numerator.
5. **Policy oversight.** Compare cohorts descriptively and with uncertainty intervals. Do not label the cohort B coefficient or an escalation coefficient as a causal improvement or deterioration.

Alert thresholds should reflect reviewer capacity and the relative cost of missed breaches versus unnecessary interventions. They are operational policy choices, not properties of the fitted survival model.

### Calibration and fairness

Evaluate time-dependent calibration at 24, 48, and 72 hours:

- compare predicted and Aalen–Johansen–estimated resolution CIFs;
- calculate calibration-in-the-large, calibration slope, and time-dependent Brier scores;
- use censoring-adjusted evaluation where appropriate;
- report both discrimination and operational quantities such as false-alert burden and positive predictive value at the chosen capacity threshold.

Perform these checks by priority and cohort, and report cohort-by-priority cell sizes. There are only eight P1 cases, so apparent subgroup differences will be highly uncertain. Examine:

- false-negative rates for cases eventually resolved after 72;
- false-alert burden for P1 versus lower priorities;
- whether the same risk score produces materially different alert rates across cohorts;
- whether calibration worsens after including stage, escalation, or inactivity features.

Because escalation and rework may be prompted by case complexity, high predicted risk can mean “needs more help” rather than “escalation is harmful.” Resource decisions should therefore not mechanically remove support from cases flagged by the model.

# Scenarios:
#   censor          : transfer is right-censoring for internal resolution

def encode_events(frame, transfer_scenario="censor", open_censor="age"):
    records = []
    for row in frame.itertuples(index=False):
        t = float(row.duration_or_age_h)
        gap = float(row.last_event_gap_h)
        event = 0
        cause = "CENSOR_ADMIN"

        if row.observed_status == "resolved":
            event, cause = 1, "R"
        elif row.observed_status == "withdrawn":
            event, cause = 1, "W"
        elif row.observed_status == "rejected":
            event, cause = 1, "J"
        elif row.observed_status == "transferred":
            if transfer_scenario == "censor":
                event, cause = 0, "CENSOR_TRANSFER"
            elif transfer_scenario == "competing":
                event, cause = 1, "H"
            elif transfer_scenario == "external_unknown":
                event, cause = 0, "CENSOR_TRANSFER"
            elif transfer_scenario == "external_upper":
                if t <= 72:
                    event, cause = 1, "R_EXTERNAL_BOUND"
                else:
                    event, cause = 1, "H"
            else:
                raise ValueError("Unknown transfer scenario")
        elif row.observed_status == "open":
            if open_censor == "last_event":
                stop = max(0.0, t - gap)
                cause = "CENSOR_LAST_EVENT"
            elif open_censor == "age":
                stop = t
                cause = "CENSOR_ADMIN"
            else:
                raise ValueError("Unknown open-case censoring rule")
        else:
            raise ValueError(row.observed_status)

        if row.observed_status != "open":
            stop = t

        records.append({
            "case": row.case,
            "cohort": row.cohort,
            "priority": row.priority,
            "status": row.observed_status,
            "start": 0.0,
            "stop": stop,
            "event": event,
            "cause": cause,
            "external_outcome_missing": int(row.observed_status == "transferred"),
            "external_success_upper_by_72": int(
                row.observed_status == "transferred" and t <= 72
            ),
        })

    encoded = pd.DataFrame(records)
    assert len(encoded) == len(frame)  # no open case is dropped
    return encoded

# 3. Aalen-Johansen estimator, including a KM-ready start-stop table
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
def aj_cif(encoded, horizon=72):
    d = encoded.copy()
    d["analysis_cause"] = d["cause"].replace({"R_EXTERNAL_BOUND": "R"})

    causes = ["R", "W", "J", "H"]
    cif = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    cif_h = {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    survival = 1.0
    survival_h = 1.0

    times = sorted(d["stop"].unique())
    for t in times:
        at_risk = (d["start"] < t) & (d["stop"] >= t)
        n_risk = int(at_risk.sum())

        # Convention: events precede censoring when tied at the same time.
        due = d[(d["stop"] == t) & (d["event"] == 1)]
        event_counts = due["analysis_cause"].value_counts().to_dict()
        n_events = len(due)

        if n_risk > 0:
            if n_events > n_risk:
                raise RuntimeError("More events than cases at risk")
            survival_before = survival
            for cause, count in event_counts.items():
                cif[cause] += survival_before * count / n_risk
            survival = survival_before * (1.0 - n_events / n_risk)

        if t <= horizon:
            cif_h = cif.copy()
            survival_h = survival

    return {
        "F_R72": cif_h["R"],
        "F_W72": cif_h["W"],
        "F_J72": cif_h["J"],
        "F_H72": cif_h["H"],
        "F_any_observed_terminal72": sum(cif_h.values()),
        "S_no_observed_event72": survival_h,
        "n_at_risk72": int(((d["start"] < 72) & (d["stop"] >= 72)).sum()),
        "n_events_total": int(d["event"].sum()),
        "n_censored_total": int((d["event"] == 0).sum()),
    }

def bootstrap_fR_ci(frame, transfer_scenario, open_censor="age",
                    n_boot=300, seed=202503):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    values = []
    for _ in range(n_boot):
        sample = frame.iloc[rng.integers(0, len(frame), len(frame))]
        try:
            value = aj_cif(
                encode_events(sample, transfer_scenario, open_censor)
            )["F_R72"]
            if np.isfinite(value):
                values.append(value)
        except RuntimeError:
            pass
    if not values:
        return (np.nan, np.nan)
    return tuple(np.percentile(values, [2.5, 97.5]))

def risk_set_counts(encoded):
    rows = []
    event_times = sorted(
        encoded.loc[encoded["event"] == 1, "stop"].unique()
    )
    for t in event_times:
        at_risk = (
            (encoded["start"] < t) & (encoded["stop"] >= t)
        )
        due = encoded[(encoded["stop"] == t) & (encoded["event"] == 1)]
        counts = due["analysis_cause"].value_counts().to_dict()
        row = {
            "risk_time": t,
            "n_at_risk": int(at_risk.sum()),
            "n_events": len(due),
            "n_censored_at_time": int(
                ((encoded["stop"] == t) & (encoded["event"] == 0)).sum()
            ),
        }
        for cause in ["R", "W", "J", "H"]:
            row["events_" + cause] = int(counts.get(cause, 0))
        rows.append(row)
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

# 6. PM4Py-compatible prefix/variant extraction
#
# No event timestamps are supplied or synthesized. Only case_id, activity,
# and the observed event order are encoded. Direct EventLog construction
# is equivalent to parse_event_log_string when the latter cannot parse the
# serialized representation without timestamps.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
def pm4py_control_flow(frame):
    records = []
    sequences = {}
    for row in frame.itertuples(index=False):
        activities = tuple(row.prefix.split(">"))
        sequences[row.case] = activities
        for event_index, activity in enumerate(activities):
            records.append({
                "case_id": row.case,
                "activity": activity,
                "event_index": event_index
            })

    try:
        import pm4py
        from pm4py.objects.log.log import EventLog

        raw_log = EventLog()
        for record in records:
            raw_log.append(record)

        try:
            log = pm4py.parse_event_log_string(str(raw_log))
            parsed_with_string_parser = True
        except Exception:
            log = raw_log
            parsed_with_string_parser = False

        try:
            prefix_raw = pm4py.get_prefixes_from_log(log)
        except AttributeError:
            from pm4py.statistics.variants.variants import (
                get_prefixes_from_log
            )
            prefix_raw = get_prefixes_from_log(log)

        prefixes = {tuple(prefix) for prefix in prefix_raw}
        variants_raw = pm4py.get_variants(log)
        variant_sets = {frozenset(variant) for variant in variants_raw}
        source = (
            "PM4Py parse_event_log_string" if parsed_with_string_parser
            else "PM4Py EventLog equivalent construction"
        )

        # Ensure exact case prefixes remain available if a PM4Py version
        # filters the log unexpectedly.
        for sequence in sequences.values():
            if sequence not in prefixes:
                prefixes.add(sequence)

    except ImportError:
        # Explicit fallback if PM4Py is unavailable.
        prefixes = set()
        for sequence in sequences.values():
            for length in range(1, len(sequence) + 1):
                prefixes.add(sequence[:length])
        variant_sets = {frozenset(sequence) for sequence in sequences.values()}
        source = "manual fallback; install PM4Py for PM4Py-native extraction"

    case_variants = {
        case: frozenset(sequence)
        for case, sequence in sequences.items()
    }
    all_variants = variant_sets | set(case_variants.values())
    ordered_variants = sorted(
        all_variants, key=lambda variant: tuple(sorted(variant))
    )
    variant_ids = {
        variant: index for index, variant in enumerate(ordered_variants)
    }

    features = frame[["case"]].copy()
    features["pm4py_prefix_length"] = features["case"].map(
        lambda case: len(sequences[case])
    )
    features["pm4py_repeated_review"] = features["case"].map(
        lambda case: sequences[case].count("Review") > 1
    )
    features["pm4py_rework_count"] = features["case"].map(
        lambda case: sequences[case].count("Rework")
    )
    features["controlflow_variant_id"] = features["case"].map(
        lambda case: variant_ids[case_variants[case]]
    )

    print("\n" + "=" * 78)
    print("PM4PY CONTROL-FLOW EXTRACTION")
    print("source:", source)
    print("number of distinct prefixes:", len(prefixes))
    print("number of distinct activity-set variants:", len(ordered_variants))
    print("variants:")
    for index, variant in enumerate(ordered_variants):
        print(f"  V{index}: {sorted(variant)}")

    merged = frame.merge(features, on="case", how="left")
    print("\nCONTROL-FLOW FEATURE BY CASE")
    print(
        merged[[
            "case", "cohort", "priority", "pm4py_prefix_length",
            "pm4py_repeated_review", "pm4py_rework_count",
            "controlflow_variant_id"
        ]].to_string(index=False)
    )
    print("\nCASES PER CONTROL-FLOW VARIANT")
    print(
        merged.groupby("controlflow_variant_id")["case"]
        .apply(lambda values: ",".join(values))
        .to_string()
    )
    return merged

# 9. Limitations and additional data

1. **Exact event timestamps are the highest-priority missing input.** Obtain `Submit`, every screening/review/rework/escalation event, terminal event, transfer, and cutoff timestamps. The aggregate duration and ordered prefix cannot establish stage-entry times, escalation delays, rework durations, or historical inactivity trajectories.

2. **External outcomes after transfer are essential.** Link S08, S21, and S32 to receiving organizations and obtain external resolution, rejection, abandonment, and timestamp data. Without these, total resolution and external completion time are not identifiable.

3. **Calendar dates are needed for administrative censoring and policy analysis.** Exact submission dates, extract cutoff date, rollout start/end dates, and case-level policy assignment are required to separate calendar effects from cohort effects and to model staggered entry.

4. **Rollout compliance must be measured directly.** “AutoCheck first” is only a proxy. Compliance should identify whether the assigned screening/escalation policy was actually applied, when it was applied, and whether deviations were deliberate or operational.

5. **The assignment mechanism must be documented.** Collect variables influencing screening, escalation, transfer, and policy assignment. Without them, propensity scores, inverse weighting, or a defensible quasi-experimental design remain unavailable.

6. **Time-varying workload and operational context are needed.** Queue length, reviewer capacity, absence, service interruptions, customer channel, complexity, and organizational ownership may explain inactivity, withdrawal, rejection, transfer, and informative censoring.

7. **Event-log completeness and semantic definitions need validation.** Determine whether silent periods can contain unrecorded terminal activity, whether all withdrawals/rejections are consistently coded, and whether “resolve” means the same thing internally and externally.

8. **The sample is too small for unrestricted process or fairness models.** Additional observations are needed for stable priority-by-cohort estimates, calibrated operational thresholds, and reliable external-transfer subanalyses.

With these additions, the extract could support a timed multi-state model, competing-risk prediction with time-varying process features, externally linked total-resolution analysis, and a substantially more credible evaluation of the cohort-policy change.
