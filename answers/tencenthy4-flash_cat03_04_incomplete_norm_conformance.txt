## 1. Conformance semantics

Classification must start from the status of the normative relation, not from whether a relation appears in a graph.

- **Forbidden behavior:** A relation is explicitly disallowed. The norm is closed-world for forbidden relations, so one observed instance is a deviation if the object identities and event ordering are trusted. Examples: N3, T6, E10. Suspected extraction errors may downgrade confidence, but they do not make the observed relation “unspecified.”
- **Missing mandatory behavior:** A mandatory relation is absent within its stated scope. The norm is closed-world only within that scope and only when the abstraction is adequate. Examples: N1, N4, T1/T2, E1–E8. If scope or abstraction is uncertain, classify as “insufficient evidence” or “abstraction mismatch,” not automatically as a violation.
- **Unexpected but unregulated behavior:** The relation is observed but the norm explicitly says it is unspecified or says nothing about it. It may be operationally notable, but it is not a compliance violation. Examples: N6, T4, T5, E9, codeath.
- **Typical-pattern absence:** A typical relation is common and desirable but not required. Absence is not a violation, and no frequency threshold is defined. Examples: N2, N5, T3.
- **Likely data-quality error:** The observed edge is probably produced by extraction, identifier reuse, or object linkage mistakes. This is not the same as benign unregulated behavior; it requires repair because it can create false forbidden/mandatory findings.
- **Abstraction mismatch:** The model family or summary abstraction cannot express the business rule directly. A directly-follows summary can miss intermediate events; an object graph can miss event-level context; an event-object table can miss object-to-object lineage.
- **Insufficient evidence:** The available summaries do not include the timestamps, identifiers, scopes, event attributes, or lifecycle states needed to decide.

Open-world assumptions apply to typical and unspecified relations and to out-of-scope cases: absence is not noncompliance. Closed-world assumptions apply to forbidden relations and to mandatory relations inside their stated scope: presence or absence has conformance meaning once the observation is reliable and the abstraction fits.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG

OC-DFG counts show observed directly-following activity pairs per object. They can establish that a particular pairwise sequence was observed, such as `Cancel Order -> Dispatch Package` twice or `Pack Package -> Dispatch Package` 198 times. They cannot establish full lifecycle paths, concurrency, intermediate activities, timestamps, or whether an “eventually” rule was satisfied through a longer route.

Counts only matter relative to scope. For N3, count 2 is enough because the relation is forbidden. For N1, 98/100 is not a statistical pass or fail: the norm gives no tolerance band, and the two missing direct links may be review-mediated. For N5, 92/110 is not a violation because N5 is typical and exceptions are legitimate. For N2, `Validate Order -> Issue Invoice` is not noncompliance merely because N2 lists `Validate Order -> Close Order`; the norm explicitly says intermediate invoice, review, and fulfilment events may occur.

A relation can be conforming at one abstraction and suspicious at another. `Create Order -> Manual Compliance Review` is unregulated under N1/E9/T5 but may explain why direct `Create Order -> Validate Order` is absent. The key danger is treating “every created Order should eventually validate” as “Create Order must be directly followed by Validate Order.” A compliant trace such as `Create Order -> Manual Compliance Review -> Validate Order` would fail a naive directly-follows check. Eventual rules require case-level reachability, not pairwise adjacency.

### OTG

OTG edges establish object-to-object relationships by type: interaction, cobirth, codeath, and similar. They can show that Order–Item interaction exists 1450 times, Package–Delivery interaction 200 times, Invoice–Payment 92 times, and one forbidden Order–Payment cobirth. They cannot show which event produced each relation, whether every event instance had the relation, or whether an attribute-based carrier reference is equivalent to a Delivery object.

Scope is central. T1 and T2 are mandatory only for their stated interaction contexts. T3 is typical, so 92 Invoice–Payment edges do not imply that all payments must be linked to invoices. T4 and T5 are unspecified, so Customer and RiskCase edges are not violations. Seven Package–Delivery codeath edges are not forbidden merely because codeath is absent from the normative OTG set.

The same edge can be conforming or suspicious depending on abstraction. `(Package, object_interaction, Delivery)` weight 200 may look conforming at type level, while ET–OT reports only 198 Dispatch events with Delivery; the discrepancy makes the dispatch relation suspicious at event level. Conversely, codeath may look alarming in an object graph but is normatively silent.

### ET–OT

ET–OT edges establish whether event types are linked to required object types. They are best for checking E1–E10, such as whether every Dispatch Package event links to a Package and Delivery, or whether Sensor Heartbeat ever links to Invoice. They cannot prove identity correctness, cardinality, business scope, or whether a missing object type is represented as an event attribute.

Counts must be read with status. E10 is forbidden, so one `Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice` edge matters even though the context suggests a data-quality cause. E6 is mandatory, so 198/200 Dispatch–Delivery links requires investigation. E8 is mandatory except for explicitly typed unapplied cash; one unlabeled payment with no Invoice is therefore a real candidate gap, even though T3/N5 make unapplied cash a legitimate business pattern if properly typed. E9 is unspecified, so Manual Compliance Review with RiskCase or Customer is not a violation.

Again, abstraction matters. A dispatch with a carrier reference may be operationally traceable but formally missing a Delivery object. An Add Item event may have an Item but no Order in ET–OT, even though the aggregate OTG Order–Item count is high. PM4Py-style graph diagnostics can surface these mismatches, but they cannot decide their legal or business meaning.
