# 1. Data audit and estimands

The center is open 12 hours per day, 07:00–19:00. I use:

- **Arrival intensity**: daily arrivals divided by 12, in cases per open hour. This is an opening-hours average, not a homogeneous Poisson rate.
- **Completion intensity**: daily completions divided by 12, in cases per open hour. It can exceed same-day arrivals when prior backlog is discharged.
- **Net daily balance**: \(A-D\), where positive values add to the unobserved backlog and negative values reduce it.
- **Service time**: supplied median and p90 active handling minutes. Neither is mean service time nor mean end-to-end flow time.
- **WIP \(L\)**: mean number in-system from 15-minute snapshots. Its exact relation to overnight backlog and snapshots at the boundaries is unspecified.
- **Capacity**: mean concurrent available FTE. Nominal open-day capacity is \(12\times\text{FTE}\) staff-hours, but actual productive capacity cannot be inferred without utilization, breaks, skills, routing, and mean work content.
- **Complex mix**: percentage of arrivals coded as the complex multi-step variant.
- **Peak concentration**: percentage of arrivals during 08:00–09:59.

“Capacity pressure” is therefore assessed through WIP per FTE, net balance, service-time tails, mix, peaks, and outage history—not through a claimed utilization \(\rho\).

### Important audit observations

Over all 28 days there were 2,780 arrivals and 2,679 completions, a net addition of 101 cases. Daily averages were 99.3 arrivals and 95.7 completions, but these are strongly confounded by weekday composition, regime changes, and backlog discharge.

At least the following threaten naive inference:

1. **Nonstationary case mix:** complex share rises from roughly 18–24% early to 39–47% later. This may be a real shift, a coding shift, or both.
2. **Outage and recovery:** the 180-minute outage on 15 April suppresses completions; 16–17 April include catch-up and are not ordinary steady-state days.
3. **Unknown opening backlog:** completions can belong to earlier cohorts, so daily \(D/A\) is not a same-day conversion rate.
4. **Overnight carry-over:** no service occurs while closed; calendar-day boundaries do not close the queueing system.
5. **Intraday burstiness:** equal daily totals can generate different congestion when arrivals are concentrated into short intervals.
6. **Weekday/weekend heterogeneity:** weekends have much lower demand and staffing, but not proportionately lower service times or WIP per FTE.
7. **Intervention timing uncertainty:** staffing began sometime during 29 April. That date is a contaminated transition day, not cleanly “pre” or “post.”
8. **Service summaries are quantiles:** medians and p90s cannot identify mean work content, service distribution, utilization, or mean flow time.
9. **WIP measurement ambiguity:** 15-minute snapshots may overweight long-lived cases; snapshot inclusion at 07:00/19:00 is unspecified.
10. **Serial dependence:** the outage, backlog recovery, sustained mix shift, and intervention produce adjacent-day dependence.
11. **Aggregate ecological bias:** daily complex percentages do not reveal within-day routing, class-specific service, or class-specific WIP.
12. **Representative-hour inconsistency:** the supplied hourly vectors do not always sum to the corresponding daily totals. For example, 7 April’s vectors sum to 101 arrivals and 99 completions and do match, but 15 April’s completion vector sums to 58 while its arrivals sum to 105 rather than the daily 105? Here arrivals do match; other representative vectors should still be treated as illustrative bins rather than a complete stochastic sample. More generally, four selected days cannot support distributional fitting.

### Estimands that are and are not identified

Defensible estimates include regime-level average daily counts, open-hour intensities, median/p90 ranges, mean snapshot WIP, WIP/FTE, mix, peak concentration, and cumulative net balance. Mean flow time is not directly observed. A Little’s-Law value \(W=L/\lambda\) is only an **implied average time in the system** under boundary and throughput assumptions; it must not be interpreted as active handling time.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different segmentation is a **factorial day-type model** rather than contiguous eras:

1. **Weekday versus weekend**
2. **Low versus high coded complexity**, for example below 30% versus at least 39%
3. **No disruption, outage, or recovery**
4. **Baseline versus augmented staffing**
5. **Low versus high morning concentration**, for example peak share below 35% versus at least 39%

Under this story, congestion is generated by interacting covariates rather than one chronological change point. The high-mix/high-peak weekdays from 20 April onward form one cell; high-mix weekends form another; augmented staffing after 29 April modifies both.

### Strengths

- Separates calendar composition from mix and staffing.
- Recognizes that weekend staffing can produce high WIP/FTE despite low counts.
- Better suited to future multilevel or regression analysis with event-level data.
- Does not assume the complex-share jump happened at one exact instant.
- Can represent a coding change explicitly if later audit data identify one.

### Weaknesses

- Cells are extremely small; several combinations have only two observations.
- Backlog state is endogenous and path-dependent. The same covariates can yield different output depending on opening inventory.
- The outage and intervention produce contaminated days that do not fit clean factor levels.
- Thresholds such as 30% or 39% are heuristics, not inferred structural breaks.
- It can obscure the chronological escalation from repeated positive net balances.

A second defensible alternative is a **latent backlog-state segmentation**: balanced (\(A\approx D\), modest WIP), accumulating (\(A>D\), rising WIP), disrupted, and discharging (\(D>A\), falling WIP). This is operationally intuitive but risks circularity because regimes are defined partly by the outcomes being explained. The chronological primary segmentation is preferable for diagnosis; the factorial model is preferable for controlled comparison once richer data are available.

## 4.2 Complex mix hides a major service and congestion shift

A clean low-mix contrast is 6–14 April versus the high-mix pre-intervention window 20–28 April:

These windows include different weekday/weekend proportions but each spans seven weekdays and two weekend days, making composition reasonably comparable. Arrival volume rises about 12%, while mean WIP roughly doubles and service tails increase sharply. A pooled median-service summary around 31 minutes describes neither regime well.

The association does not prove that complexity caused the deterioration: coding, burstiness, backlog, and demand changed together.

## 4.3 Outage and intervention distort “productivity”

- Baseline weekdays, 6–14 April: approximately 100 arrivals and 100 completions/day.
- Outage day, 15 April: 105 arrivals versus 58 completions.
- Recovery, 16–17 April: 105.5 arrivals versus 120.5 completions/day.
- Accumulation, 20–28 April: 97.0 arrivals versus 87.3 completions/day including weekends.
- Transition/discharge, 29 April–1 May: 115 arrivals versus 121 completions/day.

A pooled model could rank recovery and intervention days as intrinsically more productive because completion intensity is high. That is misleading: both have abundant inherited WIP, and 16–17 April explicitly follow an outage. Conversely, the outage day understates ordinary processing capacity.

## 4.4 Simpson’s-paradox-like risks

These are possible reversals, not proven causal paradoxes:

- **Staffing versus completions:** high-staff days have high completions, but they were selected during recovery or backlog states. Within stable low-backlog periods, the staffing effect is not identified.
- **Weekend versus weekday pressure:** weekends have lower WIP in levels but can have higher WIP/FTE.
- **Complexity versus completion intensity:** late high-complexity days after intervention may complete more than early low-complexity days because staffing and backlog differ. Pooling could make complexity appear harmless or beneficial despite much longer service tails and higher WIP within pre-intervention comparisons.
- **Peak share versus daily capacity:** a day can have an ordinary daily total but severe short-window congestion. Daily averaging can reverse the operational ranking of smooth versus bursty days.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

For each 12-bin representative vector, the code computes:

- **Fano/index of dispersion:** sample variance divided by mean across hourly bins.
- **Coefficient of variation:** sample standard deviation divided by mean.
- **Maximum-bin share:** largest hourly count divided by daily vector total.

These are descriptive across hour-of-day bins. They combine stochastic variation with deterministic diurnal seasonality and therefore are not pure estimates of a stochastic arrival-process dispersion parameter.

Qualitatively:

- **7 April** has a broad late-morning peak, with arrivals rising to 20 and then tapering. It is nonuniform even though it is the ordinary candidate.
- **15 April completions** contain three zero bins during the outage. Its dispersion reflects an intervention in service availability, not random queue variation.
- **21 April arrivals** are especially multimodal and bursty: bins of 34, 24, and 14 are separated by low-volume periods. Its daily average conceals large temporary overloads.
- **25 April** is lower-volume and smoother in absolute counts, although low means can make dispersion statistics sensitive to small changes.

The daily sequence also shows persistence:

- Complex share moves from low 20s to the 40s and remains elevated.
- WIP jumps with the outage, partially recovers, then rises again through repeated positive balances.
- Peak share rises with the high-mix period.
- After staffing augmentation, completions exceed arrivals and WIP declines.

Simple lag-1 correlations for WIP, arrivals, complex share, and net balance are descriptive only. Trends and regime shifts can generate high autocorrelation even without stationary serial dependence. Likewise, a low lag correlation in net balance would not imply independent days because backlog couples successive service opportunities.

### Change-point evidence

A transparent heuristic can compare every allowable split in mean complex share, WIP, service p90, or a standardized combination and choose the split minimizing within-segment squared error. It will likely favor the vicinity of the sustained 20 April shift, but:

- one split cannot represent the 15 April outage and 29 April intervention;
- nearby criteria may produce different dates;
- daily observations are dependent;
- a coding change can mimic an operational change;
- post-change sample sizes are short.

Thus, this is evidence for candidate boundaries, not definitive ground truth.

Four representative days are insufficient to identify a Poisson, negative-binomial, Hawkes, scheduled-batch, or other arrival process. They are selected rather than random, cover only four day types, and expose hour-of-day structure but not within-hour clustering.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

For a window with mean snapshot WIP \(L\) and daily throughput \(X\), I calculate

\[
W_{\text{open-hours}}=\frac{L}{X/12}=12L/X.
\]

This is in **open-clock hours** because \(X/12\) is cases per open hour. It is not automatically elapsed calendar time: cases waiting overnight add 12 closed hours per crossing, and the aggregate does not reveal how many crossings occur.

# 7. Operational implications

1. **Staff to peaks and mix, not only daily totals.** The 20–28 April regime combines higher demand, a much larger complex share, and 40–50% morning concentration. Shift starts, flex coverage, or cross-trained capacity should be aligned with burst windows.

2. **Use a separate recovery plan after disruptions.** Record opening backlog, outage start/end, restored capacity, and recovery completion. Recovery throughput must not be used as a normal productivity target.

3. **Protect weekend capacity under high mix.** Weekend totals are low, but WIP/FTE and service tails are substantial. Increasing from 2.5 to 3.0 FTE coincides with better balance on 2–3 May, although two observations do not establish causality.

4. **Treat 29 April as a transition day.** Evaluate intervention effects using timestamped staffing exposure and event-level outcomes, not a binary daily flag.

5. **Smooth peaks where operationally possible.** Options include appointments, callback windows, virtual queues, triage of simple cases, and reserving complex-case specialists. The bursty 21 April vector shows why daily-rate staffing can fail.

6. **Audit the complex-variant definition.** Compare coding rules, effective dates, reclassification rates, and activity traces. A coding change and a true work-content shift imply different remedies.

7. **Track backlog explicitly.** Required minimum fields include backlog at 07:00 and 19:00, case arrival and completion timestamps, status at snapshots, service-start/end intervals, class/variant, staff roster by interval, skills, breaks, outages, and abandonment or transfer.

8. **Use cohort and calendar views together.** Calendar-day output supports operations; arrival-cohort flow time supports customer experience. Completed cases should be linked to their arrival dates and overnight crossings.

9. **Model classes separately.** With event data, estimate class-specific arrival patterns, active work, waiting, routing, rework, and resource pools. A single-server-equivalent queue is unlikely to represent a multi-step complex variant.

10. **Avoid a risky pooled policy:** reducing weekend staffing because pooled weekend arrivals are low appears efficient. The segmented view shows high WIP/FTE and long tails, especially under high mix; further cuts could increase overnight carry-over into Monday.

Another risky pooled policy is setting productivity targets from 16–17 April or 29 April–1 May completion rates. Those rates are supported by inherited backlog and temporary staffing and are not sustainable steady-state capacity estimates.

        "peak_avg": g.peak_08_10_pct.mean(),
    })

pooled = summarize(d).to_frame().T
weekpart = d.groupby("weekpart", sort=False).apply(summarize, include_groups=False)
regimes = d.groupby("regime", sort=False).apply(summarize, include_groups=False)

# Comparable low/high-mix chronological windows, each with 7 weekdays + 2 weekends.
contrasts = pd.concat({
    "low_mix_06_14": summarize(d[d.date.between("2026-04-06", "2026-04-14")]),
    "high_mix_pre_20_28": summarize(d[d.date.between("2026-04-20", "2026-04-28")]),
    "outage_15": summarize(d[d.date.eq("2026-04-15")]),
    "recovery_16_17": summarize(d[d.date.between("2026-04-16", "2026-04-17")]),
    "transition_discharge_29_01": summarize(
        d[d.date.between("2026-04-29", "2026-05-01")]
    ),
}, axis=1).T

# -------- Burstiness: Fano, CV, max share; and serial-dependence indicators ----
def dispersion(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    mean = x.mean()
    sd = x.std(ddof=1)
    return pd.Series({
        "total": x.sum(),
        "hour_mean": mean,
        "fano_sample_var_over_mean": (sd ** 2) / mean if mean else np.nan,
        "cv_hourly": sd / mean if mean else np.nan,
        "max_bin_share": x.max() / x.sum() if x.sum() else np.nan,
    })

disp_rows = []
for day, g in hourly.groupby("representative_day"):
    for stream in ["arrivals", "completed"]:
        z = dispersion(g[stream])
        z["representative_day"] = day.date().isoformat()
        z["stream"] = stream
        disp_rows.append(z)
dispersion_table = pd.DataFrame(disp_rows).set_index(
    ["representative_day", "stream"]
)

# Pearson lag-1 is a simple descriptive indicator, not a stationary ACF estimate.
serial = pd.Series({
    col: d[col].autocorr(lag=1)
    for col in ["arrivals", "completed", "mean_wip", "complex_pct",
                "peak_08_10_pct", "net"]
}, name="lag1_corr")

# Transparent one-change heuristic:
# Minimize within-segment SSE on standardized complexity, WIP, and p90.
# Minimum segment length is 5. This is a candidate boundary, NOT ground truth.
features = ["complex_pct", "mean_wip", "p90_service_min"]
z = (d[features] - d[features].mean()) / d[features].std(ddof=0)
candidates = []
for split in range(5, len(d) - 4):
    left, right = z.iloc[:split], z.iloc[split:]
    sse = ((left - left.mean()) ** 2).to_numpy().sum()
    sse += ((right - right.mean()) ** 2).to_numpy().sum()
    candidates.append({
        "right_segment_starts": d.iloc[split].date.date().isoformat(),
        "split_index": split,
        "within_segment_sse": sse,
    })
change_candidates = pd.DataFrame(candidates).sort_values(
    "within_segment_sse"
).head(5)

# ---------------- Explicitly selected Little's-Law windows --------------------
# Formula W = L / (X/12) = 12L/X, in open-clock hours.
# X is average(arrivals, completions) for approximately balanced multi-day windows.
# These values remain "approximate only" due to unknown boundary backlog/overnights.
little_specs = {
    "early_ordinary_weekdays_06_14":
        (d.date.between("2026-04-06", "2026-04-14")) &
        (d.weekpart.eq("weekday")),
    "early_weekends_11_14":
        d.date.between("2026-04-11", "2026-04-14") &
        d.weekpart.eq("weekend"),
    "staffed_weekend_02_03":
        d.date.between("2026-05-02", "2026-05-03"),
}
little_rows = []
for name, mask in little_specs.items():
    g = d[mask]
    throughput_day = (g.arrivals.mean() + g.completed.mean()) / 2
    little_rows.append({
        "window": name,
        "days": len(g),
        "mean_wip_L": g.mean_wip.mean(),
        "arrivals_day": g.arrivals.mean(),
        "completed_day": g.completed.mean(),
        "net_sum": g.net.sum(),
        "throughput_proxy_X_day": throughput_day,
        "implied_W_open_hours": 12 * g.mean_wip.mean() / throughput_day,
        "verdict": "approximate only",
    })
little = pd.DataFrame(little_rows).set_index("window")
