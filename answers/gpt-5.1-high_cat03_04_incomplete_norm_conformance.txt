## 1. Conformance semantics

I interpret the norm as **closed-world only for (a) relations explicitly marked forbidden and (b) mandatory relations within their stated scope**. Everything else (typical and unspecified) is **open-world**: additional relations or missing typical patterns are not violations by default but may still be business-relevant.

Within that frame, I distinguish:

- **Forbidden behavior**  
  A relation that the norm explicitly marks as forbidden (e.g., N3, T6, E10) and that appears in the observed graphs. Because forbidden rules are closed-world, every well-typed occurrence is a prima facie violation unless downgraded to a data-quality error.

- **Missing mandatory behavior**  
  A relation marked mandatory that does not appear for all objects in scope. For OC-DFG this includes both missing directly-follows edges and missing evidence that the mandated eventual effect happened at all. For ET–OT and OTG, it includes missing object links that the norm says must always exist for a given event or interaction.

- **Unexpected but unregulated behavior**  
  An observed relation not covered (or explicitly marked unspecified) by the norm. Because the norm is open-world here, these are **not violations**. They can still be:
  - benign extensions (e.g., extra compliance review edges); or
  - symptoms that the norm is incomplete and should be refined.

- **Typical-pattern absence**  
  A normatively typical relation that is missing for some fraction of cases (e.g., N2, N5, T3). Typical status means:
  - presence is desirable and expected often;  
  - absence is allowed and may correspond to documented exceptions (e.g., write-offs, open credit terms);  
  - very low or very high occurrence rates may still justify investigation, but not automatic noncompliance.

- **Likely data-quality error**  
  A relation that strongly conflicts with domain semantics or is explicitly flagged as a join/typing problem (e.g., E10’s Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice link), or gaps in mandatory edges that align with known logging limitations. These are handled separately from genuine process violations, even when they cross a forbidden normative rule.

- **Abstraction mismatch**  
  A mismatch between the **business rule’s eventual semantics** and the **graph’s directly-follows or structural abstraction**. Typical examples:
  - N1: “Every newly created Order should eventually be validated” vs the OC-DFG summary “Create Order -> Validate Order” as a directly-follows edge (98/100). Missing a directly-follows edge does not prove that validation never happened; it may simply be separated by unmodeled steps.
  - N2 and N5: “typical” eventual behaviors approximated by single edges in OC-DFG.  
  In this class, we treat deviations as **suspicions to be checked** rather than direct violations.

- **Insufficient evidence**
  Cases where aggregated counts and graph summaries do not allow us to distinguish:
  - a legitimate but rare exception,
  - a modelling abstraction issue, or
  - a true violation.  
  These require deeper log inspection, raw payloads, and business context before drawing conclusions.

Open-world versus closed-world thus guide the classification:
- **Forbidden + mandatory (within scope)** → closed-world; absences or presences directly matter.  
- **Typical + unspecified** → open-world; observations mainly inform quality, exception handling, and norm completeness, not hard compliance.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG

**What it can establish.**  
OC-DFG summarizes directly-follows relations per object type with frequencies. It is well suited to:
- checking whether **mandatory eventual relations** have a strong direct proxy (e.g., Create Order -> Validate Order, Pack Package -> Dispatch Package);  
- assessing whether **typical flows** (e.g., Validate -> Close, Issue Invoice -> Record Payment) are common;  
- spotting **forbidden sequences** such as Cancel -> Dispatch, even when intermediate events are omitted.

**What it cannot establish.**  
OC-DFG cannot:
- distinguish between “never happens” and “happens but separated by intermediate unmodeled events”;  
- express exceptions explicitly (e.g., write-offs, credit notes) unless they appear as separate paths;  
- guarantee the **eventual** satisfaction of a rule like N1 when the rule is encoded as a single directly-follows edge.

In particular, N1’s “should eventually enter validation” and N2’s “Validate -> Close is typical” are **eventual semantics**, but the OC-DFG summary approximates them via single directly-follows edges. Thus:
- 98/100 Create -> Validate does not prove that 2 Orders were never validated; it only shows that they lack the **direct** edge.  
- 83/100 Validate -> Close may underestimate compliance with eventual closure because closure could be delayed or separated by invoicing or fulfilment.

This is the explicit danger of interpreting an “eventually expected” business rule as a directly-follows rule: we overstate nonconformance when unmodeled intermediate events exist.

### OTG

**What it can establish.**  
The OTG describes structural relationships between object types: interactions and cobirth/other lifecycle relations. With the norm:
- T1 and T2 assert mandatory interactions (Order–Item for Add Item, Package–Delivery for Dispatch);  
- T3 defines a typical Invoice–Payment interaction;  
- T6 forbids Order–Payment cobirths.

Given the observations:
- The presence of **one Order–Payment cobirth** directly violates T6 under closed-world semantics;  
- `(Invoice, object_interaction, Payment)` with weight 92 aligns with typical N5/T3 behavior;  
- `(Package, object_interaction, Delivery)` with weight 200 suggests that package–delivery structural links are in place even when ET–OT edges for Dispatch–Delivery are missing.

**What it cannot establish.**  
OTG alone cannot:
- tell which **events** created or used these relationships;  
- distinguish legitimate asymmetries (e.g., unapplied cash Payment without Invoice) from violations, because T3 is only typical;  
- explain temporal ordering or lifecycle completeness (e.g., whether codeath is expected or problematic).

Counts help: a **single T6 violation** is important despite being numerically small because forbidden rules are closed-world, while 18 Invoices lacking Invoice–Payment interactions are mostly explained by allowed exceptions and snapshot limitations.

### ET–OT

**What it can establish.**  
ET–OT edges connect events to objects. With the norm:
- E1–E8 encode **mandatory links** for many event–object pairs;  
- E9 is unspecified;  
- E10 forbids Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice.

From the observations:
- E1, E3, E4, E5, and E7 appear fully satisfied (100 Create–Order, 1450 Add Item–Item, 200 Pack–Package, 200 Dispatch–Package, 93 Record Payment–Payment).  
- E2 is slightly violated (1448 vs expected 1450 Add Item–Order).  
- E6 is slightly violated (198 vs expected 200 Dispatch–Delivery), matching the contextual note about 2 Dispatch events with only carrier attributes.  
- E8 is violated once (one Payment without Invoice), though business experts indicate unapplied cash should be allowed but is not explicitly typed.  
- E10 is violated once, but clearly due to a join error.

**What it cannot establish.**  
ET–OT cannot:
- by itself, classify unapplied cash correctly when the event type does not distinguish it;  
- tell whether an Invoice without a subsequent Record Payment has not been paid yet or is paid off-log;  
- decide whether missing Dispatch–Delivery edges are design choices (Delivery via attributes) or errors.

Relations can be conforming at one abstraction but suspicious at another. For example:
- `(Package, object_interaction, Delivery)` with weight 200 (OTG) suggests full coverage of Package–Delivery interactions, yet the ET–OT view shows only 198 Dispatch–Delivery edges; this is consistent if some interactions occur at other events or are inferred from attributes.  
- OC-DFG’s 198/200 Pack->Dispatch matches N4’s intent well but, combined with ET–OT’s Dispatch–Delivery gap, draws attention to the two Dispatch events missing Delivery objects.

## 4. Mandatory, forbidden, typical, and unregulated behavior

### Mandatory behavior (normatively required)

- **Order validation eventuality (N1)**: Every newly created standard Order should eventually be validated. The 98/100 direct `Create -> Validate` edges support strong but not perfect adherence; for the remaining 2 Orders this behavior is either missing or only indirectly visible.  
- **Package packing before dispatch (N4)**: Externally shipped Packages must follow `Pack Package -> Dispatch Package`. The OC-DFG count 198/200 suggests high but not complete coverage; missing edges may be abstraction mismatches if alternative packing events exist.  
- **Event–object links (E1, E3, E4, E5, E7)**: Create Order–Order, Add Item–Item, Pack–Package, Dispatch–Package, and Record Payment–Payment are consistently present, so mandatory behavior appears satisfied there.  
- **Add Item–Order & Dispatch–Delivery (E2, E6)**: These are mandatory but show small gaps; for the missing cases they fall into “missing mandatory behavior” unless later evidence shows they are out-of-scope variants or logging issues.

### Forbidden behavior

- **Cancel Order -> Dispatch Package (N3)**: Any occurrence of this sequence is forbidden; the 2 observed Orders are treated as violations unless later reclassified as logging errors.  
- **(Order, object_cobirth, Payment) (T6)**: Cobirth of Orders and Payments is forbidden; the single observed case is a violation unless the Payment is misclassified.  
- **Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice (E10)**: Forbidden relation; the one observed edge is almost certainly a data-quality problem rather than a real business event but remains a violation of the normative ET–OT rules.

### Typical behavior

- **Validate Order -> Close Order (N2)**: 83 Orders follow this typical path; 17 do not, but these may still be conforming variants involving invoicing, fulfilment, or cancellation.  
- **Issue Invoice -> Record Payment (N5, T3)**: 92 Invoices follow this typical pattern with an Invoice–Payment interaction; 18 do not, and those exceptions include legitimate write-offs and open credit terms.  
- **Invoice–Payment interactions (T3)**: 92 typical edges appear; the remaining Invoices may be open, written off, or missing links.

### Unregulated (unspecified) behavior

- **Reopen Order -> Close Order (N6)**: Reopenings and subsequent closures are explicitly outside the norm; this is unregulated but likely legitimate rework.  
- **Validate Order -> Issue Invoice**: This path is not encoded but aligns with the described presence of intermediate invoicing steps between validation and closure.  
- **Issue Invoice -> Write Off**: These flows are mentioned as legitimate exceptions but not assigned a status; they function as typical exceptions.  
- **Create Order -> Manual Compliance Review**, and **Manual Compliance Review links to RiskCase and Customer (E9 + Customer link)**: These reflect risk/compliance handling that the norm does not regulate; they appear benign.  
- **Package–Delivery codeath edges**: Codeath is explicitly not covered by the norm and is therefore unregulated; observed edges are treated as acceptable modelling.

Many of these unregulated behaviors would move into “typical” or “mandatory” if the organization decides to encode them explicitly (e.g., treating Validate->Issue Invoice as typical for credit-based Orders).

## 5. PM4Py conformance code sketch

```python
import pm4py

# Assumptions:
# - ocel: OCEL log
# - norm_ocdfg: dict keyed by (object_type, source_event, target_event) -> {"status": "mandatory"/"forbidden"/"typical"/"unspecified", ...}
# - norm_otg: structure (e.g., tuple or namespace) holding normative OTG edges and their statuses
# - norm_etot: structure holding normative ET–OT edges and their statuses
# The status metadata is maintained OUTSIDE the bare PM4Py graph structures.

# Discover observed models
ocdfg_obs = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
otg_obs = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot_obs = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)

# Run PM4Py's built-in conformance diagnostics
ocdfg_conf = pm4py.conformance_ocdfg(ocel, norm_ocdfg)
otg_conf = pm4py.conformance_otg(ocel, norm_otg)
etot_conf = pm4py.conformance_etot(ocel, norm_etot)

# Semantic classification layer built ON TOP of PM4Py diagnostics

def classify_relation(status, obs_count, expected_count, violation_flag, context=None):
    """
    Map normative status + PM4Py diagnostics to semantic deviation class.
    Does not overwrite PM4Py results; only adds a higher-level label.
    """
    if status == "forbidden":
        if obs_count > 0:
            # Distinguish likely data errors by external heuristics if available in 'context'
            if context and context.get("suspected_data_error"):
                return "likely data-quality error"
            return "forbidden behavior"
        return "conforming"

    if status == "mandatory":
        if violation_flag or (expected_count is not None and obs_count < expected_count):
            # Let caller decide if this is more likely abstraction mismatch or true missing behavior
            if context and context.get("abstraction_mismatch"):
                return "abstraction mismatch"
            return "missing mandatory behavior"
        return "conforming"

    if status == "typical":
        if expected_count is not None and obs_count < expected_count:
            return "typical-pattern absence"
        return "conforming or typical"

    # status == "unspecified"
    if obs_count > 0:
        return "unexpected but unregulated behavior"
    return "outside normative scope"


def add_semantic_layer_ocdfg(ocdfg_conf, norm_ocdfg):
    semantic = {}
    # Exact structure of ocdfg_conf["diagnostics"] depends on PM4Py version;
    # we assume an iterable of relations with counts and violation flags.
    for rel, diag in ocdfg_conf.get("diagnostics", {}).items():
        norm_meta = norm_ocdfg.get(rel, {})
        status = norm_meta.get("status", "unspecified")
        obs_count = diag.get("observed_count", 0)
        expected = norm_meta.get("expected_count")  # optional
        violation_flag = diag.get("violation", False)
        context = norm_meta.get("context", {})
        semantic_class = classify_relation(status, obs_count, expected, violation_flag, context)
        semantic[rel] = {
            "status": status,
            "semantic_class": semantic_class,
            "pm4py_diag": diag,
        }
    return semantic


def add_semantic_layer_generic(conf_result, norm_spec):
    """
    Generic helper for OTG and ET–OT, assuming:
    - conf_result["diagnostics"] iterates over relations/edges
    - norm_spec[edge] -> {"status": ...}
    """
    semantic = {}
    for edge, diag in conf_result.get("diagnostics", {}).items():
        norm_meta = norm_spec.get(edge, {})
        status = norm_meta.get("status", "unspecified")
        obs_count = diag.get("observed_count", 0)
        expected = norm_meta.get("expected_count")
        violation_flag = diag.get("violation", False)
        context = norm_meta.get("context", {})
        semantic_class = classify_relation(status, obs_count, expected, violation_flag, context)
        semantic[edge] = {
            "status": status,
            "semantic_class": semantic_class,
            "pm4py_diag": diag,
        }
    return semantic


# Build semantic layers; PM4Py results are kept intact and referenced
ocdfg_semantic = add_semantic_layer_ocdfg(ocdfg_conf, norm_ocdfg)
otg_semantic = add_semantic_layer_generic(otg_conf, norm_otg)
etot_semantic = add_semantic_layer_generic(etot_conf, norm_etot)

results = {
    "ocdfg": {
        "observed": ocdfg_obs,
        "conformance": ocdfg_conf,
        "semantic": ocdfg_semantic,
    },
    "otg": {
        "observed": otg_obs,
        "conformance": otg_conf,
        "semantic": otg_semantic,
    },
    "etot": {
        "observed": etot_obs,
        "conformance": etot_conf,
        "semantic": etot_semantic,
    },
}
```

## 6. Norm-completeness assessment

1. **Eventual versus directly-follows semantics for Order validation and closure (N1, N2)**  
   - **Risk**: Encoding eventual rules as single directly-follows edges makes intermediate or alternative legitimate paths look nonconformant (e.g., Orders with pre-validation review or Orders that go through invoicing before closure).  
   - **Clarification**: Express N1 and N2 explicitly as “eventually Validate” and “Validate should eventually lead to Close (possibly via Invoice, fulfilment, or cancellation)” and separate directly-follows patterns as analytical heuristics, not hard rules.  
   - **Proposed status**: Keep the eventual rule **mandatory** (must eventually validate) and **typical** for the direct or near-direct `Validate -> Close` pattern.

2. **Incomplete specification of Invoice payment and unapplied cash handling (N5, T3, E8)**  
   - **Risk**: E8’s strict requirement that every Record Payment link to an Invoice, combined with the note that unapplied cash is legitimate but not specially typed, creates apparent violations and hinders clear reconciliation.  
   - **Clarification**: Introduce an explicit event type or flag for unapplied cash and adjust E8 to: mandatory Invoice link for regular payments, forbidden for unapplied cash events, and typical Invoice–Payment interactions at the OTG level.  
   - **Proposed status**: **Mandatory** for regular `Record Payment`–Invoice, **forbidden** for Sensor/inapplicable joins, and **typical** for Invoice–Payment OTG edges, with unapplied cash explicitly **unspecified/allowed** as a documented exception type.

3. **Reopen Order semantics left unspecified (N6)**  
   - **Risk**: Without guidance, different teams may reopen Orders for both minor and major changes, or may avoid reopening where it is warranted, leading to inconsistent audit trails and unclear accountability for rework.  
   - **Clarification**: Define when reopening is allowed (e.g., only before invoicing or shipment, only with justification) and how it should typically be resolved (e.g., must eventually close or cancel again).  
   - **Proposed status**: Treat a “Reopen -> Close or Cancel” lifecycle as **typical** for approved rework scenarios, while remaining **forbidden** for certain advanced stages if the business deems them immutable.

4. **Risk and manual compliance review integration (E9 and related OC-DFG relations)**  
   - **Risk**: Manual Compliance Review is unspecified, yet we see `Create Order -> Manual Compliance Review` and OTG links to RiskCase and Customer. Without a normative stance, some risk-critical reviews may be omitted, while others may be overused without proper criteria.  
   - **Clarification**: Specify when a Manual Compliance Review is **mandatory** (e.g., for high-risk customers or large Orders), when it is **typical** (e.g., medium-risk), and when it is merely allowed. Decide whether linking to Customer should be required.  
   - **Proposed status**: For defined high-risk conditions, make the Review **mandatory** (with explicit ET–OT and OC-DFG rules); otherwise keep it **unspecified/typical** and clearly documented.

5. **Package–Delivery alignment and treatment of attribute-only deliveries (N4, T2, E6)**  
   - **Risk**: The norm requires Pack->Dispatch and Dispatch->Delivery relations but does not say whether Delivery must be modeled as an object versus as attributes. This leads to ambiguous interpretation of the 2 Dispatch events with carrier references but no Delivery object.  
   - **Clarification**: Decide whether Delivery must always be a first-class object for external shipments; if so, tighten E6 and T2 and treat attribute-only deliveries as nonconformant. Otherwise, relax E6 to “typical” and explicitly allow attribute-only carriers.  
   - **Proposed status**: For regulated external shipments, make a Delivery object **mandatory**; for internal or test shipments, either mark them out-of-scope or explicitly **unspecified**.

6. **Treatment of lifecycle codeath and other advanced object relations (codeath not covered, T6 only for cobirth)**  
   - **Risk**: Codeath edges like Package–Delivery may proliferate without clear interpretation, confusing lifecycle analytics (e.g., lead-time metrics) and leaving potential dependencies (like simultaneous cancellation) ungoverned. Meanwhile, only cobirth (T6) is regulated.  
   - **Clarification**: Document when codeath between objects is expected (e.g., Package and Delivery ending at confirmation) and when it is problematic (e.g., Order and Payment ending together could also be contentious). Extend the norm to cover selected codeath patterns if they have compliance impact.  
   - **Proposed status**: Keep Package–Delivery codeath explicitly **unspecified/typical** with documentation; consider making problematic codeath patterns (e.g., Order–Payment codeath, if relevant) explicitly **forbidden**.

7. **Sensor and other non-business event linkages to financial objects (E10 and beyond)**  
   - **Risk**: E10 only addresses Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice, but similar misjoins could affect other non-financial events (e.g., IoT measurements, telemetry) and other financial objects, undermining audit trails and KPIs.  
   - **Clarification**: Generalize the policy to restrict non-business telemetry events from linking to financial objects, and require explicit, documented integration patterns when such joins are intended.  
   - **Proposed status**: Make such telemetry–finance links broadly **forbidden** unless covered by a documented, narrower **typical** rule for specific integrations.

## 7. Investigation and remediation plan

### Immediate containment

- **Cancel -> Dispatch violations (N3)**: Stop any further logistics activity for the 2 affected Orders and their Packages; ensure that the current system prevents dispatch actions on cancelled Orders via hard checks.  
- **Order–Payment cobirth (T6)**: Temporarily flag similar patterns in real time (Orders and Payments created in the same event) and require manual authorization before such transactions are allowed to post.

### Data repair

- **Fix Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice joins (E10)**: Correct the join logic that reused numeric identifiers, remove the erroneous Sensor–Invoice edge, and scan/repair the entire dataset for analogous issues.  
- **Repair missing ET–OT links (E2, E6, E8)**: For the 2 Add Item events without Orders, the 2 Dispatch events without Deliveries, and the single Payment without Invoice, attempt to reconstruct the missing links from raw source systems or, failing that, explicitly annotate them as incomplete.  
- **Validate OC-DFG anomalies (N1, N2)**: For the Orders missing direct Create->Validate or Validate->Close, verify whether the eventual events exist and, if so, annotate them as abstraction mismatches rather than hard violations.

### Process investigation

- **Investigate Cancel -> Dispatch root causes**: Determine whether these arose from late cancellation, user override, or system defects; quantify any financial and customer impact; and adjust process or controls accordingly.  
- **Review cobirth usage**: For the Order–Payment cobirth case, confirm whether a legitimate instant-purchase process exists and, if so, whether it has adequate risk and fraud controls.  
- **Analyse unpaid and written-off Invoices**: Segment the 14 Invoices without successors by age and credit terms, and check the 4 write-offs for correct authorization and root cause (e.g., dispute, insolvency, small-balance policy).  
- **Check Add Item without Order scenarios**: Validate whether any business function legitimately creates Items without Orders; if none, treat these as modelling or integration errors to be eliminated.

### Norm revision

- **Clarify eventual vs direct rules**: Update N1 and N2 to reflect eventual semantics explicitly and codify acceptable variants such as Validate->Issue Invoice->Close.  
- **Formalize payment and unapplied cash handling**: Introduce explicit rules for unapplied cash events, adjust E8 accordingly, and encode write-off flows as a recognized typical exception pattern.  
- **Define compliance-review expectations**: Specify when Manual Compliance Review is mandatory or typical and whether Customer linkage is required.  
- **Extend sensor/telemetry policies**: Generalize E10-like restrictions to other non-business events linking to financial objects.

### Monitoring

- **Regular conformance dashboards**: Run PM4Py-based OC-DFG, OTG, and ET–OT conformance analyses periodically, with semantic classification layers highlighting forbidden behavior and missing mandatory behavior.  
- **Exception trend tracking**: Monitor frequencies of Cancel->Dispatch, cobirths, missing ET–OT links, and unpaid Invoices over time to detect regressions or emerging risks.  
- **Alerting on high-severity patterns**: Configure automated alerts when any forbidden relations (N3, T6, E10) or critical missing mandatory links (E8) appear in new data.

The two highest-severity findings—Cancel->Dispatch (N3) and Order–Payment cobirth (T6)—should drive prioritization for containment, data repair, and process investigation before broad norm revision and monitoring enhancements.

## 8. Uncertainty statement

- **Robust conclusions**: The existence of Cancel->Dispatch edges (N3), an Order–Payment cobirth (T6), and a Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice link (E10) are clear against the stated norm, even if some are later reclassified as data-quality issues. Mandatory ET–OT links that are fully satisfied (E1, E3, E4, E5, E7) also robustly indicate good conformance.  
- **Dependence on norm incompleteness**: Interpretations of Validate->Issue Invoice, Issue->Write Off, Reopen->Close, Manual Compliance Review relations, and Package–Delivery codeath rely on the norm’s open-world treatment of unspecified patterns; different organizations might choose to encode them as mandatory or typical, changing their status.  
- **Dependence on suspected data-quality issues**: The Sensor Heartbeat–Invoice edge, the Payment without Invoice, and small gaps in E2 and E6 may stem from extraction, joining, or modelling errors rather than genuine process violations; their impact depends on how successfully data can be repaired.  
- **Limits of graph summaries**: From OC-DFG, OTG, and ET–OT alone we cannot decide whether the 2 Orders without direct Create->Validate edges were validated later, whether the 14 open Invoices are within credit terms or overdue, or whether some interactions (e.g., Delivery represented only by attributes) are intentionally modelled outside the object graphs; answering these requires detailed traces, raw event payloads, and business policy context in addition to graph-level diagnostics.
