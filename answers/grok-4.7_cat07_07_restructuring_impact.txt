# 1. Mechanism map

Management’s own routing table does not show a net drop in handovers. Counted handovers rise from about 343 to 359 per 100 cases. A median-wait exposure index (frequency × stated median wait) rises from about 588 to 722 hour-equivalents per 100 cases, about +1.3 hours per case. That increment is small beside team median cycle times of 6.8–8.6 days, so median cycle time can barely move while queue tails, rework loops, and segment gaps change the process that customers and staff actually feel. Volume is about 2,100 cases per month: North 39%, South 36%, East 25%. Any all-team average is mostly a North/South result.

| Path | Now (per 100) | Management estimate | Exposure change |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Bot intake → local intake | 92 × 0.3h, rework 3% | 92 × 0.3h | unchanged |
| Local intake → local caseworker | 88 × 1.1h, 6% | 83 × 1.0h | small decline |
| Caseworker → tech | 36 × 3.2h, 9% | 41 × 4.0h to Technical Shared Service | +49 hour-equivalents |
| Tech → same/local caseworker | 31 × 1.6h, 8% | 36 × 2.2h | +30 hour-equivalents |
| Caseworker → approval | 42 × 2.4h, 7% | 49 × 3.0h shared approval | +46 hour-equivalents |
| Approval → implementation | 39 × 1.5h, 5% | 43 × 1.8h | +19 hour-equivalents |
| Caseworker → legal/risk | 9 × 9.6h, 14% | 12 × 10.5h | +40 hour-equivalents |
| Cross-team | 6 × 8.9h, 18% | 3 × 6.0h | −35 hour-equivalents |

Current tech load is uneven: N_Tech 71%, S_Tech 68%, E_Tech 82%. Pooled technical utilization is about 74% of three people. A 15% relative rise lands near 85% only if handling time per referral stays constant and no extra work is added. Legal is at 93% and Risk at 91%. Moving referrals from 9 to 12 per 100 cases is about +33% demand (about 189 to 252 referrals per month). Without added specialist capacity, implied utilization exceeds 1 and the 9.6h → 10.5h wait is not a stable-queue outcome. The routing rule also names security, and no security resource is in the current organization. Difficult Quality Check is moved into the shared queue and does not appear in the frequency table, so technical load is understated.

**Nominal handover reduction.** The reduction that is actually specified sits on two edges: cross-team cover (6 → 3 per 100) and a small intake-to-caseworker decline (88 → 83). Local specialist loops are replaced, not removed. Caseworker ↔ tech interactions go from 67 to 77 per 100 cases, and the return edge is still a subcontracting pattern (work leaves the caseworker and comes back). Approval interactions rise from 81 to 92 per 100. In the handover-of-work network the heaviest new edges are central resources. In the subcontracting network, Technical Shared Service and the shared approval queue become the dominant subcontractors. A lower cross-team count can coexist with a denser central-handover graph.

**Central queue formation.** Three mechanisms form queues. First, all high-complexity cases are forced into Technical Shared Service, so arrivals become a policy variable, not a local judgment. Second, legal, risk, and security cases share one specialist queue while Legal/Risk are already near capacity. Third, one local supervisor post is removed while medium/high-value approvals rise from 42 to 49 per 100. Three supervisors currently absorb about 882 approvals a month. Removing one-third of that capacity while demand rises about 17% requires on the order of 40% more throughput from the remaining approvers just to hold load steady. Batching in a shared queue can raise measured utilization and still lengthen wait. Pooled technical staff can smooth East’s 82% peak by using North/South slack, which is the main queueing benefit on offer. Pooling technical assessment with legal triage and difficult Quality Check raises service-time variability and undoes much of that pooling gain. Median waits of 4.0h and 2.2h describe the center of the queue; at utilizations above roughly 85–90%, the upper tail of wait grows much faster than the median.

**Specialist utilization.** Utilization of N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech should rise if the same three people absorb 41 rather than 36 technical referrals per 100 cases, plus uncounted Quality Check. That is a real capacity-use gain and it is visible in resource service time. It is also the mechanism that creates the bottleneck: the slack that now absorbs East’s overload and absence cover (cross-team is already 6 per 100, 8.9h, 18% rework) gets consumed as steady-state load. Legal and Risk have almost no slack to give. A utilization KPI near 85% for technical staff can be read as success while Legal/Risk queue time and high-complexity sojourn time deteriorate.

**Rework.** Today, local tech handovers rework at 8–9%, local approval at 5–7%, legal/risk at 14%, and cross-team at 18%. Fewer cross-team handovers remove the worst rework edge. More legal/risk detections raise exposure to the second-worst edge. Standardized specialist advice can cut rework where local practice was inconsistent, which is the plausible East effect (East rework 11.5% versus North 8.2%). The same standardization can raise rework where regional convention knowledge now prevents it: North and South show the tighter local loops and the better rework, SLA, and customer satisfaction. Rework on Quality Check, Specialist Review, and Implementation is the activity-level signal. Case-level rework is already 9.3% volume-weighted; the tail teams and the tail activities matter more than that average.

**Local expertise loss.** Embedded specialists now return work to the same local caseworker (31 per 100, 1.6h). That loop is where regional convention knowledge is applied. Routing every high-complexity case centrally, and letting any team pull advice from a shared queue, shortens the activity profile of local caseworkers. `local_expertise_flag` should fall first on high-complexity and on customer segments that need language or regional accommodation. The performance effect is delayed: early cycle time can look acceptable because specialists still carry the knowledge, then rework and repeat specialist referrals rise as local staff see fewer hard cases. Activity-based resource similarity between each team’s caseworkers and the former local tech should fall. Role discovery should show a narrower local-caseworker role and a separate central role.

**Role clarity.** A shared approval queue plus one fewer local supervisor weakens the named accountable approver. A single shared queue that holds technical review, legal review, risk review, and difficult Quality Check mixes roles that activity profiles currently keep apart (local tech versus Specialist_Legal and Specialist_Risk). Handover reason, `reassignment_count`, and cases that touch several central resources before Implementation are the log signatures. Working-together scores inside North, South, and East should fall. Links from many caseworkers to a few central resources should rise, often as serial handovers rather than joint work. Outcome quality can hold while ownership of a stalled case becomes harder to see in the log because the resource on the long wait is a queue, not a team supervisor.

**Resilience to absence.** Today, absence is absorbed by cross-team cover, which is slow and rework-prone, and case notes differ by location. A staffed pool of three technical specialists absorbs one person’s absence as a one-third capacity cut spread across all sites, which is better for the site that lost its only tech and worse as a correlated shock for the other two. East, which has no second intake resource in the roster and already runs E_Tech at 82%, is the site that gains resilience on ordinary tech absence. The shared queue is a common-mode dependency: routing-rule errors, queue outages, or loss of a legal/risk specialist hit every team in the same week. Removing a supervisor reduces the number of people who can clear medium/high-value cases when another approver is out. Resilience improves for local tech absence only while pool utilization stays low enough to have surge capacity. The utilization target spends that surge capacity in advance.

**Fairness across teams and customer segments.** East enters with the worst median (8.6d), 90th percentile (22.8d), rework (11.5%), SLA miss (9.8%), and satisfaction (3.8/5), and with the hottest local specialist. North enters with the best of each and with spare N_Tech capacity. Pooling specialist time can move East’s delay toward the three-team pool and move North and South toward the same pool, which compresses team gaps. The cases that pay for this are high-complexity, legal/risk/security, medium/high-value approvals, and customers who need regional or language accommodation. Low-complexity local cases can get faster, because they no longer wait behind a local tech who is in a complex case. The all-team median can improve while the high-complexity 90th percentile and a specific `customer_segment` worsen. That is a mix effect, and it is invisible in a single cycle-time number. Priority is in the log; a shared FIFO queue will not reproduce today’s implicit local priority and can put a high-priority East case behind a larger North arrival stream (North is 39% of volume).

# 2. Scenario forecasts

Ranges below use the management table as the optimistic operating point, not as the baseline forecast. Capacity arithmetic on Legal/Risk and on approvals is the main reason the central and adverse pictures diverge from that table.

### Optimistic

Management adds a small amount of Legal/Risk/security capacity, or the extra “detections” are short screens. Technical Shared Service takes technical assessment only. Difficult Quality Check and legal triage stay outside that queue. Case notes are standardized before routing changes. One local senior remains a named owner for each case. Specialists rotate back into teams on a fixed share of cases so local skill does not drop to zero.

- **Handover pattern.** Cross-team edges fall toward 3 per 100. Technical and approval edges rise, but return paths are one hop. Subcontracting concentrates on Technical Shared Service without a second hop to legal unless `required_specialty` requires it. Bot edges stay dominant and must be excluded when reading structural change.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern.** Technical wait stays near 4h median and under about 8h at the 90th percentile. Approval wait stays near 3h because batching is limited and two remaining supervisors are not also the escalation path for every team. Legal/Risk wait stays near 10–12h because capacity was added with the extra referrals.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** East median moves from 8.6d toward 7.5–8.0d and East’s 90th percentile moves down from 22.8d. North and South medians stay within about 0.3d of today. All-team median is flat to slightly better. The 90th percentile does not rise for high-complexity cases.
- **Rework and quality pattern.** Cross-team rework shrinks. Edge rework after technical advice stays at or below 9%. East rework moves toward North. `outcome` mix does not shift toward incomplete closes.
- **Role clarity and accountability.** Role discovery shows a clean local-intake role, a local-caseworker role, a technical-shared role, and separate legal/risk roles. `reassignment_count` does not trend up. Each case still has a local resource at Implementation and Close.
- **Fairness implications.** The East–North gap on SLA miss narrows. Gaps by `customer_segment` and `location` stay within today’s spread. High-complexity cases do not become the only segment with longer sojourn time.
- **Early warning signs that this scenario is failing.** Technical Shared Service median wait above 5h, any week with Legal/Risk utilization above 95%, rising `reassignment_count`, or North/South rework after technical return above 10%.

### Central

The restructuring is implemented largely as specified, without a matching capacity add, but staff keep some informal local advice and the shared queue does not yet absorb every hard Quality Check. This is the most likely path if the organization follows the written routing rules and the current headcount.

- **Handover pattern.** Total counted handovers sit slightly above today’s 343 per 100. The subcontracting network shows caseworker → Technical Shared Service → caseworker as a standard pair, plus a second subcontract into shared approval on medium/high-value cases. Cross-team frequency falls, but a new class of “queue-to-queue” handovers appears (technical pool to legal/risk pool, technical pool to shared approval) that the management table does not list. High-complexity variants lose Local Technical Assessment as a local activity and gain a shared-service activity.
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern.** Technical median wait meets or exceeds the stated 4.0h, with a 90th percentile well above 8h once utilization moves through the mid-80s and difficult checks land in the same queue. Shared approval median exceeds 3h in weeks when one remaining supervisor is absent. Legal/Risk cannot absorb +33% referrals at a 0.9h median-wait increase; the realistic central picture is a growing backlog on that queue, with median wait drifting up over successive weeks rather than stepping once to 10.5h. Security referrals, if the rule is applied literally, sit in that same backlog with no named specialist.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** All-team median stays near the volume-weighted team-median level of roughly 7.5d, and can improve slightly because low-complexity cases (the majority) skip a busy local tech. East median improves by a fraction of a day. North and South medians worsen by a fraction of a day. The 90th percentile stays near or above today’s volume-weighted level of about 19d and rises for `case_complexity` = high. Calendar time, customer contact, and rework loops continue to dominate the multi-day cycle; the published handover medians are not what moves the 90th percentile.
- **Rework and quality pattern.** Cross-team rework falls. Rework after technical advice drifts from about 8–9% toward 10–12% on complex and region-specific cases, because advice is less often tied to the same caseworker. Legal/risk rework stays high and applies to more cases (12 per 100 instead of 9). Net case rework can look stable near 9% while East improves and North/South complex cases worsen.
- **Role clarity and accountability.** Working-together inside the local team thins. Many cases show a long sojourn on a shared resource with no local supervisor event. `handover_reason` becomes a mix of “policy routing” and “queue pull” rather than a named expertise need. Medium/high-value cases wait on a shared approval resource who did not see the local customer contact.
- **Fairness implications.** East’s SLA miss can fall from 9.8% while North’s rises from 6.5%, so the all-team SLA miss (today about 7.6% volume-weighted) looks unchanged. Customers in segments that need regional accommodation see more handovers and lower satisfaction. High-priority cases from the smaller East stream wait behind North’s larger arrival volume unless the queue is priority-aware.
- **Early warning signs.** Week-on-week growth in open age of the Legal/Risk queue, technical-service 90th-percentile wait above two working days, approval wait above 6h median, rising rework_flag on cases with `local_expertise_flag` false, and a widening cycle-time gap between low and high complexity inside the same team.

### Adverse

Technical Shared Service also absorbs legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Check. Informal local advice stops, so referrals overshoot 41 per 100. The supervisor cut and the approval-volume increase happen in the same step. No specialist capacity is added. Case notes stay inconsistent.

- **Handover pattern.** Technical referrals move well above 41 per 100 because early local filtering is gone. Many of those cases are handed again to Legal/Risk or back for missing regional context, so subcontracting becomes multi-hop. Cross-team handovers may stay low in the metric while reassignment_count rises inside the shared queue. Variants for high-complexity cases lengthen (specialist review, queue return, second specialist review, rework, quality check).
- **Queue/waiting-time pattern.** Technical utilization is pushed toward the low-90s and the queue’s 90th percentile stretches into days, not hours. Legal/Risk demand above capacity produces a backlog that does not clear inside the week; median wait moves from about 10h toward multi-day. Shared approval becomes the second chronic queue. Bot and local-intake waits stay short, so a casual reading of “most handovers are fast” remains true.
- **Cycle-time distribution.** Low-complexity medians still look acceptable. High-complexity median moves up by days. East’s early gain is given back once the pool is saturated by all three teams. North’s 90th percentile, today 16.4d, moves toward East’s current 22.8d. SLA miss rises first in high priority, high complexity, and legal/risk specialties.
- **Rework and quality pattern.** Rework after specialist return exceeds today’s 8–14% band on complex cases. Quality Check, now centralized and late, finds defects after Implementation has started, so rework cycles add a full loop rather than a local correction. `outcome` shifts toward repeat contact and incomplete closure for region-specific segments.
- **Role clarity and accountability.** Role discovery merges technical staff, triage, and difficult quality work into one diffuse role, or shows those resources oscillating between activities. Local supervisors no longer appear on a large share of medium/high-value cases. Escalations have no stable resource. Staff stop using specialist routing as designed and invent side channels; those side channels reappear as cross-team or same-team hidden loops and are easy to miss if analysis only tracks the official shared-service activities.
- **Fairness implications.** The segment that loses is identifiable in the log: high `case_complexity`, `required_specialty` in legal/risk/security, and customer segments concentrated at a location whose conventions the pool does not know. East can be worse off than today if its cases sit behind North’s volume in a saturated FIFO queue. Satisfaction gaps widen even if the volume-weighted satisfaction score near 4.1/5 barely changes.
- **Early warning signs.** Open backlog on Technical Shared Service or Legal/Risk higher at week end than week start, specialist utilization above 90% for two weeks, surge in `reassignment_count`, collapse in `local_expertise_flag` on new complex cases, and side-channel activities that are absent from the target process map.

# 3. Leading and lagging indicators

Leading indicators can be read from the live log within days or a few weeks. Lagging indicators need completed cases and, for learning, more than one quarter.

Bot_Intake → local intake will remain the highest-frequency handover. Interpret specialist structure on a view that separates bot edges, or the central-queue change will be numerically swamped.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

Cycle time today is already a skewed distribution: North 6.8d median versus 16.4d at the 90th percentile, South 7.4d versus 18.2d, East 8.6d versus 22.8d. The proposed median handover waits are measured in hours. An all-team average, and even an all-team median, can improve or hold steady under every risk below.

**Tail latency at high utilization.** Pooled queues change the 90th percentile and the oldest-waiting case long before they change the median. Legal at 93% and Risk at 91% are already in the region where a one-third demand increase creates a backlog that median-wait snapshots understate until the backlog has aged. A technical pool taken from about 74% toward the high 80s or low 90s by uncounted Quality Check behaves the same way. The performance DFG’s mean edge time will lag the operational pain; the edge’s upper quantile and the age of open cases will not.

**High-complexity cases subsidizing the average.** The routing rule sends all high-complexity cases into the shared service and leaves simple eligibility local. If roughly 60% of cases never touch the new queue, their shorter local path pulls the median down. The 36–41% that touch specialists, and the 12% that touch legal/risk, carry longer sojourn, higher rework, and more of the SLA misses. A better average cycle time is then a composition result. Segment the duration distribution by `case_complexity` before reading any improvement.

**Loss of local knowledge on region-specific work.** North and South currently convert local loops into lower rework (8.2% and 9.0%) and higher satisfaction (4.3 and 4.1). That knowledge is not in the activity name Specialist Review; it is in the pairing of a named local tech with a named local caseworker. After centralization, early cases still benefit from specialists who used to sit in the team. Later cases get advice that is procedurally correct and locally wrong. Rework and extra customer contact then add days that never appear in the first month’s average.

**Fairness across teams.** East has the most to gain from other teams’ slack; North has the most to lose from giving up a 71% utilized local tech and a 6.8d median. Because North and South are 75% of volume, an East improvement can be invisible in the average, and a North deterioration will dominate it. The reverse error is also available: a small North gain hides an East queue problem if East’s complex cases wait behind North’s arrivals. Team-level 90th percentiles are the fairness metric. The average is the wrong summary of the same data.

**Fairness across customer segments.** Cases with language or regional accommodation needs are not evenly spread across North, South, and East. Extra handovers on those segments (`location`, `customer_segment`) raise contact count and rework even when the organization’s median falls. Priority fairness has the same shape: a shared queue that maximizes specialist utilization will serve the arrival mix, and North’s volume will set that mix, unless priority is an explicit queue discipline that is then checked in the log.

**Learning and future capacity.** Routing every hard case away from local caseworkers cuts the repetitions through which local technical skill is built. Utilization of the central pool rises now; the cost shows up later as a lower `local_expertise_flag`, a narrower local activity profile, higher referral rates than the 41 per 100 design point, and a pool that cannot be unpicked because the local skill is gone. A short pilot that ends before this lag will overstate the steady state.

**Accountability on the approval path.** Shared approval can make the decision rule more uniform and, at the same time, make it unclear who owns a case that sits between customer contact and implementation. The log will show a resource on the approval event and a long waiting time before it. It will not show a local supervisor in the working-together neighborhood of that case. Rework discovered after approval then has no obvious local owner. Average cycle time does not record that gap; `reassignment_count`, missing supervisor events, and handover_reason do.

**Exception handling and absence.** The design reduces a bad exception path (cross-team cover at 18% rework) by replacing it with a pool that has less spare capacity. Ordinary weeks look calmer. Weeks in which one of three technical specialists or one of two remaining supervisors is absent become correlated delays for every location. Exception variants — Rework after centralized Quality Check, security referrals with no named resource, side-channel advice that bypasses the queue — sit in the variant tail. Filtering to the top few variants, or reporting only mean cycle time, drops them from the picture.

# 5. Pilot design

Run a modified pilot. The full package combines a pooling change that can help East, a Legal/Risk volume increase the current specialists cannot absorb, an unstaffed security category, an uncounted Quality Check move, and a supervisor cut. Those are separable. Piloting them as one treatment makes a negative result impossible to attribute and a positive average impossible to trust.

**Pilot scope.** Phase 0, before any routing change: standardize case notes across locations for at least four weeks, and record a stable baseline. Phase 1, the only structural change in the pilot: create Technical Shared Service from N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech for technical assessment of high-complexity cases, with a capped share of each specialist’s week reserved for local presence so informal advice and learning do not go to zero. Keep Legal Review, Risk Review, security, and difficult Quality Check on their current paths. Keep all three local supervisor posts. Do not turn on the broader legal/risk detection rule and do not add security as a mandatory queue until a named specialist has spare capacity. Phase 2 starts only if Phase 1 guardrails hold: a shared approval queue for medium-value cases, still with all three supervisors, measured separately. High-value cases stay with the local supervisor through Phase 2. Removing a supervisor post is out of scope until approval waits and ownership are shown to be stable with the current headcount.

**Comparison group.** Use a difference-in-differences layout, not a single before/after on all teams. Hold North’s low- and medium-complexity cases on the current local tech path as the comparison group; North is the strongest local system and the one most likely to be harmed, so it is the informative control. Route East’s high-complexity cases into the shared technical service from the start of Phase 1; East is the team most likely to benefit, and the pilot should show that benefit explicitly. Split South by a pre-registered rule (for example, alternating weeks, or a hashed case identifier) so half of South’s high-complexity cases stay with S_Tech locally and half use the shared service. That within-team split separates the pooling effect from a South-specific calendar effect. Do not use all-team averages as the comparison.

**Pre/post observation window.** Baseline the 8–12 weeks before Phase 1 on the same log columns, long enough to cover absence patterns and the current 90th percentile (up to about 23 days), so censored open cases do not dominate. Run Phase 1 for 10–12 weeks. Read queue and handover indicators weekly from week 1. Read completed-case cycle time, rework, and SLA only on cases that both start and finish inside the window, and report open-case age beside them so long cases are not dropped from the result. Schedule a second readout at two quarters for `local_expertise_flag`, local activity mix, and referral rate, even if the routing decision is made earlier. Learning will not show up in the first month.

**Segmentation.** Every guardrail and every outcome is reported, at minimum, by `team` (North, South, East), `case_complexity`, `required_specialty`, `customer_segment`, `location`, and `priority`. Also split by whether the case touched Technical Shared Service, whether `local_expertise_flag` was set, and the variant family (simple local path, technical subcontract, legal/risk path, rework path). Publish the volume in each cell so a shift in mix is visible. A change in the share of high-complexity cases is not a performance change.

**Guardrail metrics.** Pre-register these and review them weekly:

- Technical Shared Service median wait and 90th-percentile wait, and the age of the oldest waiting event.
- Referrals to that service per 100 cases, overall and by team, against the design point of 41.
- Utilization of each specialist, with Quality Check and any triage counted separately from technical assessment.
- Legal and Risk utilization and wait, which should not worsen just because technical routing changed; any drift means scope is leaking.
- Approval wait and the share of medium/high-value cases that still have a local supervisor event.
- Rework rate overall, rework after technical return, and `get_rework_cases_per_activity` for Quality Check, Specialist Review, and Implementation.
- SLA miss and cycle-time 90th percentile by team and by complexity.
- `reassignment_count`, cross-team handover rate, and subcontracting depth.
- `local_expertise_flag` rate on new high-complexity cases.
- Customer-segment SLA miss and outcome mix for regional or language-sensitive segments.

**Staff feedback collection.** Each week, ask local caseworkers, the three techs, Legal, Risk, and supervisors four concrete questions tied to log fields: whether they gave or received informal advice outside the queue, whether a returned case lacked regional context, whether they knew who owned a waiting case, and whether they routed around the shared service. Store answers against `case:concept:name` where the feedback is about a case, so it can be joined to `handover_reason`, wait, and `rework_flag`. Review side-channel activity names in the log monthly; staff workarounds are a process variant, not an anecdote. Customer feedback stays split by segment and location rather than pooled into one satisfaction score.

**Rollback or adjustment triggers.** Any one of these, sustained over two consecutive weeks, pauses expansion and returns the affected segment to the local path:

- Technical Shared Service 90th-percentile wait above two working days, or median wait above 6 hours.
- Referrals above 50 per 100 cases, or a rising referral trend that shows local filtering has collapsed.
- Any specialist utilization above 90% once all activities they perform are included, or Legal/Risk median wait above 16 hours.
- High-complexity 90th-percentile cycle time more than 15% above that segment’s own baseline, on either East or the treated half of South.
- North comparison-group 90th percentile or SLA miss worsening by more than 2 points, which would mean the pilot is leaking load or attention onto the control.
- Rework after technical return above 12%, or Implementation rework above its baseline by 3 points.
- Median `reassignment_count` up by more than 0.5, or a rebound of cross-team handovers toward 6 per 100.
- A customer segment’s SLA miss up by more than 3 points, or staff-reported ownership failures on more than a small minority of waiting cases.
- End-of-week shared-queue backlog larger than the start-of-week backlog for two weeks (the queue is not in steady state).

Rollback is by segment: high-complexity East cases can return to E_Tech without undoing the South experiment, and the South split can be stopped without changing North. Triggering a guardrail blocks Phase 2 and blocks any supervisor reduction.

# 6. Recommendation

Pilot first, and modify the package before the pilot starts. A full rollout of the six changes together is not supported by the current log, and a flat rejection would ignore a real and uneven capacity problem: E_Tech is at 82%, East’s 90th percentile is 22.8 days, and North and South still have technical slack at 71% and 68%.

The part worth testing is a narrow one. Pool technical assessment so East’s high-complexity cases can draw on unused N_Tech and S_Tech time, keep a reserved local presence so the 1.6h same-caseworker loop is not fully destroyed, and standardize notes so a shared specialist is not working from inconsistent case files. Measure that test against a North holdout and a within-South split, by complexity, specialty, and customer segment.

The parts to keep out of the pilot, and out of any later rollout unless new capacity is in place, are the ones the management wait table cannot reconcile with the utilization figures. Legal and Risk are at 91–93%. Sending them from 9 to 12 cases per 100, and adding security, without more specialists, is a backlog, not a 0.9-hour wait increase. Putting difficult Quality Check and legal/risk triage into the same queue as technical assessment spends the pooling benefit on a more variable service mix. Removing a supervisor while approval volume rises from 42 to 49 per 100 cuts approval capacity as demand grows. Those three changes should wait for their own evidence and, in the legal/risk case, for headcount.

Expansion beyond the Phase 1 pilot is conditional. It requires stable weekly backlog, technical 90th-percentile waits inside the guardrail, no leakage of scope onto Legal/Risk, East high-complexity cycle time and SLA improving against East’s own baseline, North and the untreated South half staying inside their baselines, rework after technical return at or below today’s 8–9%, and no segment-level fairness breach. The two-quarter learning readout has to be scheduled before expansion, because a short window will not show local skill loss. If those conditions fail, the appropriate decision is to keep local embedded techs and solve East’s overload by a targeted capacity shift or a partial pool, not by centralizing approvals and specialist policy.

Early averages are a weak signal even if the pilot is going well. In the first weeks, specialist utilization should rise and the all-team median cycle time can look flat or better, because most cases are low-complexity, bot and intake waits do not change, and the hours in the handover table are small next to multi-day sojourn. That pattern does not mean queues are healthy, does not mean North and South were unharmed, does not mean high-complexity or region-specific customers are better off, and does not mean the organization has become more resilient. It also does not forecast the next two quarters: referrals can climb as local advice dries up, and rework can climb as `local_expertise_flag` falls. Read utilization and average cycle time only next to segment 90th percentiles, queue age, rework by edge, reassignment, and team-level gaps. A better average with a worse high-complexity tail, or a better average produced by East’s gain net of North’s loss, is a different operating system from the one the average suggests, and it is not a reason to remove the remaining local supervisor or to widen the shared queue.

# 7. PM4Py analysis sketch

The sketch compares a baseline window with a pilot window on the same columns. Handover frequencies alone are not the decision metric; each central edge is joined to wait, later rework, team, complexity, specialty, and customer segment. Bot edges are reported separately so they do not dominate the network.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TS = "time:timestamp"
RES = "org:resource"

# Priority-specific SLA limits in days. Replace with the real policy table.
SLA_DAYS = {"low": 15, "medium": 12, "high": 8, "urgent": 5}

CENTRAL_RESOURCES = {
    "N_Tech", "S_Tech", "E_Tech",  # after go-live these sit in one pool
    "Specialist_Legal", "Specialist_Risk",
}
BOTS = {"Bot_Intake", "Bot_Eligibility"}

def prepare(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    df = df.copy()
    df[TS] = pd.to_datetime(df[TS])
    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS
    )
    # Service and waiting time on successive events of a case.
    df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
        df, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TS, resource_key=RES,
    )
    return df

def segment(df, team=None, complexity=None, specialty=None,
            segment=None, priority=None):
    out = df
    if team is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            out, "team", [team], level="event", retain=True
        )
    if complexity is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            out, "case_complexity", [complexity], level="case", retain=True
        )
    if specialty is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            out, "required_specialty", [specialty], level="case", retain=True
        )
    if segment is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            out, "customer_segment", [segment], level="case", retain=True
        )
    if priority is not None:
        out = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
            out, "priority", [priority], level="case", retain=True
        )
    return out

def case_table(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    g = df.sort_values(TS).groupby(CASE, as_index=False)
    dur = g[TS].agg(start="min", end="max")
    dur["cycle_days"] = (dur["end"] - dur["start"]).dt.total_seconds() / 86400
    attrs = (
        df.groupby(CASE)[
            ["team", "location", "required_specialty", "priority",
             "case_complexity", "customer_segment", "reassignment_count",
             "local_expertise_flag", "outcome", "rework_flag"]
        ]
        .agg(lambda s: s.dropna().iloc[-1] if len(s.dropna()) else pd.NA)
        .reset_index()
    )
    out = dur.merge(attrs, on=CASE)
    out["sla_days"] = out["priority"].map(SLA_DAYS)
    out["sla_miss"] = out["cycle_days"] > out["sla_days"]
    return out

def quantiles(cases: pd.DataFrame, by) -> pd.DataFrame:
    return (
        cases.groupby(by)["cycle_days"]
        .quantile([0.5, 0.9, 0.95])
        .unstack()
        .rename(columns={0.5: "p50_days", 0.9: "p90_days", 0.95: "p95_days"})
    )

def handover_edges(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Resource handover list with wait and the case's eventual rework flag.
    Native SNA weights do not carry wait, rework, or segment. Keep both views.
    """
    x = df.sort_values([CASE, TS]).copy()
    x["next_res"] = x.groupby(CASE)[RES].shift(-1)
    x["next_act"] = x.groupby(CASE)[ACT].shift(-1)
    x["next_ts"] = x.groupby(CASE)[TS].shift(-1)
    x["next_team"] = x.groupby(CASE)["team"].shift(-1)
    edges = x.dropna(subset=["next_res"]).copy()
    edges["wait_h"] = (edges["next_ts"] - edges[TS]).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    edges["cross_team"] = edges["team"] != edges["next_team"]
    edges["to_central"] = edges["next_res"].isin(CENTRAL_RESOURCES)
    case_rw = df.groupby(CASE)["rework_flag"].max()
    edges["case_rework"] = edges[CASE].map(case_rw)
    return edges

def summarize_handovers(edges: pd.DataFrame, n_cases: int) -> pd.DataFrame:
    human = edges[~edges[RES].isin(BOTS) & ~edges["next_res"].isin(BOTS)]
    rows = (
        human.groupby([RES, "next_res"])
        .agg(
            n=("wait_h", "size"),
            median_wait_h=("wait_h", "median"),
            p90_wait_h=("wait_h", lambda s: s.quantile(0.9)),
            rework_rate=("case_rework", "mean"),
        )
        .reset_index()
    )
    rows["per_100_cases"] = 100 * rows["n"] / n_cases
    return rows.sort_values("n", ascending=False)

def org_views(df: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    # beta thresholds are analysis choices; report the unfiltered graph too.
    return {
        "handover": pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
            df, resource_key=RES
        ),
        "working_together": pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
            df, resource_key=RES
        ),
        "subcontracting": pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
            df, resource_key=RES
        ),
        "resource_similarity": pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
            df, resource_key=RES
        ),
        "roles": pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
            df, resource_key=RES
        ),
    }

def flow_views(df: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_dfg(df)
    perf, p_starts, p_ends = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df)
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(df)
    rework_acts = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(df)
    return {
        "dfg": dfg,
        "performance_dfg": perf,
        "variants": variants,
        "rework_by_activity": rework_acts,
        "n_variants": len(variants),
    }

def service_load(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # get_service_time plus the inserted waiting columns.
    svc = pm4py.get_service_time(df, resource_key=RES)
    by_res = (
        df.groupby(RES)
        .agg(
            events=(ACT, "size"),
            activities=(ACT, lambda s: sorted(set(s))),
        )
    )
    return by_res.assign(service_time=svc)

def variant_shift(before: pd.DataFrame, after: pd.DataFrame, top_n=15):
    vb = pm4py.get_variants(before)
    va = pm4py.get_variants(after)
    # Keep a managed set of frequent baseline variants so a new shared-service
    # path is visible as a frequency move, not only as a longer tail.
    keep = sorted(vb, key=vb.get, reverse=True)[:top_n]
    fb = pm4py.filter_variants(before, keep)
    fa = pm4py.filter_variants(after, [v for v in keep if v in set(va)])
    return fb, fa, vb, va

def fairness_and_tails(cases: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    return {
        "by_team": quantiles(cases, "team"),
        "by_complexity": quantiles(cases, "case_complexity"),
        "by_specialty": quantiles(cases, "required_specialty"),
        "by_segment": quantiles(cases, "customer_segment"),
        "by_team_complexity": quantiles(cases, ["team", "case_complexity"]),
        "sla_by_team": cases.groupby("team")["sla_miss"].mean(),
        "sla_by_segment": cases.groupby(
            ["customer_segment", "location"]
        )["sla_miss"].mean(),
        "rework_by_team_complexity": cases.groupby(
            ["team", "case_complexity"]
        )["rework_flag"].mean(),
        "expertise_rate": cases.groupby(
            ["team", "case_complexity"]
        )["local_expertise_flag"].mean(),
    }

def temporal(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # Case-duration and calendar features for drift across the pilot window.
    return pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
        df, case_id_key=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=TS
    )

def compare(before_df: pd.DataFrame, after_df: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    b, a = prepare(before_df), prepare(after_df)
    b_cases, a_cases = case_table(b), case_table(a)
    b_edges, a_edges = handover_edges(b), handover_edges(a)
    report = {
        "handover_before": summarize_handovers(b_edges, b_cases[CASE].nunique()),
        "handover_after": summarize_handovers(a_edges, a_cases[CASE].nunique()),
        "org_before": org_views(b),
        "org_after": org_views(a),
        "flow_before": flow_views(b),
        "flow_after": flow_views(a),
        "service_before": service_load(b),
        "service_after": service_load(a),
        "fair_before": fairness_and_tails(b_cases),
        "fair_after": fairness_and_tails(a_cases),
        "reassignment_before": b_cases["reassignment_count"].quantile([0.5, 0.9]),
        "reassignment_after": a_cases["reassignment_count"].quantile([0.5, 0.9]),
    }
    # Same views on the slices the average would hide.
    for label, kwargs in {
        "east_high": {"team": "East", "complexity": "high"},
        "north_high": {"team": "North", "complexity": "high"},
        "legal": {"specialty": "legal"},
        "risk": {"specialty": "risk"},
    }.items():
        report[label] = {
            "before": quantiles(case_table(segment(b, **kwargs)), "team"),
            "after": quantiles(case_table(segment(a, **kwargs)), "team"),
            "handover_after": summarize_handovers(
                handover_edges(segment(a, **kwargs)),
                segment(a, **kwargs)[CASE].nunique(),
            ),
        }
    report["temporal_after"] = temporal(a)
    return report

# Example reads once `report = compare(baseline, pilot)` exists:
# - report["handover_after"]: per-100 frequency, median wait, p90 wait, rework
# - report["org_after"]["subcontracting"]: caseworker → pool → caseworker loops
# - report["org_after"]["resource_similarity"]: mixed pool vs coherent role
# - report["org_after"]["roles"]: blurred approver / specialist roles
# - report["fair_after"]["by_team_complexity"]: median can improve while p90 does not
# - report["flow_after"]["rework_by_activity"]: Quality Check and Specialist Review
# - open-case age, computed outside completed-case quantiles, for queue stability
```

Read the result in this order. First the segmented handover table (frequency per 100, median wait, 90th-percentile wait, rework), not the single SNA weight. Then subcontracting depth and working-together within `team`. Then resource similarity and discovered roles, to see whether Technical Shared Service is one role or a mixed queue. Then cycle-time quantiles and SLA miss by team × complexity × customer segment. Then rework by activity and `local_expertise_flag` over successive months. An improved all-team median that appears only in the unsegmented case table, with a worse high-complexity 90th percentile or a wider team gap, is the pattern the pilot is designed to catch.
