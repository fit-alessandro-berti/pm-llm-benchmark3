# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness in this setting cannot be reduced to “equal approval rates.” The process must be evaluated across several dimensions because applicants may be treated differently before a decision is made, may face different burdens while seeking the same benefit, and may experience different access to rights-preserving support.

## Outcome parity

Raw approval rates differ substantially:

- Group A: 78%
- Group B: 63%
- Group C: 69%
- Group D: 58%

However, raw outcome parity is not sufficient evidence of unfairness because the groups differ in case mix. Group B has more incomplete documentation and language-support needs; Group C has more accommodation requests; Group D has much higher measured risk and prior-case counts. These factors can legitimately affect eligibility, routing, and complexity.

Therefore, raw approval gaps should be treated as a screening signal, not as proof of discrimination.

## Adjusted outcome disparity

The preliminary adjusted model reduces, but does not eliminate, differences:

- Group B: -5.5 percentage points approval probability, +2.6 days cycle time
- Group C: -2.2 percentage points approval probability, +4.1 days cycle time
- Group D: -3.1 percentage points approval probability, +0.9 days cycle time

These adjusted gaps are more relevant than raw gaps, but they still do not prove discrimination. They may reflect:

- unmeasured case complexity,
- measurement bias in risk_score_band or documentation_completeness,
- regional capacity differences,
- applicant behavior not captured in the log,
- legitimate policy routing,
- or genuine procedural unfairness.

The adjusted differences are large enough to warrant further investigation, especially because they coexist with higher procedural burden for Groups B and C.

## Procedural burden

Procedural burden concerns whether some applicants must complete more steps, wait longer between steps, undergo more handovers, or provide more documentation to obtain the same substantive consideration.

The evidence shows meaningful procedural burden differences:

- Group B has a +9.5 percentage point adjusted increase in “Request Missing Documents.”
- Group C has a +4.2 percentage point increase in “Request Missing Documents.”
- Group B has a +8.0 percentage point increase in “Supervisor Review.”
- Group C has a +5.5 percentage point increase in “Supervisor Review.”
- Group D has a much higher share of the fraud-review variant V3: 28% versus 9% for Group A.

Some burden may be legitimate. Fraud review may be appropriate for high-risk cases; accommodation review is required for disability accommodations; language support is a right for minority-language applicants. But if these required steps are systematically slower, more fragmented, or more likely to generate rework, then the process may be procedurally unfair even if the steps themselves are lawful.

## Timeliness

Timeliness is a core procedural fairness value. Applicants should not experience materially slower treatment because they exercise a protected need, such as language support or accommodation.

The adjusted cycle-time differences are concerning:

- Group B: +2.6 days after controls
- Group C: +4.1 days after controls
- Group D: +0.9 days after controls

The waiting-time table shows where delays occur:

- Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment: 3.8 days for Group B
- Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment: 4.7 days for Group C
- Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents: 1.6 days for Group B versus 0.4 days for Group A
- Supervisor Review  Decision Draft: 1.9 days for Group B versus 1.0 days for Group A

These delays are not merely “complexity.” They appear to be queue and capacity effects, especially for bilingual staff and accommodation specialists.

## Consistency of routing

Routing should be consistent with policy and should not depend on group membership once legitimate needs and risk are held constant.

Legitimate routing includes:

- high risk_score_band  Risk Screening/Fraud Review,
- disability accommodation request  Accommodation Review,
- minority-language preference  Language Support Contact.

The concern is whether routing is consistent within comparable cases. For example:

- Are Group B and Group A applicants with the same language preference and documentation status equally likely to receive language support?
- Are Group C applicants with accommodation requests processed promptly, or do they wait longer because the specialist queue is under-resourced?
- Are Group D applicants routed to fraud review because of validated risk, or because risk_score_band encodes prior contact with the system?

The current evidence supports some legitimate routing, especially for Group D’s higher fraud-review share, but it does not prove that routing is free from bias.

## Quality and accessibility of support

Language support and accommodation review are rights-preserving steps. They should improve access, not become penalties.

The evidence suggests a fairness problem:

- Group B cases are concentrated among only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers.
- Group C cases are concentrated among 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review.
- The policy does not include separate timeliness thresholds for language support or accommodation review.

If support steps are necessary but chronically under-resourced, then the process may formally provide access while materially delaying it. That is a procedural fairness concern.

## Appeal and rework burden

Appeals and rework can indicate either legitimate scrutiny or poor initial decision-making, poor explanation, or inaccessible communication.

The raw appeal rates are:

- Group A: 6%
- Group B: 13%
- Group C: 10%
- Group D: 16%

Adjusted appeal probability remains higher for:

- Group B: +4.4 percentage points
- Group D: +5.7 percentage points

Reopen/rework rates are also higher for Groups B, C, and D. This may reflect:

- more complex cases,
- more documentation problems,
- applicant confusion,
- poor decision explanations,
- language-access failures,
- or genuinely higher error or dispute rates.

Higher appeals are not automatically unfair, but when combined with slower support steps and higher documentation requests, they suggest weaker procedural quality for some groups.

## Resource-allocation fairness

Resource allocation is procedurally important even if final outcomes are similar.

The evidence shows unequal resource structures:

- Group A cases are spread across 52 caseworkers.
- Group B cases are concentrated among 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers.
- Group C cases are concentrated among 5 accommodation specialists.

This can create fairness problems if the specialized resources are bottlenecks. Applicants with protected needs may receive formally correct but slower and more fragmented service. Resource allocation fairness therefore requires checking whether comparable cases receive comparable staffing quality, workload, and timeliness.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The goal is to separate:

- **disparity**: an observed difference between groups;
- **legitimate routing**: a difference explained by valid case needs, risk, or statutory requirements;
- **discrimination or unfair treatment**: a difference that remains after accounting for legitimate factors and that reflects avoidable burden, bias, or unequal access;
- **uncertainty**: a difference that cannot be interpreted confidently because of missing data, measurement bias, or model limitations.

## Matching or stratification variables

Use a layered comparison. Do not rely on one global regression alone.

### Base case-mix variables

Stratify or match on pre-routing case characteristics:

- risk_score_band,
- documentation_completeness,
- channel,
- household_size_band,
- prior_case_count,
- region,
- month,
- language_preference,
- disability_accommodation_requested.

These are the main observed complexity factors.

### Caution with post-routing variables

Some variables may be affected by group membership or process treatment:

- language support contact,
- accommodation review,
- request missing documents,
- supervisor review,
- fraud review.

If the objective is to estimate the total procedural disparity experienced by a group, do not control away these steps as though they are neutral pre-existing case features. They may be part of the mechanism through which unequal burden occurs.

For example, if Group B applicants receive more “Request Missing Documents” because language barriers make forms harder to complete, controlling for documentation requests may hide the fairness problem.

### Recommended estimands

Estimate several quantities:

1. **Raw disparity**: difference by applicant_group without adjustment.
2. **Case-mix-adjusted disparity**: adjust for risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month.
3. **Within-variant disparity**: compare groups among cases following the same variant.
4. **Within-routing disparity**: compare groups within the same risk band, language preference, accommodation status, and region.
5. **Support-step timeliness disparity**: compare waiting times after language support or accommodation review, conditional on need.

This avoids treating one adjusted number as the sole truth.

## Path/variant comparison

Variant analysis is essential because groups are distributed differently across paths.

Compare:

1. **Variant distribution by group**
   - Are Group B cases overrepresented in V4, the language-support path?
   - Are Group C cases overrepresented in V5, the accommodation path?
   - Are Group D cases overrepresented in V3, the fraud/supervisor path?
   - Are Group A cases overrepresented in V1, the fastest clean path?

2. **Variant-specific cycle time by group**
   - Within V2, do Group B cases take longer than Group A cases?
   - Within V5, do Group C cases wait longer than comparable accommodation cases in other groups?
   - Within V3, do Group D cases receive longer fraud-review waits than comparable high-risk cases?

3. **Variant legitimacy**
   - Does V4 occur mainly for minority-language applicants?
   - Does V5 occur mainly for accommodation-requested applicants?
   - Does V3 occur mainly for high risk_score_band cases?

If a group is concentrated in slower variants but those variants are appropriate for observed needs, the routing may be legitimate. If the group still experiences longer waits within the same variant, that is stronger evidence of procedural unfairness.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Resource analysis should test whether groups receive comparable staffing once complexity is held constant.

Compare:

- number of distinct resources per case,
- number of handovers per case,
- fraction of cases handled by overloaded resources,
- fraction of cases handled by specialized resources,
- resource workload at the time of assignment,
- resource experience or role,
- whether the same resource performs both support and review functions.

Important checks:

- Group B: Are cases disproportionately assigned to the 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded officers even after controlling for language preference and channel?
- Group C: Are cases disproportionately assigned to the 5 accommodation specialists even after controlling for accommodation request and risk?
- Group D: Is fraud-review assignment proportional to risk_score_band, or does group membership predict fraud review after risk is held constant?
- In the two regions with extra handovers, do Group B and Group C cases experience more handovers than similar cases in other regions?

Use handover-of-work and working-together networks to identify fragmented subnetworks.

## Waiting-time decomposition

Cycle time should be decomposed into service time and waiting time.

For each group, compute:

- total case duration,
- waiting time between activities,
- service time within activities,
- queue time before specialized steps,
- time spent in rework loops,
- time from decision draft to notification.

Key comparisons:

- Submit  Identity Verification by channel and group.
- Completeness Check  Request Missing Documents by language preference and documentation completeness.
- Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment for Group B and any other minority-language applicants.
- Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment for Group C and any other accommodation-requested applicants.
- Risk Screening  Fraud Review by risk_score_band.
- Supervisor Review  Decision Draft by region.

This distinguishes delays caused by applicant complexity from delays caused by queue design or resource scarcity.

For example, if Group B and Group A applicants with the same documentation status and language preference both require document requests, but Group B waits 1.6 days while Group A waits 0.4 days, that is a procedural timeliness concern.

## Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates

risk_score_band and documentation_completeness should not be treated as unquestionably neutral.

Recommended sensitivity checks:

1. **Exclude risk_score_band**
   - Re-estimate group differences without risk_score_band.
   - If gaps shrink dramatically, investigate whether risk_score_band is a legitimate complexity measure or a proxy for historical treatment.

2. **Exclude documentation_completeness**
   - Re-estimate without documentation_completeness.
   - If Group B’s gap shrinks substantially, consider whether documentation incompleteness is itself caused by language barriers or inaccessible forms.

3. **Stratify by risk_score_band**
   - Compare Group D and Group A within the same risk band.
   - If Group D still receives more fraud review or slower treatment, routing may not be fully explained by measured risk.

4. **Stratify by documentation_completeness**
   - Compare applicants with complete documentation.
   - If Group B still receives more document requests, this may indicate differential scrutiny or communication problems.

5. **Alternative definitions of complexity**
   - Use household_size_band, prior_case_count, channel, and region as complexity controls.
   - Test whether results are stable across definitions.

6. **Bias bounds**
   - Estimate how strong an unobserved confounder would need to be to explain away the adjusted differences.
   - This does not prove causality but clarifies uncertainty.

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

Fairness issues may be local rather than system-wide.

Check:

- **By region**
  - Are the two regions with supervisor-review backlogs concentrated in Group B or Group C cases?
  - Do regional handover patterns differ by group?
  - Are bilingual resources unevenly distributed across regions?

- **By channel**
  - Do in-person Group B cases wait longer at identity verification?
  - Do online Group B cases still experience higher document requests?
  - Are language-support delays worse for in-person or online applicants?

- **By language preference**
  - Compare Group B applicants who need language support with non-Group B applicants who need language support.
  - If Group B still waits longer, the issue may be group-specific handling rather than language support itself.

- **By accommodation status**
  - Compare Group C accommodation-requested applicants with non-Group C accommodation-requested applicants.
  - If Group C still waits longer, the issue may be routing or resource allocation rather than accommodation itself.

- **By risk band**
  - Compare Group D and Group A applicants in the same risk band.
  - This helps assess whether fraud routing is risk-based or group-associated.

- longer wait before missing-document requests,
- more handovers in some regions,
- higher supervisor-review probability after adjustment,
- higher appeal probability after adjustment.

This suggests that Group B’s procedural burden is partly caused by resource scarcity and routing design, not only by applicant complexity.

## Group C: accommodation-review bottleneck

Group C shows a distinct accommodation-related disparity:

- V5, the accommodation path, accounts for 31% of Group C cases, compared with 2% for Group A.
- Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment has a median wait of 4.7 days.
- Group C cases are concentrated among 5 accommodation specialists.
- Two accommodation specialists also perform supervisor review.
- In some regions, Group C cases have more handovers: general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor.
- Adjusted cycle time is +4.1 days.
- Adjusted probability of Supervisor Review is +5.5 percentage points.
- Adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is +4.2 percentage points.
- Raw reopen/rework rate is 24%, compared with 11% for Group A.

### Potentially legitimate aspects

Accommodation Review is required for disability accommodation requests. Group C has a 62% accommodation-requested share, so more accommodation routing is expected.

### Concerning aspects

The delay appears concentrated after the accommodation step:

- 4.7 days from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment,
- only 5 accommodation specialists,
- dual roles with supervisor review,
- higher adjusted cycle time even after controls.

This suggests that the accommodation process may be formally available but operationally slow. That is a procedural fairness concern because applicants exercising a protected right may be penalized with longer waits.

## Group D: risk routing, fraud review, and appeals

Group D shows a different pattern:

- V3, the risk/fraud/supervisor path, accounts for 28% of Group D cases, compared with 9% for Group A.
- V6, the appeal path, accounts for 13% of Group D cases, compared with 4% for Group A.
- Risk Screening  Fraud Review wait is 2.8 days for Group D, compared with 2.2 days for Group A.
- Group D has a 51% high risk_score_band share.
- Adjusted approval probability is -3.1 percentage points.
- Adjusted cycle time is +0.9 days.
- Adjusted appeal probability is +5.7 percentage points.

### Potentially legitimate aspects

Policy requires higher risk_score_band to receive Fraud Review. Group D has much higher measured risk, so more fraud routing is expected.

The relatively small adjusted cycle-time difference, +0.9 days, suggests that fraud routing may not be causing as much delay as language or accommodation bottlenecks.

### Concerning aspects

risk_score_band may not be neutral. If historical enforcement, prior contact, or regional practices are baked into the score, then Group D may be over-routed based on a biased proxy.

The high appeal rate is also concerning. It may reflect:

- more adverse decisions,
- poorer explanation,
- lower trust,
- more complex history,
- or actual errors in risk classification.

Group D’s case therefore requires auditing the risk score and appeal reasons, not simply accepting the routing as legitimate.

## Summary of most important disparities

| Group | Main disparity | Likely legitimate component | Main fairness concern |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Group B | Language-support path, missing documents, longer waits | Language need, incomplete documentation | Bilingual bottleneck, handovers, adjusted delays, higher appeals |
| Group C | Accommodation path, specialist queue, longer cycle | Accommodation requests | 4.7-day accommodation delay, limited specialists, dual-role bottleneck |
| Group D | Fraud review, supervisor review, appeals | Higher measured risk | Risk score may encode bias; high appeal burden |
| Group A | Fast clean path, broad resource distribution | Lower measured complexity | Serves as baseline; unequal access to efficient processing |

The strongest procedural fairness concerns are not simply lower approval rates. They are the delays and handovers associated with rights-preserving support steps, especially for Groups B and C.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Several mechanisms could generate the observed patterns. Some are legitimate; others are concerning. The same observed disparity can have multiple explanations.

## Mechanism 1: Legitimate case-mix routing

Higher measured risk, accommodation requests, and language-support needs can legitimately produce different paths.

Examples:

- Group D has more high risk_score_band cases, so more Fraud Review is expected.
- Group C has more accommodation requests, so more Accommodation Review is expected.
- Group B has more minority-language needs, so more Language Support Contact is expected.

This mechanism explains part of the variant distribution and some of the raw outcome differences. However, it does not fully explain why the support steps are slower or why adjusted gaps remain.

## Mechanism 2: Resource scarcity for rights-preserving steps

Language support and accommodation review may be under-resourced.

Evidence:

- only 7 bilingual caseworkers for Group B language-support cases,
- only 5 accommodation specialists for Group C accommodation cases,
- long waits after Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review,
- overloaded regional officers for Group B,
- dual roles for some accommodation specialists.

This mechanism is concerning because it turns protected needs into procedural delays. The process may be formally accessible but practically burdensome.

## Mechanism 3: Measurement bias in risk_score_band or documentation_completeness

risk_score_band and documentation_completeness may not be neutral measures.

Possible issues:

- risk_score_band may reflect prior system contact, which may itself be unevenly distributed.
- documentation_completeness may reflect form design, translation quality, or caseworker interpretation.
- Group B applicants may be classified as incomplete because forms are not accessible in their preferred language.
- Group D applicants may be classified as high risk because of historical scrutiny.

If so, controlling for these variables may understate unfairness. This mechanism is concerning because it can make biased routing appear legitimate.

## Mechanism 4: Handover fragmentation and coordination loss

Handovers can delay cases and reduce accountability.

Evidence:

- Group B has more general  bilingual  general handovers in two regions.
- Group C has more general  accommodation specialist  supervisor handovers.
- Group B and Group C have higher reopen/rework rates.

This mechanism can generate longer cycle times, more requests for documents, and more appeals even if each individual step is performed correctly. It is concerning because the burden arises from process design rather than applicant characteristics.

## Mechanism 5: Poor communication or decision explanation

Higher appeal rates for Groups B and D may reflect poor communication.

Possible issues:

- decision notices may not be adequately translated,
- accommodation-related decisions may not be clearly explained,
- risk-based adverse decisions may be opaque,
- applicants may not understand what documents are missing.

This mechanism is concerning because it can create appeals and distrust even when the underlying decision is legally correct.

## Mechanism 6: Regional capacity differences

The scenario notes supervisor-review backlogs in two regions and in-person applications waiting longer.

Regional effects could explain:

- longer Supervisor Review  Decision Draft waits,
- more handovers,
- uneven access to bilingual or accommodation specialists.

This mechanism may be neither group-based nor intentionally discriminatory, but it can still produce unfair outcomes if protected groups are concentrated in under-resourced regions.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available event log is useful but insufficient for a definitive fairness judgment. Several important factors are missing or potentially biased.

## risk_score_band may encode historical disparities

risk_score_band is a key routing variable, but it may not be neutral.

It could reflect:

- prior investigations,
- prior case count,
- regional enforcement practices,
- referral source,
- historical bias in fraud detection,
- or correlated socioeconomic factors.

If Group D is higher risk because of prior system contact that was itself biased, then controlling for risk_score_band may legitimize an unfair pipeline. The log does not show how the risk score was constructed or validated.

## documentation_completeness may be endogenous

documentation_completeness may be affected by:

- language barriers,
- inaccessible forms,
- poor translation,
- lack of digital access,
- caseworker discretion,
- prior requests that were not understood.

If Group B applicants are more likely to be classified as incomplete because the process is not linguistically accessible, then documentation_completeness is not merely a neutral complexity variable. It may be part of the unfair burden.

## case_outcome does not capture decision quality

case_outcome appears to capture approval or denial, but procedural fairness also depends on:

- whether the decision was correct,
- whether the reasoning was adequate,
- whether the applicant understood the decision,
- whether the applicant was treated with dignity,
- whether the decision was made within a reasonable time,
- whether the applicant could effectively exercise appeal rights.

A process can have similar approval rates but still be procedurally unfair.

## Appeal quality and appeal grounds are missing

appeal_flag shows that an appeal occurred, but not:

- why the applicant appealed,
- whether the appeal succeeded,
- whether the appeal revealed errors,
- whether the appeal was due to poor explanation,
- whether the appeal was frivolous or substantive.

Higher appeal rates could indicate applicant confusion, legitimate disagreement, or poor initial decisions. The log alone cannot distinguish these.

## Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity

The resource data show assignments and queues, but workload is only daily. This limits inference because:

- two caseworkers may have the same daily assignment count but different case complexity,
- some resources may be interrupted or multitasking,
- bilingual staff may have untranslated tasks not visible in the log,
- accommodation specialists may have medical documentation review time not captured,
- supervisor review backlog may vary within the day.

Without finer workload data, it is hard to know whether delays are due to individual resource behavior or structural capacity limits.

## Missing applicant needs and circumstances

The log does not fully capture:

- digital literacy,
- internet access,
- disability type,
- need for interpreter modality,
- caregiver responsibilities,
- housing instability,
- urgency of benefit need,
- prior communication attempts,
- whether the applicant received help completing forms.

These factors can affect documentation completeness, appeals, and cycle time.

## Channel and region may confound group effects

Group B has more in-person applications. In-person applications wait longer at identity verification. But the log may not fully capture:

- office capacity,
- walk-in volume,
- regional staffing,
- local translation availability,
- regional policy interpretation.

Therefore, some observed group differences may actually be channel or region effects.

## Causal inference is limited

The data are observational. Even adjusted comparisons may not identify discrimination because:

- group membership is not randomly assigned,
- unobserved case complexity may differ,
- some controls may be mediators,
- risk and documentation measures may be biased,
- regional practices may differ.

The evidence can support suspicion and prioritize audits, but it cannot alone prove unlawful or intentional discrimination.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Mitigations should preserve rights-preserving steps while reducing their burdens. The goal is not to remove language support or accommodation review, but to make them timely, well-resourced, and fair.

## Add capacity for language support and accommodation review

### Option

Increase capacity where bottlenecks are evident:

- hire or train more bilingual caseworkers,
- create a backup pool of trained language-support staff,
- add accommodation specialists,
- cross-train general caseworkers on basic accommodation handling,
- provide interpreter support for eligibility interviews.

### Expected benefit

This directly addresses the long waits:

- Language Support Contact  Eligibility Assessment: 3.8 days,
- Accommodation Review  Eligibility Assessment: 4.7 days.

It also reduces dependence on a small number of specialists.

### Side effects and trade-offs

- Higher staffing cost.
- Training time before productivity improves.
- Risk of lower quality if newly trained staff handle complex language or accommodation issues without support.
- Possible dilution of specialist expertise if cases are spread too widely.
- Need to maintain privacy and dignity for applicants requiring support.

## Add service-level targets for support steps

### Option

Create separate timeliness standards for rights-preserving steps:

- maximum wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment,
- maximum wait from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment,
- maximum wait from Request Missing Documents to follow-up support,
- escalation when support-step queues exceed thresholds.

### Expected benefit

This prevents support steps from becoming invisible delays. It also makes resource shortages visible to management.

### Side effects and trade-offs

- Staff may rush support interactions to meet targets.
- Managers may reclassify activities to avoid SLA breaches.
- Caseworkers may prioritize easier support cases over harder ones.
- Targets must be paired with quality checks, such as applicant comprehension or translation accuracy.
- If capacity is not increased, SLAs may simply shift delays elsewhere.

## Redesign routing to reduce handovers

### Option

Reduce fragmentation by changing assignment rules:

- assign a single case owner for language-support cases,
- use paired teams: general caseworker plus bilingual specialist,
- keep accommodation cases with the accommodation specialist through eligibility where possible,
- avoid routing back to general caseworkers after support unless necessary,
- align supervisor review with the team that performed the substantive work.

### Expected benefit

This can reduce:

- handover delays,
- lost context,
- repeated document requests,
- rework,
- applicant confusion.

It is especially relevant for Group B’s general  bilingual  general pattern and Group C’s general  accommodation specialist  supervisor pattern.

### Side effects and trade-offs

- May reduce flexibility in workload distribution.
- May increase dependence on individual case owners.
- May reduce specialization if too many tasks are shifted to generalists.
- Requires role redesign and possibly new access permissions.
- Could increase burnout for case owners if they become single points of responsibility.

## Improve decision explanations and applicant communication

### Option

Improve the quality and accessibility of communications:

- translated decision notices,
- plain-language explanations,
- clear lists of missing documents,
- accessible formats for disability accommodations,
- explanation of risk-based adverse decisions where lawful,
- contact point for questions before appeal.

### Expected benefit

This may reduce:

- unnecessary appeals,
- repeated document submissions,
- applicant confusion,
- perceived unfairness.

It is especially relevant for Group B and Group D, which have higher adjusted appeal probabilities.

### Side effects and trade-offs

- More time needed per case initially.
- Translation and accessibility costs.
- Risk of over-explaining or disclosing sensitive fraud methods if not carefully designed.
- May increase short-term cycle time unless templates and tools are provided.
- Requires legal review for risk-based explanations.

## Monitor adjusted disparities continuously

### Option

Create a fairness monitoring dashboard with:

- raw and adjusted approval rates,
- cycle time by group and variant,
- waiting time for support steps,
- handover counts,
- appeal rates,
- rework rates,
- resource concentration,
- regional exceptions,
- confidence intervals or uncertainty bands.

### Expected benefit

This allows early detection of emerging disparities and evaluation of interventions.

### Side effects and trade-offs

- Risk of overreacting to small-sample noise.
- Risk of gaming if staff are evaluated on crude metrics.
- May create perverse incentives to avoid complex cases.
- Requires governance to distinguish legitimate variation from unfair patterns.
- Needs privacy protection when monitoring small groups or regions.

## Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness

### Option

Audit the two most sensitive routing variables:

- validate risk_score_band against actual fraud or adverse-event outcomes,
- test whether risk_score_band has disparate impact by group after legitimate factors are controlled,
- review documentation_completeness criteria,
- test whether documentation classifications vary by language preference or channel,
- examine whether incomplete documentation is due to applicant failure or inaccessible process design.

### Expected benefit

This helps determine whether routing is legitimate or biased. It is especially important for Group D and Group B.

### Side effects and trade-offs

- Audits may temporarily slow processing.
- If the risk score is found to be biased, the agency may need to change routing, which could affect fraud detection.
- Documentation standards may need redesign, requiring form changes and staff retraining.
- Human review may be needed for cases affected by flawed scores.
- There may be legal or policy constraints on changing risk-based routing.

## Additional operational mitigations

### Regional load balancing

Create regional capacity buffers for supervisor review and specialized support.

Side effects:

- may reduce local accountability,
- requires cross-region training,
- may complicate applicant communication.

### Queue visibility for support steps

Make language-support and accommodation queues visible in management reporting.

Side effects:

- may encourage queue manipulation,
- requires reliable event logging,
- must avoid treating support steps as optional.

### Applicant-facing support before submission

Provide help with forms, translation, and document upload before submission.

Side effects:

- upfront cost,
- may increase application volume,
- requires outreach to affected communities.

        "name": "fraud_review_after_high_risk",
        "condition": "risk_score_band == 'high'",
        "required_activity": "Fraud Review",
        "after_activity": "Risk Screening"

def check_required_activity_before_target(event_log, condition, required_activity, before_activity):
    violations = []

    for case_id, case_df in event_log.groupby("case:concept:name"):
        case_df = case_df.sort_values("time:timestamp")
        activities = case_df["concept:name"].tolist()

        # Simplified condition checking; in production use case attributes safely
        if condition == "language_preference == 'minority_language'":
            applies = case_df["language_preference"].iloc[0] == "minority_language"
        elif condition == "disability_accommodation_requested == True":
            applies = bool(case_df["disability_accommodation_requested"].iloc[0])
        elif condition == "risk_score_band == 'high'":
            applies = case_df["risk_score_band"].iloc[0] == "high"
        else:
            applies = False

        if not applies:
            continue

        if before_activity in activities:
            target_index = activities.index(before_activity)
            prior_activities = activities[:target_index]

            if required_activity not in prior_activities:
                violations.append(case_id)

    return violations

language_violations = check_required_activity_before_target(
    log_main,
    "language_preference == 'minority_language'",
    "Language Support Contact",
    "Eligibility Assessment"
)

accommodation_violations = check_required_activity_before_target(
    log_main,
    "disability_accommodation_requested == True",
    "Accommodation Review",
    "Eligibility Assessment"
)

fraud_violations = check_required_activity_before_target(
    log_main,
    "risk_score_band == 'high'",
    "Fraud Review",
    "Eligibility Assessment"
)

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does not appear straightforwardly unfair based only on raw approval rates, because Groups B, C, and D have materially higher documented complexity, language-support needs, accommodation needs, and measured risk. Raw outcome differences therefore cannot be treated as evidence of discrimination by themselves.

However, the evidence does show meaningful procedural fairness concerns after controlling for observed complexity. Group B and Group C experience longer adjusted cycle times, higher probabilities of document requests and supervisor review, higher rework rates, and concentration in slower variants associated with language support and accommodation review. The waiting-time evidence suggests that these delays are not merely due to applicant complexity. They are associated with scarce specialized resources: 7 bilingual caseworkers for Group B language-support needs and 5 accommodation specialists for Group C accommodation needs. Rights-preserving support steps appear to be creating avoidable delay and fragmentation because they lack sufficient capacity and separate timeliness standards.

Group D presents a more uncertain picture. The higher fraud-review share is consistent with higher measured risk, and the adjusted cycle-time difference is relatively small. But risk_score_band may encode historical disparities, and Group D has the highest appeal rate. Therefore, Group D’s treatment cannot be deemed fair without auditing the risk score and appeal grounds.

Overall, the process is best characterized as **procedurally mixed with credible evidence of unfair burden, but not definitively discriminatory on the available data**. The strongest concern is not simply unequal approval, but unequal timeliness, handover burden, and resource allocation around language support, accommodation review, documentation requests, and appeals. The appropriate next step is not to remove support steps, but to audit risk and documentation measures, add capacity and service-level targets for support steps, reduce handovers, improve decision explanations, and monitor adjusted disparities by group, region, channel, and variant.
