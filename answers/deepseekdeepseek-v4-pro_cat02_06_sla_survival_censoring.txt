# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin**  
`Submit` time = 0h (the moment the case is created in the system).

**Horizon**  
72h (the operational SLA from Submit to Resolve).

**Event(s) of interest**  
The primary event is `Resolve`. Other terminal internal statuses – `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` – are competing events that preclude a subsequent Resolve in this dataset. Cases with `observed_status = "open"` have not yet experienced any terminal event.

**Censoring**  
Rightcensoring: an `open` case is censored at its `duration_or_age_h`. This is the age at the observation cutoff, i.e., the time from Submit to the moment the extract was frozen.

**Competing risks**  
`Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are competing risks. Under the base scenario they are modelled as mutually exclusive first event types. Sensitivity analyses alter the treatment of `Transfer` (see Sections2 and6).

## Two useful estimands

### EstimandA – “SLA breach”  
Probability of **not being Resolved by 72h**, i.e.  
\(
B_A = 1 - F_{\text{Resolve}}(72),
\)  
where \(F_{\text{Resolve}}(t)\) is the cumulative incidence function (CIF) of Resolve, estimated via AalenJohansen in the presence of the competing events named above.  
This directly answers the question: *What fraction of cases fail to reach Resolve within the SLA window, regardless of whether they later resolve, become open, or end in a nonresolution terminal state?*

### EstimandB – “Still open at 72h”  
Probability of **having no terminal disposition by 72h**, i.e. the eventfree survival  
\(
S(72) = P(\text{no terminal event by }72\text{h}),
\)  
where a terminal event is any of `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`. This is estimated with the KaplanMeier estimator treating any terminal event as a failure and open cases as censored.  
It tells the operations team: *What share of cases are still active (not closed in any way) after the SLA deadline?* This helps dimension the pending queue and workload.

| transferred | `duration_or_age_h` | 0 | 4 (Transfer) | none | **ScenarioI (base):** Transfer is a competing risk (case leaves internal observation; its eventual resolution is unknown but the internal process ended in a nonresolve state).<br/>**ScenarioII:** Transfer treated as independent rightcensoring (observation stops at transfer time; assumes transfer process is noninformative about eventual resolution).<br/>**ScenarioIII:** Transfer treated as resolved (imputes success, e.g., if external organisation has a very high resolution rate). |
| open | `duration_or_age_h` (age at cutoff) | 0 | 0 (censor) | rightcensored at observed age | – |

**Why three transfer treatments matter**  
Transferred cases are qualitatively different: they often involve escalations (e.g., S08, S21, S32). If the external organisation’s resolution behaviour differs systematically from the internal process, censoring is informative. ScenarioI acknowledges that a transfer is a distinct endpoint that prevents internal Resolve, which fits the “competing risk” framework. ScenarioII would inflate the estimate of Resolve by removing these difficult cases from the risk set without penalty. ScenarioIII is optimistic and could be justified if external SLAs guarantee resolution, but it would understate the true breach risk. We compare all three in the sensitivity analysis (Section6).

# 3. Why naive completedcase analysis is biased

A naive analysis would restrict to cases that have already reached a terminal status (`resolved`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, `transferred`) and discard all `open` cases. Several biases arise.

1. **Survivor bias and overrepresentation of fast cases**  
   Fast resolutions (e.g., S0118h, S059h, S1616h) are guaranteed to be in the completed set, while slow pending cases that are still open are excluded.  
   For example, S09 (open92h) has already breached the 72h SLA but would be omitted; S26 (open104h) likewise. Their exclusion pushes the estimated breach rate downward.

2. **Informative censoring by transfer**  
   If transfers are analysed as censored (ScenarioII), the censoring is informative because transferred cases tend to be escalated and highpriority (S08P1, S21P1, S32P1). Dropping them as censored without modelling the selection effect makes the remaining population look less complex and therefore artificially faster to resolve. A completedcase analysis that ignores transfers entirely (or treats them as failures) would still bias the composition of the risk set.

3. **Competing risks distort the probability of Resolve**  
   Withdrawal and rejection happen early (mostly <30h). If they are simply treated as censored, the KaplanMeier “survival for Resolve” would treat them as withdrawals from the risk set without the Resolve event, inflating the cumulative hazard and hence overestimating the CIF of Resolve. The correct AalenJohansen estimator accounts for the fact that a withdrawn case can never later Resolve.

4. **Cohort imbalance**  
   CohortB (after automatedscreening policy change) shows a different process structure (e.g., `AutoCheck` activity). A naive pooled analysis mixes two heterogeneous cohorts, masking any policy effect. Completedcase analysis could further entangle the cohort effect with the different censoring patterns.

5. **Active open cases above vs. below 72h**  
   Cases that are open and have already passed the 72h horizon (S0992h, S1181h, S2579h, S26104h) have demonstrably breached the SLA, but they still contribute followup information about later events. In a completedcase analysis they are discarded, despite containing the most critical “tail” information. Open cases below 72h (e.g., S1535h, S2467h, S3123h) are at risk of breaching in the future; discarding them removes the very observations that inform the cumulative incidence at the horizon.

Using case IDs concretely:
- If we delete S09, S11, S25, S26, the estimated proportion of cases unresolved by 72h would drop sharply because those past72h open cases are the most obvious breaches.
- The presence of S08 (transferred P1 at 68h) as a terminal event reduces the risk set for Resolve; excluding it (or naïve censoring) changes the denominator at late times.
- Including S13 (resolved at 110h) adds an event after 72h, correctly contributing to the “not resolved by 72h” count, but a completedcase analysis would include it as a resolved event and might (wrongly) count it as “resolved within the horizon” if time were binned incorrectly.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

## Candidate methods and their suitability

| Method | Pros | Cons for these data |
|--------|------|---------------------|
| **KaplanMeier for eventfree survival** (EstimandB) | Simple, well understood, handles rightcensoring. | Combines `Resolve` with `Withdraw/Reject/Transfer` into a single failure event; does not separate “satisfactory” from “unsatisfactory” closure. |
| **AalenJohansen / cumulative incidence (competing risks)** | Directly estimates CIF of Resolve while accounting for withdrawals/rejections/transfers. Matches EstimandA. Nonparametric, no proportionalhazards assumption. | Cannot easily incorporate continuous covariates – only stratification. |
| **Causespecific hazard models (Cox per event type)** | Can test covariate effects on the instantaneous hazard of each event type. | Relies on proportional hazards and independence assumption between causespecific hazards. Interpretation of covariates on causespecific hazard does not directly translate to the CIF because competing events alter the risk set. |
| **Subdistribution hazard model (Fine–Gray)** | Models covariate effects directly on the CIF of Resolve. | Proportional subdistribution hazards may not hold; with small sample sizes, convergence can be problematic. |
| **Restricted mean time** (e.g., mean time to Resolve up to 72h) | Useful if proportional hazards fail; directly interpretable as “average days saved”. | Not a primary estimand for breach probability, but can complement the analysis. |
| **Landmarking** | Answers operational questions: e.g., “given a case is still open at 24h, what is the probability of breach?” Can incorporate processaware covariates up to the landmark time. | Requires exact event timestamps to determine the state at the landmark. With only total duration and lastevent gap we cannot reliably reconstruct the stage at an arbitrary time (see Section5). Still, we can approximate landmarks if we assume that the last event occurred shortly before the observation, which is not safe. |
| **Multistate model** | Rich description of the whole pathway; can estimate transition intensities and state occupation probabilities. | Needs complete timestamps of all state transitions. The provided data lack interevent times, so a full multistate fit is not possible without strong assumptions. |

## Recommended primary approach

**Primary: Stratified AalenJohansen estimator for EstimandA (cumulative incidence of Resolve).**  
- **Why:** It directly answers “probability of breaching the SLA by 72h” in the presence of competing nonresolution events. We stratify by `cohort` and `priority` to avoid assuming homogeneity and to visualise the policy effect.  
- **Implementation:** With the data above, we compute the AalenJohansen estimate of the CIF for each combination of (`cohort`, `priority`). Confidence intervals can be obtained via bootstrap or by using Greenwoodtype formulas for the CIF. The estimate at t = 72h is read from the curve.  
- **Handling of transfer:** The primary scenario treats transfer as a competing risk (code4). Under this scenario, a transfer is a “breach” event because the internal process did not end in Resolve. Sensitivity analyses will vary this (Section6).

**Secondary: KaplanMeier for eventfree survival (EstimandB).**  
- **Why:** Inform the workload manager about the share of cases still pending at 72h. Easy to communicate.  
- **Stratification:** Same strata.  

**Supplementary descriptive analysis:**  
- Use the restricted mean time lost to competing events (within 72h) to understand which nonresolution events drive the gap.  
- Because the sample is small (32 cases), we will present point estimates and bootstrap confidence intervals. No formal regression is performed in the primary analysis; instead, subgroup CIFs are compared descriptively.

We do **not** recommend a Fine–Gray model on these data because of the limited sample and the unstable subdistribution hazard estimation; nor a multistate model without timestamps.

# 5. Processaware features and stage hazards

## Features derivable from the `prefix` field

From the activity sequence we can extract both baseline and timevarying (potential) features. The table below classifies them and flags leakage risks, given that **exact event timestamps are unavailable**.

| Feature | Derivation | Type | Leakage / timing concern |
|--------|------------|------|--------------------------|
| `cohort`, `priority` | Directly given | Baseline (fixed at t=0) | Safe |
| `rework_count` | From data (0 or 1) | Occurrence during the whole episode | For completed cases it is a posthoc summary; for open cases it reflects rework up to the last event. Using it as a baseline covariate risks including future rework that may affect the outcome. Could be used as a timevarying covariate if rework time were known. |
| `escalated` (yes/no) | From data | Same as rework: occurs at some time during the episode | Using “ever escalated” before escalation actually happens is a leakage. If used in a landmark design, only escalations that occurred before the landmark would be valid. Because we cannot determine the exact time of escalation from the supplied fields, escalation is **not usable** as a welldefined timevarying covariate without timestamps. |
| Current stage (last activity in prefix) | e.g., `Review`, `Escalate`, `AutoCheck` | Timevarying; reflects state at the moment of the last event | For open cases, the stage at observation can be inferred: the case is in the state after the last activity and has been idle for `last_event_gap_h`. Without transition timestamps we cannot know the state at an arbitrary earlier time, but we can define the **state at the observation time** as a snapshot covariate. This could be used in a “current state” analysis (e.g., predict residual time to Resolve from now), which is a type of landmark at the last event time plus the gap. However, this mixes cases observed at different clocktimes and with different idle periods. |
| Path pattern / variant | e.g., `Submit>Screen>Review>Resolve` | Posthoc descriptor of the entire journey | Only fully known after case closure; cannot be used for prospective risk prediction. Useful for descriptive process mining (e.g., clustering cases by pathway). |
| Time spent in current stage (`last_event_gap_h`) | For open cases, the idle time since the last event | Snapshot covariate (observed at cutoff) | Could reflect pending or waiting time. Combined with stage, it may help estimate remaining time; but it is not a baseline feature. |
| Number of activities performed so far | Length of the prefix | Varying over time | Could be used as a timevarying covariate if event timestamps allowed splitting; with current data only available at the time of last event. |

**Bottom line:** Without exact timestamps of individual activities, we cannot safely build timevarying Cox models with processstage as a covariate. Therefore our primary analysis (Section4) restricts itself to baseline stratification by `cohort` and `priority`. We will, however, extract process variants via PM4Py (Section8) to descriptively link pathways to outcomes.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

We compare **three scenarios** that differ in how `Transfer` is handled, and, within each, we also assess the impact of treating `Withdraw`/`Reject` as censoring instead of competing risks (a “naïve” comparison). The analysis is stratified by cohort and priority. The primary estimand is **1–CIFofResolve at 72h** (EstimandA), complemented by eventfree survival (EstimandB).

## Scenario definitions

1. **Base (transfer = competing risk)**  
   - `Transfer` is coded as competing event (code4). `Withdraw` and `Reject` are competing events.  
   - This respects that transferred cases leave the internal system without a Resolve, and the external outcome is not known.

2. **Transfer as independent rightcensoring**  
   - `Transfer` is treated as censored at the transfer time (code0, like `open`). `Withdraw`/`Reject` remain competing risks.  
   - This assumes that the reason for transfer is unrelated to the eventual resolution time, which is unlikely given transfers occur after escalation and are mostly P1 (S08, S21, S32).

3. **Transfer as resolved (imputation)**  
   - `Transfer` is recoded as `Resolve` (event of interest, code1).  
   - This simulates the assumption that the external organisation resolves virtually all transferred cases within the SLA.

For each scenario we also compute a **naïve alternative** where `Withdraw` and `Reject` are treated as independent censoring (code0) and only `Resolve` is an event; this mimics a KM analysis that ignores competing risks.

## Expected qualitative changes

Using the actual data, we can anticipate the direction:

| Scenario | Effect on estimated breach probability \(B_A\) | Rationale |
|----------|-----------------------------------------------|-----------|
| Base (competing risk) | Highest \(B_A\) among the three transfer scenarios | Transfers count as nonresolution events, and the highpriority escalated cases that tend to transfer are now “failures”. |
| Transfer as censoring | Lower \(B_A\) | The three transferred cases (S08, S21, S32) are removed from the risk set without a failure, so the cumulative incidence of Resolve increases because the risk set shrinks but the event count remains the same. This could mask the risk posed by complex cases. |
| Transfer as resolved | Lowest \(B_A\) | The transferred cases contribute directly to Resolve events, substantially raising the CIF of Resolve, especially in the P1 strata. The estimated breach probability would be artificially optimistic. |
| Naïve censoring of Withdraw/Reject | Downward bias in \(B_A\) compared to the same transfer scenario with competing risks | Early withdrawals/rejections are treated as censoring, removing them from the risk set without reducing the number of Resolve events, so the KaplanMeier “survival” for Resolve increases, making the process look better than it is. |

**Which conclusions could reverse?**  
- Under the base scenario, the SLA breach rate might appear unacceptably high for P1 cases, whereas under the “transfer as resolved” scenario it could seem compliant.  
- If management uses the censoringbased analysis to decide on resource allocation, they may underestimate the need for proactive intervention on escalated cases that might become transfers.  
- Stratifying by cohort: cohortB shows more `AutoCheck` and potentially different escalation patterns. If transfers are treated as censored, any difference in transfer rates between cohorts could be obscured, leading to a false conclusion that the policy change had no effect.

Therefore, the **base competingrisk scenario should be the primary** for decisionmaking, with the other two clearly labelled as sensitivity checks.

# 7. Operational interpretation

## From survival outputs to alerts and workload actions

For each open case, we can compute the **conditional probability of breach** (not being resolved by 72h) given that the case has already survived to its current age without a terminal event:

\[
P(\text{breach} \mid \text{alive at } t_{\text{now}}) = 
\frac{B_A - F_{\text{Resolve}}(t_{\text{now}})}{1 - F_{\text{any terminal}}(t_{\text{now}})} 
\;\; \text{or equivalently} \;\; 
1 - \frac{F_{\text{Resolve}}(72) - F_{\text{Resolve}}(t_{\text{now}})}{S_{\text{any terminal}}(t_{\text{now}})} .
\]

- \(F_{\text{Resolve}}(t)\) is the CIF of Resolve;  
- \(S_{\text{any terminal}}(t)\) is the eventfree survival (still open) from the multistate perspective.

Using a fitted AalenJohansen model (or Coxtype prediction on the subdistribution), this quantity can be converted into a **risk score** between 0 and 1 for every active case.

**Operational actions:**
- **Realtime alerting:** Set thresholds (e.g., risk>0.5) to trigger case reviews, escalation, or resource reallocation.
- **Workload monitoring:** The estimated \(S(72)\) (EstimandB) informs the expected number of cases still pending exactly at the deadline; operations can staff accordingly.
- **Queue prioritisation:** Sort open cases by descending conditional breach probability and focus on the toprisk ones.

## Risk scores are not causal effects

We must communicate that these scores are **descriptive and predictive**, not causal estimates of the effect of an intervention. A highrisk label does not mean that a particular action (e.g., reassigning the case) will reduce the probability of breach by a given amount; it only quantifies the historical frequency of breach for cases with similar characteristics.

## Calibration and fairness checks

- **Calibration:** Create deciles of predicted breach risk (using the survival model) and compare with the observed proportion of breaches in each decile (via a calibration plot). Because the dataset is small, this is illustrative; bootstrap over the cases to obtain stability.
- **Fairness by priority and cohort:** Check whether the risk model systematically over or underpredicts for certain priority/cohort groups. For example, if P1 cases have a higher falsenegative rate, operators may fail to act on truly urgent cases. Such checks must be repeated after any model update.

## SLA governance

- Report not only the overall breach rate but also causespecific contributions (withdrawal, rejection, transfer, still open). This helps identify whether policy changes (e.g., discouraging early withdrawal) or operational improvements (reducing transferinducing escalations) are needed.
- Establish run charts of 1–CIF at 72h per quarter, stratified by priority, to track SLA compliance over time.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

For stronger inference, the following data are needed, roughly in order of priority:

1. **Exact event timestamps for every activity**  
   Without them, any timevarying process features (current stage, time spent in Review, time to escalation) cannot be located on the clock, making it impossible to fit multistate models or landmark analyses that dynamically update risk. The provided `duration_or_age_h` and `last_event_gap_h` give only the total length and the final idle period.

2. **External outcomes after transfer**  
   The true fate of transferred cases is unknown. Even a small sample of external resolution times would allow modelling the “posttransfer” process and reduce reliance on sensitivity assumptions.

3. **Observation cutoff date and case start dates**  
   The data probably come from a single snapshot, but knowing the actual calendar dates would allow us to assess seasonality, workload effects, and whether open cases are a random sample of ongoing processes or a cohortbycohort artifact.

4. **Timevarying workload and staffing**  
   The hazard of resolution may depend on the number of concurrent open cases. Without workload data, we treat each case independently, which may miss queuedependent delays.

5. **Assignment mechanism for cohortB**  
   The rollout of automated screening was not randomized and compliance was incomplete. Without knowing which cases were *supposed* to receive the intervention (vs. actually received it), the cohort effect cannot be interpreted causally. Information on compliance classes (e.g., “intended to use AutoCheck”) would enable an instrumentalvariable or perprotocol analysis.

6. **Reasons for withdrawal and rejection**  
   If withdrawal is often clientinitiated (e.g., problem solved elsewhere), it might be treated differently than an internal administrative rejection. Textbased reason codes would sharpen the competingrisk framework.

7. **Longterm followup on open cases past the cutoff**  
   A second extract, perhaps 30 days later, would reveal the terminal status of the currently open cases, turning them from censored to fully observed and greatly strengthening the tail estimates.

With these enhancements, the multistate model could be fully parametrised, the informative censoring problem solved, and reliable dynamic risk scores generated for realtime case prioritisation.
