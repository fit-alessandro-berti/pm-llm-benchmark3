Sequence position, elapsed work time, clock time, queue conditions, and assigned cases change together. # 2. Integrated explanation

The early improvement is compatible with both warm-up and changing work allocation. Workers begin with inherited backlog: complex cases, urgent work, and possibly unfinished investigations requiring context reconstruction. The first bin has mean complexity 0.72 and a 41% high-priority share. By positions 31–50, those figures are 0.50 and 25%. Consequently, part of the reduction from 18.8 to 12.9 minutes may simply reflect easier work. The initial decline in errors is consistent with learning or stabilization, but does not separate these from case mix or resource composition.

The late pattern is qualitatively different. Mean queue length rises from 31 at positions 31–50 to 88 at 76+, while service falls from 12.9 to 10.8 minutes and errors rise from 2.7% to 7.9%. Seven-day rework rises from 5.0% to 13.7%. Easier cases continue to arrive in these bins, yet measured quality deteriorates. The WIP table reinforces a possible speed–quality tradeoff: service under high WIP is about 25–26% shorter within each reported complexity band, while errors increase by 3.7 percentage points for low complexity and 6.7 points for high complexity. These are descriptive comparisons, not adjusted causal estimates.

Pressure can therefore produce apparent productivity gains through faster decisions, reduced checking, task switching, or batches of quick completions. The gains may disappear when downstream correction time, repeat contacts, and reopened cases are included. Additional rework can then increase future WIP, creating a feedback loop between congestion and poor quality.

Fatigue may coexist with that pressure and amplify it. A tired worker can maintain or increase speed by sacrificing verification, particularly when facing a growing queue. Conversely, later low-WIP error rates remain near early rates for several groups; this weakens a simple claim that elapsed time alone explains deterioration. It does not prove that fatigue is absent: low-WIP periods may permit recovery, and the later low-WIP sample may contain different work. Juniors' particularly large deterioration under high WIP suggests an experience–pressure interaction, but their assignments and review coverage must be considered before drawing individual conclusions.

Shift composition adds another layer. Day has rising demand and the highest reported late error rate; Early clears inherited difficult work; Night has fewer events and more urgent exceptions. These patterns do not rank workers' endurance or isolate circadian effects. Clock time, staffing, incoming demand, case mix, and time on duty overlap. Night staff also form a distinct resource group, making a separate night effect especially difficult to identify without rotation or other comparable observations.

Finally, the timing of measurement matters. Delayed Quality Check can move detection of an early mistake to the end of a shift. Document Review batches can shorten recorded event duration without shortening total effort proportionately. Bot-origin errors may become human correction events. A credible explanation must distinguish when work occurred, when a defect originated, when it was detected, and which resources or systems contributed.

There is also selection into sequence bins: reaching 76+ events depends on task duration, concurrency, activity mix, and worker pace. Slower workers handling complex cases may never enter that bin. Sequence position is therefore both an exposure proxy and an outcome of earlier work, not an interchangeable substitute for elapsed duty time.

# 3. Proposed analyses

**Establish a reliable event and outcome model.** Compute event interval duration as completion minus start; retain activity and resource identity. Validate timestamp ordering, duplicates, missing starts, zero durations, and overlapping intervals. Convert instants consistently and derive shift/clock features in the center's local timezone. Assign overnight work to its recorded shift occurrence rather than splitting it at midnight. Resolve events crossing a shift boundary explicitly. Determine whether `shift_id` identifies a schedule type or an actual dated shift; the analysis needs a resource–shift-occurrence key.

Recompute sequence by start time within each human resource–shift occurrence, with an explicit tie rule. Compare it with the supplied sequence field, which may use another ordering or scope. Validate that the many events implied by high sequence positions are compatible with recorded duty time: concurrency, batch logging, and elapsed versus active time can invalidate a simple sum of service durations. A recorded start-to-finish interval is not necessarily continuous hands-on work.

Define WIP and queue length before analysis: their unit, scope, inclusion of the current case, and treatment of waiting versus in-service work. The proposed alert uses **WIP > 70**, whereas the automation clue uses **queue length > 70**. These thresholds refer to different variables. Start-of-event samples also overrepresent busy periods with many starts; time-weighted queue exposure requires snapshots or a sufficiently complete queue history.

Audit `error_flag` and `rework_flag`: do they describe the current event, a prior event, detection, or a later case outcome? Establish whether unreviewed work can receive an error flag. Do not treat unreviewed events as verified correct. Distinguish unique events, unique cases, and eligible reviewed decisions; the WIP table's “Cases/events” label leaves the denominator unresolved. Ensure complexity is measured before the decision being analyzed, rather than updated because an error or rework occurred.

**Build comparisons with adequate overlap.**

- **Within resource:** Compare the same person performing the same activity/family under similar complexity, priority, shift conditions, and WIP. Use resource effects to account for stable worker differences; retain repeated cases and shifts in uncertainty calculations. Compare early and late work only where both occur under comparable conditions.
- **Within shift:** Analyze sequence position and elapsed minutes using flexible curves. Fit them separately first, then jointly only where there is enough independent variation. Track family-specific repetitions, cumulative workload, and known recovery periods. Use fixed elapsed-time windows as a sensitivity analysis so high event counts do not define the comparison population. Do not infer a break solely from a logging gap.
- **WIP/queue stratification:** Reproduce low/medium/high-WIP summaries separately by activity, complexity, shift phase, and experience. Estimate continuous curves rather than imposing an unsupported discontinuity at 70. Examine joint WIP/queue distributions, staffing capacity, and correlations before including both predictors. Show empty or sparse comparison cells.
- **Complexity and selection:** Use nonlinear complexity adjustment, priority, activity, carryover age, previous case steps, and weekend/backlog indicators where available. Standardize results to a common eligible case mix. Inspect the cases offered to each worker, not just accepted/completed cases, if assignment data exist. Do not extrapolate seniors' complex-case performance to juniors who rarely receive those cases.
- **Shift and clock time:** Include shift type, local clock time, date/day of week, and demand conditions. Overlapping shifts provide some clock-time contrasts at different elapsed times; rotating workers provide stronger within-resource comparisons. With fixed schedules, clock time and elapsed time can be inseparable, so avoid assigning a causal label to whichever fitted curve absorbs the variation.
- **Experience interactions:** Test workload-by-experience, elapsed-time-by-experience, complexity-by-workload, and workload-by-activity effects. Prefer a small prespecified set of interactions. A nearly constant experience measure is absorbed by resource fixed effects; estimate its main effect through a justified hierarchical/between-resource model, or use actual longitudinal changes. Do not interpret the “Night staff” group as an experience level.

**Model speed and quality separately, then interpret them together.** For human events, an illustrative service-time model is:

`log(duration) ~ resource + activity + shift_type + calendar + f(complexity) + priority + f(elapsed_minutes) + f(WIP) + cyclic(clock_time) + selected interactions`

Use an appropriate positive-duration model or quantile regression; analyze valid zero-time human records separately rather than silently adding an arbitrary constant. Fit a corresponding hierarchical logistic model for event-level errors. Use case and resource–shift dependence structures, or justified clustered/bootstrap uncertainty, because observations from the same worker, shift, case, or batch are not independent. With only 13 human resources, worker-level generalization and cluster-based inference require particular caution.

Estimate alternative specifications for sequence versus elapsed time and WIP versus queue length. Add validated batching and review-coverage measures where appropriate. Report standardized predicted duration and error probability, uncertainty intervals, and overlap diagnostics. Split predictive validation by later shifts/dates, keeping linked cases together; a random event split would leak repeated-case and shift information. No adjusted coefficient or statistical significance can be computed from the supplied aggregates alone.

Define the estimand before choosing controls. The total association of late duty with quality includes mechanisms operating through pressure or batching; a model conditioning on them estimates a different, conditional association. WIP is measured before the current event but is affected by previous speed and rework. Such feedback, together with unmeasured staffing and selection, prevents ordinary regression from proving causality. A staffed intervention randomized across comparable teams or shift periods can better identify total effects. Account for shared queues, spillovers, and time trends; do not adjust away a trial's WIP reduction when estimating its total benefit.

**Use process mining to locate mechanisms.** Analyze full case trajectories alongside human-event comparisons. Examine activity/resource dotted charts and hourly event distributions for review batches and correction bursts. Discover batches within resource/activity and validate sequential runs against queue-batch IDs, which are not among the listed fields and would need joining from another source. Repeat conclusions across plausible batch-gap thresholds. A sequence of similar tasks alone does not establish a common batch.

Discover performance DFGs for coherent case cohorts and compare delays around Risk Classification, Quality Check, Specialist Referral, and Rework Correction. Examine activity repetitions, loops, handovers, and long waiting paths. A DFG edge duration is a configured transition measure, not automatically active service or true queue waiting; verify its timestamp basis. Removing selected events before discovery can create artificial direct-follow edges, so retain complete traces when comparing routes. Cluster complete traces by route/task mix, if useful, to identify exception pathways; clustering does not establish a causal mechanism and should not use the target error outcome to manufacture groups.

The following is **PM4Py-oriented pseudocode**. It illustrates the workflow; helper routines require local definitions and PM4Py keyword compatibility must be checked in the installed version. The prompt contains aggregates, not the raw event log, so this is not an executed analysis.

import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
RES = "org:resource"
START = "start_timestamp"
END = "time:timestamp"

# Helpers below encode the measurement and cohort rules described in the text.
x = raw_events.copy()
x = normalize_timestamps(x, source_timezone=center_timezone)

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    x, case_id=CASE, activity_key=ACT, timestamp_key=END,
    start_timestamp_key=START

# Full-case enrichment precedes worker/activity filtering.

    log.copy(), start_timestamp_key=START
)
case_features = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(case_features)
# Audit the service/waiting definitions when case activities overlap.
# First/last logged events are arrival/finish proxies unless validated.
temporal_features = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log)

human = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log, RES, ["Bot_Risk"], level="event", retain=False
)
activity_service = pm4py.get_service_time(
    human, aggregation_measure="median", start_timestamp_key=START
)

# Event-window views are for timing diagnostics, not complete-case routing.
events_in_window = pm4py.filter_time_range(
    human, window_start, window_end, mode="events"
)
pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph(events_in_window, distr_type="hours")
pm4py.view_dotted_chart(
    events_in_window, attributes=[END, RES, ACT]
)

# Run per dated resource-shift so unrelated shifts cannot merge into batches.
review = human[human[ACT].eq("Document Review")]
batch_results = []
for _, part in review.groupby("resource_shift_occurrence"):
    batch_results.append(pm4py.discover_batches(part))
# Validate detected types/gaps against sequential runs and queue-batch IDs.
human = attach_validated_batch_features(human, batch_results)

# Model tables preserve index-event time, review status, and origin attribution.
analysis = make_index_event_table(human, full_log=log)
analysis = attach_error_and_rework_outcomes(
    analysis, full_log=log, horizon_days=7,

# Keep immature outcomes censored/pending, never label them as no rework.
fits = fit_resource_adjusted_duration_error_and_rework_models(analysis)
curves = standardize_predictions_on_common_support(fits, analysis)

# Select cohorts by the index exposure, then recover every event of each case.
for label, case_ids in define_case_cohorts(analysis).items():

    dfg, start_activities, end_activities = (

    repetition_counts = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(traces)

Audit PM4Py's case service/waiting fields against concurrency and timestamp semantics. Where activities overlap, elapsed case time minus summed event durations is not a valid waiting measure; compute the union of service intervals for that purpose, while labeling other inter-event gaps separately.

Use `filter_case_performance` and `filter_paths_performance` to inspect long-running cases and specific slow transitions, while retaining an unfiltered reference population. Conditioning the main effect analysis on cases that finished quickly would select on an outcome. Time filters used for plots must not truncate the downstream follow-up used for quality analysis.

**Attribute downstream outcomes and their timing.** Define eligible index decisions, link errors and corrections back to those decisions when the evidence permits, and preserve separate origin, detection, and correction timestamps. Report both event-level defects and case-level outcomes without counting one defective case repeatedly as several independent failures. If attribution to one resource is ambiguous, retain shared or unknown attribution rather than blaming the correcting worker. Separate bot-origin, human-origin, and unresolved defects.

For “rework within seven days,” specify the anchor, such as the eligible decision's completion, and use the same definition across groups. Only fully observed cohorts support a simple seven-day percentage; otherwise use censoring-aware time-to-rework analysis. Include cross-shift corrections, rework service minutes, repeat callbacks, reopenings, and total time to stable resolution. Analyze errors by origin time as well as discovery time to determine whether late Quality Check bursts are moving reported errors into the final hour. Use consistently sampled audits to distinguish true quality changes from changes in detection coverage.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

- **Selection makes raw rankings unfair.** Seniors receive complex early backlog; juniors and night staff face different tasks and support. Workers reaching high sequence positions are a selected population. Even within-worker comparisons retain time-varying assignment bias. Compare supported, comparable work and show uncertainty rather than presenting an adjusted score as a causal measure of ability.
- **Measurement can misassign responsibility.** Completion clicks, batch timestamps, overlaps, delayed flags, and missing reviews distort service and quality. Correcting another person's or a bot's mistake must not automatically count against the correcting worker. Repeated activity can be legitimate investigation, not rework caused by an error.
- **Management controls important exposures.** Staffing, arrival smoothing, handovers, queue targets, training, and review capacity shape pressure. A growing queue is a system condition; asking workers to clear it faster without support can generate the observed tradeoff. Evaluate management decisions alongside worker behavior.
- **Automation belongs inside quality accountability.** Near-zero bot duration is not proof of useful productivity. Include human validation, correction effort, false classifications, downstream consequences, and bot version changes. Humans reviewing poor output should not absorb the automation system's performance failures.
- **Detection and reporting are valuable work.** Higher recorded errors may reflect more complete checking or honest reporting. Punitive metrics can suppress flags and shift defects out of view. Preserve reporting channels and corroborate trends with independent, consistently sampled audits.
- **Use worker information proportionately.** Explain dashboard purpose and definitions, restrict access to individual details, suppress sparse comparisons, and involve staff in interpreting anomalies. Do not infer medical fatigue, sleep problems, or motivation from event logs. Collect additional recovery information only where necessary and with appropriate privacy protections.
- **Protect claimants and the whole operation.** Monitor priority-specific delays, defect severity, and outcomes for difficult cases so speed targets do not reward cherry-picking. Assess effects across receiving queues and shifts, not only the team whose headline throughput improves.

Show denominators, cohort dates, uncertainty, and reference case mix beside quality measures. Use trends and sustained deviations with minimum-volume rules rather than red/green judgments based on a few cases. An actionable alert should name the queue, workload condition, and responsible support action. Evaluate every apparent service-time improvement against waiting, review coverage, mature downstream quality, and workload distribution across the full process.
