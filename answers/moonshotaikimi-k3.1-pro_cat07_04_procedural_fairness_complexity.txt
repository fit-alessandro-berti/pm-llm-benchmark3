| **Quality/accessibility of support** | A Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review event does not establish effective communication or an effective accommodation. The 3.8-day language-support queue, 4.7-day accommodation queue, and additional handovers suggest that rights-preserving steps may be delivered through slower, more fragmented procedures. Comprehension, interpreter quality, accommodation adequacy, accessibility of notices, and dignity are not measured. |
| **Appeal/rework burden** | B and D retain adjusted appeal elevations (+4.4 and +5.7 points), while C’s is smaller (+1.1). Higher appeals can indicate error or poor explanations, but can also reflect better advocacy or access to a corrective right; lower appeals can likewise reflect barriers. Raw rework is much higher for B, C, and D, but no adjusted rework analysis is provided. |
| **Resource-allocation fairness** | B and C cases are concentrated in small specialist pools and selected overloaded or dual-role resources, while A cases are spread more widely. This is consistent with capacity bottlenecks, but staff counts alone do not prove inequitable allocation without FTE, caseload, and queue data. Equitable allocation may require **more**, not merely numerically equal, capacity for higher-need pathways. |

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

A **disparity** is an observed difference. An **adjusted disparity** is a residual difference under a particular model. **Legitimate routing** requires a valid policy reason, consistent application, and reasonable implementation; the mere presence of a risk score or required activity is insufficient. **Discrimination** is a stronger causal and possibly legal conclusion that this observational evidence cannot establish by itself.

1. **Define cohorts, estimands, and endpoints first.**
   - Start at application submission and include pending cases appropriately, rather than analyzing only conveniently closed cases.
   - Report both total group disparities and complexity-adjusted disparities.
   - Separate time to initial decision, time to notification, time to benefit activation, appeal duration, and total closure time. V6 includes an appeal and is not directly comparable with V1’s initial approval/activation path.
   - Analyze medians and upper quantiles, routing, repeated requests, handovers, rework, and approval, with confidence intervals and absolute standardized differences.

2. **Match or stratify on baseline characteristics, while checking overlap.**
   - Candidate variables are region, month/backlog period, channel, household size band, prior case count, recorded risk band, and documentation completeness measured at a clearly defined intake point.
   - Use language preference, validated language-support need, and accommodation request for need-specific routing analyses and effect-modification checks.
   - Exact/coarsened matching, propensity weighting, or stratified regression can be used, with covariate-balance and common-support diagnostics. A may not provide a credible comparison for every B or C support-need stratum; unsupported extrapolation should not be reported as an adjusted fact.
   - Resource, variant, waiting time, case outcome, and appeal are downstream variables, not ordinary baseline controls.

3. **Use several adjustment sets rather than one allegedly definitive model.**
   - A total-disparity model should avoid controlling away operational mechanisms through which unequal treatment occurs.
   - A second model can add recorded risk and intake documentation to approximate the preliminary “same recorded complexity” comparison.
   - Subsequent models can add path and resource assignment to decompose, rather than redefine, the disparity. If a gap disappears after adding resource fixed effects, that may identify unequal queue allocation as the mechanism; it does not automatically make the process fair.
   - Compare both between-queue and within-queue/officer differences, with uncertainty clustered appropriately by resource and region where feasible.

4. **Compare paths and variants conditionally.**
   - Estimate V3 use among comparable high-risk cases, V4 among comparable support-need cases, and V5 among accommodation requests.
   - Within sufficiently populated common variants, compare stage waits, handovers, rework, and outcomes by group.
   - Standardize results to a common variant distribution to separate differences associated with path mix from within-path differences. Treat this as descriptive standardization unless its causal assumptions are defended.
   - Check both over-routing, such as unsupported additional scrutiny, and under-routing, such as requested support not being delivered.

5. **Decompose waiting time.**
   - At case level, distinguish activity service time, internal queue time, handoff time, applicant/external document time, rework time, and calendar effects where timestamps permit.
   - Separate differences due to longer-path frequency from differences in the same stage within the same path. Analyze agency-controllable queue time separately, but do not simply classify all applicant-held time as neutral: inaccessible forms or notices can create that delay.
   - Use case-level totals or appropriate regression/bootstrap methods; subtracting group medians edge by edge does not provide an exact decomposition.

6. **Conduct sensitivity analyses for questionable controls.**
   - Estimate models with and without `risk_score_band`, within recorded risk bands, and under scenarios of differential risk-score error. A risk score should not be treated as neutral merely because it is observed.
   - Measure documentation completeness at submission, after support, and before each later request where possible. Compare treating it as baseline complexity versus a potential mediator of language/access barriers.
   - Repeat analyses with and without channel adjustment, because channel can represent both case mix and differential access.
   - Use quantitative bias analysis or bounding to ask how strong unobserved severity or workload differences would need to be to explain the residual estimates.

7. **Prespecify subgroup checks.**
   - Stratify by region and online/in-person channel, especially comparing the two backlog/high-handover regions with other regions.
   - Examine group-by-region and group-by-channel interactions, language-support and accommodation strata, high-risk strata, and decision type.
   - Report small-cell uncertainty and avoid concluding that effects differ merely because one subgroup is statistically significant and another is not.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The variant distribution shows why raw cycle-time comparisons are insufficient, but it does not explain away the adjusted or operational findings.

- **Path composition matters.** V1 has a 5.8-day median and accounts for 42% of A cases, but only 19%, 22%, and 18% of B, C, and D cases. Longer V2–V6 paths are more common in the comparison groups. However, these are only top paths, their endpoints differ, and their median times are pooled across groups. They therefore cannot provide a complete causal decomposition.

- **Group B has several converging warning signs.** Its case mix—46% incomplete documentation, 38% in-person, and 57% needing language support—plausibly explains some use of V2 and V4. Nevertheless, B has adjusted differences of +2.6 days, +9.5 points for Request Missing Documents, and +8.0 points for Supervisor Review. It also has a 3.8-day support-to-eligibility wait, a 1.6-day completeness-to-document-request wait versus 0.4 days for A, and a 1.9-day supervisor-to-decision wait versus 1.0 day. Concentration among seven bilingual workers and three overloaded officers, plus generalist–bilingual–generalist handovers, is consistent with an operational mechanism. The residual documentation and review differences could still reflect unobserved need or coarse covariates, so they require case review rather than an inference of staff bias.

- **Group C’s strongest concern is implementation of accommodation, not the accommodation requirement itself.** V5 accounts for 31% of C cases and has a 21.6-day median. The specialized queue contributes a 4.7-day wait before eligibility assessment, and C retains the largest adjusted cycle-time difference (+4.1 days). Five specialists, two also performing supervisor review, together with specialist–supervisor handovers, create a plausible bottleneck. Accommodation Review may be necessary and beneficial; that does not justify avoidable queueing or fragmentation. C’s adjusted approval and appeal differences are smaller, but cannot be declared negligible without uncertainty estimates.

- **Group D’s routing is more plausibly complexity-related, but not fully cleared.** V3 accounts for 28% of D cases, consistent with its 51% high-risk share. The risk-to-fraud wait is only moderately longer than A’s (2.8 versus 2.2 days), and D’s adjusted cycle-time, documentation-request, and supervisor-review differences are comparatively small. However, its adjusted appeal elevation (+5.7 points), raw long-tail time, and raw rework remain relevant. If the risk score systematically overstates D’s risk, apparently legitimate routing could reproduce historical disadvantage.

- **Some operational differences are more concerning than the existence of additional steps.** The supervisor-review backlog in two regions and repeated specialist handovers are not inherent consequences of language or accommodation need. By contrast, the decision-to-notification differences are relatively small. The n/a entries for support-specific waits must not be treated as zero waits for other groups; those 3.8- and 4.7-day figures need comparison across regions, periods, and support-need strata, and against explicit service standards.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

1. **Legitimate case-mix and documentation needs.** Incomplete files, in-person verification, and unobserved case complexity can genuinely require more requests and review. Some additional elapsed time may reflect applicant document retrieval, although accessibility barriers can also contribute to that time.

2. **Legitimate policy-required routing.** Language support, accommodation review, and fraud review can protect applicant rights or program integrity. V4, V5, and V3 are therefore not inherently unfair. Their presence, however, does not justify any amount of waiting or any handoff pattern.

3. **Capacity–demand mismatch.** Limited bilingual and accommodation capacity, dual specialist roles, and regional supervisor backlogs could generate longer queues without any individual intending differential treatment. This is a structural fairness concern, not merely a neutral explanation.

4. **Fragmented handovers and context loss.** Repeated transfers can cause rereading, duplicated requests, inconsistent instructions, and accountability gaps. That could connect B/C handovers with longer waits and higher raw rework.

5. **Inconsistent discretionary gatekeeping.** Staff could interpret completeness, escalation, or supervisor-review criteria differently when language, accommodation, channel, or prior-history cues are present. This could generate residual routing disparities, but unobserved legitimate complexity could generate similar patterns.

6. **Biased measurement and feedback loops.** Historical enforcement or prior-case patterns could inflate recorded risk for D, leading to more fraud review and more opportunities for delay or adverse decisions. Language barriers or inaccessible forms could depress measured documentation completeness for B, triggering further requests and reinforcing delay.

7. **Communication and appeal-access effects.** Poorly translated or unexplained decisions could produce avoidable appeals and rework. Conversely, better advocacy or outreach in some communities could increase appeals by making a corrective right more usable.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

- **Risk-score validity is unresolved.** The log contains no score provenance, independent fraud outcome, calibration, or group-specific false-positive/false-negative analysis. Adjusting for the score may remove legitimate complexity, certify a biased measurement, or do some of both. `prior_case_count` may carry related historical bias.
- **Documentation completeness is ambiguous and potentially endogenous.** Its timing, definition, and missing-data handling matter. A coarse intake measure may leave residual confounding; a post-contact measure may already reflect language access and staff assistance. Adjusting for it could therefore understate total procedural burden.
- **Important applicant needs are missing.** Benefit or case type, detailed accommodation requirements, dialect and interpreter availability, literacy and digital access, urgency, applicant availability, representation, and actual case severity could affect both routing and outcomes. Group labels also bundle heterogeneous and potentially intersecting needs.
- **Support presence is not support quality.** The log does not measure whether contact was successful, whether interpretation was accurate, whether an accommodation was granted and effective, or whether the applicant understood the process. `case_outcome` does not capture explanation quality, dignity, or correctness.
- **Resource workload is underobserved.** Daily workload cannot reliably distinguish active service, queueing, applicant waiting, simultaneous assignments, absences, or within-day bottlenecks. Staff counts do not reveal FTE or capability, and resource identifiers do not by themselves establish individual culpability.
- **Appeal and rework attributes are incomplete.** There are no appeal grounds, representation, outcome/reversal rate, decision correctness, or appeal-processing quality measures. Rework is not separated into applicant-caused, agency-caused, and quality-improving repetition.
- **Time and variant summaries can be selective.** Calendar versus business time, paused cases, pending-case censoring, and differing path endpoints are unresolved. The top variants are not exhaustive, and the supplied adjusted results lack confidence intervals and model diagnostics.
- **Residual disparity is not identified causation.** Unobserved severity, model misspecification, lack of overlap, region-specific backlogs, and selection into decided or closed cases could all affect the estimates. Lack of statistical significance in a later model would likewise not prove fairness.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Mitigation should preserve and improve support steps, not remove them to make aggregate cycle time look better.

| Option | Proposed action | Side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Add language-support and accommodation capacity** | Target staffing, qualified overflow support, and cross-training to observed queues and need; pilot in backlog regions and evaluate stage waits, quality, and handovers. | Cost and staff diversion may lengthen other queues. Rapid cross-training can produce inadequate expertise, and improved access may initially increase recorded demand. Capacity should be based on workload and need, not simply equal staff counts. |
| **Create service-level targets for support steps** | Measure time from identified need to first effective support and from support/review to the next decision stage, including upper percentiles. Pair targets with quality and accommodation-effectiveness measures. | Targets can encourage premature closure, superficial “contact” events, timestamp gaming, or prioritization of easy cases. Missed internal targets should trigger assistance, not applicant penalties or skipped review. |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers** | Use a support-trained case owner, warm handoffs, shared structured notes, and specialist consultation without full case transfer where appropriate. Parallelize only genuinely independent work. | Excessive generalization can reduce expertise; parallel work can duplicate effort; and fewer checks can weaken independence. Fraud and supervisor safeguards should remain where justified. |
| **Improve decision explanations and requests** | Provide specific, accessible reasons, consolidated document requests, clear next steps, and appeal rights in the applicant’s language and required format. Test comprehension and audit whether repeated requests decline. | Upfront staff time may increase, translation errors can create new burdens, and clearer appeal information may initially increase appeals—a potentially beneficial access effect rather than a failure. |
| **Monitor raw and adjusted disparities** | Maintain a dashboard for stage waits, p90 times, routing, handovers, rework, appeals, and outcomes, with within-path and region/channel analyses, confidence intervals, and alternative adjustment sets. Add stratified qualitative case reviews. | A single adjusted dashboard can hide biased controls or control away the mechanism. Small cells create privacy risks, and targets can invite gaming. Resource-adjusted results should not replace between-resource allocation analysis. |
| **Audit risk scores and documentation measures** | Validate risk against independent outcomes, assess calibration and error rates by group and region, examine historical proxies, and review overrides. Standardize completeness definitions and test forms/notices for language and disability accessibility. | Historical labels may themselves be biased. Changing risk thresholds can alter fraud detection or shift discretion to staff; changing documentation rules can create inconsistency if poorly implemented. Audits also require privacy safeguards and adequate manual-review capacity. |
| **Audit appeals and rework** | Review stratified samples for decision error, explanation quality, repeated evidence requests, and agency-caused rework; monitor appeal outcomes and processing time rather than trying simply to minimize appeal rates. | Detailed review adds workload and can chill discretion if used punitively. Applicant surveys may have differential nonresponse and should supplement, not replace, case evidence. |

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The strongest supported classification is **indeterminate overall, with specific and potentially material procedural-fairness concerns**. Raw approval gaps do not establish discrimination, and required language, accommodation, and fraud-review paths can be legitimate. Nevertheless, case mix cannot be used as a blanket explanation: adjusted cycle-time and routing disparities remain, and B/C support pathways show queues, specialist concentration, and handovers consistent with unequal implementation. D’s routing is more plausibly risk-related, but risk-score bias and its elevated adjusted appeal probability remain unresolved.

The evidence does not justify a finding of intentional discrimination, but it also does not support a clean bill of procedural fairness. Capacity, queue, handoff, documentation, and explanation problems warrant remediation now, alongside within-path, region/channel, resource-allocation, and measurement-bias audits.
