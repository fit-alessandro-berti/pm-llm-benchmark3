## 1. Executive memo

Management should adopt a guarded hybrid (E), with an immediate interim scope equivalent to the safe part of C: retain coordinator ownership for simple, low-risk houses; suspend survey skipping for medium-risk or uncertain addresses; and prohibit billing start before a successful activation test. Do not scale R nationally and do not perform a blanket rollback.

R has a real, but narrow, benefit. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, handovers of three or more fell from 34% to 12%, and simple low-risk houses improved both speed and first-time-right results. These gains are consistent with the merged validation/scheduling path and clearer case ownership. However, the pilot also produced a materially worse customer and operational tail: p90/p95 rose to 38.5/62.0 days from 31.0/45.0, first-time-right fell to 76%, complaints rose to 9.8%, second visits to 18%, and manual activation to 15%. The most affected groups are apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, and business customers. Coordinator utilization of 93% and the increased failed-visit/second-visit delay suggest capacity and resilience problems, not merely a reporting artifact.

The apparent cost saving is unsafe to treat as realized: visible pre-complaint cost fell to EUR 161, while the incomplete downstream estimate rose to EUR 207. The pilot is also confounded by regional mix, incomplete early segment attributes, sparse start timestamps, and a six-week observation window. High model fitness with precision of only 0.51 means the discovered model explains the observed traces but permits too many alternative paths; it is not evidence that those paths are controlled or desirable.

Immediately gate billing on successful activation, restore permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings, and place a load cap or backup coverage around coordinators. Monitor p90/p95, first-time-right, complaints, second visits, manual activation, and billing-before-activation by segment and region. Instrument the missing timestamps and reason codes, and follow costs for at least 60 days after billing. Then run a stratified controlled test of survey skipping and validate its risk rule against field-survey outcomes.

Broader rollout should require, with confidence intervals and adequate follow-up, no material increase in p90/p95 or SLA breaches versus baseline, no deterioration in first-time-right or 30-day complaints for any high-risk segment, full cost no worse than baseline, and near-zero billing-before-successful-activation. If those conditions fail, limit R to the simple-house segment and redesign the exception and capacity model.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

- **High fitness but low precision:** Fitness asks whether observed traces can be replayed by the model. Because many pilot cases use the coordinator path, a flexible model can fit them well. Precision asks whether the model rejects behavior not actually supported by the log. A precision of 0.51 indicates that the model allows many extra combinations—such as skipped surveys, manual activation, second visits, and billing before activation. Thus high fitness is compatibility with observed behavior, not proof of a disciplined process or good outcomes.

- **Improved median but worse tail:** The median is dominated by the common, uncomplicated path. Removing a handoff and merging tasks can make that path much faster. A smaller population of apartments, permit-sensitive buildings, failed visits, or overloaded queues can nevertheless accumulate large delays, raising p90 and p95. The segment table directly shows this: simple houses improve while old buildings have a 25-day p90 deterioration.

- **Fewer handovers but lower resilience:** Fewer transfers reduce coordination friction when the owner is available. They also concentrate tacit knowledge and decisions in one coordinator. The absence observation, 93% utilization, centralized working-together network, and increased delay under high load indicate a single-point-of-failure effect. A simpler handover graph can therefore coexist with a less resilient organization.

- **Lower visible cost but more rework/downstream cost:** The EUR 161 measure stops before much complaint handling. Revisit, activation-specialist, customer-service, and billing-dispute work occurs later, while first-time-right and second-visit metrics worsen. The inclusive estimate already moves from EUR 198 to EUR 207, though it is incomplete. R may reduce booked upstream work while externalizing cost into later queues.

- **Simple-house improvement but complex-building deterioration:** The skip rule removes a survey where the address is easy to validate and the physical risk is low. Apartments and old buildings have more shared infrastructure, permits, access constraints, and installation uncertainty; the missing permit checks and poor rule performance expose those differences. Aggregated results mix unlike treatment effects, so an apparently contradictory overall result is expected from heterogeneous effects.

## 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | When reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures speed and ownership gains quickly; potentially lowers routine-path effort. | Exposes the entire network to worse p95, rework, complaints, manual activation, compliance-like permit gaps, and coordinator bottlenecks. | Only after controlled evidence shows safe tail, full cost, activation, and segment outcomes; current evidence does not meet that bar. |
| **B. Roll back** | Restores known controls and avoids further downstream harm while simplifying operations. | Sacrifices a strong simple-house benefit and may retain unnecessary handovers; does not reveal which component caused harm. | Reasonable if billing/permit defects cannot be contained immediately or if customer harm is escalating faster than measurement can support. |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend skips and post-billing activation** | Isolates the most plausible low-risk benefit while restoring survey and activation safeguards. | Ownership alone may still overload coordinators and centralize knowledge; benefits may be smaller than R’s full effect. | Good immediate containment option, provided staffing, backup coverage, and queue limits accompany it. |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Identifies causal effects and supports risk-stratified decisions; limits exposure. | Delays broad benefits and requires instrumentation, randomization, and operational discipline. | Necessary for testing survey skipping, risk thresholds, and ownership/capacity variants before scale. |
| **E. Hybrid deployment plus controls and measurement** | Preserves proven simple-house gains, protects complex segments, and learns while operating. | More routing complexity and governance; requires accurate risk classification and monitoring. | Best fit now, with E implemented as C immediately and D run before expanding eligibility. |

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

Ranked by expected decision value:

1. **M1 — Randomized survey-skipping pilot by building-risk stratum.** This most directly tests the key intervention and whether its benefit is confined to low-risk homes. It can determine whether to restore, narrow, or expand skipping.
2. **M6 — Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes.** A bad or poorly calibrated rule could explain the apartment/old-building harm. Validation sets the eligibility threshold and identifies protected classes.
3. **M4 — Measure full downstream cost for 60 days after billing.** This resolves whether the EUR 161 saving is real or cost shifting. It can reverse the economic case for scaling.
4. **M7 — Conformance monitoring for billing before activation and permit-check absence.** This tests whether hard controls are working and quantifies operational/customer risk. Failure should block rollout regardless of median speed.
5. **M2 — Instrument start timestamps for installation, activation, and manual activation.** This distinguishes queue/wait delay from execution delay and makes tail and resource interventions actionable.
6. **M3 — Add structured reason codes for failed visit, manual activation, complaint, and second visit.** This identifies root causes and prevents a permissive discovery model from masking distinct exception types.
7. **M5 — Compare coordinator ownership with shared-team ownership during coordinator absence.** This estimates resilience and the value of backup/context sharing, important for the operating model but less urgent than customer and compliance controls.
8. **M8 — Separate customer experience for appointment speed from working service after installation.** This clarifies the trade-off and improves guardrails, but the existing complaints and NPS already show that the reliability side worsened.

These should be designed as one measurement package where possible: stratify or randomize by building risk and region, pre-specify tail and harm metrics, and report confidence intervals and treatment effects rather than only aggregate averages.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: E, implemented immediately as a controlled C and followed by D.**

- **Immediate action:** keep coordinator ownership for simple, low-risk houses; add coordinator load limits, backup coverage, and shared case context. Gate billing on successful activation and restore permit checks for older or permit-sensitive buildings.
- **Pause:** survey skipping for medium-risk, apartment, old-building, and unclassified cases; post-billing activation; any national rollout.
- **Scale:** the merged validation/scheduling and ownership pattern only within the low-risk eligibility band, with segment-level monitoring.
- **Measure next:** prioritize M1, M6, M4, and M7, supported by M2 and M3; use M5 for the resilience design and M8 for customer guardrails.
- **Broader-rollout threshold:** expand only if a pre-specified, adequately powered follow-up shows p90/p95 and SLA breach rates no worse than baseline, first-time-right and 30-day complaints no worse in every protected high-risk segment, full 60-day cost no worse than baseline, and billing-before-successful-activation effectively eliminated. If any material harm persists, restrict R to the simple-house segment and redesign the risk rule, exception ownership, and backup model before further expansion.
