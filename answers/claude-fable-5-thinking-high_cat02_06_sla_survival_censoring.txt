# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin.** t = 0 at the `Submit` event of each case. *Assumption A1:* `duration_or_age_h` is elapsed clock time from `Submit` to the terminal event (terminal rows) or to the observation cutoff (`open` rows). This is needed because no raw timestamps are supplied; the SLA is explicitly defined from `Submit`, so this single case-level clock is the correct time scale.

**Horizon.** The SLA horizon is tau = 72 h. Curves are estimated on [0, ~110 h] (the largest observed time), but all SLA statements are evaluated at t = 72.

**Events and censoring (definitions used throughout).**
- *Event of interest (resolution):* first arrival in `Resolve`.
- *Competing terminal events:* `Withdraw`, `Reject` — after these, internal resolution can never occur, so they are **competing risks**, not censoring.
- *`Transfer`:* the case leaves the observation window with unknown eventual outcome. It is *either* a competing event (for internal estimands) *or* informative right censoring (for whole-lifecycle estimands) — treated under multiple assumptions (Section 2).
- *Right censoring:* `open` rows are administratively right-censored at the cutoff, at time = age. Crucially, an open case aged > 72 h has **already been observed to breach** any "by 72 h" criterion — its breach status is known even though its eventual outcome is censored. Censoring truncates knowledge of the *final outcome*, not of the *72-h status*, for such cases.

**Estimands.**

- **E1 — Probability of not being resolved by 72 h:** P(T_res > 72), where T_res is time to internal resolution and withdrawal/rejection (and, in the base case, transfer) are competing events that preclude resolution. Estimated as 1 − CIF_resolve(72) via Aalen–Johansen. *Decision match:* this is the **customer-facing SLA breach rate** — "of cases submitted, what fraction gets a resolution within 72 h?" It is the right quantity for SLA reporting to clients and for penalty exposure, because a withdrawn or rejected case genuinely does not deliver a resolution within 72 h.
- **E2 — Cumulative incidence of *late* resolution:** P(T_res > 72 and case eventually resolves) = CIF_resolve(∞) − CIF_resolve(72). *Decision match:* sizing the **late-resolution backlog** — how much work will still complete but breach — which drives overtime/queue-drain staffing decisions, distinct from cases that will never resolve at all.
- **E3 — Probability of no terminal disposition at 72 h:** S_any(72) = P(min(T_res, T_wd, T_rej, T_trans) > 72), a standard survival function where *any* terminal disposition is "the event" and only `open` rows are censored. *Decision match:* **work-in-progress control and alerting** — "which fraction of intake is still on our desks at 72 h?" This governs queue-aging alarms and capacity planning regardless of eventual outcome.
- **E4 — Cause-specific 72-h risks:** CIF_k(72) for k in {resolve, withdraw, reject, transfer}. *Decision match:* **process-improvement targeting** — e.g., whether breaches are driven by slow resolution versus leakage into withdrawal (customer gives up) or transfer (offloading), which call for different interventions.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Coding convention: `event_code` 0 = right-censored, 1 = resolve, 2 = withdraw, 3 = reject, 4 = transfer; `time` = `duration_or_age_h`.

| observed_status | Event indicator (E1: resolution) | Competing-risk code | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| resolved | 1 if resolution (event of interest) | 1 | none | none needed; check late (>72) vs on-time split |
| withdrawn | 0 for resolution; event for "any disposition" (E3) | 2 (competing) | never censor at withdrawal for E1 | (a) competing event (primary); (b) worst-case: recode as "unmet demand" breach; (c) censor at withdrawal *only* to bound what naive censoring would claim (known-biased benchmark, not an estimate) |
| rejected | 0 for resolution; event for E3 | 3 (competing) | never censor | same three variants as withdrawal; rejection is arguably a *legitimate* disposition (S05 at 9 h met a decision-SLA even though it never "resolved") — report E1 both counting and excluding rejects from the denominator |
| transferred | scenario-dependent | 4 | scenario-dependent | **T-a (primary):** competing event — internal resolution becomes impossible; transfer contributes to CIF_transfer. **T-b:** non-informative right censoring at transfer time — case continues "somewhere", eventual resolution possible; valid only if transferred cases resolve like comparable retained cases (implausible: all three transfers S08, S21, S32 are escalated P1 cases — transfer is outcome-related). **T-c (worst case):** transfer = breach (never resolves by 72 externally). **T-d (best case):** transfer = resolution at transfer time. T-c/T-d bound the truth. |
| open, age ≤ 72 (S10 47, S15 35, S24 67, S29 51, S31 23) | 0 (censored) | 0 | right-censor at age; genuinely unknown 72-h status | stress-test with worst case (all breach) / best case (all resolve immediately after cutoff) |

Three explicit transfer treatments (used in Sections 4, 6, 8): **T-a** competing risk, **T-b** censoring at transfer, **T-c** deterministic breach (with T-d as the optimistic bound). T-a is primary because the *internal* SLA question is about internal resolution; T-b is retained only as a sensitivity because its non-informativeness assumption is contradicted by the transfer profile (all P1, all escalated).

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Survivor / completed-case bias.** A naive analyst computes "share of resolved cases breaching 72 h": overall 4/13 ≈ 31% (S03 76, S13 110, S20 74, S28 88 out of 13 resolutions). This conditions on eventual resolution — a *future* outcome — and silently drops 19 of 32 cases. The deterministic bounds from the full risk set tell a different story: **at least 15/32 ≈ 47% breached E1** (4 late resolutions + 4 open cases already past 72: S09, S11, S25, S26 + 7 withdraw/reject cases that never resolved: S05, S06, S12, S14, S22, S23, S30), and **at most 23/32 ≈ 72%** (adding 3 transfers and 5 open cases under 72). The naive 31% is below even the optimistic bound because slow open cases haven't "graduated" into the resolved sample yet — classic survivorship: the fast finishers are over-represented among completed cases at any frozen cutoff.

**Informative censoring.** Treating `open` as non-informative censoring assumes open cases resolve at the same rate as comparable cases still under observation. The `last_event_gap_h` field contradicts this: S26 is 104 h old with **72 h of silence** (nothing since ~32 h), S11 has a 55 h gap, S25 48 h. These are plausibly abandoned (latent withdrawals). Non-informative censoring would let them "borrow" the resolution hazard of active cases and *understate* breach risk. Conversely S15 (gap 2 h), S24 (gap 4 h), S31 (gap 3 h) are demonstrably active, and censoring them is close to non-informative.

**Competing risks.** Kaplan–Meier "1 − KM" for resolution with withdrawals censored treats S06 (withdrawn 27 h) and S12 (15 h) as if they might still resolve, redistributing their probability mass onto resolvers and **overstating** cumulative resolution incidence (hence understating E1 breach). With 7 withdraw/reject cases in 32 (~22%), this is not a rounding error. The correct device is the Aalen–Johansen CIF, which sends their mass to their own causes.

**Cohort imbalance.** Cohort B is not exchangeable with A: it has 5 P1 cases vs A's 3, was assigned non-randomly after a policy change with incomplete rollout compliance (visible in-band: S20, S23, S26, S30 are cohort-B cases *without* `AutoCheck` in their prefix — apparent non-compliers), and has more open cases (6 vs 4) at the frozen cutoff, i.e., differential administrative censoring. A raw A-vs-B comparison confounds policy effect with case mix, compliance, and intake timing. At minimum, stratify or adjust by priority, and compare B-compliant vs B-non-compliant descriptively without causal claims.

**Open cases below vs above 72 h.** These are epistemically different. S09 (92 h), S11 (81), S25 (79), S26 (104) are *observed breaches with censored final outcome* — dropping them deletes known SLA failures. S10 (47), S15 (35), S24 (67), S29 (51), S31 (23) have *genuinely unknown* 72-h status — S24 at 67 h has only 5 h of runway and will very likely breach, while S31 at 23 h may still resolve on time. Any 72-h point estimate must count the first group as breaches and handle the second by risk-set methods (or bounds), never by deletion.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

- **Kaplan–Meier** is appropriate *only* for E3 (any-disposition survival), where every terminal status is the event and only `open` is censored — there is no competing-risk structure by construction. Used for the WIP/aging view. For E1 it is invalid: censoring withdrawals/rejections violates the competing-risk structure (Section 3).
- **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence (primary for E1, E2, E4).** Nonparametric CIFs for resolve / withdraw / reject / transfer sum to 1 − S_any(t) and respect the fact that competing events remove cases from risk. With n = 32 this is estimable with wide but honest confidence bands; report CIF_resolve(72) with Greenwood-type SEs. **Recommended primary analysis:** AJ CIFs, stratified by cohort and by priority (separately, given the small n — full cross-stratification produces cells of 1–3 cases).
- **Cause-specific hazards (Cox)**: models the instantaneous rate among those still at risk; right tool for *etiology*-flavored questions ("does escalation-in-prefix accelerate resolution among survivors?") and accommodates time-varying covariates. With 13 resolution events, at most 1–2 covariates per model (events-per-variable), so use it sparingly (e.g., priority only, cohort only).
- **Subdistribution hazards (Fine–Gray)**: models the CIF directly, so covariate effects translate to 72-h risk statements — attractive for SLA prediction. Deferred to secondary status here: n is too small for stable partial-likelihood estimation with several covariates, and the primary deliverable (stratified CIFs at 72 h) does not need regression.
- **Restricted mean time (RMST) to 72 h**: E[min(T, 72)] gives "average hours consumed of the SLA budget" — a useful governance KPI that is robust and well-defined even with heavy censoring beyond 72; report as a secondary summary from the E3 KM curve.
- **Landmarking (recommended secondary analysis).** Fix landmark times (e.g., 24 h, 48 h); at each landmark, form the risk set of cases still without disposition, define covariates *using only the prefix observed by the landmark* (escalated-by-24 h, stage-at-24 h), and estimate residual 72-h breach risk. This is the clean answer to the immortal-time problem with `Escalate` (Section 5) and directly matches the operational question "this case is 24 h old and escalated — what now?" *Constraint:* activity-level timestamps are not supplied, so landmark covariates from prefixes are only approximable (we know order, not times); with current data, landmarking can use only age and terminal/open status exactly, which is why exact event timestamps top the data wishlist (Section 9).
- **Multi-state model (design proposal, not fittable well at n = 32).** States from prefixes: Submitted → {AutoCheck, Screen} → Review ⇄ {Rework, Escalate} → absorbing {Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer}. An AJ estimator on the multi-state transition matrix would give stage-occupation probabilities at 72 h and stage-specific sojourn hazards — the most process-faithful representation — but it needs per-event timestamps and more cases. Kept as the target architecture for the full log.

**Recommendation and justification.** *Primary:* Aalen–Johansen CIFs for the four causes, evaluated at 72 h, under transfer scenarios T-a/T-b/T-c, stratified by cohort and by priority — it matches the estimands exactly, handles competing risks correctly, uses all 32 cases, and makes no regression assumptions the data cannot support. *Secondary:* (i) KM + RMST for E3 aging control; (ii) 24 h/48 h landmark breach-risk tables as the leakage-safe way to use escalation information. Fine–Gray and the multi-state model are specified now and fitted once the full timestamped log is available.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

All numbers evaluated at t = 72 on the full 32-case risk set; deterministic bounds are computable exactly by classification (per Section 3), stochastic versions via AJ.

1. **Transfer treatment (T-a vs T-b vs T-c/T-d).** With only 3 transfers (S08 68 h, S21 26 h, S32 96 h; S32 already past 72 at transfer), moving between "competing event" (T-a), "censored" (T-b), and "breach" (T-c) shifts E1 by roughly 2/32 ≈ 6 points (S08, S21 are the two whose 72-h status flips; S32 breaches under every treatment). *Reversal risk:* modest overall, but **concentrated in P1** (all transfers are P1): the conclusion "P1 has acceptable resolution SLA performance" can flip between T-b and T-c, since 3 of 8 P1 cases are transfers.
2. **Withdrawal/rejection as breach vs competing vs censored.** Counting withdraw/reject as breaches raises E1's lower bound from 15/32 to a much higher figure (adds 7 cases); censoring them (invalid, benchmark only) lowers apparent breach markedly. *Reversal risk:* whether the process "mostly meets the SLA" (breach < 50%) reverses on this choice alone, so the report must fix E1's definition contractually before quoting one number.
3. **Informative censoring of stale open cases.** Recode open cases with `last_event_gap_h` ≥ 24 (S11, S25, S26; borderline S09 gap 31) as latent withdrawals at their last-event time (age − gap: S26 at ~32 h, S11 at ~26 h, S25 at ~31 h). Effect: S_any(72) for E3 drops (dispositions happen earlier), CIF_withdraw rises, CIF_resolve(72)'s complement (E1) is *unchanged in its lower bound* (these cases were breaches anyway) but the *forecast* residual resolution probability for aged cases collapses. *Reversal risk:* the operational conclusion "aged open cases are still worth working" vs "aged open cases should be proactively closed/contacted" hinges on this.
4. **Cohort/policy effect robustness.** Compare A vs B under: (i) intention-to-treat (as assigned), (ii) per-protocol (B restricted to AutoCheck-compliant cases S16–S19, S21, S22, S24, S25, S27–S29, S31, S32), (iii) priority-stratified. B looks faster on resolved cases (median resolved duration B 39 h vs A ~47.5 h) and has fast wins (S16, S18), but B's open cases (6, incl. S26 at 104 h) and 2 transfers erode this under T-c. *Reversal risk:* "the policy change improved SLA performance" can reverse between (i) and (ii)+T-c; given non-random assignment, no causal claim survives regardless — report as descriptive with both codings.

# 7. Operational interpretation

- **Alerts from E3 (aging curve).** Convert S_any(t) into aging thresholds: alert when a case crosses ages where the historical residual-breach probability exceeds tolerance (e.g., at 48 h an un-disposed case has limited runway — S24-type cases at 67 h are near-certain breaches and need same-shift action). Add an **inactivity alert** independent of age: `last_event_gap_h` above ~24 h (S11, S25, S26 pattern) triggers owner re-assignment or customer contact, because stalled cases dominate the informative-censoring risk.
- **Workload actions from E2/E4.** The late-resolution incidence (E2) sizes the "will finish, but late" queue for surge staffing; CIF_withdraw identifies where customers give up (Review stage) — reduce Review latency rather than adding SLA reporting. Transfer incidence among P1 flags an escalation path that exports the hardest work — track post-transfer outcomes contractually.
- **SLA governance from E1.** Publish E1 with its definition attached (transfers = T-a, withdraw/reject counted as non-resolution) plus the deterministic bounds [47%, 72%] so a frozen-cutoff snapshot is never quoted as a settled rate. Never report the completed-case 31% figure.
- **Not causal.** Risk scores (e.g., "escalated-by-24 h cases breach more often") are prognostic associations on non-randomized data; escalation is triggered by case difficulty, so acting on the score must not be read as "escalation causes breaches". Policy (cohort B) effects likewise remain descriptive until assignment and compliance are modeled.
- **Calibration.** Check that predicted 72-h breach probabilities match observed frequencies within priority × cohort cells (small-sample: use pooled deciles or exact binomial bands); recalibrate after any policy change, since cohort B shifts the hazard structure (AutoCheck-first paths).
- **Fairness.** Compare breach rates, alert rates, and time-to-first-action across priority and cohort: P1 cases here are escalated (5/8) and transferred (3/8) disproportionately — verify the escalation policy is not converting high-priority into "exported" cases; verify cohort-B non-compliers (S20, S23, S26, S30) are not systematically a customer segment being under-served by the new screening.

TAU = 72.0

# ---- Estimand encodings; nobody is dropped. event codes: 0 cens,1 resolve,2 withdraw,3 reject,4 transfer
def encode(d, transfer_mode):
    """transfer_mode: 'competing' (T-a) | 'censor' (T-b) | 'breach' (T-c) | 'resolve_at_transfer' (T-d)."""
    e = d.copy()
    base = {"resolved":1,"withdrawn":2,"rejected":3,"open":0}
    e["code"] = e.status.map(lambda s: base.get(s, 4))
    if transfer_mode == "censor":
        e.loc[e.status=="transferred","code"] = 0
    elif transfer_mode == "breach":              # never resolves internally: keep as competing cause 4
        pass                                     # (identical risk-set math to T-a; differs in bound classification)
    elif transfer_mode == "resolve_at_transfer":
        e.loc[e.status=="transferred","code"] = 1
    e["any_terminal"] = (e.code > 0).astype(int)  # E3 event indicator
    return e

# ---- Deterministic 72h classification (bounds for E1; open<=72 & transfers drive the interval)
def bounds_E1(d):
    res_on_time = ((d.status=="resolved") & (d.time<=TAU)).sum()
    open_lo     = ((d.status=="open") & (d.time<=TAU)).sum()      # unknown: optimistic -> resolve on time
    trans       = (d.status=="transferred").sum()
    lo = 1 - (res_on_time + open_lo + trans) / len(d)             # optimistic breach (T-d + open resolve)
    hi = 1 - res_on_time / len(d)                                 # pessimistic breach (T-c + open breach)
    return lo, hi

# ---- Nonparametric Aalen-Johansen CIF (fallback implementation; lifelines used if present)
def aj_cif(d, cause, t_eval=TAU):
    """CIF_cause(t_eval): S(t-) * d_cause/n summed over event times; censored leave risk set only."""
    dd = d.sort_values("time"); n_at_risk = len(dd); S = 1.0; cif = 0.0
    for t, grp in dd.groupby("time"):
        d_all = (grp.code > 0).sum(); d_c = (grp.code == cause).sum()
        if t <= t_eval and n_at_risk > 0:
            cif += S * d_c / n_at_risk
            S   *= (1 - d_all / n_at_risk)
        n_at_risk -= len(grp)                    # events and censorings both exit after t
    return cif

def km_S(d, t_eval=TAU):                          # E3: any-disposition survival (KM)
    dd = d.sort_values("time"); n = len(dd); S = 1.0
    for t, grp in dd.groupby("time"):
        if t <= t_eval and n > 0:
            S *= (1 - grp.any_terminal.sum() / n)
        n -= len(grp)
    return S

lo, hi = bounds_E1(df)
print(f"E1 deterministic bounds, P(not resolved by 72h): [{lo:.2%}, {hi:.2%}]  (naive completed-case figure would be {4/13:.2%})")

print("\n--- Transfer-treatment sensitivity (E1 via AJ: 1 - CIF_resolve(72)) ---")
for mode in ["competing","censor","resolve_at_transfer"]:

# 9. Limitations and additional data

Prioritized wishlist (highest value first):

1. **Per-event timestamps.** Only case-level durations and one gap are given, so escalation/rework *timing* is unknown; landmarking, time-varying Cox, multi-state sojourn hazards, and immortal-time-safe use of `Escalate` all require activity timestamps. This single addition unlocks most of Sections 4–5.
2. **Post-transfer outcomes.** Whether S08, S21, S32 resolved externally (and when) bounds E1 tightly; the T-a/T-c gap is currently irreducible. A data-sharing agreement or a follow-up sample of transferred cases would settle it.
3. **Cutoff and submission calendar dates.** Needed to verify cohort A/B do not differ merely in follow-up length (differential administrative censoring), to check intake seasonality, and to convert ages into calendar aging alerts.
4. **Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance records.** Who/what decided cohort B membership and why 4 cohort-B cases lack `AutoCheck`; without it, policy comparisons remain descriptive. Compliance logs enable per-protocol and instrumental-variable-style analyses.
5. **Time-varying workload/queue state** (open-case counts, staffing per shift): hazards likely depend on congestion; omitting it biases both prediction and any policy comparison if the B period had different load.
6. **Reason codes for withdrawal, rejection, and transfer** to adjudicate whether withdrawal is SLA-relevant "customer gave up waiting" (breach-like) or exogenous, and whether transfers are demand-driven or capacity-driven.
7. **Larger n.** With 32 cases and 13 resolutions, all regression models are underpowered; interval estimates at 72 h span tens of percentage points. The design above is chosen to remain valid (bounds, stratified nonparametrics) at this sample size and to scale unchanged to the full log.
