## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee — Fiber Operations
**From:** Process Excellence & Decision Support
**Re:** Redesign R — scale, roll back, or contain?

**Recommendation.** Do not scale Redesign R nationally, and do not roll it back. Split the bundle. Make permanent what demonstrably works — coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling for simple, low-risk houses. Suspend what demonstrably harms — survey skipping for medium-risk addresses and billing before activation. Restore the omitted safeguard — permit checks for older buildings. Buy the missing evidence before any wider rollout. This is Option E, modified.

**Why not scale.** The headline wins — median cycle time 14.210.8 days, visible cost EUR 184161, multi-handover cases 34%12% — are real but concentrated. Simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot volume) captured nearly all of it: 6.0 days median, +3pp first-time-right. Nearly everything else moved the other way: p95 rose 4562 days, first-time-right fell 8276%, complaints rose 6.19.8%, second visits 1118%. National scaling would export these failures to every region and create a compliance-type exposure: expected permit checks are systematically absent from the log for older apartment buildings. The pilot's own diagnostics point at the mechanism — the redesigned process now tolerates many uncontrolled path combinations (discovered-model precision collapsed 0.790.51), and deviations cluster exactly where harm clusters: repeat installation visits without surveys, manual activation, billing before activation.

**Why not roll back.** The baseline is not a safe harbor: 13% SLA breaches, 82% first-time-right, and heavy coordination overhead. Rollback would forfeit verified gains for roughly a third of volume and reverse the one structural improvement — end-to-end accountability — that shows up in the complaint mix: "waiting for appointment" complaints fell; complaints now concentrate on "installed but not working," which is a solvable execution problem, not a design flaw of ownership.

**The trade-off.** R traded robustness for speed. Handovers, routine surveys, and pre-billing checks were redundant controls that silently absorbed variability. Removing them made the typical case faster and the abnormal case much worse — and pushed cost past our measurement boundary (downstream complaint handling is excluded after week 4; the fully loaded estimate is EUR 207 vs 198 and incomplete). Fewer handovers also bought less resilience: with coordinators at 93% utilization in two regions, an absence now stalls cases that peers used to pick up. Aggregate metrics hide all of this because they average segments moving in opposite directions.

**Actions.**
1. **Contain (this week).** Suspend the survey-skip rule for medium-risk addresses; reinstate mandatory permit checks for older buildings; end billing-before-activation except tightly logged exceptions; cap coordinator utilization near 85% with named backups and shared case notes.
2. **Bank (weeks 2–6).** Extend coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule to simple low-risk houses in remaining regions, with an explicit capacity plan. Leave apartments, old buildings, and business customers on the baseline path with restored checks.
3. **Learn (days 30–90).** Stand up conformance monitoring on billing-before-activation and missing permits; validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes; capture 60-day post-billing cost; add start timestamps and structured reason codes.

**Expansion rules (proposed for ratification).** Widen survey skipping only if the recalibrated rule proves 90% accurate on validation and the target segment holds first-time-right within 2pp of baseline with p90 no worse than baseline. Extend the coordinator model beyond simple houses only with utilization 85% and a passed absence-resilience test. Scale nationally only when fully loaded 60-day cost per case is at or below baseline and complaints are within 0.5pp of baseline.

**What would change this call.** If the skip rule cannot be salvaged, medium-risk addresses stay on mandatory surveys permanently. If full-cost accounting shows even simple-house economics erode over 60 days, we roll back entirely. If coordinator overload proves structural, we move to shared-team ownership with a single accountable lead instead of a single owner.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness measures whether the discovered model can replay observed cases; precision measures whether the model forbids behavior never observed. The pilot genuinely contains many compliant variants (coordinator path, merged steps, skipped surveys, manual activations, repeats), so any model that fits well must allow many combinations — hence precision 0.51. Low precision here is not primarily a modeling failure; it is a *symptom* that R made the process legitimately variable. The drop in generalization (0.700.56) reinforces this: behavior patterns are unstable week to week, consistent with an overloaded, exception-driven system rather than a stable new standard.

**Improved median but worse tail.** The interventions remove fixed steps (waits for scheduling, surveys, pre-billing checks), which helps almost every *routine* case — pulling the median down. The same removals create failure modes (wrongly skipped surveys, missing permits, billing disputes) that trigger loops — repeat visits, manual activation, complaints — affecting a minority of cases but adding weeks each. Loops act only on the tail, so p90/p95 explode while the median improves. Two artifacts amplify this: pilot regions contain more complex buildings (composition confound), and the 6-week window risks right-censoring the longest cases.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handover count is a flow metric, not an outcome metric. Some handovers were redundancy: each transfer carried context and created an implicit backup. Consolidating ownership simplified the handover network but centralized the working-together network around coordinators and activation specialists. At 93% utilization there is no slack to absorb absence or spikes, and the temporal profile shows failedsecond-visit delays growing precisely in high-utilization regions. We removed a cost (coordination overhead) and unknowingly removed an insurance policy (distributed context).

**Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost.** The cost ledger has a boundary: complaint handling after week 4 is excluded. R shifts spend across that boundary — fewer surveys and handovers reduce front-end cost, while second visits, manual activation labor, billing disputes, and complaint handling land later and largely unmeasured. The visible EUR 184161 improvement and the fully loaded EUR 198207 deterioration are therefore not contradictory; they are the same phenomenon viewed inside and outside the measurement window. Any scaling decision based on visible cost alone would systematically overvalue R.

**Simple houses improve; apartments/old buildings deteriorate.** R removes *checks*; the value of a check depends on whether the case needed it. Simple low-risk houses rarely need surveys or permits, so removal is nearly pure time savings. Apartments and old buildings genuinely need them — shared infrastructure raises survey value, and permit-sensitive buildings face regulatory steps — so removal converts a small time saving into large rework (skip rule "often wrong"; +25d p90; 11pp FTR). Aggregated KPIs average these opposite effects and can even mask them entirely (a Simpson's-paradox pattern); only the segment table reveals that R is effectively two different processes wearing one name.

## 4. Decision alternatives

| Alt. | Advantages | Risks | Reasonable if… |
|------|-----------|-------|----------------|
| **A. Scale nationally** | Captures median/time gains everywhere fast; simple message; avoids parallel-process complexity | Exports tail (+17d p95), rework, and billing-before-activation harm to all regions; permit-compliance exposure; replicates 93% coordinator bottlenecks at higher volume; decision rests on confounded aggregates | Essentially **not reasonable now**; only after tail, FTR, and activation issues are fixed and full-cost economics are proven |
| **B. Roll back** | Immediately stops harm in complex segments; returns to known performance; low analytical burden | Forfeits verified gains for ~34% of volume (simple houses); restores 34% multi-handover overhead; baseline itself has 13% SLA breach; destroys learning and team credibility | Harm proves structural (e.g., coordinator model inherently fragile), a regulatory incident occurs, or full-cost data shows losses even for simple houses |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only; suspend skip + post-billing activation** | Keeps the most credible source of median gain (accountability, merged scheduling); removes the two clearly harmful components; simpler than E | Applies coordinator model to segments with weak/no evidence (rural, business); ignores the 93% utilization and resilience problem; no measurement upgrade, so the next decision is equally blind | Paired with a capacity plan, utilization cap, and absence-resilience fix — i.e., C converges toward E |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Highest causal validity; resolves mix confounds; tests components separately | Weeks-to-months delay while known harms continue; control-arm customers held on an inferior path; instrumentation cost; produces insight but changes nothing by itself | Stakes justify rigor **and** interim guardrails (suspend skip/post-billing activation, monitor conformance) limit harm meanwhile |
| **E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale** | Matches the actual granularity of the evidence; banks proven gains on ~34% of volume; contains harm; builds the measurement base; fully reversible | Operating two process variants adds routing complexity and misclassification risk at intake; measurement burden; coordinator capacity still needs planning; partial rollout may create perceived inequity | Segment/risk classification at intake is reliable; conformance guardrails are live; capacity is planned — conditions we can meet |

**Assessment.** No alternative dominates all metrics — that is the core finding. A violates the evidence; B overcorrects and destroys real value; C is directionally right but blind to segment boundaries and capacity; D is rigorous but passive about ongoing harm. **E is the only option that simultaneously banks proven gains, contains demonstrated harm, and purchases the information needed for the next decision.** It should be executed with C's suspensions and D's instrumentation built in.

**Sequencing logic.** Deploy M7 immediately (guardrail, near-zero regret). Run M6 and M4 in parallel — together they resolve the two questions that actually fork the decision: can skipping be saved, and does the economics survive full costing? M2/M3 are measurement infrastructure that makes every subsequent threshold trustworthy. M1 is deliberately deferred behind M6: paying for randomization before knowing whether the rule is recalibratable would be buying precision on a possibly dead option. M5 gates any expansion of coordinator ownership beyond current volumes; M8 is refinement, not decision-critical.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Option E (modified hybrid) — contain, split, and instrument. Neither national scaling nor rollback.**

- **Immediate action (weeks 0–2).**
  - Suspend the survey-skip rule for medium-risk addresses; apartments return to mandatory field surveys.
  - Reinstate mandatory permit checks for older/permit-sensitive buildings.
  - End billing-before-activation except individually logged, supervisor-approved exceptions.
  - Cap coordinator utilization at ~85%; assign named backups and require shared case notes to restore resilience.
  - Switch on M7 conformance monitoring (billing-before-activation; permit absence) with a weekly executive dashboard.

- **What to pause.** All further geographic or segment expansion of any R component; any new deployments of the skip rule; the practice of reporting visible cost without the 60-day downstream supplement.

- **What to scale.** Coordinator ownership + merged validate/schedule, restricted to **simple, low-risk house installations**, extended to remaining regions with an explicit capacity plan. Apartments, old buildings, and business customers remain on the baseline path with restored checks.

- **What to measure next.** M6 + M4 immediately in parallel (decision-forking); M2 + M3 within 60 days (measurement integrity and root causes); M5 before any broadening of coordinator ownership; M1 only if M6 shows the skip rule is salvageable; M8 at the next NPS wave.

- **Thresholds justifying broader rollout (proposed for management ratification).**
  1. *Survey skipping re-extension:* recalibrated rule 90% correct-skip on validation; segment FTR within 2pp of baseline; segment p90 no worse than baseline; confirmed by M1 randomization.
  2. *Coordinator model beyond simple houses:* sustained utilization 85%; passed absence-resilience test (no case stalled >24h under simulated absence).
  3. *National scale:* fully loaded 60-day cost per case  baseline (EUR 198-equivalent); complaints  6.6% (baseline +0.5pp); p95  50 days; discovered-model precision recovering toward 0.65 as variability is brought under control.

- **Kill criteria.** Complaint rate >8% in any scaled segment for two consecutive weeks  pause that segment. Any confirmed permit-compliance incident  immediate reinstatement audit and segment freeze. If M4 shows even simple-house economics eroding over 60 days  escalate to full rollback (B).

**Bottom line:** R is not one intervention but four, and the evidence says they have opposite effects by segment. Management should not choose between scaling and rolling back a bundle — it should unbundle the result, keep the winning third, fence off the failing two-thirds, and spend 60–90 days buying the information that turns the next decision from a gamble into a calculation.
