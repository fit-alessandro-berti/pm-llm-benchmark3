# 1. Objectives and constraints

The policy is a constrained, multi-objective decision rule rather than a claim of one mathematically obvious optimum. It seeks a defensible balance among:

- SLA protection and short-term throughput.
- Correctness and regulatory safety.
- Continuity of ownership and avoidance of costly handovers.
- Fair workload and overtime exposure.
- Documented skill development.
- Resilience through broader skill coverage and avoidance of specialist bottlenecks.

## Safety and compliance constraints

These are hard constraints unless an explicitly logged exception is approved:

1. Skill 0 is ineligible.
2. Security Review requires:
   - skill 3 acting independently, or
   - skill 1–2 with explicit supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo.
3. Final Approval requires skill 2–3 to finalize. A skill-1 worker may only prepare it under a qualified finalizer.
4. High/urgent-priority work cannot go to skill 1 without supervision.
5. No resource receives more than four new assignments in this batch.
6. Quality work is batched only if priority is normal/low and more than 24 hours remain.
7. Assignments must fit plausible remaining capacity; an assignment that creates expected overtime needs a recorded exception.
8. The supervisor must have capacity and is accountable for the supervised checkpoint.

## Preference trade-offs

Subject to the hard constraints, prefer:

- Higher urgency and lower projected SLA slack.
- Competent skill fit, without sending every item to a specialist.
- Current-owner continuity, especially for complex or repeatedly reassigned cases.
- Lower workload and overtime risk.
- Training aligned with documented priorities where SLA and quality risks permit.
- Distribution of work away from scarce Security and Approval specialists where safe.
- At least 20% of normal/low assignments supporting documented training priorities.

Continuity is not absolute: keeping a weakly skilled owner adds a historical median 3.3 service hours and rework risk. Conversely, changing the owner of a high-complexity case adds a median 2.7 waiting hours, while more than two reassignments is associated with substantially higher rework and appeal rates. Those effects must be weighed case by case.

# 2. Assignment policy

## Decision procedure

```text
Inputs:
  cases, resource skills, capacity, workload, overtime risk,
  ownership, handover cost, training priorities, SLA thresholds

1. Order cases by:
   a. projected SLA breach risk,
   b. priority,
   c. complexity,
   d. reassignment count,
   e. smallest remaining SLA time.

2. Generate candidate assignments for each case:
   - independent worker;
   - supervised worker + authorized supervisor, where permitted;
   - current owner with another worker only as a consultation arrangement;
   - hold/escalate if no safe candidate exists.

3. Apply hard eligibility filters:
   - reject skill 0;
   - enforce Security and Approval rules;
   - reject unsupervised skill 1 for high/urgent work;
   - reject assignments over the four-item batch cap;
   - reject infeasible capacity;
   - allow batching only under its Quality rule.

4. Score feasible candidates:
   total =
       0.30 * SLA protection
     + 0.20 * skill match
     + 0.17 * continuity
     + 0.13 * workload/overtime fairness
     + 0.08 * training value
     + 0.12 * resilience
     - explicit exception penalties.

5. Check portfolio-level constraints:
   - training share among normal/low cases is at least 20%;
   - urgent work is covered first;
   - specialist and supervisor demand remains feasible;
   - no worker exceeds four assignments;
   - avoid high-overtime-risk workers where safer alternatives exist.

6. Select the best feasible portfolio, not merely the best candidate
   for each case in isolation.

7. Produce an assignment reason and risk flag for every decision.
```

Weights should be calibrated from observed outcomes and discussed with workers; they are not immutable.

## Component scores

### SLA urgency

Use projected slack rather than time remaining alone:

\[
slack = SLA\_remaining - expected\_waiting - expected\_service
        - handover\_delay - supervision\_delay
\]

The urgency score rises sharply as projected slack approaches zero. Urgent/high priority and complexity increase the consequence component. Cases already likely to miss SLA are escalated rather than hidden by a low score.

### Skill match

- Skill 3: strongest immediate quality score, with a scarcity penalty where skill 2 is sufficient.
- Skill 2: full competence and generally preferred for ordinary work.
- Skill 1: development candidate only when permitted, capacity exists, and supervision mitigates risk.
- Skill 0: ineligible.

Thus, the score rewards sufficient skill rather than automatically maximizing skill.

### Continuity and handover cost

Reward the current owner according to:

- handover-cost band;
- case complexity;
- existing reassignment count;
- whether the owner already has relevant context.

Penalize a change particularly for high-complexity/high-cost cases. Reduce or reverse the continuity reward when the owner has skill 0–1 and safe supervision is unavailable.

### Workload and overtime fairness

Reward lower normalized load:

\[
load_i =
\frac{open\ cases_i + weighted\ new\ work_i}
     {hours\ remaining_i}
\]

Apply an additional penalty for medium/high overtime risk and for repeated concentration of desirable or undesirable work. High-overtime-risk assignment requires an SLA-based justification when an acceptable lower-risk option exists.

### Training and development

Reward only assignments that:

- match a documented priority;
- are normal/low priority unless safe supervision is explicit;
- leave adequate projected SLA slack;
- include a named coach and checkpoint where needed;
- do not convert trainees into cheap capacity.

The training share is measured over assignments, not merely opportunities offered.

### Resilience and bottleneck avoidance

Reward the safe use of competent non-specialists and supervised development in scarce skills. Penalize unnecessary use of scarce Security/Approval specialists and excessive dependence on one resource. Supervised assignments incur supervisor capacity, so nominal diversification must not conceal a bottleneck.

## Tie-breaking and exceptions

Ties are broken by:

1. Lower projected SLA risk.
2. Current ownership.
3. Lower overtime risk.
4. Lower normalized workload.
5. Greater resilience contribution.
6. Stable rotation rather than arbitrary worker identifiers.

If nobody is safe and feasible, the policy does not make an invalid assignment. It escalates to the duty manager for reprioritization, temporary capacity, an approved SLA exception, or case splitting. Manual overrides require an `assignment_reason`, approver, and reviewable reason code.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

| Dimension | First-available | Specialist-only | Proposed dynamic policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected throughput | Often lowers initial waiting but may lose gains through mismatch, handovers, and rework | Fast service on individual items but queues behind scarce specialists | Slightly more decision/review overhead, with fewer avoidable transfers and better portfolio flow |
| SLA risk | Helps easy cases start quickly, but may assign scarce capacity to low-risk work and overlook projected service time | Protects cases once started but can create Security/Approval queues | Uses projected slack, priority, service time, handover delay, and supervisor capacity |
| Error/rework risk | Highest when the least-loaded eligible person is weakly skilled | Usually lowest immediate execution error | Keeps competence floors; allows only controlled, auditable development |
| Workload fairness | Appears fair by current case count but ignores complexity, remaining hours, overtime, and ownership | Tends to overload specialists | Uses normalized workload and overtime risk, while allowing justified continuity exceptions |
| Skill development | Accidental rather than planned | Minimal; specialists monopolize work | Planned assignments with supervision and a measurable target |
| Continuity | Commonly broken whenever another person is less loaded | Broken whenever a higher-skilled specialist exists | Explicitly rewards ownership for costly, complex, or repeatedly transferred cases |
| Resilience | Broad exposure, but potentially unsafe and unsupported | Low; dependency on a small specialist pool grows | Builds backup capability without removing safety gates |
| Explainability | Simple, but “least loaded” may feel arbitrary or misleading | Simple, but specialists may experience permanent overload | More nuanced; every decision needs a concise reason and visible safeguards |

Illustratively, pure first-available routing would tend to favor low-open-case resources such as R10, R6, and R3 whenever their skill is nonzero. That can reduce immediate waiting but would be unsafe if “skill 1” were treated as ordinary eligibility. Specialist-only routing would concentrate Security with R7/R8, Approval with R6, and Quality with R8/R9/R10, reinforcing the documented bottlenecks.

The dynamic policy is not always superior on every single metric. Specialist-only may produce fewer immediate defects on an isolated case, while first-available may start some routine cases sooner. The proposed policy accepts limited supervision and occasional delayed starts in exchange for continuity, learning, fairness, and longer-term resilience.

# 5. Explanation to workers

Assignments are based first on safety, authorization, and customer deadlines. After those requirements are met, we consider who already knows the case, current workload and hours remaining, overtime risk, and documented development goals.

Keeping ownership matters most when a case is complex, has already moved several times, or would lose substantial context. We may still transfer a case when the current owner is not authorized or when the deadline cannot otherwise be protected. Those transfers are recorded with a reason.

Specialists will not automatically receive every matching case. Competent colleagues may handle routine work, and selected development cases may be completed with a named supervisor. Supervision counts as real work and should be visible in workload planning.

The policy is decision support, not a performance ranking. A higher workload, training assignment, or transfer is not a judgment about a worker. People can flag missing context, unrealistic service-time estimates, capacity changes, or wellbeing concerns. Overrides are allowed, documented, and reviewed to improve the policy rather than punish reasonable judgment.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

Indicators should be segmented by activity, priority, complexity, team, assigned skill, supervision status, and assignment policy. Correlation must not be presented as proof of causation.

| Indicator | Definition / use |
|---|---|
| Handover frequency | Resource changes between consecutive events or ownership-assignment events, per 100 cases and per activity |
| Reassignment count | Mean, median, percentiles, and share with more than two reassignments; compare with rework and appeal outcomes |
| Skill mismatch | Share assigned at skill 0, skill 1 without required supervision, or below activity-specific rule; target is zero for hard violations |
| Workload distribution | New-work counts, complexity-weighted load per remaining hour, Gini coefficient, range, and team/resource percentiles |
| Overtime-risk exposure | Assignments and weighted effort allocated to medium/high-risk workers; record whether continuity or urgent SLA justified them |
| SLA misses | Breach rate and projected-slack-at-assignment; distinguish poor routing from unexpected service-time variation |
| Rework/appeal after assignment | Rework Correction or appeal outcome following assignment, stratified by skill, transfer count, complexity, and policy |
| Training assignment share | Documented training assignments divided by normal/low assignments; verify training-priority match, supervision, completion, and outcomes |
| Specialist bottlenecks | Queue length, waiting time, utilization proxy, work-in-progress, and concentration share for Security/Approval specialists |
| Policy deviations | Assignments that fail eligibility, score substantially below the best feasible candidate, breach caps, or are manually overridden |
| Continuity retention | Share retained by current owner, segmented by handover cost and complexity |
| Handover penalty | Incremental waiting/service time after owner changes, especially for high-complexity cases |
| Supervision burden | Number and estimated duration of supervised cases by supervisor; overdue reviews and supervision-related waiting |
| Batch effectiveness | Waiting saved or added, service-time change, SLA impact, and defect rate for eligible Quality batches |
| Assignment reason completeness | Percentage of decisions with a valid reason, score inputs, supervisor where relevant, and override approver |
| Resilience coverage | Number of independent or supervised competent resources per critical skill and concentration of work among the top one/two resources |

Fairness auditing should examine allocation and outcomes, not just equal case counts. Complexity, undesirable work, interruption load, supervision, training access, and overtime exposure should all be visible. Worker representatives should participate in threshold and indicator reviews.

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# Event-level log. Assignment events should include snapshots so that
# decisions can be reconstructed as they were known at the time.
df = pd.read_csv("events.csv")

df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(
    df["time:timestamp"], utc=True
)
df["sla_deadline"] = pd.to_datetime(
    df["sla_deadline"], utc=True, errors="coerce"
)

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Resource interaction, handovers, and collaboration
# ---------------------------------------------------------
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df)
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df)

# Depending on PM4Py version, these networks can be rendered/saved:
# pm4py.view_sna(handover)
# pm4py.save_vis_sna(handover, "handover_network.png")

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Empirical capability, role, and resilience evidence
# ---------------------------------------------------------
similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df)
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df)

# Use these as evidence for reviewing the declared skill matrix,
# not as automatic authorization. Historical performance does not
# override compliance rules or create credentials.

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Service, waiting, and end-to-end performance
# ---------------------------------------------------------
service_time = pm4py.get_service_time(df)
timed_df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)
performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(df)
variants = pm4py.get_variants(df)

# Repeated Diagnosis/Rework variants can identify loops and routing
# patterns associated with long waits or repeated assignment.

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Batching analysis
# ---------------------------------------------------------
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(df)

quality = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    df, "concept:name", ["Quality Check"], retain=True
)
# Audit that observed Quality batches involved only normal/low cases
# and had >24 hours of remaining SLA slack at batching time.

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Attribute and performance slicing
# ---------------------------------------------------------
security = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    df, "required_skill", ["Security"], retain=True
)

urgent = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    df, "case_priority", ["urgent"], retain=True
)

# Example: compare slow and fast cases. Exact thresholds are calibrated.
slow_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
    df,
    min_performance=24 * 3600,
    max_performance=365 * 24 * 3600
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Event-level audit calculations
# ---------------------------------------------------------
assigned = df[df["concept:name"] == "Assignment"].copy()

# In production, join a versioned skill table using resource,
# activity, and assignment timestamp.
skills = pd.read_csv("versioned_skill_matrix.csv")
assigned = assigned.merge(
    skills,
    left_on=["org:resource", "required_skill"],
    right_on=["resource", "skill_name"],
    how="left"
)

assigned["supervised"] = assigned["supervisor"].notna()
assigned["skill_violation"] = (
    assigned["skill_level"].fillna(0).eq(0)
    | (
        assigned["case_priority"].isin(["urgent", "high"])
        & assigned["skill_level"].lt(2)
        & ~assigned["supervised"]
    )
    | (
        assigned["required_skill"].eq("Security")
        & assigned["skill_level"].lt(3)
        & ~assigned["supervisor"].isin(["R7_Gita", "R8_Hugo"])
    )
)

assigned["sla_miss"] = assigned["completion_timestamp"] > assigned["sla_deadline"]
assigned["high_overtime_exposure"] = assigned["overtime_risk"].eq("high")

normal_low = assigned["case_priority"].isin(["normal", "low"])
training_share = (
    assigned.loc[normal_low, "training_assignment"].fillna(False).mean()
)

# Transitions between distinct resources within each case approximate
# handovers; dedicated assignment/owner-change events are preferable.
ordered = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
ordered["previous_resource"] = ordered.groupby(
    "case:concept:name"
)["org:resource"].shift()

ordered["handover"] = (
    ordered["previous_resource"].notna()
    & ordered["org:resource"].notna()
    & ordered["previous_resource"].ne(ordered["org:resource"])
)

handover_rate = ordered["handover"].sum() / ordered[
    "case:concept:name"
].nunique()

workload_stats = assigned.groupby("org:resource").agg(
    new_assignments=("case:concept:name", "nunique"),
    mean_snapshot=("workload_snapshot", "mean"),
    overtime_exposure=("high_overtime_exposure", "sum"),
    policy_deviations=("policy_deviation", "sum"),
)

print({
    "handover_rate": handover_rate,
    "training_share_normal_low": training_share,
    "skill_violations": int(assigned["skill_violation"].sum()),
})
print(workload_stats)
```

For full auditability, assignment events should additionally store:

- policy and skill-matrix version;
- candidates considered and hard-filter reasons;
- component scores and selected score;
- expected service, waiting, handover, and supervision time;
- supervisor and checkpoint;
- best feasible alternative;
- override reason and approver;
- whether the assignment counts toward a training objective.

The PM4Py findings should feed periodic policy review rather than silently changing assignments. Changes to weights, thresholds, or eligibility rules should be versioned, tested for disparate workload and development effects, and discussed with affected teams.
