# 1. Data audit and estimands

## Operating convention and quantities

The center is open 12 hours per day, 07:00–19:00. Unless stated otherwise:

- **Daily arrival intensity**:  
  \[
  \lambda_d = A_d/12
  \]
  in arrivals per open hour, where \(A_d\) is arrivals observed during that day’s opening hours.

- **Daily completion intensity / observed departure rate**:  
  \[
  \mu^{obs}_d = C_d/12
  \]
  in completions per open hour, where \(C_d\) may include cases that arrived earlier.

- **Net daily queue change proxy**:  
  \[
  \Delta Q_d \approx A_d-C_d.
  \]
  This is useful for direction of backlog movement, but it is not an exact end-of-day queue change because midnight carry-over, unobserved starting backlog, and possibly other case-state definitions are not supplied.

- **Mean WIP**: mean cases “in the system” from 15-minute snapshots during opening hours. It is a time average over the open interval, not necessarily a 24-hour WIP average.

- **Service time**: active handling time only. The supplied median and p90 exclude waiting and therefore are not case flow time / sojourn time.

- **Capacity pressure**: assessed using demand, completions, staffing FTE, active service time, WIP, and the daily arrival–completion imbalance. It cannot be reduced to a precise utilization estimate because mean service time, staffing schedules within the day, skill matching, breaks, and parallel work are unknown.

A useful rough workload proxy is active service minutes per arrival. Because only median and p90 are known, no exact mean can be estimated. A defensible descriptive range is therefore the observed median–p90 interval, not an invented mean. For example, a weekday median service time of 35 minutes with p90 of 82 minutes indicates substantially more workload and tail risk than a 25-minute median with p90 of 46 minutes, even before waiting is considered.

## Limitations and threats to naive inference

At least the following threats make a single pooled queueing inference unsafe.

1. **Arrival and completion counts do not match by case cohort.**  
   Cases completed today may have arrived yesterday or earlier. Consequently, \(C_d/A_d\) is not a same-day service probability, and \(A_d-C_d\) is only a rough backlog-direction signal.

2. **Opening-hours truncation and overnight carry-over.**  
   The queue can persist overnight while no service occurs. A 07:00 opening WIP and 19:00 closing WIP are not supplied, so daily windows are not naturally closed cohorts.

3. **WIP is an open-hours snapshot average, not necessarily comparable to daily arrivals or all-calendar-time throughput.**  
   Applying Little’s Law requires a stable population, compatible measurement windows, and a throughput proxy representing the same system. Those requirements are weakest during outage, recovery, and rapid growth/decline in backlog.

4. **Median service time is not mean flow time.**  
   It cannot be substituted into Little’s Law, and it cannot produce an exact staffing workload estimate. The distribution is visibly right-tailed because p90 is much higher than the median.

5. **Non-stationarity is evident.**  
   The observed series contains an outage on 2026-04-15, elevated complex-case percentages beginning around 2026-04-20, elevated WIP, demand changes, and a staffing intervention beginning sometime on 2026-04-29.

6. **Implementation timing of the staffing intervention is unknown.**  
   2026-04-29 is an ambiguous transition day: its staff FTE is already 7.0, but no logged implementation time permits a clean before/after causal interpretation.

7. **Outage and recovery are mechanically linked.**  
   Low completions during the outage and high completions on subsequent days partly reflect suppressed capacity followed by backlog clearing, not necessarily an underlying productivity change.

8. **Daily observations are serially dependent.**  
   Backlog carries across days; therefore high WIP, later completions, and elevated staffing can all be responses to prior conditions. Treating the 28 rows as independent samples would overstate certainty.

9. **Case-mix measurement may have changed.**  
   Complex percentage rises from roughly 18–27% in the early period to roughly 40–47% later. The prompt explicitly permits both a real mix shift and coding change, so causal attribution to complexity alone is not justified.

10. **Intraday arrivals are highly non-Poisson-looking in representative days.**  
    The ordinary weekday, outage day, bursty weekday, and weekend all have different hourly shapes. A daily average hides time-of-day capacity mismatches.

11. **Staff FTE is concurrent mean capacity, not necessarily productive service capacity.**  
    It may include staff with different skills, breaks, non-case work, or partial availability. FTE cannot be equated directly with servers in an \(M/M/c\) model.

12. **The representative hourly vectors are not fully reconciled to the daily table.**  
    For example, the 2026-04-07 hourly arrival vector sums to 101, matching the table, but its completions sum to 99; other vectors should be treated as representative intraday profiles, not a complete event-level record.

The strongest evidence of capacity pressure is not a single ratio but the joint pattern:

- Higher arrivals after 20 April;
- Higher complex-case share;
- Longer service-time quantiles;
- Higher WIP;
- Repeated completion deficits before 29 April;
- Stable weekday FTE around 6.0 before intervention;
- Improvement in completions and WIP after staffing rises.

It is consistent with backlog accumulation but cannot identify whether the limiting mechanism is insufficient staffing, more labor per complex case, changed routing, coding change, within-day arrival bursts, or some combination.

The post-29-April weekday pattern reverses: arrivals are about 115/day and completions about 121/day. That is consistent with backlog recovery under higher capacity, but it is not a causal estimate of the staffing intervention because the date is partly treated, and the completed cohort is not the same as the arriving cohort.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

## Alternative: two-dimensional calendar-and-state segmentation

A materially different approach separates the data first by **calendar operating mode** and then by **queue state**, rather than assigning every day to one chronological regime.

### Calendar operating mode

- **Weekdays:** Monday–Friday, generally 5.5–7.0 FTE and roughly 92–121 arrivals/day.
- **Weekends:** Saturday–Sunday, generally 2.5–3.0 FTE and roughly 36–53 arrivals/day.

### Queue-state overlay

- **Normal / low-WIP state:** mean WIP below roughly 25.
- **Recovery state:** completions exceed arrivals while WIP is elevated or declining.
- **Accumulation state:** arrivals exceed completions and WIP is elevated or rising.
- **Disrupted state:** outage day or comparable availability interruption.
- **Intervention-exposed state:** 2026-04-29 onward.

Under this alternative, a weekday on 2026-04-16 is not merely “post-outage”; it is a weekday, high-WIP recovery day. A weekend such as 2026-04-25 is low-volume but still under high-complexity and accumulation conditions. This avoids treating weekend demand scale as if it were the same regime as weekdays.

| Alternative segment | Operational story | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekday baseline, low WIP | Ordinary demand/capacity relation before major disruption | Separates day-of-week staffing and arrival scale | Small sample; may miss gradual change before 20 April |
| Weekend baseline, low WIP | Lower-volume operation with lower staffing | Avoids misleading weekday/weekend pooled averages | Weekend sample is sparse and may have different customer mix beyond complexity |
| Outage / disrupted | Temporary loss of service capacity | Correctly prevents outage from being treated as ordinary service performance | Exact outage timing and affected work are approximate |
| High-complexity accumulation | Larger complex share, high WIP, repeated daily deficits | Captures state pressure independent of exact calendar date | Complexity could partly be a coding artifact |
| Intervention-exposed recovery | Higher FTE, completion surplus, falling WIP | Aligns with operational action and backlog dynamics | Cannot establish a clean causal intervention effect without implementation time and controls |

## Comparison with the primary segmentation

The primary segmentation is best for explaining the observed chronological narrative: baseline, outage, recovery, high-complexity pressure, then higher-staffing recovery. It is operationally intuitive and directly useful for incident review.

The alternative is better for policy design because it distinguishes structural weekend operating conditions from temporary high-WIP conditions. It reduces the risk of concluding that “weekends are efficient” merely because WIP and arrivals are lower; low weekend WIP can coexist with high service-time tails and lower staffing.

The alternative is weaker for formal estimation because combining non-contiguous dates based on a state threshold can create selection effects. For example, selecting “high WIP” days partly conditions on an outcome of prior demand and capacity. It should therefore be used for descriptive operational monitoring, not as proof that WIP itself caused longer service times.

## Simpson’s-paradox-like reversals

A full Simpson’s paradox cannot be proven from these aggregates because there are no case-level cross-classified outcomes. However, Simpson’s-paradox-like reversals are plausible.

1. **Staffing versus productivity.**  
   A simple pooled comparison might find higher staffing on high-completion days and infer staff productivity increased. But high completions can be caused by clearing prior backlog, while staffing was increased precisely because the system was already stressed. Within a stable weekday/mix/backlog stratum, the relationship may differ.

2. **Complexity versus completions.**  
   The higher-complexity pre-intervention period has lower observed completions relative to arrivals, whereas post-intervention days retain high complexity but have completion surpluses. Pooling high-complexity days could therefore make complexity appear less consequential than it is under fixed capacity.

3. **Weekend “efficiency.”**  
   Weekends have lower WIP and lower arrival volumes. If one pools all days, lower weekend WIP might be interpreted as better performance despite lower staffing and still elevated service-time medians/p90s relative to the earliest weekday baseline.

## Robust comparisons rather than unsupported causal means

Useful ranges are more defensible than a single grand mean:

- **Baseline weekday median service:** 24–26 minutes; p90 44–50 minutes.
- **High-complexity weekday pre-intervention median service:** 34–36 minutes; p90 78–88 minutes.
- **Intervention-exposed weekday median service:** 32–33 minutes; p90 72–76 minutes.
- **Baseline weekday WIP:** roughly 18–23.
- **High-pressure weekday WIP:** roughly 37–50.
- **Post-intervention weekday WIP:** 32–43, declining over the three observed weekdays.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

## Hourly burstiness in representative days

The hourly vectors demonstrate that daily averages are insufficient for queueing design.

### Ordinary weekday candidate: 2026-04-07

Arrivals by hour are:

\[
[3,5,12,20,16,10,8,7,6,5,5,4].
\]

They peak at 10:00–11:00 with 20 arrivals, while the daily average is only about 8.4 arrivals/hour. The hourly arrival coefficient of variation is therefore materially above zero, and the count dispersion is greater than a flat-rate process would imply. Even without fitting a distribution, the 20-arrival peak is about 2.4 times the daily hourly average.

The completion profile is smoother than arrivals but still varies. This is a plausible ordinary weekday with intraday congestion risk around late morning even though its daily total appears ordinary.

### Outage day: 2026-04-15

The arrival profile remains concentrated around 09:00–11:00, while completions are:

\[
[5,7,10,8,0,0,0,4,6,7,6,5].
\]

The zero completions from approximately 11:00–14:00 produce extreme dispersion in completions. A Poisson-like stationary completion model is inappropriate: zeroes are operationally imposed by the outage, not random low-rate fluctuations. The daily completion count of 58 should not be used to estimate ordinary service capacity.

### Bursty post-mix-change weekday: 2026-04-21

Arrivals are:

\[
[2,3,8,34,8,5,24,5,3,14,5,4].
\]

The 10:00–11:00 bin contains 34 arrivals and 13:00–14:00 contains 24, against an hourly mean near 9.6. The arrival profile has multiple sharp peaks rather than one smooth morning wave. This is a stronger indication of intraday overdispersion and time-varying intensity than the ordinary weekday.

The completion pattern is much flatter than arrivals. That mismatch implies WIP growth during bursts even if daily capacity eventually approaches daily arrivals.

### Weekend candidate: 2026-04-25

Weekend arrivals are lower and comparatively smoother:

\[
[1,2,4,7,8,7,6,5,4,3,2,2].
\]

The hourly profile still has a midday concentration, but its peaks are smaller in absolute terms. A separate weekend arrival profile is appropriate; applying weekday peak staffing logic to weekends would overstaff some intervals, while applying a pooled profile could miss weekday peaks.

## Dispersion indicators

Two useful indicators are:

1. **Fano factor / index of dispersion for counts**
   \[
   F = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(N_h)}{\operatorname{E}(N_h)}
   \]
   across hourly bins. Under an ideal homogeneous Poisson count process, \(F\) is near 1. Values materially above 1 indicate overdispersion, though with only 12 hourly bins and deterministic time-of-day variation, this is not a formal Poisson test.

2. **Coefficient of variation**
   \[
   CV = \frac{\operatorname{SD}(N_h)}{\operatorname{E}(N_h)}.
   \]
   This quantifies hourly unevenness independent of scale.

The bursty 2026-04-21 arrivals should have a substantially larger Fano factor and CV than the weekend profile. The outage completion vector should also be highly dispersed, but that dispersion should be attributed to known availability interruption rather than stochastic demand variability.

## Serial dependence and daily non-stationarity

The daily sequence shows visually structured persistence:

- WIP rises sharply on 2026-04-15 and stays high through recovery.
- Complex percentage increases persistently from 20 April onward.
- High WIP and service-time tails persist through 28 April.
- After 29 April, completions exceed arrivals and WIP declines over consecutive days.

A simple lag-1 correlation of daily arrivals, completions, WIP, or complex percentage can be calculated, but it should be treated as descriptive only. With 28 observations, weekday seasonality, an outage, a potential coding change, and an intervention, lag correlation conflates genuine queue carry-over with calendar and structural shifts.

The clearest change-point evidence is not a formal statistical test but a multivariate level shift:

- **15 April:** known outage and operational discontinuity.
- **20 April:** complex percentage jumps from the low-to-mid 20s to around 40%+, accompanied by longer service times, higher WIP, and demand pressure.
- **29 April:** staffing rises and the backlog trend reverses, though treatment timing is uncertain.

A transparent segmentation heuristic might minimize within-segment squared deviations in a standardized set of daily variables while enforcing a minimum segment length, or use pre-specified operational dates. Neither identifies causal ground truth. The known outage and ambiguous intervention date should override a purely algorithmic segmentation if the two conflict.

## Why four representative days are insufficient for a full stochastic model

The four hourly profiles are selected representatives, not a random sample of all 28 days. They cannot establish:

- A stable arrival distribution;
- Day-of-week-by-hour arrival rates;
- Independence of hourly increments;
- Service-time distribution parameters;
- Staffing availability by hour;
- Queue-length trajectories;
- Correlation between complexity, arrivals, staffing, and service requirements;
- The probability of future outage or burst events.

They support the limited conclusion that a homogeneous daily-rate queueing model is too coarse. They do not support fitting a reliable \(M/M/c\), \(M/G/c\), or nonhomogeneous Poisson queue without raw timestamps and service/resource information.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law is:

\[
L=\lambda W.
\]

For these data, \(L\) is mean open-hours WIP, \(\lambda\) must be a compatible throughput rate, and \(W\) would be mean time in system over a compatible open-hours window. The resulting \(W\) is not active service time and should not be compared directly with the supplied median service time.

For the calculations below, the implied open-hours flow-time proxy is:

\[
\widehat W_{\text{open hr}} =
\frac{\overline{\text{mean WIP}}}
{\overline{\text{arrivals}}/12}.
\]

Arrivals are used only where the chosen window is approximately balanced and not dominated by net backlog change. Completion-based alternatives are shown where relevant, but neither resolves boundary mismatch fully.

# 7. Operational implications

## Staffing

1. **Plan weekday staffing by intraday peak and complexity state, not by pooled daily average.**  
   A pooled average near 5.15 FTE is not a sensible roster target. Ordinary weekdays already operate around 6.0 FTE; high-complexity pressure days at the same nominal FTE accumulate backlog.

2. **Use a surge-capacity trigger.**  
   A practical trigger could combine:
   - WIP above a locally calibrated threshold;
   - complex share above a threshold;
   - repeated hourly arrival bursts;
   - arrivals exceeding completions for more than one operating day.

   The supplied evidence supports a dynamic trigger more than a fixed “average staffing” rule.

3. **Separate staffing for recovery from steady-state staffing.**  
   Clearing backlog requires capacity above contemporaneous arrivals. The 16–17 April and 29 April onward completion surpluses are operationally useful, but they should not become the assumed normal throughput target.

4. **Protect against outage-induced backlog.**  
   A 180-minute interruption created a large completion shortfall. A documented outage playbook should specify triage, customer communication, deferred work queues, restart sequencing, and temporary staffing or overtime capacity.

## Backlog recovery

- Monitor both **daily net flow** \(A-C\) and **opening/closing WIP**, which are currently missing.
- Do not declare recovery based only on a high completion count. High completions can be good recovery performance while customer waiting remains poor.
- Track backlog age, not just count. A stable WIP could conceal an aging tail of cases.
- During recovery, distinguish completions of pre-existing backlog from same-day arrivals; this requires case-level arrival and completion timestamps.

## Peak smoothing

The 2026-04-21 representative pattern shows that peak concentration can be more harmful than the daily count alone. Potential actions include:

- Appointment or callback slots away from the 10:00–11:00 spike;
- Demand communication encouraging later or earlier arrival;
- Fast-track routing for simple variants;
- Cross-trained floating staff scheduled specifically around peak hours;
- Staggering non-case work away from observed peaks;
- Virtual queue or token systems that reduce physical or visible queue congestion.

## A policy that looks beneficial in pooled averages but is risky when segmented

A pooled analysis might recommend holding staffing near the average 5.15 FTE because aggregate completions nearly equal aggregate arrivals over 28 days. That policy is risky.

Segmented evidence shows that:

- Weekday demand is much higher than the pooled average;
- The high-complexity pre-intervention weekdays accumulated backlog under roughly 6.0 FTE;
- The outage created a temporary but large capacity gap;
- Post-intervention completion surpluses are partly needed for backlog recovery, not excess normal capacity.

Thus, average-balance staffing can produce periodic overload, customer delay, and backlog aging even if monthly arrivals and completions appear balanced.

## Data collection recommendations

Priority additions are:

1. Case-level arrival timestamp, service-start timestamp, completion timestamp, and cancellation/abandonment state.
2. Case identifier linking arrivals to completions across days.
3. Resource identifier, skill group, actual staffed hours, breaks, and non-case activity.
4. Opening WIP and closing WIP by day, plus queue age distribution.
5. Timestamped outage start/end, systems affected, and partial-service capability.
6. Event-level activity sequence and complexity definition/version so that the observed complex-case increase can be separated from coding changes.
7. Hourly or 15-minute arrivals, service starts, completions, staffing, and WIP for all days rather than four representative profiles.

With these data, process mining could distinguish waiting, active handling, rework, routing, handoffs, and variant-specific workload rather than relying on aggregated service-time quantiles.

# Two hourly burstiness indicators: Fano factor and coefficient of variation.
def dispersion_metrics(counts):
    x = np.asarray(counts, dtype=float)
    # ddof=1 gives sample variance; only 12 hourly bins, so interpret descriptively.
    mean = x.mean()
    variance = x.var(ddof=1)
    fano = variance / mean if mean > 0 else np.nan
    cv = x.std(ddof=1) / mean if mean > 0 else np.nan
    peak_to_mean = x.max() / mean if mean > 0 else np.nan
    return pd.Series({
        "hourly_mean": mean,
        "hourly_sample_variance": variance,
        "fano_variance_over_mean": fano,
        "cv_sd_over_mean": cv,
        "peak_to_mean": peak_to_mean,
    })

# Transparent change-point heuristic:
# For each candidate split, calculate reduction in within-segment SSE for standardized
# variables. It is deliberately descriptive, and does not claim causal or definitive
# change-point identification. Dates with fewer than 4 rows on either side are excluded.
features = ["arrivals", "completed", "median_service_min", "p90_service_min",
            "mean_wip", "staff_fte", "complex_pct"]
z = (daily[features] - daily[features].mean()) / daily[features].std(ddof=1)

def sse(frame):
    center = frame.mean(axis=0)
    return ((frame - center) ** 2).to_numpy().sum()

total_sse = sse(z)
cp_candidates = []
min_segment_size = 4
for split in range(min_segment_size, len(daily) - min_segment_size + 1):
    before = z.iloc[:split]
    after = z.iloc[split:]
    segmented_sse = sse(before) + sse(after)
    cp_candidates.append({
        "candidate_boundary_before_date": daily.iloc[split]["date"].date().isoformat(),
        "sse_reduction_vs_one_segment": total_sse - segmented_sse,
        "rows_before": len(before),
        "rows_after": len(after),
    })
change_point_heuristic = (
    pd.DataFrame(cp_candidates)
    .sort_values("sse_reduction_vs_one_segment", ascending=False)
    .head(5)
    .round(2)
