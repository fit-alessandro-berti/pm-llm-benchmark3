## 1. Conformance semantics

I distinguish seven deviation classes, each with distinct reasoning requirements:

**Forbidden behavior.** A relation explicitly marked `forbidden` in the norm, observed in the log under the stated scope. Under the closed-world assumption applied to forbidden rules, any positive count is a violation unless a documented exception or data-quality explanation exists. The burden of proof falls on the observation to justify itself.

**Missing mandatory behavior.** A relation marked `mandatory` whose expected presence is not fully observed. Under the closed-world assumption applied to mandatory columns within scope, absence is presumptively non-conforming—but the directly-follows abstraction may mask a conforming trace with an unmodeled intermediate event. Low-count gaps (e.g., 2 of 200) may also fall within a noise band the norm does not define.

**Unexpected but unregulated behavior.** A relation observed in the log that the norm does not mention, mark as forbidden, or scope in any way. Under the open-world assumption, silence is not prohibition. Such observations are informational unless they directly implicate a forbidden rule at a different abstraction level.

**Typical-pattern absence.** A relation marked `typical` with fewer observations than a naive reader might expect. Because `typical` explicitly permits exceptions, absence is not a violation; it warrants monitoring only if the absence rate is extreme or business context suggests systemic failure.

**Likely data-quality error.** An observation whose normative status is `forbidden` or `mandatory` but whose surrounding context—reused identifiers, join artifacts, attribute-level evidence—strongly suggests the edge is an artifact rather than a genuine process execution. This class demands a dual-track response: investigate the data pipeline and, until resolved, treat the finding as both a potential violation and a potential false positive.

**Abstraction mismatch.** A mandatory directly-follows expectation that is violated at the graph summary level but may be satisfied at the trace level when intermediate, unmodeled activities intervene. The norm itself acknowledges this risk ("the summary approximates this with a directly-follows expectation"). Counts must be scrutinized: 2 missing out of 100 is far more likely to be an abstraction artifact than 40 missing out of 100.

**Insufficient evidence.** A case where neither the graph summary, attribute data, nor domain context permits a confident classification. The correct response is to flag, investigate, and defer judgment rather than default to either conformance or violation.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG

The OC-DFG captures **directly-follows relations between activities conditioned on object type**. It can establish temporal ordering within an object's lifecycle and reveal structural gaps (missing successors). It **cannot** establish whether an intervening, unrecorded activity exists between two observed events, nor can it distinguish a genuine control failure from an abstraction artifact.

**Counts and scopes.** N1 expects that every newly created Order "eventually enters validation." The norm explicitly warns that the OC-DFG summary approximates this with a directly-follows expectation. Two of 100 Orders lack the direct edge, but 3 Orders have a `Create Order  Manual Compliance Review` edge that could precede validation. If those 2 orders are among the 3, the mandatory rule is satisfied at the business-event level even though the directly-follows graph shows a gap. This is the textbook **abstraction mismatch**: the norm's own language flags the gap between the business rule and its graph-level proxy.

N3 (forbidden: Cancel Order  Dispatch Package) is **not** subject to this defense. The forbidden rule operates at the object lifecycle level—"no package may be dispatched after that same Order is cancelled." Even with intervening events, a post-cancellation dispatch is forbidden. The 2 observed instances are genuine violations unless the cancellation timestamp is erroneous.

N4 (mandatory: Pack Package  Dispatch Package) shows 198 of 200. The context says both exceptions have a carrier reference in an attribute, suggesting dispatch genuinely occurred. The missing edge may be an object-linking gap (packing event not linked to Package) rather than a skipped packing step. This is an abstraction/data-quality hybrid.

**Danger of directly-follows interpretation.** If an analyst reads N1 as "Validate Order must immediately follow Create Order with no intervening activity," they would flag 100 % non-conformance for the 3 Manual Compliance Review cases—cases that are explicitly allowed by the open-world OTG norm (T5: unspecified). Treating every intermediate event as a violation would produce false positives across the log. The norm's wording ("should eventually enter validation") is deliberately weaker than a directly-follows constraint, and the analysis must respect that.

### OTG

The OTG captures **which object types interact, co-birth, or co-death**. It can establish structural relationships between object lifecycles but cannot reveal temporal ordering or causal dependency.

**T6 (forbidden: Order–Payment cobirth).** One instance observed. Cobirth means both objects were created in the same business event. This is forbidden because, in the normative model, payments should follow invoicing, which follows order creation. A single atomic creation of Order and Payment bypasses the entire control chain. The observation may also be a logging artifact (two events collapsed into one log entry). Either explanation requires investigation.

**T2 (mandatory: Package–Delivery interaction).** All 200 Packages have a Delivery interaction—conforming. The 7 co-death edges are unregulated and benign; they simply mean the Delivery and Package lifecycles ended together.

**T3 (typical: Invoice–Payment interaction).** 92 of 110 Invoices show the interaction. 14 have no observed successor (open Invoices) and 4 have Write Off instead. Since T3 is `typical` and the norm explicitly names credit notes, write-offs, and open credit terms as legitimate exceptions, the 18 non-payment successors are not violations.

**T4 and T5** are `unspecified`. The observed Order–Customer (100) and Order–RiskCase (3) edges are outside the norm's coverage entirely. No inference about conformance is possible or warranted.

### ET–OT

The ET–OT table links event types to the object types they must create or modify. It is the most granular model family and the most susceptible to data-quality artifacts.

**E10 (forbidden: Sensor Heartbeat  Invoice).** One instance. The context attributes this to a join on a reused numeric identifier—almost certainly a data-quality error. However, the norm is closed-world for forbidden rules: the observation technically violates E10 regardless of cause. Classification as "likely data-quality error" is appropriate, but remediation of both the data pipeline and the underlying join logic is needed.

**E2 (mandatory: Add Item  Order).** 1448 of 1450 Add Item events carry the Order link. The 2 missing could be extraction failures. The gap is small and consistent with ETL noise.

**E6 (mandatory: Dispatch Package  Delivery).** 198 of 200. The 2 missing Dispatch Package events do have carrier attributes, suggesting the event is real but the Delivery object was not linked. This is a data-quality gap in object linking, not a process gap.

**E8 (mandatory: Record Payment  Invoice).** 92 of 93. The one exception has a Payment but no Invoice. T3 permits unapplied cash, but the event is **not labeled as such**. The norm's scope for E8 says "except events explicitly typed as unapplied cash; none are so typed in this snapshot." Without the explicit label, the observation falls under the mandatory rule and is a potential violation—though the resolution may simply be to apply the label.
