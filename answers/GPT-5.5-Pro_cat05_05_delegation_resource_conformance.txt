## 2. Rule Interpretation

**RR1 — Drafter/approver separation.** For each case, after alias normalization, the human performing `Draft_Change_Request` must differ from the human performing `Approve_Change`. A violation is clear when the same normalized human performed both. If one side is an unresolved service account, the result is not conforming by default; it becomes an identity problem unless reliable session evidence maps the service action to a human.

**RR2 — Implementer/verifier separation.** For each case, after alias normalization and service-account attribution, the human performing `Implement_Change` must differ from the human performing `Verify_Implementation`. If the verifier is `svc_qms` and cannot be reliably attributed to a human, technical log comparison alone is insufficient; the control objective is not proven even if the raw resource string differs from the implementer.

**RR3 — High-risk approval authority.** For non-emergency HIGH-risk cases, `Approve_Change` must be performed by a standing QM or a valid QM delegate at the approval timestamp. Validity requires all of the following: the event timestamp falls inside the delegation interval; the case scope is covered; the principal-absence or unavailability condition is satisfied if required; the delegation evidence existed before use or is independently contemporaneous; and no prohibited subdelegation occurred.

**RR4 — Emergency substitution.** For a HIGH-risk emergency change, an approval by the IC or a valid IC delegate can be accepted as an emergency substitution only if the emergency flag and safety rationale are credible. A QM or valid QM delegate must then perform `Retrospective_QM_Review` within two business days. I count business days using the site calendar: Monday through Friday are business days; 2026-08-15 and 2026-08-16 are weekend days; no site holiday exists in the period. The trigger is the `Implement_Change` timestamp. The trigger day is day 0; the next business day is day 1; the deadline is the end of the second following business day in UTC. Thus, an emergency implemented on Tuesday 2026-08-11 has a retrospective-review deadline of Thursday 2026-08-13 23:59:59 UTC.

**RR5 — Identity evidence.** A service account is not a human and cannot itself hold QM, IC, verifier, or other human authority. Aliases belonging to the same human are collapsed before checking RR1 and RR2. Session evidence is graded: FIDO plus badge evidence close to the action is stronger than workstation ownership plus room entry without re-authentication.

**RR6 — Suspicion versus proof.** Repeated handovers, reciprocal approvals, shared workstations, and concentrated collaboration are risk indicators. They justify targeted investigation, but they do not prove collusion without stronger evidence such as communications, deliberate bypasses, falsified records, financial benefit, manipulated assignment, or inconsistent review substance.

A principal acting while on leave: annual leave does not revoke Asha’s standing QM role. If Asha herself performed an approval while on leave, the action can still be role-authorized. However, because security cannot exclude credential sharing, the identity evidence must be examined. Also, if a delegate’s authority depends on the principal being absent, a principal action at the same time can weaken the factual basis for absence during that specific interval, though it does not necessarily invalidate delegate actions at other times.

Later database entry as delegation evidence: a later QMS database record cannot by itself prove that an earlier delegation existed before use. D-QM-01 is stronger because a signed PDF is timestamped 2026-08-09 18:00, before the delegation interval and before the relevant approvals. The database migration record created on 2026-08-12 09:15 is corroborative, not the source of retroactive authority.

## 4. Collusion and Alternative-Explanation Analysis

The repeated Clara–Ben pattern is a risk indicator, not proof. In the preceding 60 days, Clara drafted and Ben approved 18 of 23 HIGH-risk production changes. A benign explanation is that Clara and Ben are assigned to the same production cell, have relevant local expertise, and no formal pair-rotation policy exists. An adverse explanation is that a stable pair can normalize weak challenge, rubber-stamp high-risk changes, or intentionally route around independent review. Discriminating evidence would include assignment rules, queue data, whether other eligible approvers were available, review-note substance, rejection/rework rates, message logs, and independent quality outcomes for Clara–Ben cases versus other pairs.

The reciprocal pattern is also suspicious but not determinative. Ben drafted four LOW-risk training changes that Clara approved. A benign explanation is ordinary cross-functional collaboration on training updates. An adverse explanation is reciprocal approval trading: “I approve yours, you approve mine.” Evidence that would discriminate includes timing proximity between reciprocal approvals, communications, whether approvals were unusually fast or content-light, whether changes benefited either person, and whether similar reciprocal pairs exist elsewhere.

Shared workstations create both operational convenience and evidential weakness. For CHG-503, `svc_qms` came from Diego’s normally assigned workstation after Diego implemented the change, and Diego entered the room, but the terminal is shared and no individual re-authentication occurred. A benign explanation is that another verifier used a shared station. An adverse explanation is that Diego verified his own implementation through a service account. Discriminating evidence would include individual FIDO re-authentication, screen/session ownership logs, precise badge timestamps, keyboard/video evidence, or e-signature metadata.

Absence conflicts are among the strongest identity concerns. Asha’s CHG-504 approval occurred during a VPN session while she was on annual leave, and she says she personally handled one urgent case. The benign explanation is a real principal QM action during leave. The adverse explanation is credential sharing or delegated work performed under Asha’s account. For Ben, the concern is sharper: CHG-506 was approved with Ben’s account one hour after sick leave began, Ben denies performing it, and the IP was an office workstation. Ben also appears as retrospective reviewer for CHG-505 during sick leave. A benign explanation would require a benign HR/timekeeping mismatch or a brief authorized on-site action, but the supplied facts lean toward identity or credential-control failure. Discriminating evidence would include badge entry, endpoint login, FIDO/MFA, session lock, IP-to-device mapping, and HR sick-leave timing.

Credential compromise and shared-account practices are systemic risks. The adverse scenario is not only collusion; it could also be poor account hygiene, unattended logged-in sessions, or service accounts used for human-controlled GxP actions. The benign scenario is a legacy QMS design that did not enforce individual authentication at every controlled action. Either way, the control objective is weakened because the plant cannot reliably prove who performed a regulated action.

The D-IC-04 attempted subdelegation is more than a pattern indicator: it is a concrete authority failure for CHG-510. Farid may have been a valid IC delegate under D-IC-03, but D-IC-03 explicitly prohibited subdelegation. A benign explanation is misunderstanding during an emergency. An adverse explanation is deliberate construction of a paper trail to justify an action by someone without authority. Discriminating evidence would include incident-command communications, timestamps of the emergency decision, who was actually available, and whether Diego or Farid directly approved outside the log.

def second_business_day_deadline(trigger_ts):
    # Exclude trigger day. Count Monday-Friday only. Deadline is end of day UTC.
    current_day = trigger_ts.normalize()
    count = 0
    while count < 2:
        current_day += pd.Timedelta(days=1)
        if current_day.weekday() < 5:
            count += 1
    return current_day + pd.Timedelta(hours=23, minutes=59, seconds=59)

## 6. Control Improvements

Delegation creation should be centralized in an electronic controlled workflow that records principal, delegate, scope, effective interval, absence condition, subdelegation permission, evidence timestamp, and reason. A later migration-created database row should never overwrite or obscure the original creation timestamp.

Effective-time recording should be append-only and audit-preserving. The plant should store both “delegation effective from/to” and “record created/approved at” timestamps. Reports should distinguish “valid at event time” from “visible in the current master.”

Principal availability should be integrated with HR leave, sick leave, on-call rosters, VPN sessions, and controlled-action logs. If a principal acts while formally absent, the system should require explicit “principal self-action while absent” confirmation and strong authentication, not silent account use.

Alias management should enforce one canonical human identity across all QMS accounts. Known aliases such as `u_bk` and `b.koch` must be normalized before SoD checks. New aliases should trigger identity-governance review, and SoD logic should operate on the human identity, not the login string.

Individual authentication should be mandatory for GxP-relevant actions. Service accounts should not be allowed to perform approval, verification, or retrospective-review actions unless the action is bound to a named human through FIDO, badge, e-signature, or equivalent evidence. Shared workstations should auto-lock and require re-authentication at each controlled action.

Emergency review controls should require incident ID, emergency flag, safety rationale, IC or IC-delegate authority, and automatic retrospective-QM-review deadline calculation. The system should reject prohibited subdelegation chains and escalate overdue or out-of-scope retrospective reviews.

Pair rotation should be introduced for high-risk production changes. The goal is not to ban collaboration between Clara and Ben, but to prevent overreliance on one drafter–approver pair. Rotation exceptions should be documented with capacity or expertise reasons.

Network monitoring should use handover-of-work and working-together networks as triage signals, not as misconduct findings. Dashboards should control for production cell, shift, expertise, availability, risk, and change type before escalating.

Evidential retention should include badge logs, FIDO/MFA evidence, VPN sessions, endpoint login/session-lock data, IP-to-device mapping, workstation assignment, CCTV where lawful, e-signature metadata, delegation PDFs, QMS database history, review notes, and incident-command communications. Retention periods should cover the full audit and deviation-investigation window.

## 7. Executive Conclusion

The apparent nonconformance is mixed. Some cases are likely valid or mostly valid: CHG-501 is a strong valid QM delegation case; CHG-504 is role-authorized if Asha truly acted; CHG-503 has a valid Ben approval but a serious verification identity gap. Several cases show genuine authority or SoD failure: CHG-502, CHG-507, CHG-508, and CHG-510 are clear violations; CHG-505 has at least a late and unauthorized retrospective QM review; CHG-509 likely fails because Eva’s D-QM-02 principal-absence condition is not supported. CHG-506 is less a simple role failure than a high-priority identity problem because Ben’s account was used during certified sick leave and Ben denies performing the approval.

Systemically, I would characterize the plant’s risk as three overlapping problems: real authority failures in emergency and high-risk approvals, identity/data-quality weaknesses around aliases and service accounts, and organizational-pattern risk from concentrated Clara–Ben handovers and reciprocal low-risk approvals. The evidence supports urgent investigation, but not a blanket conclusion of collusion.

The three highest-priority investigations are: first, Ben-account activity during sick leave, covering CHG-506 and the CHG-505 retrospective review; second, emergency cooling-change authority, especially CHG-510’s invalid subdelegation and CHG-505’s late/out-of-scope retrospective review; third, service-account use for verification, especially CHG-503, where RR2 cannot be proven without stronger individual attribution.
