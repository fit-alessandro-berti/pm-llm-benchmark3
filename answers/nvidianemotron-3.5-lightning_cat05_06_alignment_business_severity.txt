## 1. Materiality Framework

**Severity dimensions** (used to assign a 1–5 score in Section 2):
- **Control objective**: Does the deviation strike at the core regulatory or internal control (e.g., payment before approval, missing fraud screen) or a peripheral operational detail (e.g., UI telemetry, retry artifacts)?
- **Amount/exposure**: Size of the claim and its risk level; larger amounts and HIGH risk amplify severity.
- **Reversibility**: Whether the action can be easily undone or corrected without financial/operational loss (e.g., a withdrawn claim is reversible; a paid claim before approval may not be).
- **Timing**: Deviations that breach mandatory windows (e.g., Notify Claimant within 24 hours, or bank-change look-back period) carry higher timing-based severity.
- **Customer/regulatory harm**: Potential for client harm, claimant confusion, regulatory scrutiny, or audit findings. Involvement of HIGH risk or large sums typically raises this dimension.
- **Recurrence**: Whether the pattern is isolated or systematic; recurring patterns across multiple cases increase severity because they indicate a structural issue rather than a one-off error.
- **Evidence reliability**: How confidently can we assert what actually happened? Missing-but-external evidence (e.g., external adjuster reports, verbal checks) may mitigate severity if the activity is confirmed elsewhere, but reduces confidence.
- **Model scope**: Whether the process model intended to cover the case type (payable claims only vs. inclusive of denials/withdrawals). Misapplication of a payable-claim model to denied or withdrawn claims is a scope violation that elevates severity.

**Confidence vs. severity**: Confidence reflects how well the available evidence (log records, business context, alignment notation) supports the factual interpretation of what happened. Severity reflects the business impact of the deviation if the interpretation is true. A case can have high severity but moderate confidence (e.g., CL-602: critical control failure on a 480k claim, but confidence is high because the policy overlay is explicit; likewise CL-609: high policy violation but confidence high because the alignment and policy are clear). ## 6. Why Alignment Cost Is Not Business Risk

The supplied cost function assigns a uniform technical cost of 1 for every visible log/model move (L or M) and 0 for synchronous moves or hidden transitions (T). This design was not calibrated to business risk dimensions; therefore, raw_cost can misleadingly suggest severity where none exists, or mask critical failures behind a zero cost. Five illustrative cases demonstrate this dissociation:

1. **Cost-function design ignores business conditions (CL-609, CL-610)**. Both cases have raw_cost=0 despite violating P1: CL-609 uses Auto Approve on a 18k MEDIUM-risk claim (amount >5k), and CL-610 uses Auto Approve after a beneficiary-bank change within seven days. The cost function sees only synchronous moves and assigns no penalty, but the business exposure is policy non-compliance that could enable systematic under-approval. The cost function’s uniform weighting fails to differentiate a technically "correct" flow from one that breaches contractual/regulatory thresholds.

2. **Hidden moves can technically fit yet represent control failures (CL-605)**. The alignment contains T(tau,c=0), a silent model move that costs 0 and is marked as "technically fitting." In isolation, this is innocuous because the claim (800, LOW risk, no bank change) meets all P1 conditions. However, the same notation pattern in CL-609 and CL-610 shows that zero cost can conceal data-aware policy violations when the hidden move represents an unmodeled condition (e.g., amount check) that the cost function does not evaluate. Business risk thus depends on what the silent move *means*, not on its cost.

3. **Scope and activity granularity determine risk independent of cost (CL-607, CL-612)**. CL-607 and CL-612 both carry raw_cost=4, driven by multiple M moves. Yet CL-607 models a denied claim going through Pay Claim and Notify—an absurd path that inflates cost but reflects a structural model gap, not an operational loss (no payment was made). CL-612 models a withdrawn claim similarly. The cost function counts model moves that are factually inactive for the claim outcome, yet the business severity differs: CL-607’s scope issue may mislead regulators; CL-612’s withdrawal is low-exposure. Cost alone cannot distinguish model-scope violations from actual operational deviations.

4. **Missing-but-external evidence alters risk perception without changing cost (CL-602, CL-606, CL-611)**. In CL-602, the manual approver relied on a verbal fraud check with no documented evidence; the alignment marks M(Fraud Screen,c=1) with cost 1, but the business impact is critical because a HIGH-risk, 480k claim was paid without formal fraud validation. In CL-606, the assessment occurred externally (external adjuster report), yet the core log lacks Assess Loss; the alignment cost 1, but the business risk is moderate because the activity did happen, just under a different label. In CL-611, the label mismatch (Assess Damage vs Assess Loss) costs 2, but the assessment completed successfully. In all three, the cost function treats the model-log divergence as the primary signal, whereas business risk depends on whether the underlying activity is substantiated elsewhere—cost does not capture evidential reliability.

5. **Irreversibility and timing make cost a poor proxy for exposure (CL-604)**. CL-604 has raw_cost=3, but its business narrative is a severe control failure: payment executed at 10:03, manual approval recorded at 10:20, 17 minutes late per P3. The cost function aggregates three L/M moves equally, yet the timing breach is irreversible (money already paid) and carries high regulatory risk. Conversely, CL-603’s raw_cost=1 reflects a retry artifact that is easily corrected and carries no financial exposure. Cost function design gives no weight to irreversibility, timing windows (P4’s 24-hour Notify requirement), or the compounding effect of multiple small deviations that collectively create systemic risk.

Beyond these five, the full case set reveals additional design gaps: duplicated telemetry inflates cost without business meaning (CL-601, the 74-retry pattern), data conditions absent from the process tree produce blind spots (CL-610’s bank change, CL-609’s amount/risk), and the uniform cost of 1 per move cannot represent that some moves (e.g., Pay Claim before approval) are qualitatively more dangerous than others (e.g., Open Workbench telemetry). A business-severity assessment must therefore externalize the cost function, mapping each technical move to one or more of the severity dimensions (control objective, amount/exposure, reversibility, timing, harm, recurrence, evidence reliability, model scope) and weighting them according to the organization’s risk appetite. Raw_cost remains a useful diagnostic *input* for identifying where log-model divergence exists, but it must be supplemented with contextual business evaluation to convert technical conformance into actionable risk management.
