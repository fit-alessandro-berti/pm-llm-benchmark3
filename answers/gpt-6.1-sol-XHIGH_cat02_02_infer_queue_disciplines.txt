# 2. Reconstructed dispatch decisions

## Definitions and assumptions

The decision set is resource-specific: a job must have arrived, remain unfinished, possess the required qualifications for that resource, and be released for service. A job already running elsewhere is excluded. A suspended job can be a candidate for resumption.

The tables distinguish physically waiting, skill-compatible jobs from appointment release restrictions:

- An arrival scan at time a implies a possible physical arrival between a minus two minutes and a. A question mark marks a job that might already have arrived despite a later scan. Recorded earlier arrivals are treated as waiting unless a hold is explicitly hypothesized.
- FIFO uses original queue-entry age, approximated by the recorded arrival. Retaining that age after suspension is an assumption; placing resumed jobs at the back would give different predictions.
- P selects the lowest numerical priority, with FIFO as an assumed tie-breaker. At ordinary free-resource decisions, non-preemptive and preemptive priority predict the same next job. At an interruption, their predictions differ.
- SEPT uses the supplied arrival-time estimate for unstarted jobs. For B01, remaining estimates are calculated by subtracting observed service, assuming no estimate revision. A shortest-remaining-time policy may interrupt service; ordinary non-preemptive SEPT does not.
- The appointment column tests a specified comparison policy: hold a job until its target time, then promote due appointments, with FIFO as the fallback. This is an assumption, not a universal definition of appointment scheduling. A policy that protects an exact future slot is discussed separately.
- Skill-based routing alone predicts a feasible set, not a unique next job. “Any W” means any member of the displayed waiting set, subject to appointment release. It does not mean all choices are equally desirable.

All timestamps are interpreted at the supplied minute resolution. No hidden holds, extra jobs, priorities, or resource outages are assumed as observations.

The 19-minute calculation assumes B01's resumption has no penalty and compares time to the next completion: 31 minutes for B01 against 12 plus a switching penalty for B03. It is not a measured cost or a general scheduling optimality result. Other objectives could rationally favor resumption without that literal penalty.

Preemption costs may include restoring tools, diagnostic context, setup, or coordination. Conversely, meeting-driven suspension could let D05 use the otherwise idle 24-minute interval before noon. Neither the permission to suspend D05 nor the cost of doing so is recorded. A blanket assumption of cost-free preemption would distort QD's feasibility; a blanket large-cost assumption would conceal QB's unexplained choices.

Absence of an interruption reason does not prove there was no operational reason. Nevertheless, an unrecorded reason remains a hypothesis; it is not evidence that the claimed discipline was followed.

# 6. Additional event data needed

1. **Capture the decision and its actual eligibility set.** For every dispatch, continuation, suspension, resumption, and deliberate idle decision, record decision time, commitment time, execution time, selected job, resource, all candidate jobs, and each exclusion reason. Include holds, appointment release, resource binding, qualification failure, and inability to finish before a calendar boundary. The most valuable checks are B03 at 09:31, B05 at 09:48, C06 at 10:32, C07 at 11:40, and D10 on D-F at 11:36. Logging only selected jobs cannot establish the choice set.

2. **Separate physical arrival, scan time, and queue-ready time.** Record actual receipt where observable, scanner timestamp, queue-entry timestamp, readiness/hold transitions, and the point at which dispatch can see the job. Preserve clock source, timestamp precision, and ordering sequence for events within the same minute. This resolves boundary cases such as C05 at 10:10 and D03 at 11:01 without applying a blanket two-minute correction.

3. **Record resource state and routing history.** Distinguish busy, free, reserved, setup, calibration, meeting, off-shift, and blocked states, with start/end times and planned calendars. Record qualifications, provisional and binding assignments, reassignment permission, backfilling permission, and reservation buffers. Prioritize D-X's 11:20–11:30 interval and D-F's 11:36–12:00 interval; these determine whether QD is pooled or resource-bound.

4. **Make appointment semantics explicit.** Capture appointment target, earliest and latest permissible start, any required completion window, early-service permission, lateness tolerance, reservation resource, and rules for admitting work that may overrun a future slot. Apply this to A03, A06, D06, and B07. A “late” flag relative to the target is insufficient to identify a window-based policy.

5. **Log preemption and resumption decisions as lifecycle events.** Retain job and segment identifiers, stop time, trigger job/event, operational reason, authorizing dispatcher, priority snapshot, remaining work, resume eligibility, and resumption ordering rule. Separate productive work from teardown/setup and capture interruption response latency. This would distinguish B01's urgent takeover from its later dispatcher reassignment and quantify costs rather than inferring them from suspension lengths.

6. **Preserve estimate and urgency histories.** Record the original visible estimate, every revised total and current estimated remaining time, estimator/source, revision time, current priority, priority changes, deadline, and calculated SLA risk or latest-start slack used at dispatch. For QC, determine whether C04 was already committed before C06 arrived, whether C05 triggered a policy change, and whether C01's age or C07's deadline was explicitly considered. Future estimate-outcome comparisons can assess accuracy or incentives; this sample cannot.

7. **Identify policy version and collect repeated distinguishing opportunities.** Attach the active policy, configuration, batch identifier, override reason, and threshold to each decision. Collect additional shifts where age, priority, estimate, appointment urgency, and skill compatibility disagree, while recording workload and calendars. Evaluate rule predictions only within comparable eligibility sets. Repeated FIFO-versus-priority conflicts on D-M, resumption-versus-waiting conflicts on B1, and newly arrived short jobs during QC batches are particularly informative.

Represent each job as a case with queue-entry, service-start, suspend, resume, and completion events, retaining segment and resource identifiers. Reconstruct resource occupancy and dispatch risk sets from those lifecycle events. A process-discovery model can describe the lifecycle, but control-flow discovery or completion order alone cannot identify the scheduling rule; the decisive analysis is the choice among eligible alternatives at each resource decision.

# 7. Final synthesis

QA is the most identifiable full dispatch pattern: FIFO with a supported appointment promotion and non-preemptive service explains the observed choices with few additions. QD has the clearest skill constraints and substantial evidence for local FIFO, but its idle intervals leave routing and reservation details unresolved.

QC is least identifiable. SEPT influences some decisions, yet the sequence requires unobserved commitments, policy overrides, eligibility changes, or estimate revisions. No specific SLA-switch or aging threshold can be established.

The most dangerous claim to infer is strict preemptive priority in QB. The first urgent takeover supports it superficially, but later higher-priority arrivals are bypassed, including B05, unless unrecorded eligibility restrictions apply. Treating that queue as reliably protecting urgent work would create an unsupported service guarantee.
