# 1. Stress scenarios

Acceptability should be assessed by case type and tail performance, not by the overall average:

- **Urgent:** breaches of the contractual 18-hour limit, predicted deadline slack, and p90 cycle time.
- **Routine:** percentage released within 72 hours and age of the oldest case, so priority rules cannot hide starvation.
- **Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure:** p90, oldest-case age, and resource-specific WIP. No external target is given, so an internal tail limit should be agreed rather than mixing them into the overall median.
- **All types:** first-pass yield, queue exposure over 12 hours, cancellations, and recovery time after disruption.

Because the log is flattened by order, capacity planning should use expected assay runs and validation tasks, not order counts alone.

| Scenario | Demand and disruption definition | Main stress signature |
|---|---|---|
| **S1. Medical, specialist, and multi-assay workload therefore rises much faster than order count. |

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

## S1. Winter mix shift

The likely sequence is:

**Courier/intake  pre-analytical check and correction  preparation/assay  technical validation  medical validation  specialist review**

Evidence for this sequence includes:

- Courier intake reaches 88% utilization and a 7.5-hour median peak queue.
- Pre-analytical check reaches 96% utilization and a 9.5-hour queue; correction specialists reach a 14-hour queue.
- Preparation reaches an 11-hour queue, assay machines an 18-hour queue, and technical validation a 16-hour queue at 101% utilization.
- Medical validation reaches a 22-hour queue, while specialist review reaches 36 hours.
- The performance DFG shows 24 hours from Technical validation to Medical validation and 48 hours from Hold for specialist review to Medical validation.

The mix explains why the downstream validation load grows disproportionately. The Medical validation  Release edge occurs about 1,330 times/week at peak versus 521/week normally. Specialist holds rise from roughly 36/week to 203/week. The share of technical-validation branches entering specialist hold also rises from about 1.8% to 7.5%.

Bottleneck migration risks are substantial:

- **Automating registration, I8**, may reduce the 2.8-hour registration queue, but the released work immediately reaches a pre-analytical queue of 9.5 hours and then an assay queue of 18 hours.
- **Strict Urgent-first dispatch, I1**, may improve some Urgent cases but fragments assay schedules. Routine cases then age beyond 72 hours, while non-Urgent Complex panels can also be starved despite requiring scarce downstream validators.
- **Relieving preparation or assay** sends more work to technical validation, already at 101%, and then to medical validation because Urgent and Complex-panel cases require it.
- **Adding an evening medical shift, I5**, can move the bottleneck to specialist review. If the specialist backlog is also cleared, the next surge reaches medical validation and eventually release.
- **Larger fixed Routine batches, I2**, improve setup efficiency but are unsafe when late Urgent arrivals need the same machine. Batch-wait branching rises from about 4.4% normally to 12.7% at peak, and Batch wait  Assay run increases from 9 to 22 hours.

## S2. Assay degradation or outage

The likely sequence is:

**Assay machine  upstream preparation buffer  repeats  technical validation surge after recovery  medical validation**

During the outage, preparation benches continue producing ready samples until storage or WIP limits are reached. Technical and medical queues may temporarily fall, which can falsely suggest spare downstream capacity. Once the machine returns, the assay backlog is released in a burst.

M2 degradation is particularly dangerous because Repeat-after-failure cases already cluster after M2 use and night shifts. The Assay run  Repeat assay branch rises from approximately 6.7% of assay outcomes normally to 14.8% at peak, while Repeat assay  Assay run takes 26 hours at peak. These repeats consume effective machine capacity and later create additional validation work.

Migration effects include:

- **Permanent M1 dedication under I3** protects some Urgent and Complex-panel cases, but sends nearly all Routine load to the higher-failure M2. More Routine repeats can consume the capacity supposedly saved for priority cases.
- **Urgent-first dispatch during an outage** can reduce machine throughput if frequent setup changes are needed. Routine cases accumulate upstream, and older Complex panels may miss specialist windows.
- **Repairing the machine or rapidly clearing its queue** moves the bottleneck to technical validation at 101% peak utilization. Because many cleared cases are Urgent or Complex, the subsequent bottleneck is medical validation rather than release.
- **Routing all repeat assays to M1** can improve first-pass recovery but makes M1 the new bottleneck unless repeat admission is capped.

## S3. Medical-validator or specialist-review absence

Two related paths fail differently:

- Missing medical validator:  
  **Technical validation  Medical-validation queue**
- Missing specialist:  
  **Technical validation  Hold for specialist review  Medical-validation queue**

Complex panels are 3.1 times more likely to require medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter specialist review. An absence therefore affects workload selectively rather than uniformly.

Migration effects include:

- If an evening medical shift is added while the specialist is absent, it does little for cases already in Hold for specialist review.
- If specialist capacity is restored first, accumulated holds are released into medical validation, which was already at 93% peak utilization.
- Prioritizing all Urgent cases at medical validation may protect the 18-hour contract but can leave Complex panels waiting for days. Conversely, prioritizing all Complex panels because they need specialist coordination would create Urgent penalties.
- Selective outsourcing can relieve assay and specialist workload, but the 12-hour transport delay can make an early in-house case slower if it was not genuinely at risk. It also creates receiving, reconciliation, and traceability work on return.
- Holding Routine releases under I9 does not materially solve this scenario. Medical validation  Release is only 5 hours at peak, whereas the major waits are before medical and specialist validation. I9 would mainly add Routine delay.

## S4. Courier compression or delay

The likely sequence is:

**Courier intake  registration exceptions  pre-analytical check  accession correction/recollection  preparation and assay  late validation surge**

A delayed route may initially make the laboratory look underloaded. When the route arrives, courier intake at 88% and pre-analysis at 96% cannot absorb the burst. The Register order  Receive sample edge is already 8 hours at peak, and Receive sample  Pre-analytical check rises to 9 hours.

Migration effects include:

- **I7 courier smoothing** reduces the intake burst, but if all rescheduled routes are shifted toward an inadequately staffed evening period, the bottleneck merely changes time of day.
- **I8 automation** can clear registration faster, exposing pre-analysis and assay sooner.
- A large group of late Urgent orders may all appear equally high priority. A strict Urgent-first rule then fragments assay schedules and starves Routine work.
- Once intake is relieved, a smooth but high-rate stream can keep assay and technical validation continuously loaded. Smoothing eliminates bursts but does not create total capacity.
- Recollection cases returning in the same courier windows further amplify the intake wave.

## S5. Collection-quality shock

The likely sequence is:

**Early defect triage/pre-analysis  accession correction and recollection  courier/intake return wave  preparation/assay for accepted cases**

At peak, Request recollection plus Manual accession correction represents about 12.3% of initially received orders, versus 7.0% normally. The correction process is also less successful: Manual accession correction  Request recollection rises from 14% normally to 26% at peak. Recollection  Receive sample takes 60 hours at peak and generates substantial cancellation.

Migration effects include:

- **I6 early rejection** prevents poor samples from occupying preparation and assay capacity, but initially increases visible recollection work and physician communication.
- Successful recollections return later and can create a second courier and pre-analytical peak.
- **I4 moving technical validators to accession correction** may clear the 14-hour correction queue, but technical validation is already at 101% utilization with a 16-hour queue. The bottleneck can therefore move directly from correction to technical validation.
- If collection quality improves, more cases pass first time and reach preparation sooner. This is beneficial overall, but assay and validation must be ready for the higher first-pass flow.

# 3. Robust redesign package

## A. Schedule by expected work and deadline slack, not by order count

At registration, assign each order a resource vector:

- expected number and type of assay runs;
- machine compatibility and failure risk;
- need for technical, medical, and specialist validation;
- contractual deadline or agreed internal due date;
- sample-stability deadline.

This is important because a Complex-panel order may represent several assays, while the flattened log counts it once.

Use finite-capacity release between pre-analysis, preparation, and assay:

1. Perform intake and defect triage quickly.
2. Hold accepted samples in a visible, controlled “ready” buffer when downstream capacity is unavailable.
3. Release work based on predicted remaining-path time and available capacity at assay, technical, medical, and specialist validation.

Dispatch should be **deadline-aware with aging protection**, not strict Urgent-first:

- An Urgent case with insufficient remaining slack receives the next compatible rescue slot.
- Complex panels receive coordinated assay and validation reservations rather than assay priority alone.
- Routine cases gain priority as their remaining 72-hour slack falls, preventing indefinite leapfrogging.
- Repeat-after-failure cases receive a recovery route based on sample stability and root cause, not an unconditional highest priority.

Routine batches can vary between 12 and 24. A batch of 24 is used only if the oldest included sample still has enough predicted residual slack and the batch will not consume reserved Urgent capacity.

**Why robust:** it addresses mix shifts, outages, and absences without pretending that every class can be prioritized simultaneously.

**Dependency and failure mode:** it requires credible remaining-time estimates and stable samples in the controlled buffer. A hard WIP cap without deadline overrides could itself cause Urgent or Routine breaches.

**Migration control:** assay work is not released solely because preparation is free; technical and medical workload are included in the admission decision.

## B. Combine I6, I7, and a gated form of I8 at the front end

Implement:

- **I6:** a risk-based sample-integrity and metadata screen within two hours of receipt;
- **I7:** rescheduled courier slots based on staffed two-hour intake capacity, with a separate express path for genuinely Urgent samples;
- **I8:** straight-through registration only for orders passing complete digital-metadata rules. Exceptions remain in a staffed manual queue.

Use clerical time freed by I8 for defect notification and collection-site feedback rather than longer shifts; registration overtime beyond nine hours risks creating more errors.

Early rejection should be accompanied by standardized electronic recollection notices and collection-site defect reports. Otherwise the laboratory merely detects defects sooner without reducing their frequency.

**Why robust:** this reduces burstiness and prevents known-bad samples from consuming scarce assay and validation capacity.

**Dependency and failure mode:** courier partners must comply with slots, and automation rules must have high precision. Systematic metadata errors could pass faster and affect many cases.

**Migration control:** automation is coupled to downstream WIP limits. Faster registration must not become uncontrolled release into pre-analysis and assay. Returned recollections are forecast as future intake, not treated as unexpected new work.

## C. Use reliability-aware machine routing rather than permanent dedication

Do not implement I3 as a permanent rule. Instead:

- Reserve forecast-based M1 windows for cases with short deadline slack, high failure consequence, or limited specimen stability.
- Allow Routine work on M1 when reserved capacity is not needed.
- Cap M2 loading when its repeat rate rises, especially after night shifts.
- Route a repeat to the more reliable machine only when the failure is plausibly machine-related.
- Trigger maintenance or reduced M2 operation from repeat and quality-control signals, even though M2 has a faster nominal runtime.

**Why robust:** reducing repeat demand creates effective capacity in demand peaks and machine-degradation scenarios.

**Dependency and failure mode:** machine-specific repeat data must be reliable. Overprotection of M1 can strand capacity or make M1 the bottleneck.

**Migration control:** successful first-pass assays reach technical validation earlier, so machine schedules must be constrained by predicted technical and medical capacity.

## D. Build validation flexibility, but activate it selectively

Prepare the capabilities before winter:

- Cross-train the two technical validators for accession correction as proposed in I4, but do not permanently reassign them.
- Pre-authorize I5 evening medical-validation shifts and align them with forecast assay completions.
- Publish specialist availability and reserve review windows for expected Complex panels.
- Prequalify the partner laboratory, including chain-of-custody, result reconciliation, sample stability, and maximum accepted volume.

I4 should activate only when accession correction is materially worse than technical validation. I5 should activate when medical work is the constraint, not when the true constraint is unavailable specialist review.

**Why robust:** this provides options for several disruptions without paying for blanket capacity everywhere.

**Dependency and failure mode:** cross-training requires the stated four-week lead time, medical shifts require credentialed staff, and the partner may be affected by the same regional infection wave.

**Migration control:** moving validators to correction is prohibited when the technical queue is unsafe. Specialist backlog must be considered before adding medical capacity, and release workload must be watched when a validation backlog is cleared.

## E. Use process mining and scenario replay as part of the operating design

The event log should be enriched with:

- tube and assay identifiers linked to the order;
- machine, batch, shift, and operator;
- queue-entry, service-start, and completion timestamps;
- defect reason, repeat reason, and recollection source;
- case deadline and outsourcing handoffs.

Mine variants separately by case type, machine, night/day shift, courier route, and specialist calendar. In particular, verify the M2/night-repeat association rather than treating correlation as proven causation.

Before changing dispatch rules, replay the observed normal and peak arrival traces in a discrete-event model, then inject machine outages, absences, and courier compression. Compare class-specific SLA breaches, p90s, maximum WIP, repeats, and recovery time—not only mean cycle time.

## F. Do not deploy the high-risk proposals unchanged

- **I1:** replace strict Urgent-first with bounded deadline rescue and aging.
- **I2:** use dynamic, slack-tested batches rather than a permanent size of 24.
- **I3:** use protected M1 windows rather than permanent machine dedication.
- **I4:** cross-train, but reassign only under dual-queue guardrails.
- **I9:** do not hold clinical Routine results merely to form a report batch; release is not the current primary bottleneck.
- **I10:** prequalify it as a conditional overflow path, not a default Complex-panel route.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

Thresholds below are initial operating values and should be recalibrated from several weeks of enriched event data. On/off thresholds deliberately use hysteresis to avoid switching policies every hour.

| Scenario mode | Switch on | Activated policy | Switch off or rollback |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Winter mix mode** | Seven-day forecast has Urgent + Complex-panel above 40%—normal is 31%, observed peak is 49%—or forecast assay/medical workload exceeds 90% of available capacity. | Start forecast-aligned I5 evening medical shifts; use coordinated Complex-panel assay/review reservations; reserve assay slots for Urgent deadline risk; use Routine aging protection. | Mix below 35%, forecast load below 80%, and medical and technical predicted waits below 8 hours for three consecutive days. |
| **Urgent rescue mode** | An Urgent case has less than four hours of positive predicted slack, or predicted remaining p90 exceeds time to the 18-hour deadline. | Give the case the next compatible preparation, assay, and medical slot. Do not globally reorder every queue; preserve an aging floor for Routine and Complex cases. | No Urgent case has less than four hours’ slack for two review cycles and the predicted breach rate is below the agreed warning level. |
| **Machine degradation/outage mode** | A machine loses more than 15% of scheduled effective capacity for over one hour, fails quality control, or repeat outcomes exceed 10% over a rolling 100 assays for that machine/shift. | Stop or reduce the implicated machine; preserve M1 windows for deadline-risk and sample-stability cases; gate Routine release to preparation; route eligible repeats based on failure cause; use smaller mixed batches if setup fragmentation is less costly than deadline breaches. | Machine passes quality control for two shifts, repeat rate is below 8%, and assay predicted wait is below 8 hours. |
| **Medical/specialist absence mode** | Known absence, forecast medical workload above 90%, medical wait above 12 hours, or specialist predicted wait above 24 hours. | If medical capacity is short, activate I5. If specialist capacity is short, reserve available review windows and use credentialed remote/partner review. Outsource selected Complex panels only when predicted in-house completion exceeds partner completion—12-hour transport plus partner queue and handoff buffer—by at least six hours. | Coverage restored and medical wait below 8 hours and specialist wait below 12 hours for two shifts. Stop new outsourcing before draining already accepted partner cases. |
| **Courier compression mode** | Projected arrivals in any two-hour window exceed 120% of staffed intake capacity, or a route ETA slips by more than two hours. | Invoke I7 reslotting; stagger existing intake coverage; separate Urgent arrivals; enable clean-order automation; forecast the downstream assay and validation surge rather than releasing it blindly. | Projected arrivals are below 90% of two-hour capacity for two consecutive windows and route delays are under one hour. |
| **Defect-shock mode** | Initial recollection plus manual-correction rate exceeds 10% over 200 receipts, correction-to-recollection exceeds 20%, or accession predicted wait exceeds 8 hours. | Activate two-hour I6 triage, collection-site feedback, and standardized notification. Deploy I4 only if accession wait exceeds 8 hours while technical wait is below 6 hours and technical forecast load is below 85%. | Initial defects below 8%, correction-to-recollection below 15%, and accession wait below 5 hours for three days. Immediately withdraw I4 if technical wait exceeds 8 hours or forecast load exceeds 90%. |
| **Large Routine-batch mode** | At least 24 compatible Routine samples are ready, assay forecast load is below 85%, reserved Urgent capacity remains intact, and every included case has sufficient 72-hour slack using predicted residual p90 plus a safety buffer. | Run a batch of 24. Continue to expose the oldest Routine age to the dispatcher. | Revert to 12 or smaller when an Urgent case enters rescue mode, assay load exceeds 90%, or the oldest selected Routine case loses its safety margin. |

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **Correlated disruptions remain dangerous.** A winter mix shift combined with an assay outage and specialist absence can exceed the flexibility of the redesign. It is not reasonable to promise normal SLAs during an unlimited-duration outage.
- **Recollection is largely external to the laboratory.** The 60-hour peak recollection loop can make an Urgent breach unavoidable even if the laboratory rejects the original sample within two hours.
- **Specialist review remains a concentrated skill.** Calendar coordination reduces surprise but does not eliminate the single-point risk of the every-second-Friday absence.
- **The current order-level log understates Complex-panel work.** Until tube- and assay-level events are captured, work-content forecasts and WIP limits may be wrong.
- **Partner capacity may be correlated with local demand.** The partner laboratory may face the same regional infection wave, while transport adds 12 hours and creates traceability risk.
- **M2 reliability remains a structural concern.** Reducing M2 use during a failure signal lowers nominal capacity and can make M1 the immediate bottleneck.
- **A vulnerability created by the redesign is controlled-buffer delay.** WIP gating can move waiting from a visible station queue into a post-check buffer and can disproportionately delay Routine cases unless that buffer is timestamped and aging is enforced.
- **Another redesign-induced risk is a recollection return wave.** I6 detects defects sooner and protects assay capacity, but successful recollections may return together and overload courier intake and pre-analysis.
- **Automation can scale errors.** A bad metadata rule under I8 can send systematically incorrect orders downstream faster than manual registration would.
- **Targeted evening validation can move workload into release and reconciliation.** I5 should not be judged successful solely by a falling medical queue.
- **Long overtime is not a safe fallback.** Registration error rates rise after nine-hour shifts, potentially creating the correction and recollection load the process is trying to avoid.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

## Leading indicators

These should update intraday during peaks, even if reviewed formally each morning.

| Leading indicator | Definition and warning bands | Why it leads |
|---|---|---|
| **Next-24/48-hour workload-to-capacity ratio by resource** | Calculate expected assay runs, technical tasks, medical tasks, and specialist minutes—not just orders. Green below 80%; amber 80–90%; red above 90%. For specialist review, treat uncovered capacity or above 85% as red because work is lumpy. | Predicts which bottleneck will receive current WIP before queues fully form. |
| **Case mix and route workload** | Urgent + Complex-panel: green below 35%, amber 35–40%, red above 40%, but only in combination with workload-to-capacity. Also show expected assays per order. | The observed peak reached 49%, and these types disproportionately consume medical and specialist resources. |
| **Predicted wait and oldest age by station and case type** | Pre-analysis/preparation: amber 6–10 hours, red above 10. Accession, assay, technical, and medical: amber 8–12, red above 12. Specialist: amber 12–24, red above 24. Always show the oldest case, not only the median. | Warns of migration and starvation before completed-case cycle times deteriorate. |
| **Deadline slack** | `due time  current time  predicted remaining p90`. Urgent amber at 0–4 hours and red below zero. Routine amber below 12 hours and red below zero. Use an agreed due or sample-stability limit for Complex panels and repeats. | Directly supports bounded rescue rather than blanket priority. |
| **Inbound concentration and courier ETA** | Highest projected two-hour arrivals divided by staffed intake capacity: green below 90%, amber 90–120%, red above 120%. Red also for route delay above two hours. | Detects a delayed avalanche while the laboratory may still look idle. |
| **Pre-analytical first-pass quality** | `(Request recollection + Manual accession correction) / receipts`, rolling 200: green below 8%, amber 8–10%, red above 10%. Correction-to-recollection: green below 15%, amber 15–20%, red above 20%. | Predicts future recollection, clerk, intake, and cancellation workload. |
| **Assay repeat and machine-health signal** | Assay repeat outcomes by machine, shift, and type: green below 8%, amber 8–10%, red above 10%. Immediate red for failed QC or outage. Compare M2 night performance with M1 and daytime rather than using only the aggregate. | A repeat is both a quality event and new future assay demand. |
| **Validation coverage calendar** | Available medical and specialist hours versus forecast work for the next two days; flag every known absence and uncovered review window. | Prevents assays from being started without a feasible downstream validation path. |

## Lagging outcome metrics

These remain necessary, but should not be used alone to dispatch current work:

- Number and percentage of **Urgent cases released over 18 hours**, plus Urgent median and p90.
- Percentage of **Routine cases released within 72 hours**, Routine p90, and maximum age.
- Median, p90, and oldest age for **Complex-panel** and **Repeat-after-failure** cases.
- Percentage of cases experiencing **any queue over 12 hours**, by case type and station.
- Overall rework, assay-repeat rate, recollection completion time, and cancellation rate.
- Outsourcing transport exceptions, traceability failures, and result-reconciliation time.
- Time required for each station’s WIP and SLA breach rate to return to the green band after a disruption.
