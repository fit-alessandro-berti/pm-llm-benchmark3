# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed profile is best understood as a **changing mixture of mechanisms**, not as one monotonic human-performance curve.

During the first five events, resources face complex carryover work: mean complexity is 0.72, 41% of events are high priority, and the backlog includes work inherited from the prior shift. Senior resources preferentially take these cases. Longer service time at this point is therefore partly appropriate effort on difficult work rather than evidence that workers are initially inefficient. The 3.4% error rate is also far below the 7.9% observed at positions 76+, despite the early cases being harder.

Between approximately positions 6 and 50, three favorable changes occur together:

1. Resources become familiar with the current queue, systems, and recurring case types.
2. The backlog and queue decline.
3. The work mix becomes easier and less urgent.

A genuine warm-up effect is plausible in this interval. Its strongest supporting evidence is that service time falls while error does not rise. However, the raw reduction from 18.8 to 12.9 minutes cannot be assigned entirely to learning because complexity, priority, queue pressure, resource assignment, and probably activity mix are all changing.

After position 50, the interpretation changes. Mean complexity continues to fall, but queue length rises from 31 to 62 and then 88. Service time falls further, to 10.8 minutes, while errors and rework increase sharply. This is the signature of a **speed-quality tradeoff under pressure**, potentially amplified by batching and fatigue.

The WIP-stratified results make this distinction clearer. Within both complexity bands, high WIP is associated with roughly a 25% reduction in service time but more than a doubling of error rate. Therefore, late speed is not simply the continuation of beneficial learning. It appears that workers respond to congestion by processing cases more quickly, reducing checking, deferring some work, or using batch routines. The effect is especially severe for juniors, whose later error rate is 4.4% under low WIP but 12.4% under high WIP.

Fatigue may still contribute. Attention loss late in a shift can interact with pressure: a workload that is manageable in hour three may produce errors in hour seven. But the available data do not support fatigue as a universal main effect. Later low-WIP quality is stable for most experience groups, and Early-shift workers end with lower errors than Day-shift workers even though both are late in their shifts. The difference in queue trajectories—Early clears backlog while Day accumulates it—is more consistent with pressure-dependent fatigue or pressure-induced shortcuts than with elapsed time alone.

Circadian effects are even less identified. Night events are slower, but the night resources and night case mix are distinctive. Night staff handle fewer but more urgent and complex exceptions. Without workers rotating across shifts or comparisons at overlapping clock hours, slower night performance cannot be attributed confidently to circadian physiology.

Batching adds two distinct possibilities:

* **Real operational compression:** repeated Document Reviews are completed rapidly with reduced checking, potentially creating downstream rework.
* **Measurement compression:** work is performed earlier, but start or completion events are recorded together. In this case, apparent service-time reductions are partly logging artifacts.

The end-of-shift Quality Check burst also complicates error attribution. An error detected at 16:45 may have originated in a Document Review hours earlier. A dashboard that assigns errors to the detection time or to the Quality Check worker will incorrectly manufacture a late-shift “error creator” effect.

Automation further redistributes risk. Bot_Risk completes classifications instantly, but corrections rise when queues exceed 70. This may reflect harder cases, incomplete upstream documentation, rushed human verification, or automation bias. It does not prove that the bot mechanically deteriorates under load, and human correction should usually be treated as successful interception rather than worker failure.

Finally, sequence position is itself a selected variable. A worker reaches position 76 only by completing many preceding events. Fast resources, short activities, easy cases, and batched work are therefore overrepresented in the `76+` bin. Service time partly determines sequence position, so interpreting sequence position as an external cause of service time risks reverse causality. Elapsed minutes, cumulative workload, WIP, activity, and within-resource comparisons should carry more causal weight.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## Analysis design principles

Service time should first be analyzed separately by activity or activity family. A ten-minute Initial Triage and a ten-minute Quality Check do not represent the same workload or error opportunity. Bot_Risk should be excluded from human service-time models and analyzed in a separate automation panel.

The analysis should also distinguish three timestamps or roles:

* when an error was **introduced**;
* when it was **detected**;
* when it was **corrected**.

Without this lineage, Quality Check and Rework Correction events can be blamed for errors they actually discovered or repaired.

For seven-day rework, only events with a complete seven-day observation window should enter the denominator. Events near the extraction date are right-censored and will otherwise make recent or late-shift cohorts look artificially better.

## Within-resource comparisons

Use resource fixed effects or resource-specific intercepts so that stable differences in skill, role, and work style are not mistaken for shift-position effects. The primary comparisons should be within the same:

* resource;
* activity or narrow activity family;
* shift type;
* complexity and priority range;
* WIP range.

Resource-by-activity effects are preferable to a resource effect alone because workers may be fast at one activity and slow at another. Standard errors should account for repeated events within both cases and resource-shift instances.

A useful matched analysis would pair an early and later event from the same resource and activity with similar complexity, priority, and WIP. The resulting estimate is less model-dependent than comparing raw group means.

## Within-shift position effects

Model `sequence_position_within_shift` and `minutes_since_shift_start` separately. They represent different possible exposures:

* sequence position approximates accumulated task repetitions;
* elapsed minutes approximates time on duty;
* cumulative weighted workload approximates actual mental and operational load.

Position should be modeled flexibly and interpreted cautiously because it is partly an outcome of prior speed. A worker who handles long complex cases may never reach position 76. Sensitivity analyses should therefore use:

* elapsed-time bins;
* percentage of scheduled shift completed;
* cumulative complexity-weighted event count;
* the first 20 events versus comparable later events;
* the final two scheduled hours, regardless of event count.

A warm-up hypothesis predicts a decline early and then a plateau under low WIP. A fatigue hypothesis predicts a late quality deterioration after controlling for WIP and mix. A pressure interaction predicts little late deterioration under low WIP but a strong deterioration under high WIP.

## WIP and queue stratification

Model WIP and queue length continuously with splines, while retaining the `<30`, `30–70`, and `>70` bands for communication. A hard threshold at 70 should not be assumed unless operations actually change at that threshold.

Important terms include:

* WIP × complexity;
* WIP × experience;
* WIP × elapsed minutes;
* WIP × activity family;
* WIP × batch membership.

Queue length and WIP may be strongly correlated. Models should examine them separately and together, report collinearity, and consider additional pressure measures such as queue age, recent arrival rate, queue growth over the previous 30 minutes, and duration continuously above 70.

Because WIP is endogenous, stronger evidence should come from changes that are plausibly external to the focal worker: arrival surges, absenteeism, system outages, or randomized additional staffing.

## Complexity and selection adjustment

The supplied complexity score should be combined with variables available at event start:

* priority;
* activity;
* case age;
* number of previous events;
* previous callbacks or document cycles;
* prior handovers;
* whether the case is carried over from another shift;
* known exception or referral status.

Complexity adjustment should use flexible functions rather than assuming that a change from 0.4 to 0.5 has the same effect as a change from 0.8 to 0.9.

To assess selection, estimate the probability that a case is assigned to a resource group, shift period, or senior reviewer. Then report outcomes standardized to a common case mix. Strict propensity weighting should be restricted to regions with assignment overlap; a junior should not be statistically compared with a senior on cases the junior was never eligible to handle.

## Shift and time-of-day controls

Use clock time and elapsed shift time in separate models and exploit overlapping shifts:

* From 09:00–14:00, Early workers are later in their shift while Day workers are earlier.
* From 14:00–17:00, Day workers are later while Late workers are earlier.

Comparisons within these windows help distinguish time-on-shift from clock time. A cyclic spline can represent time-of-day effects, while date or day-of-week effects account for demand patterns.

Night_L and Night_M create a serious identification problem if they never work other shifts. In that case, “night effect,” “Night_L/Night_M effect,” and night-case-mix effects are not separately estimable. The result should be reported as a combined night-system effect rather than a circadian estimate.

## Resource-experience interactions

Treat `resource_experience_months` continuously where possible, with nonlinear effects and interactions. The central hypothesis is not merely that juniors have higher errors, but that **pressure sensitivity differs by experience**.

Estimate adjusted error and service-time curves at representative experience levels under low, medium, and high WIP. Resource-specific random slopes for WIP can identify whether only some individuals are pressure-sensitive, although shrinkage and minimum sample sizes are essential.

Long-term learning should be tested using calendar time or cumulative completed events across shifts. A within-shift reset is evidence of warm-up, not necessarily durable learning.

## Batching detection

Use both PM4Py batch discovery and explicit run features:

* consecutive events with the same resource and activity;
* gap between the previous completion and next start;
* run size and event position within the run;
* fraction of a resource’s shift contained in long runs;
* end-of-shift concentration;
* downstream error and rework after the run.

For Document Review, compare non-batched events with the first, middle, and last events in runs of 9–18. A decline in recorded duration only in the middle of runs suggests batching or timestamp compression. A rise in downstream rework provides stronger evidence of harmful shortcutting.

For Quality Check, measure the age of work awaiting review and the share completed in the final 45 minutes. A burst of error flags may indicate delayed detection rather than late creation.

## Rework and downstream process analysis

Use two complementary outcomes:

1. `error_flag`, with explicit detection and attribution semantics;
2. occurrence of Rework Correction within seven days of the focal event.

The seven-day outcome should be associated with the upstream event or decision that plausibly created the defect. Multiple upstream events within the same case require either attribution rules or a multistate model rather than assigning the same rework indiscriminately to every event.

Performance DFGs should be compared across prespecified cohorts, such as low versus high WIP at Initial Triage or the first Risk Classification. Retain complete case traces after selecting the cohort. Filtering individual high-WIP events out of traces can create artificial directly-follows relations.

Useful path analyses include:

* `Risk Classification → Specialist Referral`;
* `Risk Classification → Quality Check → Rework Correction`;
* `Document Review → Customer Callback`;
* `Eligibility Decision → Quality Check`;
* `Quality Check → Rework Correction`;
* cross-shift handovers before correction.

## Statistical model specification

A suitable service-time model is:

[
\begin{aligned}
\log(\text{service minutes}_{e}) ={}&
f_1(\text{sequence position}) +
f_2(\text{minutes since shift start}) +
f_3(\text{clock time}) \
&+ f_4(\text{WIP}) +
f_5(\text{queue length}) +
f_6(\text{complexity}) \
&+ \text{activity} + \text{priority} + \text{shift type}

* \text{batch flag} \
  &+ \text{WIP}\times\text{experience}
* \text{WIP}\times\text{complexity}
* \text{WIP}\times\text{elapsed time} \
  &+ \text{resource/activity effects}
* \text{date or shift-instance effects}.
  \end{aligned}
  ]

Use a binomial mixed model or GEE with the same structure for `error_flag`. For seven-day rework, use a mature-cohort logistic model or a survival model with censoring.

Report standardized predictions such as:

* predicted error over elapsed shift time at WIP 20, 50, and 90;
* predicted service time over WIP at fixed complexity;
* adjusted early-versus-late contrast within each experience group;
* adjusted batch-versus-nonbatch contrast.

The two-dimensional WIP-by-elapsed-time surface is more informative than either marginal curve.

## PM4Py-oriented implementation skeleton

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
END = "time:timestamp"
START = "start_timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"

events = pd.read_parquet("claims_event_log.parquet").copy()

for col in [END, START, "shift_start_time"]:
    events[col] = pd.to_datetime(events[col], utc=True, errors="coerce")

events = events.dropna(subset=[CASE, ACTIVITY, END, START, RESOURCE])

events = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=END,
    start_timestamp_key=START,
)

events["service_minutes"] = (
    events[END] - events[START]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

# Remove impossible durations from inferential models, but retain them for
# a separate data-quality report.
events["invalid_duration"] = (
    (events["service_minutes"] < 0) |
    events["service_minutes"].isna()
)

events["is_human"] = events[RESOURCE].ne("Bot_Risk")
human = events.loc[events["is_human"] & ~events["invalid_duration"]].copy()

# Derive a shift type if shift_id represents a unique shift occurrence.
start_hour = human["shift_start_time"].dt.hour
human["shift_type"] = start_hour.map({
    6: "Early",
    9: "Day",
    14: "Late",
    22: "Night",
}).fillna("Other")

human["clock_minute"] = (
    human[START].dt.hour * 60 + human[START].dt.minute
)

human["position_bin"] = pd.cut(
    human["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, np.inf],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"],
)

human["wip_band"] = pd.cut(
    human["wip_at_start"],
    bins=[-np.inf, 30, 70, np.inf],
    labels=["low", "medium", "high"],
    right=False,
)

human["last_two_hours"] = human["minutes_since_shift_start"].between(
    360, 480, inclusive="left"
)

# PM4Py case-level performance and temporal enrichment.
case_enriched = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(events.copy())
case_enriched = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(case_enriched)
temporal_features = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(case_enriched)

# Activity-level service and rework summaries.
service_by_activity = pm4py.get_service_time(human)
rework_cases_by_activity = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(events)

# Clock-time and resource/activity diagnostics.
pm4py.save_vis_events_distribution_graph(
    human,
    "events_by_clock_hour.png",
    distr_type="hours",
)

pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(
    human,
    "resource_activity_dotted_chart.png",
    attributes=[ACTIVITY, RESOURCE, "shift_type"],
)
```

Batch-oriented diagnostics can combine PM4Py with explicit runs:

```python
# PM4Py filter syntax may vary slightly by installed version.
document_reviews = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    human,
    ACTIVITY,
    ["Document Review"],
    level="event",
    retain=True,
)

candidate_batches = pm4py.discover_batches(document_reviews)

runs = human.sort_values(
    ["shift_id", RESOURCE, START, END]
).copy()

previous_end = runs.groupby(["shift_id", RESOURCE])[END].shift()
gap_minutes = (runs[START] - previous_end).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

same_resource = runs[RESOURCE].eq(
    runs.groupby("shift_id")[RESOURCE].shift()
)
same_activity = runs[ACTIVITY].eq(
    runs.groupby("shift_id")[ACTIVITY].shift()
)

new_run = (
    ~same_resource |
    ~same_activity |
    gap_minutes.gt(5) |
    gap_minutes.isna()
)

runs["run_id"] = new_run.groupby(runs["shift_id"]).cumsum()
run_keys = ["shift_id", RESOURCE, "run_id"]

runs["run_size"] = runs.groupby(run_keys)[ACTIVITY].transform("size")
runs["run_position"] = runs.groupby(run_keys).cumcount() + 1
runs["batch_candidate"] = runs["run_size"].ge(5)

batch_quality = (
    runs.groupby(
        [ACTIVITY, "batch_candidate", "wip_band", "shift_type"],
        observed=True,
    )
    .agg(
        events=(CASE, "size"),
        mean_service=("service_minutes", "mean"),
        error_rate=("error_flag", "mean"),
        rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean"),
        mean_complexity=("case_complexity_score", "mean"),
    )
    .reset_index()
)
```

For performance DFG comparisons, define the cohort using an anchor event and then retain complete traces:

```python
# Example: classify each case by WIP at its first Initial Triage.
anchor = (
    human.loc[human[ACTIVITY].eq("Initial Triage")]
    .sort_values([CASE, END])
    .drop_duplicates(CASE)
    [[CASE, "wip_band"]]
    .rename(columns={"wip_band": "triage_wip_band"})
)

full_with_cohort = events.merge(anchor, on=CASE, how="inner")

low_wip_cases = full_with_cohort.loc[
    full_with_cohort["triage_wip_band"].eq("low")
]
high_wip_cases = full_with_cohort.loc[
    full_with_cohort["triage_wip_band"].eq("high")
]

dfg_low, starts_low, ends_low = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(
    low_wip_cases
)
dfg_high, starts_high, ends_high = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(
    high_wip_cases
)

# Retrospective diagnostic tools:
variant_clusters = pm4py.cluster_log(events)

long_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
    events,
    min_performance=3 * 24 * 60 * 60,   # seconds
    max_performance=np.inf,
)

slow_qc_to_rework = pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
    events,
    ("Quality Check", "Rework Correction"),
    min_performance=0,
    max_performance=7 * 24 * 60 * 60,
)
```

`cluster_log` should be used for retrospective case-mix exploration, not to construct predictive features from future events. Clusters based on complete traces can leak downstream outcomes into an upstream performance model.

It is not an acceptable organization-wide policy merely to simplify analysis.                                                                                | Assignment balance by complexity, priority, activity, and experience; overrides and override reasons; waiting time; error and rework; continuity; specialist-referral rate; and adverse outcomes. Prefer stratified randomization among qualified resources in a limited trial.                  |
| **5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours**    | Provides a direct test and possible mitigation of fatigue or attention depletion. It may particularly benefit juniors and staff exposed to sustained high WIP.                                                          | Breaks reduce immediate capacity and may worsen queues unless staggered. A rigid break can occur at an operationally unsafe moment. Improved post-break quality may be offset by larger queue pressure on colleagues.                                                                                                        | Break timing and duration, adjusted pre/post-break service and error, queue/WIP during breaks, work transferred to colleagues, post-break batch behavior, seven-day rework, and fairness of break access. A staggered randomized pilot is preferable.                                            |
| **6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift**        | Controlled smoothing could reduce the final-45-minute Quality Check burst and prevent rushed checking.                                                                                                                  | Delayed detection can allow errors to propagate, create context loss, increase cross-shift handovers, and manipulate measured error timing. It must never become removal of quality checks or an artificial throughput improvement.                                                                                          | Age of pending Quality Checks, share completed in final 45 minutes, cross-shift QC backlog, detection delay, escaped defects, correction severity, handovers, SLA impact, and next-shift queue. Permit deferral only within explicit maximum-age and risk rules.                                 |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during Day-shift demand peaks** | Likely to reduce high-WIP exposure, provide rapid review for complex or junior-handled cases, and intercept bot or eligibility errors. It targets the shift with queue growth from 38 to 75 and a 7.5% late error rate. | Moving a senior from ordinary diagnosis or resolution work may delay other queues. The senior can become a new bottleneck, and indiscriminate second review may reduce throughput.                                                                                                                                           | WIP duration above 70, queue end level, senior review turnaround, errors and rework by originating resource group, bot overrides, junior escalation rate, senior workload, service delay in work from which the senior was reassigned, and total-system waiting time.                            |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates**        | No reliable operational benefit is established. At most, it creates an appearance of accountability.                                                                                                                    | Raw rates are confounded by case assignment, WIP, activity, shift, automation, detection practices, and small samples. Penalties encourage under-reporting, avoidance of hard cases, delayed error recording, and unsafe speed. Workers may be punished for correcting bot errors or detecting upstream defects.             | This intervention should be rejected. For supportive coaching, use confidential, risk-adjusted trends with uncertainty intervals, case-mix balance, WIP exposure, detection-versus-origin attribution, and evidence of adequate sample size. Monitor under-reporting and case-selection changes. |

The most defensible operational sequence is to implement a **WIP alert tied to concrete actions**, pilot **additional senior coverage during Day peaks**, and run a staggered experiment on **micro-breaks**. Complex-case smoothing and Quality Check deferral should be narrowly risk-controlled rather than blanket policies. Randomization is useful only within qualified assignment strata. Shift-length caps require evidence about overtime, and punitive use of raw error rates should be ruled out.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions are particularly vulnerable to unfairness because exposure is not randomly distributed.

Senior resources take complex early cases, night staff receive urgent exceptions, and juniors may be left with high-volume work during demand peaks. A worker with a higher raw error rate may therefore have handled harder cases, worked under higher WIP, performed more error-detecting activities, or corrected more automation failures. Conversely, a low observed error rate may reflect easy assignments, deferred Quality Checks, or under-reporting.

Several measurement problems require explicit safeguards:

* A recorded service interval may include inactive time, or a short interval may reflect batched timestamp entry.
* `error_flag` may identify the detecting event rather than the originating event.
* `rework_flag` may be attached to the correction worker even when another event created the defect.
* Quality Checks concentrated at shift end can make errors appear to originate late.
* Recent events lack a mature seven-day rework window.
* Bot_Risk has effectively zero service time and should not be included in human speed comparisons.
* Sequence positions above 75 are available mainly to fast workers, short activities, and batched workflows; they are not a comparable exposure shared by all workers.

Queue pressure is also partly management-created. Staffing plans, shift overlaps, inherited backlog, intake scheduling, automation design, and policies that defer Quality Checks determine the environment in which individuals work. It is ethically and analytically inappropriate to assign individual blame for errors generated by a predictable system-level overload.

Automation quality must be audited independently. A human override can mean that the control system worked as intended. Counting overrides as human inefficiency would discourage verification and create automation bias. The log should preserve the bot recommendation, confidence or reason code, input completeness, reviewer action, and final validated outcome.

Any worker-facing analysis should therefore use:

* minimum sample-size rules;
* uncertainty intervals and empirical-Bayes shrinkage;
* activity-, complexity-, priority-, and WIP-adjusted comparisons;
* separate measures of error creation, detection, and correction;
* transparent definitions and an opportunity to contest data errors;
* team- and system-level remediation before individual performance action.

Metrics should not create incentives to process quickly, avoid complex cases, defer error detection, or suppress rework reporting. The appropriate objective is a balanced combination of timeliness, quality, customer risk, and sustainable workload—not minimum service time.

The dashboard should combine leading indicators—WIP, arrival pressure, batching, Quality Check age—with lagging indicators such as mature error and rework. Every quality panel should show sample size, observation-window maturity, and uncertainty intervals.

Three process-mining views are especially useful:

* an events-distribution graph by clock hour and shift;
* a dotted chart by resource, activity, and shift to expose batches and end-of-shift bursts;
* matched performance DFGs for low- and high-pressure cohorts, retaining complete traces.

Alerts should be action-oriented. For example, sustained WIP above 70 combined with rising queue age and batch formation can trigger temporary senior review or workload redistribution. Individual raw error rates should never generate automatic punitive alerts.
