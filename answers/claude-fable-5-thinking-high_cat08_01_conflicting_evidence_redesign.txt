# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The core finding is that the organization does not have a "fast path vs. slow path" problem; it has a **risk-blind routing** problem. The same fast lane serves EUR-20 accessories and EUR-400+ devices, and the same heavyweight manual lane serves both as well. Every contradiction in the evidence dissolves once outcomes are read per segment rather than on average.

**The central contradiction, stated explicitly.** The most frequent path (Intake -> Auto eligibility screen -> Fast approval -> Dispatch replacement -> Close; roughly 2,550 of 3,600 auto-screened cases reach Fast approval, 2,790 in total counting the 240 arriving via Fraud/risk review) is attractive on speed and unit cost: Auto screen costs EUR 0.20/case and the edge times are short (Intake -> Auto screen median 0.5h, Auto screen -> Fast approval median 0.4h, Fast approval -> Dispatch median 1.8h). But the rework statistics show the cost advantage is largely an accounting illusion for part of the volume: Fast approval cases are **31% cheaper before rework but only 9% cheaper after** complaints, reshipments, and service credits. Complaint/reopen hits **14.1% of Fast-approval cases vs. 3.3%** of cases on the Repair quote -> Customer approval -> Repair device path. Crucially, this is segment-dependent: for Premium high-value devices the fast path produces **22% complaints vs. 5%** on the reviewed path, while for Standard low-risk accessories it is 8% vs. 4% — a tolerable gap given the speed win and the fact that Premium accessory claims incur service-credit exposure whenever cycle time exceeds 24 hours (fast path median 8h, manual path 46h). So the fast path is genuinely superior for low-risk accessories and genuinely value-destroying for high-value devices. The slow path is not "better"; it is better *only where risk justifies it*, and it is actively harmful for Premium accessories.

Specific problems, anchored in the artifacts:

1. **Compliance exposure concentrated on the fast path (conformance findings).** Fitness 0.91 with only 68% fitting traces, and the alignment diagnostics name exactly the dangerous deviations: *Fast approval before evidence completeness* (R2 violation), *Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices* (R1 violation), and *Customer complaint/reopen after close* (R3 exposure). Low precision of the discovered model confirms the process permits policy-forbidden combinations of auto approval, risk review, and repair. The 350-case edge Fast approval -> Quality/compliance close (skipping fulfillment entirely) is a further anomaly worth auditing.

2. **An evidence ping-pong loop that dominates the tail (frequency + performance DFG + rework stats).** Request missing evidence -> Manual eligibility review has mean 34h per iteration (p90 88h), the loop repeats in 26% of cases that enter it, and 320 of the 390 abandonments exit from this loop. The "Unclear defect / missing evidence" segment (22% of cases) has a 140-hour median cycle time and the highest abandonment. This loop, not the reviewed path itself, is what makes the slow paths look expensive.

3. **Wrong-replacement long tail (performance DFG + temporal profile).** Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen (260 cases) has mean 96h / p90 180h and is explicitly linked to incorrect replacement part selection, with expected wrong-replacement cost EUR 410 for high-value devices vs. EUR 95 for accessories. This is where the fast path's hidden cost lives.

4. **Fragile control-point capacity (resource information).** Fraud/risk review has only 2 specialists (EUR 52/h) and is already a Monday/post-campaign bottleneck, yet R1 makes it (or manual review) mandatory before Fast approval for >EUR 400 claims. Manual review (7 reviewers × 36 productive h/week) exhibits sharply nonlinear queueing above 82% utilization. Meanwhile the 2 quality inspectors are *underused* — slack capacity sitting next to the wrong-replacement problem. The Escalation board (one 45-minute daily slot) has strong CSAT effect but cannot scale, and its edges to Dispatch/Repair (65+45 cases) bypass Manual eligibility review, likely violating R3 for reopened claims.

5. **Premium latency violations on the reviewed path (temporal profile).** Manual eligibility review -> Repair quote (mean 22h, p90 60h) is flagged as too slow for Premium cases; combined with Repair quote -> Customer approval (mean 30h, p90 80h — mostly waiting for part-price confirmation), the reviewed path breaches Premium expectations even when it is the right path.

In short: speed is being bought with compliance debt and downstream rework for high-value claims, quality is being bought with unacceptable latency for Premium claims, and a fifth of all volume is stuck in an evidence loop that serves nobody.

# 2. Redesign A: Risk-tiered triage — segment-aware auto-screen with enforced control points

**Redesigned flow.** Replace the single outdated auto-screen with a segment- and value-aware triage at intake, creating three lanes:

- **Green lane (low-risk accessories, both segments; ~43% of volume):** Intake -> segment-aware auto screen -> Fast approval -> Dispatch -> Close. No manual touch. This is explicitly compliant: R2 already exempts Premium low-risk accessory claims, and R1 does not bind below EUR 400. Premium accessories get priority dispatch to stay under the 24-hour service-credit threshold.
- **Red lane (high-value devices; ~35% of volume):** Fast approval from auto screen is *structurally disabled* — the auto screen can only route to Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review, making R1 violations impossible by design rather than by policy. Risk review is triaged: a short rules-based risk score reserves the 2 fraud specialists for genuinely suspicious cases; the rest go to manual review.
- **Amber lane (mid-risk devices, unclear attributes):** auto screen with a lightweight secondary check; escalate on doubt.

**Targets.** The R1/R2 violations named in the alignment diagnostics; the 22%/18% fast-path complaint rates on high-value devices; the Premium-accessory service-credit exposure.

**Mechanism.** The improvement comes from *reallocating* review effort, not adding it: today 1,400 cases enter manual review at intake with no evidence they are the riskiest 1,400, while 2,790 cases get Fast approval including high-value devices. Routing by risk means low-risk volume stops consuming any human attention (freeing manual capacity) while high-value volume is forced through the control point that the segment data shows cuts complaints from 22% to 5% (Premium high-value). The prompt says the rules update deploys in two weeks — this is the cheapest available lever.

**Deliberate sacrifices.** High-value claims get slower on median (toward the 96–120h reviewed-path times) — this is accepted, because for that segment speed was fake (rework-adjusted). Manual-review inflow rises 15–35% per the prompt, which risks crossing the 82% utilization knee; the design bets that removing low-risk cases from manual review offsets this, but that is an assumption to test, not a fact. The evidence ping-pong loop and the wrong-part problem are left unresolved.

# 3. Redesign B: Evidence-first intake — kill the ping-pong loop before routing

**Redesigned flow.** Move evidence completeness *before* eligibility assessment instead of discovering gaps mid-process:

- Intake becomes a structured, defect-type-driven checklist (guided evidence portal): the claim cannot enter eligibility assessment until the evidence set required for its defect type and value tier is complete, or the customer explicitly opts into an **assisted channel** (phone/chat agent completes the checklist with them) to protect low-digital-literacy customers.
- Request missing evidence as a downstream activity is retained only as a rare exception with a hard cap: **one** consolidated evidence request per case (all gaps batched), with a proactive status page. A second gap triggers the assisted channel automatically instead of another asynchronous letter.
- Eligibility (auto or manual) then acts on complete files, which also makes R2 ("no dispatch before evidence completeness confirmed") structurally satisfied for every dispatched claim.

**Targets.** The Unclear defect / missing evidence segment (22% of cases, 140h median, highest abandonment); the Request missing evidence loop (26% multi-pass rate, 34h mean per return edge, 320 abandonments); the R2 deviations.

**Mechanism.** Each avoided loop iteration removes ~34h of elapsed time and a manual-review touch (890 return transitions currently feed back into manual review — a large share of reviewer workload is *re-reading files*). The prompt's estimate of 30–45% fewer repeated evidence requests translates directly into reviewer capacity headroom and shorter tails, and complete files should also improve first-time-right technical decisions and reduce the abandonment that currently converts served customers into lost ones.

**Deliberate sacrifices.** Intake gets heavier: first-touch time increases for everyone, including simple claims that never needed the rigor (mitigate by making checklist depth proportional to value tier). Abandonment may *shift earlier* — customers who would have abandoned at the third evidence request now abandon at intake; the assisted channel is the hedge, and it costs labor. This redesign does nothing about fast-path complaint rates, wrong replacements, or R1.

# 4. Redesign C: Guarded fulfillment — pre-dispatch part-verification gate and a conformant reopen loop

**Redesigned flow.** Leave the front of the process untouched and control the *exit*:

- Insert a **lightweight pre-dispatch quality gate for high-value replacements only** (~where Fast approval / risk review hands off to Dispatch): a part-selection verification against defect type and device model, staffed by the currently underused quality-check inspectors (the prompt notes they are idle except after repair campaigns). Low-risk accessories dispatch unchecked — a EUR 95 expected error does not justify 6–18h of latency; a EUR 410 one plausibly does.
- Rebuild the reopen loop to be R3-conformant and scalable: every Customer complaint/reopen routes through a **fast-track manual eligibility re-review** (a dedicated reviewer slot, SLA-bound) before any resolution or close; the Escalation board is reserved for Premium and disputed-denial cases and stops dispatching/repairing directly (its current 110 direct edges to Dispatch/Repair bypass R3).
- Feed every confirmed wrong-part case back into the auto-screen/part-catalog rules weekly, so the gate shrinks its own workload over time.

**Targets.** The Dispatch replacement -> Customer complaint/reopen long tail (260 cases, mean 96h, p90 180h, attributed to wrong part selection); the EUR 410 wrong-replacement cost on 35% of volume; the R3 deviations and "complaint after close" alignment finding; the unscalable escalation board.

**Mechanism.** This attacks the exact place where the fast path's 31%-to-9% cost-advantage collapse happens. Preventing a wrong dispatch avoids the reshipment, the ~96h complaint round-trip, the service credit, and a second fulfillment — a gate that adds 6–18h and catches even a moderate fraction of the 260 wrong-part cases is cheap by comparison, and it uses slack capacity rather than new headcount. Fixing the reopen loop converts complaints from a compliance hazard into structured signal.

**Deliberate sacrifices.** It is symptomatic containment: cases that should never have been fast-approved (R1) still are — compliance exposure at *approval* remains. High-value fast-path cycle time grows 6–18h. Upstream evidence chaos is untouched. It accepts complaints will still occur and optimizes their handling rather than only their prevention.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**Redesign A**
- *Failure 1 — queue collapse:* the +15–35% manual-review inflow lands on 7 reviewers already near the 82% nonlinearity; if green-lane relief materializes slower than red-lane load, reviewed-path times blow past 120h and Premium high-value cases breach expectations on the "correct" path too.
- *Failure 2 — misclassification at triage:* segment and defect-type attributes are only as good as intake data; mislabeled high-value claims leak into the green lane, silently recreating R1 violations while dashboards show compliance "fixed".
- *Bottleneck migration:* from the auto-screen (unconstrained) to **Fraud/risk review's 2 specialists**, whose Monday/post-campaign peaks will now gate the entire high-value lane.

**Redesign B**
- *Failure 1 — abandonment moves upstream:* low-digital-literacy customers drop at the portal instead of at the third evidence letter; total abandonment could rise even as the repeat-request metric improves — the metric wins while the customer loses.
- *Failure 2 — checklist theater:* customers upload low-quality evidence to satisfy the gate; completeness improves on paper but decision quality does not, and manual review inherits worse-not-better files.
- *Bottleneck migration:* from mid-process reviewers to **intake / assisted-channel agents**; a secondary shift back onto manual review if faster-completing files arrive in bursts.

**Redesign C**
- *Failure 1 — the gate becomes a queue:* the prompt's 6–18h range depends on resource design; with only 2 inspectors, a campaign spike turns the gate into a new p90 driver, and Premium high-value cases eat the delay without the service-credit protection accessories get.
- *Failure 2 — rubber-stamping:* under backlog pressure the gate approves everything, adding latency while catching nothing; measurable as a catch rate trending to zero while wrong-part complaints persist.
- *Bottleneck migration:* from post-dispatch complaint handling to **pre-dispatch inspection and the fast-track re-review slot**; also risk that the escalation board's caseload shifts into the manual re-review queue.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 1 (weeks 1–4): pilot Redesign A's rules update, scoped to high-value devices only.** It deploys in two weeks, is reversible, and attacks the largest compliance and rework-adjusted-cost exposure. Do *not* yet enable the accessory green lane — first observe what forced routing does to review capacity.
- *Metrics:* manual-review utilization and queue length (daily), fraud-review Monday backlog, R1 violation rate from continuous conformance replay, high-value fast-path share, complaint/reopen rate for high-value cohorts *started* during the pilot.
- *Stop/go:* stop if manual utilization sits above the low-80s% for more than a week or reviewed-path median for Premium high-value drifts materially beyond the current ~96h; go if R1 violations approach zero and utilization stays in the 70s%. If stopped, add the accessory green lane immediately to free capacity, then retry.

**Stage 2 (weeks 3–8, overlapping): pilot Redesign C's pre-dispatch gate on high-value replacements**, staffed by the idle quality inspectors. Sequenced second because Stage 1 shrinks the fast-path high-value volume the gate must handle, and the two experiments touch different resources (reviewers vs. inspectors), so effects remain attributable.
- *Metrics:* gate added latency (median and p90, against the 6–18h envelope), wrong-part catch rate, Dispatch -> complaint edge frequency and duration for gated vs. ungated cohorts, inspector utilization.
- *Stop/go:* stop if added latency exceeds the upper end of the envelope or catch rate is negligible after ~4 weeks (gate theater); go if wrong-part complaints on gated cohorts fall clearly relative to a holdout.

**Stage 3 (months 2–4): pilot Redesign B's evidence-first intake as a randomized A/B on the unclear-defect segment**, with the assisted channel live from day one. Sequenced last because it is the biggest build, and because Stages 1–2 will have changed reviewer load — the portal's business case (reviewer re-reads avoided) should be measured against the *new* baseline.
- *Metrics:* repeat-evidence-request rate (target: within the promised 30–45% reduction band), abandonment split by channel and by digital-literacy proxy (age band / channel choice), end-to-end median and p90 for the segment vs. the 140h baseline, intake-abandonment specifically.
- *Stop/go:* stop if total abandonment for the treated arm rises above the control arm's, even if repeat requests fall — that would mean losses shifted upstream; go if repeat requests drop into the target band with flat-or-better abandonment.

**Guarding against improvements that are merely displaced:** (1) evaluate by *case cohort* (cases started in-period, followed to true completion including any reopen), never by activity-level averages, so work pushed downstream still counts against its cohort; (2) track reopens for 30–60 days after close, since "complaint after close" is a documented deviation — a close-rate improvement with rising post-close reopens is displacement, not improvement; (3) report *rework-adjusted* cost per case (including reshipments and service credits), the metric that already exposed the 31%-vs-9% illusion; (4) rerun token-replay/alignment conformance on post-change logs each stage — fitness and fitting-trace share should rise, and precision should improve as forbidden path combinations disappear; if conformance improves while complaints do not, the redesign fixed the model, not the process; (5) keep small randomized holdouts in every stage so seasonal effects (marketing campaigns, Monday peaks) are not misread as treatment effects.
