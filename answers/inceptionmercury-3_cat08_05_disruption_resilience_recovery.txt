# 1. Disruption timeline

**T0 — Baseline (Day -30 to Day -1)**  
Operational state: all core systems stable; the normative process is followed with high conformance.  
Process variants: near-standard path from Receive order to Invoice close, with small compliance-hold, backorder, and repack loops.  
Risk posture: low. Traceability exceptions are 0.8%, missing cold-chain documentation is 0.4%, and missing controlled-substance dual-control is 0.2%. Process fitness is 0.96, with 91% of traces fitting the normative model.

**T1 — Day 0 morning: WMS unavailable**  
Operational state: sudden loss of warehouse management system; order intake, inventory reservation, scanning, and dispatch verification are unavailable.  
Process variants: no substantial order processing yet; teams begin informal triage.  
Risk posture: acute operational risk, especially for hospital-critical and cold-chain/controlled-substance products. The system failure is the trigger for improvised adaptation.

**T2 — Day 0 afternoon to Day 2: manual workaround phase**  
Operational state: paper and spreadsheet-based operations.  
Process variants: Emergency triage is used instead of normal validation; manual authorization checks and “skip system authorization check” both appear; manual inventory snapshot checks feed manual pick lists; manual pack is followed by either temporary release control or dispatch without scan; proof of delivery is collected by phone; invoice hold is tracked in a spreadsheet.  
Risk posture: throughput is partially preserved, but hidden compliance risk rises sharply. Traceability exceptions jump to 9.5%; missing cold-chain temperature documentation reaches 4.8%; missing controlled-substance dual-control reaches 2.9%; rework is 12.5%; cancellations are 7.8%. Hospital-critical fill rate is maintained at 91%, but pharmacy-standard fill rate falls to 61%. Process fitness drops to 0.71 with only 44% fitting traces.

Conformance findings in T2:
- Move-on-log deviations: Skip system authorization check, Dispatch without scan, Phone proof of delivery, Spreadsheet invoice hold.
- Move-on-model deviations: missing Quality/release check, missing scan, missing Proof of delivery, missing temperature-control record.
- Temporal deviations: Backlog queue  Manual pick list exceeds baseline p95; Dispatch without scan  Phone proof of delivery has highly variable delay.

**T3 — Day 3 to Day 6: partial recovery**  
Operational state: order intake system returns, but inventory synchronization and scanning remain unreliable.  
Process variants: hybrid manual/system operation; backlog begins to be prioritized; manual reservations and scan gaps are still being reconciled.  
Risk posture: improving but still elevated. Traceability exceptions are 5.4%; missing cold-chain documentation is 2.3%; missing control-substance dual-control is 1.4%; rework is 9.0%; cancellation is 5.2%. Process fitness improves to 0.82, with 59% fitting traces.

**T4 — Day 7 to Day 21: stabilization**  
Operational state: normal systems mostly return; operational metrics improve, but audit remediation and reconciliation work persist.  
Process variants: Backlog prioritization, Reconcile manual reservation, Proof-of-delivery remediation, and Audit remediation appear as distinct paths.  
Risk posture: controlled but not fully recovered. Traceability exceptions remain 2.2% versus 0.8% baseline; missing cold-chain documentation is 0.9% versus 0.4%; missing controlled-substance dual-control is 0.5% versus 0.2%; rework is 5.8% versus 3.5%; cancellations are 3.0% versus 2.1%. Process fitness recovers to 0.90 and 76% fitting traces, but the audit-remediation queue continues after operational recovery.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience is the ability to maintain the essential objectives of the process while the normal system is unavailable. Those objectives include patient safety, legal authorization, product identity, cold-chain integrity, controlled-substance security, and proof of delivery. A resilient adaptation replaces a lost control with an equivalent, visible, and documented control. It may change the workflow, but it does not delete the control.

Concealed noncompliance is different: it preserves throughput by removing a control and leaving no compensating mechanism. It may look successful in the short term because orders continue to flow. It becomes visible only later, during reconciliation or audit, and by then the evidence may be impossible to recover.

In this incident:

- **True resilience:** Manual authorization check (B), temporary release control for controlled substances (F), backlog prioritization if formally scored (J), reconciliation if performed continuously (K), and deferral if communicated transparently (I). These adaptations preserve a control or create a transparent alternative.

- **Risky shortcuts / concealed noncompliance:** Skip system authorization check (C) and dispatch without scan (G) are the most serious examples. They remove mandatory controls entirely. “Reconstructed” scans and after-the-fact remediation (E and L) are also risky because they can create a false impression that real-time compliance occurred.

- **Ambiguous adaptations:** Hospital-critical fast lane (A) is resilient if a manual authorization is performed and documented, but noncompliant if the authorization is simply skipped. Manual inventory snapshot (D) is useful early in T2 but becomes dangerous as the snapshot ages. Manual pick lists (E) are necessary but become a traceability risk if the paper evidence is not standardized. Phone POD (H) is acceptable for low-risk ambient products but not for controlled products. Deferral (I) is resilient if orders are explicitly queued and customers are notified, but harmful if used as a silent backlog. Backlog prioritization (J) is resilient only if the priority rules are formal and auditable.

The key distinction: resilience preserves the control objective through an alternative method. Concealed noncompliance preserves throughput by eliminating the control and hoping it will not be inspected.

# 4. Recovery process model

The redesigned recovery process uses a BPMN-style flow with four phases: **Contingency entry**, **Safe manual execution**, **Recovery and reconciliation**, and **Compliance close**. The structure is a sequence of phases, with parallel lanes for low-risk and high-risk orders.

## Trigger conditions

- Enter contingency mode when the WMS is unavailable for more than 30 minutes or when scanning/inventory synchronization is unreliable.
- Remain in hybrid mode after partial system return if reconciliation mismatch rates are above a defined threshold or if backlog age exceeds target service levels.
- Escalate to a formal incident review when any of the following occur:
  - Critical high-risk order is released without an approved compensating control.
  - Cold-chain temperature documentation is missing for any cold-chain order.
  - Controlled-substance dual-control is missing.
  - Audit-remediation queue grows faster than it is resolved.

## Triage rules

At Receive order, calculate a priority score based on customer criticality, product risk, clinical urgency, order age, and contractual penalties.

- Hospital-critical orders are always highest priority.
- Controlled-substance, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive orders are always placed in the high-risk lane.
- Pharmacy-standard orders with clinical or chronic medication needs come next.
- Wholesale-bulk and non-urgent orders are lowest priority and may be deferred only with notification.

## Minimum controls by product/customer risk

| Risk tier | Minimum required controls |
|---|---|
| Low-risk ambient, known customer | Manual authorization check from customer file; timestamped inventory snapshot; manual pick list; temporary release check; dispatch manifest; phone or digital POD. |
| Hospital-critical | Fast lane, but mandatory manual authorization check or documented conditional release; direct communication with hospital contact; every order tracked separately. |
| Cold-chain | Temperature logger or indicator check at dispatch and receipt; temperature record attached to the order; quarantine if evidence is missing. |
| Controlled-substance | Dual-control check with two licensed staff signatures; offline verification of authorization; secure transport; POD with signature/ID verification. |
| Recall-sensitive | Offline recall list checked before picking; any matching lot blocked; decision recorded by a designated reviewer. |

## Manual evidence capture

Every incident order should have an “incident order envelope” containing:

- Paper pick list with product ID, lot number, quantity, and operator ID.
- Authorization form or documented exception.
- Release checklist with pharmacist/approver signature.
- Dispatch manifest with courier signature.
- POD form with time, location, and recipient identification.

All forms should be scanned or photographed at each touchpoint and stored in a segregated “incident evidence” repository. Any skipped automated control must generate a compensating event in an exception log with reason, time, and approver.

## Backlog prioritization

When systems return, reopen the backlog using the same priority score used in triage:

- Sort by criticality, product risk, age, and contractual commitment.
- Notify customers of expected dates and escalation options.
- Do not cancel orders silently. Cancellation requires manager approval and should be recorded as a separate event.

## Reconciliation and audit-remediation flow

Reconciliation should begin during the incident, not only after operational recovery.

- Perform a daily comparison between the manual ledger and the electronic system once available.
- Create reconciliation tickets for order ID, inventory reservation, pick, dispatch, POD, and invoice mismatches.
- Resolve tickets before invoice close where possible.
- For each missing compliance event, classify the evidence:
  1. Alternative evidence exists — attach it and close the ticket.
  2. The control was performed but not recorded — create a compensating event with witness/approver signatures.
  3. No evidence exists and the control cannot be verified — mark as an unresolved exception, report it to compliance, and decide whether to hold the invoice.

Do not convert unresolved exceptions into completed records. The audit-remediation queue should be time-boxed and visible to management.

# 5. Resilience indicators

## Leading indicators

Leading indicators are early warning signals that can be observed during the incident.

| Area | Leading indicators |
|---|---|
| Throughput | Orders started per shift versus plan; queue ingress rate; manual order completion rate. |
| Service protection | Number of hospital-critical orders in flight; time from receipt to first action; percentage of hospital-critical orders on track. |
| Traceability | Percentage of orders with a complete manual event chain; count of active missing-event flags; time since last inventory snapshot refresh. |
| Control completeness | Number of skipped automated controls without a compensating event; percentage of high-risk orders with required manual sign-offs. |
| Backlog age | Median age of backlog by customer class; count of orders older than target service level. |
| Rework | Open rework requests; mispick/mislabel reports; manual release check failures. |
| Fairness | Variance of service times across customer classes; number of escalation appeals; voluntary cancellations per class. |

## Lagging indicators

Lagging indicators confirm whether the resilience effort actually worked.

| Area | Lagging indicators |
|---|---|
| Throughput | Median and p90 cycle time by segment; total cases completed. |
| Service protection | Fill rate by customer class; cancellation rate by customer class; customer-reported shortages. |
| Traceability | Traceability exception rate; cold-chain temperature documentation missing rate; controlled-substance dual-control missing rate. |
| Control completeness | Rework/correction case rate; quality/release check failure rate; number of unresolved audit remediation cases. |
| Backlog age | Backlog size at end of period; longest wait time; number of orders still in audit-remediation queue. |
| Rework | Rework/correction case rate; cost of rework. |
| Fairness | Fill-rate differential between hospital, pharmacy, and wholesale; cancellation-rate differential; number of fairness complaints. |

Process conformance can also be used as a lagging indicator: baseline fitness 0.96 with 91% fitting traces; stabilization reached only 0.90 with 76% fitting traces, showing that the process had not fully returned to normal even when operational metrics looked stable.

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build a contingency toolkit and runbook before the next disruption. | Medium | Faster activation, fewer improvised skips, consistent controls. | Toolkit may become outdated if not maintained and tested through drills. |
| 2 | Introduce an official “Incident Mode” with exception registration and compensating controls. | Medium | Makes deviations visible and sanctioned; separates resilience from noncompliance. | Teams may overuse incident mode; require strict entry/exit criteria. |
| 3 | Deploy offline evidence capture tools: barcode printers, portable scanners, structured PDFs, digital POD. | High | Eliminates scanless dispatch and phone-only POD; preserves traceability. | Hardware connectivity failure; need manual forms as backup. |
| 4 | Formalize triage and priority scoring. | Low | Fair backlog handling; protects hospital-critical and pharmacy-standard service. | Scores can be gamed; audit scoring and appeals. |
| 5 | Implement continuous reconciliation during the incident. | Medium | Reduces hidden workload after recovery; catches inventory and reservation errors early. | Requires dedicated staff and rotation; may fatigue teams. |
| 6 | Set an audit-remediation policy with time-boxes and unresolved-exception reporting. | Low | Prevents an indefinite compliance tail; exposes true residual risk. | Management may dislike hard deadlines, but unresolved exceptions must be visible. |
| 7 | Cross-train compliance staff and create a surge team. | High | Reduces the bottleneck in manual authorization and release controls. | Higher labor cost; training quality must be maintained. |
| 8 | Automate resilience dashboards with leading indicators. | Medium | Early detection of control erosion, backlog age, and fairness issues. | Metric overload; choose a small set of high-value indicators. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept a modest increase in cycle times and some clearly communicated deferral of noncritical orders during a disruption if high-risk controls remain intact. They should also accept the cost of redundant manual evidence capture, reconciliation, and audit resources as a legitimate business-continuity expense. What management should not accept is unlogged authorization skips, unmitigated dispatch without scan, or the treatment of after-the-fact remediation as equivalent to real-time compliance. If future evidence shows that a controlled digital contingency workflow can preserve equivalent traceability with lower manual burden, this recommendation should be revised. Until then, the organization should err on the side of observable compensating controls and transparent exception reporting. The ultimate test is not whether orders moved, but whether every critical product can be traced from authorization to proof of delivery with controls that were actually performed at the time.
