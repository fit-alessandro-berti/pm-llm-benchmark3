# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness should be assessed as a combination of whether similarly situated applicants receive comparable treatment, whether legitimate differences in process are administered fairly, and whether applicants can effectively access rights-preserving supports.

- **Outcome parity.** Raw approval rates differ substantially: 78% for Group A, versus 63%, 69%, and 58% for Groups B, C, and D. These differences are important screening signals, but they are not by themselves evidence of discrimination. Groups differ materially in risk bands, documentation completeness, channels, prior-case counts, language-support needs, and accommodation requests.

- **Adjusted outcome disparity.** After the preliminary adjustment, Group B still has an estimated approval probability 5.5 percentage points below Group A, Group C is 2.2 points below, and Group D is 3.1 points below. These are residual associations after observed controls, not proof that similarly eligible applicants were treated differently. They warrant investigation because key determinants of substantive eligibility and decision quality are not observed.

- **Procedural burden.** A fair process should not impose avoidable additional steps, document requests, supervisor reviews, handovers, or repeated work on one group after accounting for legitimate case needs. Group B has adjusted increases in both requests for missing documents (+9.5 points) and supervisor review (+8.0 points); Group C also has elevated rates of both. These may represent legitimate complexity, but the residual differences indicate that observed complexity does not fully explain the routing patterns.

- **Timeliness.** Equal access to a lawful process includes timely access. Group B and Group C have materially longer adjusted cycle times (+2.6 and +4.1 days), especially in the upper tail of cycle time. Long waits at language-support and accommodation steps are particularly concerning because those steps exist to preserve access, not to create an additional penalty for using support.

- **Consistency of routing.** Some routing is policy-required: high-risk cases may need Fraud Review; accommodation requests may need Accommodation Review; language needs may require Language Support Contact. Fairness requires that this routing be consistently applied to applicants with comparable needs and that non-required escalation, repeated document requests, and supervisor review not vary systematically by group without documented reasons.

- **Quality and accessibility of support.** The presence of Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review is not sufficient evidence of fair treatment. The log does not show whether translation was adequate, whether the accommodation was effective, whether contact was timely, whether applicants understood requests, or whether staff communicated in an accessible and respectful manner.

- **Appeal and rework burden.** Group B has a higher adjusted appeal probability (+4.4 points), and Group D has the largest adjusted appeal difference (+5.7 points). Rework rates are also high for Groups B and C. Appeals and rework can reflect legitimate disagreement or complex cases, but they can also signal poor explanations, inconsistent initial decisions, inaccessible communications, or avoidable administrative errors.

- **Resource-allocation fairness.** Resource assignment is a central procedural issue. Group B is concentrated among seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded regional officers; Group C is concentrated among five accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. Even if the specialized routing is legitimate, a system that under-resources these rights-preserving services can make applicants who need them bear a predictable queueing burden.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should distinguish four different claims:

1. **Disparity:** groups experience different outcomes or process burdens.
2. **Legitimate routing:** differences arise from valid, pre-specified service needs or risk-management requirements.
3. **Potential discrimination or unjustified differential treatment:** comparable cases receive different treatment without a valid reason, or policies/resources systematically disadvantage a group.
4. **Uncertainty:** observed differences may reflect omitted case facts, biased covariates, measurement error, or unobserved workload.

## Define separate estimands

Do not use one adjusted model for every question.

- **Total experienced burden:** Compare total time, handovers, rework, document requests, and appeals as actually experienced by each group. Do not “adjust away” language support or accommodation review when the question is whether using those rights-preserving services results in a disproportionate burden.

- **Complexity-standardized treatment:** Compare applicants with similar pre-routing characteristics to assess whether they receive different routing, waiting time, or outcomes. This is appropriate for assessing consistency.

- **Within-route queue fairness:** Among applicants who legitimately require Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review, or Fraud Review, compare waits, handovers, and completion rates. This isolates whether the service is equitably resourced and delivered.

- **Decision-outcome fairness:** Compare approval and appeal outcomes among applicants with similar documented eligibility-relevant characteristics. This remains limited because the available data lack many facts needed to establish true substantive eligibility.

## Matching or stratification variables

Use matching, coarsened exact matching, entropy balancing, or stratification on variables known before discretionary routing or decision-making:

- risk_score_band;
- documentation_completeness measured at submission, if available;
- channel;
- household_size_band;
- prior_case_count;
- region;
- submission month or period;
- language_preference;
- disability_accommodation_requested.

For some analyses, add interaction strata such as:

- language preference × channel;
- accommodation request × region;
- risk band × prior-case count;
- documentation completeness × channel.

Important cautions:

- Do **not** control for post-routing activities such as Supervisor Review, Fraud Review, Appeal Intake, rework, resource assignment, or eventual outcome when estimating total procedural burden. Those can be mediators of the disparity being assessed.
- Documentation completeness may be partly caused by inaccessible forms, language barriers, or inadequate assistance. Treating it as an unquestionably exogenous “complexity” control can mask an access problem.
- Risk score band may be a legitimate routing input, but it should not be treated as automatically neutral or unbiased.

## Path and variant comparison

Compare both exact variants and broader pathway categories.

- Exact variants identify common end-to-end paths, such as V4 for language support and V5 for accommodation review.
- Broader categories reduce sparsity and make comparisons interpretable, for example:
  - no-support standard route;
  - documentation-rework route;
  - language-support route;
  - accommodation route;
  - fraud-review route;
  - supervisor-escalation route;
  - appeal route.

For each pathway category, estimate:

- probability of entering the path;
- number of repeated activities;
- number of handovers;
- time spent between key steps;
- probability of reopening, appeal, activation, and closure;
- outcome conditional on reaching the route.

The key comparison is not merely whether Group B or C uses support more often. It is whether, among applicants with the same support need and similar case complexity, their path length, queue time, repeat requests, and decisions differ.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Estimate whether assignment to scarce resources is predictable from group membership after accounting for need, region, channel, and time period.

Examples:

- Among cases requiring language support, compare assignment to bilingual caseworkers, regional officers, and general caseworkers by group and region.
- Among accommodation cases, compare assignment to accommodation specialists and supervisor-review queues.
- Measure number of resource changes per case and the frequency of general caseworker  specialist  general caseworker transitions.
- Compare daily workload, case arrivals, pending inventory, and completion throughput for bilingual workers, accommodation specialists, and other caseworkers.

A residual association between group and assignment to a more overloaded resource pool is a resource-allocation fairness concern, though causal attribution requires better workload and staffing data.

## Waiting-time decomposition

Decompose cycle time into:

- submission-to-verification delay;
- document-request delay;
- language-support queue delay;
- accommodation-review queue delay;
- fraud-review queue delay;
- supervisor-review queue delay;
- decision-to-notification delay;
- time spent in repeated loops;
- active service time, if activity-start and completion timestamps are available.

Estimate group differences in each component before and after complexity standardization. A long total cycle time can be legitimate if it reflects additional substantive review, but long waits for a scarce support queue are not automatically justified merely because the support step is required.

## Sensitivity analysis for potentially biased covariates

Run multiple specifications rather than treating the preliminary model as definitive:

1. Adjust for all preliminary controls.
2. Exclude documentation completeness from the adjustment set.
3. Treat documentation completeness as a mediator of language access and estimate a total effect of language-related burden.
4. Use alternative risk-score specifications: bands, ordered score, and low/medium/high collapse.
5. Re-estimate after excluding borderline risk bands where assignment may be especially discretionary.
6. Conduct sensitivity analysis for an unobserved eligibility or complexity factor that could explain the residual approval or time differences.
7. Audit whether risk-score bands have comparable calibration, error rates, and routing consequences across groups, subject to the availability of valid outcome labels.
8. Report overlap diagnostics: do matched Group A cases actually exist for the Group B, C, and D cases being compared?

Residual disparities that disappear only when controlling for potentially biased variables should not be characterized as resolved.

## Subgroup checks by region and channel

Estimate group effects separately by:

- region;
- online versus in-person channel;
- language preference;
- accommodation requested versus not requested;
- risk-score band;
- documentation-completeness level.

Include group × region and group × channel interactions. This is important because the evidence already identifies two regions with excessive Group B and Group C handovers, and in-person cases have longer initial waits. Aggregate results may conceal a severe regional problem or, conversely, make a localized backlog appear system-wide.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Several differences appear plausibly related to legitimate case complexity, but the available evidence also identifies material procedural fairness concerns.

## Differences plausibly explained in part by legitimate routing

- **Group D and the fraud-review route.** Group D has a much larger high-risk share than Group A (51% versus 18%) and uses V3, the Fraud Review/Supervisor Review path, at 28% versus 9%. Since policy requires Fraud Review for higher risk-score bands, much of this difference is plausibly legitimate.

  However, Group D still has an adjusted approval difference of -3.1 percentage points and an adjusted appeal difference of +5.7 points. Its median Risk Screening  Fraud Review wait is 2.8 days, higher than all other groups. The routing is legitimate only if the risk band is valid, consistently applied, and not itself reproducing historical bias.

- **Group B and the language-support route.** Group B has much greater language-support need (57% versus 2% for Group A) and enters V4 at 24% versus 1%. A Language Support Contact is therefore expected and rights-preserving. The existence of the route should not be labeled unfair.

- **Group C and the accommodation route.** Group C has a high accommodation-request rate (62% versus 4% for Group A) and enters V5 at 31% versus 2%. Accommodation Review is policy-required and appropriate for many of these cases.

- **Documentation-rework routes.** Group B and Group C have more incomplete documentation than Group A, making greater use of V2 plausibly expected: 34% for Group B and 29% for Group C, versus 24% for Group A.

## Differences that remain concerning after accounting for legitimate need

- **Long waits associated with rights-preserving support.** Group B waits a median 3.8 days between Language Support Contact and Eligibility Assessment. Group C waits 4.7 days from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment. These are large queue delays attached to services that applicants should be able to use without disproportionate penalty.

- **Concentrated, under-resourced support pools.** Only seven bilingual caseworkers serve a disproportionate share of Group B cases. Five accommodation specialists serve a disproportionate share of Group C cases, and two also perform supervisor review. This is a plausible structural cause of delay rather than an inherent feature of applicants’ cases.

- **Adjusted cycle-time differences.** Even after the preliminary adjustment, Group B has +2.6 days and Group C +4.1 days of cycle time compared with Group A. Group C’s adjusted delay is particularly large relative to its raw approval difference. This pattern is consistent with a substantial procedural burden associated with accommodation routing and specialist capacity.

- **Excess document requests.** Group B has an adjusted +9.5 percentage-point probability of Request Missing Documents and Group C +4.2 points. Some difference may remain because documentation completeness is measured imperfectly, but the magnitude for Group B is a warning sign for communication, translation, form design, or inconsistent completeness review.

- **Supervisor-review differences.** Group B and C have adjusted increases of +8.0 and +5.5 percentage points in Supervisor Review. Their waits from Supervisor Review to Decision Draft are also higher than Group A’s: 1.9 days for Group B and 1.6 for Group C, versus 1.0. Backlogs in two regions provide a plausible operational explanation, but unequal exposure to those backlogs remains procedurally relevant.

- **Handover burden.** The general caseworker  bilingual caseworker  general caseworker chain for Group B and the general caseworker  accommodation specialist  supervisor chain for Group C create extra coordination points. More handovers can lead to repeated explanations, lost context, inconsistent advice, and avoidable delay even when each specialist step is legitimate.

- **Appeal and rework.** Group B’s appeal rate is 13%, versus 6% for Group A, and Group D’s is 16%. Group B and C have reopen/rework rates of 22% and 24%, versus 11% for Group A. These data do not show that initial decisions were wrong, but they are consistent with a higher administrative burden and warrant review of decision quality, communications, and error correction.

The most defensible interpretation is that support and fraud routes are often legitimate, but their current implementation appears to place disproportionately high waiting, handover, and rework burdens on Groups B and C. Group D’s risk routing is more plausibly complexity-driven, but it cannot be assumed fair without a risk-score audit.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

At least several mechanisms could jointly produce the observed patterns.

1. **Legitimate policy-driven complexity.** Higher risk scores can legitimately require Fraud Review, and accommodation or language needs can legitimately require specialized review. This can increase path length even in a fair process.

2. **Specialist-capacity bottlenecks.** Seven bilingual caseworkers and five accommodation specialists may be insufficient for demand. Queueing at these services can explain the 3.8-day language-support delay and 4.7-day accommodation-review delay without implying individual staff misconduct.

3. **Fragmented routing and excessive handovers.** Returning cases to general caseworkers after specialist contact may create avoidable handovers, duplicated checks, and context loss. This is especially plausible in the two regions with known multilingual and accommodation-related handover chains.

4. **Communication and form-access barriers.** Group B’s high incomplete-documentation share and residual document-request difference could arise because notices, forms, translations, or explanations are difficult to understand. A document may be coded “incomplete” even where the system failed to make requirements accessible.

5. **Inconsistent discretionary review.** Caseworkers or supervisors may apply completeness standards, escalation criteria, or benefit rules differently across channels, regions, languages, or applicants with accommodation needs. The adjusted Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents differences are consistent with this possibility but do not prove it.

6. **Historically biased risk measurement.** Risk-score bands may incorporate prior enforcement, prior case activity, location, documentation patterns, or other features correlated with group membership. If so, Group D’s greater Fraud Review exposure could reflect both legitimate risk management and historical bias embedded in the score.

7. **Poor decision explanations or inaccessible notices.** Elevated appeals and rework may result from unclear denial reasons, untranslated communications, inaccessible formats, or applicants not understanding how to resolve deficiencies. Conversely, some appeals may reflect valid disagreement rather than a process defect.

8. **Regional workload and channel effects.** In-person submissions wait longer at the first verification step, and two regions have supervisor backlogs. If Group B is more likely to apply in person or in those regions, operational disparities can compound group disparities even without explicit group-based rules.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available log is useful for process analysis but insufficient to determine whether individual decisions were substantively correct or whether any disparity constitutes unlawful discrimination.

- **Measurement bias in documentation completeness.** The field may reflect actual missing evidence, but it may also capture language barriers, inaccessible instructions, staff discretion, or prior failures of support. Its timing matters: if it is recorded after Language Support Contact, it may be partly caused by the process itself.

- **Historical bias in risk-score bands.** A risk score can be policy-relevant without being neutral. The log lacks the score’s construction, validation population, calibration by group, error rates, and downstream consequences. Historical data used to build it may encode prior unequal scrutiny.

- **Missing substantive eligibility facts.** The log does not include the complete legal merits of the claim, income verification, program-specific eligibility facts, evidence quality, or reasons for approval and denial. Therefore, adjusted approval differences cannot establish that equally eligible applicants received different decisions.

- **Missing applicant needs and preferences.** Language preference is not the same as an actual request for interpretation, literacy support, translated materials, or a particular communication format. Accommodation requested does not indicate the type, urgency, adequacy, or fulfillment of the accommodation.

- **Unobserved workload and capacity.** Resource workload is observed only daily. The log does not show queue position, concurrent tasks, sick leave, schedules, qualifications, vacancies, case priority, work interruptions, or whether a named resource was actually available when the case entered a queue.

- **Insufficient timing data for true service time.** With only one timestamp per event, inter-event time can be measured, but active work time versus waiting time cannot be reliably separated unless activity-start or lifecycle data are available.

- **Limited evidence on appeal quality.** `appeal_flag` indicates an appeal but not whether it was timely, accessible, resolved, successful, withdrawn, caused by error, or associated with a changed outcome. It also does not capture informal complaints or applicants who abandoned the process.

- **No measure of explanation, dignity, or trust.** The outcome field does not capture whether applicants received understandable reasons, respectful treatment, meaningful participation, accessible communication, or an effective opportunity to correct errors.

- **Potential recording and censoring problems.** Some cases may be open, administratively closed, transferred, or missing events. Path summaries that end at Decision or Appeal Review may reflect abbreviated variants, incomplete logging, or true notification/closure omissions; this must be checked before treating them as policy violations.

- **Broad group categories mask intersections.** Group-level analysis may conceal important combinations, such as Group B applicants using in-person channels in a particular region, or Group C applicants who also need language support.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation | Expected fairness benefit | Possible side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Add bilingual caseworker capacity and accommodation-specialist capacity | Reduces queue delays for language support and accommodation review; lowers dependence on a few workers; improves continuity. | Requires funding, recruitment, training, and retention. Small specialist pools may initially be hard to expand, and reallocating staff could increase delays elsewhere if not planned carefully. |
| Create service-level targets for support steps | Makes Language Support Contact  Eligibility and Accommodation Review  Eligibility waits visible and accountable; prevents rights-preserving steps from becoming hidden delay sources. | Targets can encourage gaming, superficial contacts, or inappropriate prioritization of easy cases. Targets should be risk-adjusted, monitored for quality, and paired with safeguards against rushed support. |
| Redesign routing to reduce handovers | Use case ownership, warm handoffs, shared case notes, multilingual generalist capacity, and specialist consultation without unnecessary return transfers. Preserve the specialist review while reducing repeated explanations and context loss. | Generalists may need training; specialists may lose some control over quality; a simplified workflow must not weaken accommodation, interpretation, fraud, or supervisory safeguards. |
| Improve decision explanations and applicant communications | Provide translated, plain-language, accessible-format notices that state the missing evidence, decision rationale, next steps, and appeal rights. This may reduce avoidable rework and appeals. | Higher drafting and translation workload; more detailed notices may initially increase appeals or expose sensitive fraud-detection logic. Explanations should remain meaningful without compromising legitimate investigative methods. |
| Provide front-end documentation assistance | Offer translated checklists, accessible forms, document-upload help, and pre-submission completeness review. This can reduce the need for later document requests. | May increase intake time and demand for support staff. If poorly designed, it could become an additional gatekeeping step rather than assistance. |
| Rebalance workloads and introduce queue triage | Shift cases or specialist capacity across regions, monitor daily backlog, and reserve capacity for delayed support cases. | Redistribution can disrupt continuity, create travel or remote-work burdens, and move workload to already stressed teams. Triage rules must not deprioritize high-need cases. |
| Monitor adjusted disparities routinely | Maintain dashboards for standardized approval, delay, handover, document-request, rework, and appeal differences, with confidence intervals and regional/channel breakdowns. | Models can create false precision, embed biased covariates, and create incentives to optimize metrics rather than applicant experience. Monitoring should trigger qualitative case review, not automatic conclusions. |
| Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness | Test risk-score calibration, routing rates, false positives, and fraud-review consequences by group. Review whether completeness coding is consistent and whether it reflects access barriers. | Changing scores or thresholds can affect fraud detection, workload, and program integrity. Valid outcome labels may themselves be biased, so audits require careful governance and independent review. |
| Add human review for adverse or repeated-routing cases | Require a documented rationale before repeated document requests, repeated supervisor escalation, or prolonged support-related delay. | Adds workload and could itself increase processing time unless narrowly targeted and adequately staffed. |

None of these options should remove Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review merely to improve cycle time. The appropriate objective is to preserve the rights-protecting step while reducing avoidable queueing, fragmentation, and repeated administrative burden.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence does not justify concluding that raw approval-rate differences alone are discriminatory. Substantial case-mix differences make some routing differences expected, particularly Group D’s greater fraud-review exposure, Group B’s language-support use, and Group C’s accommodation-review use.

However, the process cannot yet be characterized as demonstrably procedurally fair. After preliminary adjustment, Groups B and C still experience materially longer cycle times, elevated document-request and supervisor-review probabilities, and heavier appeal or rework burdens. The most concerning pattern is that rights-preserving language and accommodation steps are associated with long specialist-queue waits, multiple handovers, and concentrated assignment to small, overloaded resource pools.

The evidence supports a finding of credible procedural fairness risk rather than a definitive causal finding of discrimination. Further matched, route-specific, regional, workload-aware, and sensitivity analyses are needed, especially audits of documentation completeness and risk-score bands. In the meantime, reducing delays and handovers around support services while preserving the services themselves is a justified operational priority.
