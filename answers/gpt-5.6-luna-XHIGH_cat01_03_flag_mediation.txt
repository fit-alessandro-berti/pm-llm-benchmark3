## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

I use policy-level, longitudinal interventional estimands rather than natural direct and indirect effects. A natural decomposition would combine a case's outcome under one flag policy with the mediator value it would have had under another policy, requiring a cross-world assumption that is especially implausible when queue state and reviewer behavior respond to treatment. An interventional indirect effect instead changes a specified stochastic routing, waiting-time, or escalation distribution while retaining an explicitly stated policy. A controlled direct or separable effect is appropriate only when an operational action literally fixes that component, such as reserving review slots. Queue interference means that the primary population should be defined over incoming cases and randomized queue blocks or shifts, not treated as independent case-level units.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | Pk versus P0, for each k in {1,2,3,4} | All eligible incoming cases under a specified arrival and capacity regime | Case cycle time, quality, and queue spillover | Total policy effect | Yes, if the policy, assignment rule, capacity, and time horizon are specified | No exchangeability in the observational data and interference through shared queues |
| E2 | P1 versus P0, with the automated priority-lane rule stated explicitly | Eligible cases, preferably stratified by risk score, complexity, load, and arrival block | Arrival-to-triage-done, triage waiting, and triage service time | Stage-specific total effect, not a natural direct effect | Yes only after deciding whether a hidden flag can still control the lane | Confounding by case mix and congestion; P1 may alter human handling without altering machine routing |
| E3 | P2 versus P0, decomposed through a stochastic intervention on manual-review occurrence | All eligible cases, including those who would not otherwise be reviewed | Cycle time and quality under a changed probability of manual review | Interventional indirect analogue through binary manual_review | Yes if “mandatory” is replaced by a documented review-exemption rule | Manual review is post-treatment and its outcome is confounded by risk, judgment, and queue state; review also affects other cases |
| E4 | P3 versus P0, retaining review occurrence but shifting the distribution of manual queue waits | Flagged cases eligible for the protected capacity, plus unflagged cases for spillover measurement | Total cycle time and triage-done-to-manual-start contribution | Interventional indirect analogue through manual-review waiting | Yes if protected slots, queue discipline, and capacity withdrawal are specified | Wait is selected by upstream routing and is jointly determined by other cases' arrivals and treatment |
| E5 | P4 versus P0, changing escalation criteria or senior capacity while holding the upstream routing rule fixed | Cases eligible for senior review and all cases sharing the senior queue | Cycle time, escalation wait, and adjudicated quality | Interventional indirect analogue through escalation occurrence and waiting | Yes only for a concrete threshold or capacity rule, not for “change criteria” in the abstract | Escalation depends on post-review judgment and hidden risk; senior-team capacity creates spillover |
| E6 | P2 versus P0 or P3 versus P0, with the full policy bundle applied | All eligible cases in the pilot or operational population | Close minus arrival, in minutes or a tail percentile | Total effect on total cycle time | Yes | Censoring, time-varying congestion, and changed case composition can obscure the policy effect |
| E7 | P1, P2, or P4 versus P0 with adjudicated truth labels | Eligible cases with an independent later adjudication or verified downstream outcome | False-negative, false-positive, severity-weighted loss, and compliance error | Total policy effect on decision quality | Yes after defining the loss function and adjudication window | Approve and Reject alone do not reveal correctness; labels may be selective or delayed |
| E8 | A literal intervention fixing manual_review, manual waiting, and escalation to common stochastic rules, versus the active flag policy | Cases in the common-support region of risk, complexity, load, and queue state | Cycle time or quality | Controlled direct or separable policy analogue | Only if the organization can actually enforce those component rules without changing other pathways | The proposed fixed mediator may be incompatible with the policy and may itself alter queues and reviewer behavior |

The flag is downstream of imperfect risk_score, complexity, and unmeasured risk, so it is not the underlying risk itself. Interference arises because another case's flag, review, or escalation changes shared queue state and hence this case's waits. Manual-review occurrence and triage timing are exposure-induced variables: they affect who enters a queue, while queue state and reviewer judgment also affect later escalation and outcome. This creates treatment-confounder feedback; adjusting for post-flag queue state or review judgment can block part of the policy effect or induce collider bias. arrival_load is only a coarse pre-treatment proxy, not the actual queue state. Natural mediation additionally requires incompatible cross-world mediator assignments, so it is not assumed here.

Flagged cases clearly receive faster initial triage: median arrival-to-triage-start is 12 minutes versus 150 for unflagged cases, and triage service is also shorter, 36 versus 57 minutes. Downstream, every flagged case enters manual review, with a median 405-minute triage-to-review wait, compared with 105 minutes among the four reviewed unflagged cases. Six flagged cases then wait a median 420 minutes for escalation. Consequently, median total cycle is 987 versus 345 minutes; within flagged cases it is 1,086 at high load versus 735 at normal load. These contrasts are compatible with priority acceleration followed by shared manual and senior queues. They are not causal estimates: flagged cases have different risk and complexity, review and escalation are selected mediators, resources differ, load is coarse, and ten cases cannot separate policy effects from case mix or peer-case spillovers.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis

| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| flag -> priority_lane -> triage timing | A direct policy-bundle effect accelerates triage assignment and possibly triage service | Flagged median triage wait is 12 versus 150 minutes; service is 36 versus 57 | The lane is actually triggered by the flag, and comparable arrivals would otherwise have similar triage capacity | Flagged cases may arrive at easier times or be easier to process; high risk may prompt human urgency independently | Randomize visible flag suppression or lane eligibility, or compare cases locally around a documented score cutoff | Priority may displace unflagged cases and increase their triage wait; rushed triage may reduce information gathered |
| flag -> manual_review occurrence | Mandatory review is a principal route by which the flag lengthens completion | Manual review occurs in 10/10 flagged versus 4/10 unflagged cases | Review rules and human compliance are stable and the intervention changes occurrence without changing hidden risk | High-risk or complex cases would have been reviewed anyway; flag may merely reveal that judgment | P2 review exemption with explicit safety criteria | Review capacity is released, but false negatives or later rework may increase |
| flag -> manual_queue_wait | Flag-driven review increases demand in a shared scarce queue | Flagged reviewed cases wait a median 405 minutes versus 105 for unflagged reviewed cases | Queue state, service capacity, and queue discipline are measured at entry; no unmeasured scheduling rule explains the gap | Flagged cases may be more complex and require different review priority or arrive in a backlog | P3 protected review slots or a queue-aware routing rule | If capacity is not truly added, unflagged review waits or another queue may worsen |
| flag -> escalation | Flagged reviews may use a lower escalation threshold or attract more senior scrutiny | Escalation occurs in 6/10 flagged versus 2/10 unflagged cases; flagged escalation wait median is 420 minutes | Escalation criteria, reviewer judgment, and senior capacity are specified and comparable | Risk and complexity drive escalation even without the flag; only the most difficult flagged cases are selected | P4 audit of threshold, capacity, and escalation prioritization | Fewer escalations may miss danger; more capacity may draw cases away from other senior work |
| complexity/risk -> flag and downstream stages | The flag partly proxies underlying risk and intake complexity rather than causing duration | High-complexity cases have much longer cycles and more escalation; score ranges differ by flag | Relevant risk and complexity are measured before treatment and adequately modeled; support overlaps | The risk score may omit clinically or operationally important risk; complexity may be misclassified | Local-threshold design, risk-calibrated stratification, and improved score covariates | Recalibration can change who is flagged, creating new queue demand and fairness tradeoffs |
| shared-queue interference | Treatment of one case changes other cases' waits, so a flagged delay is partly a system effect | Flagged high-load cycle median is 1,086 versus 735 at normal load; unflagged cases share review and senior queues | Other arrivals, service completions, priority rules, and queue positions are observed or modeled | arrival_load may be too crude, and calendar time may confound load with staffing | Cluster-randomized queue blocks with direct and spillover outcomes | A benefit for flagged cases can be offset by slower unflagged cases or senior backlog |
| reviewer/resource assignment | T1-T4 and M1-M3 may differ in speed, expertise, or escalation propensity | Resource identities are recorded, but subgroup counts are small and assignment is nonrandom | Resource effects are stable, measured, and not themselves chosen in response to unobserved severity | Resource is a proxy for shift, queue, case severity, or local policy | Balanced rotation or random resource assignment where safe; include resource in process monitoring | Balancing can reduce specialization or move difficult work to less experienced staff |
| decision-quality tradeoff | Faster triage or fewer reviews may trade cycle time against error detection and compliance | Outcomes include approvals and rejects, but no independent truth label; flagged and unflagged outcomes are selected | A validated adjudication target and stable loss function exist; outcome ascertainment is unaffected by policy | Reject rate reflects case mix, reviewer style, or escalation selection rather than correctness | P2/P4 pilot with blinded adjudication and mandatory safety exclusions | Shorter cycles can increase false approvals, false rejections, appeals, or regulatory exceptions |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Does the priority policy itself accelerate triage? | Document the flag rule and compare cases narrowly on either side of its operational risk-score threshold; estimate a local intent-to-treat effect of receiving the flag policy | Risk score running variable, complexity, arrival load, calendar block, and any pre-flag covariates; do not adjust for post-flag queues | A deterministic threshold exists, cases cannot precisely manipulate score, and potential outcomes are smooth at the cutoff | Plot covariate and arrival-time continuity, bandwidth sensitivity, and placebo cutoffs; check for a jump in triage start but not in pre-triage variables | No threshold discontinuity, no overlap, or a discontinuity in pre-treatment case mix would make the local result unreliable |
| Is downstream waiting explained by congestion rather than service duration? | Construct queue position and available capacity at arrival, triage completion, manual entry, and escalation entry; estimate stage-specific wait models and a policy-standardized g-computation contrast | Pre-entry queue length, active reviewers, senior capacity, arrivals ahead, load, resource, complexity, and time block; keep post-treatment queue variables out of a total-effect model | Queue snapshots are accurate and capture the relevant queue, and service/resource assignment is sufficiently measured | Wait should increase monotonically with queue position; P3 should reduce flagged wait at comparable occupancy without increasing total workload unexpectedly | The flagged wait gap disappears after valid pre-entry queue adjustment, or protected capacity fails to reduce it |
| What is the causal effect of changing handling? | Randomize display suppression, review exemption, or protected capacity; use cluster or shift randomization when cases share queues and report ITT plus spillovers | Stratify randomization by risk band, complexity, load, site, and shift; analyze all assigned cases | Equipoise, adherence measurement, no differential censoring, and a safe exclusion rule for urgent cases | Balance checks, first-stage change in flag visibility or capacity, treatment-effect estimate, and unflagged queue outcomes | No first stage, substantial protocol violations, quality harm, or a large unanticipated spillover would reject the proposed policy |
| Is manual occurrence a mediator of cycle time? | Fit a longitudinal interventional mediation analysis: model review occurrence, then service/wait and escalation under each policy, and simulate stochastic mediator distributions | Baseline risk score, complexity, load, time, resources, plus valid time-varying queue covariates; account for mediator selection | Sequential exchangeability for each mediator-outcome step, consistency, positivity, and a correctly specified queue/interference regime | A simulated intervention reducing review occurrence should shorten cycle while quality remains within bounds; compare observed and simulated stage distributions | A review reduction does not reduce cycle, or quality loss appears before the expected time benefit, would weaken the mechanism |
| Is there residual confounding before triage? | Collect a negative-control outcome such as registration-system latency or pre-triage data-validation time that the flag cannot affect, and optionally an unrelated pseudo-flag as a negative-control exposure | Arrival block, system, load, complexity, and risk-score strata | The negative control is unaffected by flag handling but shares the relevant timing and case-mix confounders | A null association for the negative control supports temporal comparability; a positive association signals residual confounding or timestamp problems | A sizable negative-control association would reduce confidence in the observed flag-to-wait contrast |
| How strong would hidden risk need to be to erase the result? | Perform quantitative bias analysis and partial-identification bounds, varying the prevalence, strength, and calibration of an unobserved risk factor; validate the score on adjudicated cases | Observed score, complexity, load, resource, outcome, and measured risk features; vary hidden-risk sensitivity parameters | The sensitivity model spans plausible hidden-risk effects and missingness is not selectively extreme | Report the hidden-risk strength needed to explain the triage acceleration, review routing, and cycle gap separately | Plausible hidden-risk values that reverse both timing and quality results would reverse a causal policy interpretation |
| Does treatment spill over across cases? | Randomize whole queue blocks, shifts, or teams to protected capacity or current policy; record every arrival and queue transition, not only flagged cases | Block/shift, queue lane, arrivals ahead, treatment status of neighbors, resources, and service capacity | Interference is captured by a known queue exposure mapping and clusters are large enough | Estimate direct flagged effects, unflagged spillover, total workload, and block-level capacity; look for conservation of delay | If flagged gains are accompanied by equal-or-larger unflagged delays, the case-level benefit and P3 recommendation would be reconsidered |
| Are timestamp intervals measuring work rather than documentation? | Audit event emission, clock synchronization, and late/backfilled timestamps; repeat analyses using event logs and, where possible, work-start telemetry | Resource, site, local clock, correction indicator, and documented timestamp source | Start and done timestamps have the stated semantics and are comparable across stages and resources | Nonnegative interval checks, duplicate/backfill rates, clock-offset checks, and comparison with active-work telemetry | If triage or queue starts are documentation times rather than operational starts, the stage decomposition cannot support the mechanism claim |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | Fast flagged triage and slower unflagged triage, as observed | Mandatory flagged review and selective escalation consume shared capacity | Baseline; flagged cases have a 987-minute median in this sample | Current quality is unknown because Approve and Reject lack truth labels | Existing delays are redistributed across review and senior queues | Highest for description, none for causal counterfactuals | Keep as comparator and monitor; do not treat it as an optimized baseline |
| P1: suppress flag display, retain score and other information | Automated lane is unchanged if it reads a hidden flag; visible human prioritization may weaken | Review demand changes only if humans used the displayed flag; score-driven review can persist | Could fall, be neutral, or change unpredictably depending on the UI and lane implementation | Risk of inconsistent decisions, missed high-risk cases, or continued opaque automation | Possibly less human-review demand, but score-based or unflagged demand may replace it | Very weak without a precise display and routing specification | Use only as a clearly specified behavioral experiment, not as “flag equals zero” |
| P2: retain priority triage, remove mandatory review | Triage should remain fast for flagged cases | Manual-review occurrence and its queue demand should fall; escalation may also fall | Likely shorter flagged cycles and less backlog | Highest risk of missed risk, false approvals, and compliance violations without safety exceptions | Likely relief for review and senior queues, but possible rework or appeals later | Mechanistically plausible, not identified by these data | Consider only for a tightly bounded, adjudicated pilot |
| P3: retain routing, add protected flagged-review capacity | Triage unchanged | Flagged manual wait should fall; effect on unflagged queue depends on genuinely added capacity | Likely lower flagged cycle time with review and quality preserved | Lower decision risk than P2, provided protected review is equivalent and adequately supervised | May displace unflagged capacity or increase cost; measure both direct and spillover effects | Best first mechanism test, but still observationally unsupported | Recommended limited pilot with a hard capacity and quality guardrail |
| P4: retain review, change escalation criteria or capacity | Triage unchanged | Escalation occurrence and/or senior wait changes; manual queue may be unchanged | Could shorten cycles if escalation is better targeted or capacity expands; could lengthen them if thresholds widen | Threshold changes can cause missed severe cases or unnecessary senior review | Senior-team backlog and other escalations may be affected | Weak until escalation labels, thresholds, and capacity are audited | Test after baseline escalation quality and capacity are measured |

I would pilot P3 because it targets the most visible downstream bottleneck while preserving the current review decision and making the counterfactual operationally concrete.

- Eligibility: flagged cases that would normally enter manual review during selected service windows; exclude legally mandated immediate escalation and cases requiring a fixed emergency path.
- Assignment: randomize comparable shifts or queue blocks, stratified by complexity, score band, site, and expected load, to protected capacity or P0. Cluster assignment limits contamination from shared queues.
- Guardrail: reserve at most a prespecified fraction, such as 15–20%, of review capacity; suspend the intervention if occupancy, senior backlog, or unflagged service-level limits are exceeded.
- Primary outcome: flagged eligible arrival-to-close time, analyzed by intention to treat. Secondary outcomes: manual queue wait, escalation wait and occurrence, p95 waits for unflagged cases, rework, appeals, and independently adjudicated error/compliance loss.
- Minimum data: synchronized arrival, stage start/done and close timestamps; flag, score, complexity, load, queue position, capacity, resources, cluster, adherence, and adjudicated quality labels.
- Stop if adjudicated quality crosses a pre-registered harm margin, urgent-case compliance is breached, flagged p95 cycle worsens, or unflagged p95 wait exceeds its spillover limit.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. If adjudicated or newly measured hidden risk is substantially concentrated among flagged cases, and plausible risk adjustment removes the cycle-time contrast or shows that suppressing handling increases false negatives, the interpretation would reverse toward risk selection and continued protection rather than a queue-only mechanism.
2. If queue-level analysis shows that protected flagged capacity causes equal-or-larger delays for unflagged cases, or increases total system work and senior backlog, the favorable P3 recommendation would reverse toward a capacity-neutral or different queue policy.
3. If independent adjudication shows that P2 or flag suppression preserves or improves decision quality with a material cycle-time gain, while current mandatory review adds no safety benefit, the recommendation would reverse toward review exemption; the opposite quality finding would rule it out.
4. If timestamp audit shows that starts are documentation or batch-entry times, clocks are misaligned, or completion is recorded after administrative close, the apparent triage acceleration and downstream waiting decomposition would reverse from process evidence to a measurement artifact.
5. If there is no common support across flag, risk score, complexity, load, and queue state, or a supposed threshold has too few observations on one side, observational comparisons would not identify the stated policy effect and no causal recommendation should be based on them.
6. If reviewers change escalation, service time, or compliance behavior when the flag is hidden or when protected slots are introduced, the measured mediator pathways would not transport; a quality-preserving P3 interpretation could reverse to P1, P2, or P4 depending on the observed behavior.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed: flagged cases start triage much sooner than unflagged cases, with median arrival-to-triage-start of 12 versus 150 minutes. They also have somewhat shorter triage service, but all ten proceed to manual review, where their median wait is 405 minutes. Six reach escalation and wait a median 420 minutes. Median total cycle time is therefore 987 minutes for flagged cases versus 345 for unflagged cases. The data also show longer flagged cycles at high arrival load and much longer cycles when escalation occurs.

A plausible mechanism is a policy bundle: priority-lane handling accelerates triage, mandatory review increases demand in a shared queue, and selected cases then encounter a scarce senior queue. Complexity and unmeasured underlying risk can cause both flag assignment and longer work, while other cases' treatments create spillovers.

What remains unidentified is the flag's causal effect, any natural direct or indirect effect, and decision quality. Approve and Reject are not correctness labels, and the sample cannot separate risk, reviewer selection, resource assignment, queue state, and policy.

The most useful next evidence is a cluster-randomized P3 pilot with protected review capacity, synchronized timestamps, queue snapshots, resource assignments, spillover outcomes, and independent quality adjudication. It tests the downstream bottleneck while preserving the safety review and making the intervention measurable.
