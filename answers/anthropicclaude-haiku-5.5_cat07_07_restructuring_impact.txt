# 1. Mechanism map

**Key arithmetic check.** The management table does not show a nominal handover reduction. Summing the handover frequencies per 100 cases:

- Current: 92 + 88 + 36 + 31 + 42 + 39 + 9 + 6 = **343**
- Proposed: 92 + 83 + 41 + 36 + 49 + 43 + 12 + 3 = **359** (about +5%)

Only the cross-team handover falls (6  3). The local tech loop (tech  same caseworker) is not removed. It becomes a longer loop through the shared service, with 41 + 36 = 77 tech-related handovers per 100 cases versus 36 + 31 = 67 now. Legal/Risk referrals rise by a third (9  12). The approval handovers rise from 42 to 49. The restructuring therefore appears to *re-route* handovers rather than eliminate them. The management benefit depends on whether the 15% higher specialist utilization is achievable, and the table implies it is not: inbound tech demand rises about 14% (36  41 per 100 cases) while the shared service also absorbs legal/risk triage and difficult Quality Checks.

**Mechanism chain**

| Restructuring action | Expected mechanism | Observable process-mining effect | Direction and confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pool N_Tech, S_Tech, E_Tech into one service | Risk pooling lowers average wait for a given utilization, but only if total capacity is adequate | Lower mean tech waiting time; possibly higher p90 if utilization exceeds about 85% | Ambiguous: depends on capacity and arrival mix |
| Route all high-complexity cases to shared tech | Expertise concentrates; fewer inconsistent reviews | More handovers into tech, longer waits for complex cases, possibly lower rework on complex cases | Improves quality if queue is controlled; hurts tail latency if not |
| Route legal/risk/security to shared queue | Broader detection of legal/risk cases | More cases in the legal/risk queue (9  12 per 100); longer waits (9.6h  10.5h) on an already 91–93% utilized resource | Likely adverse for tail latency unless capacity is added |
| Shared approval queue, one local supervisor removed | Standardized decisions | Approval handovers rise (42  49); approval queue age becomes a new bottleneck signal; ownership becomes ambiguous in the log | Standardization is positive; accountability is at risk |
| Remove informal local tech advice | Fewer informal consultations | Fewer short tech  caseworker loops in the logs, but these loops were partly invisible informal advice, so the log may under-record the effect | Risk: unnecessary formal referrals rise and loss of early advice is hidden in the data |
| Shared queue for completed advice | Any team can pull advice | Cross-team handovers may fall, but advice reuse depends on case-note quality | Works only if notes are standardized; currently they are not |
| Concentrated work in a central queue | Specialists handle more cases with less context switching | Higher specialist utilization, but more reassignments and ping-pong patterns if notes are weak | Adverse if notes are inconsistent |

**Effects on the named dimensions**

- **Nominal handover reduction:** Not supported by the management table. Handover count rises about 5%. What may fall is the *cross-team* and *ad hoc* handovers, which are currently costly (18% rework, 8.9h wait). Measure handovers per case by type, not just total.
- **Central queue formation:** Pooling creates a single queue with a single arrival stream. Queue age and arrival/service ratio become the main health signals. Shared queues often hide cohort effects (for example, one team's bulk submission floods the queue).
- **Specialist utilization:** Current tech utilization is 71% (North), 68% (South), 82% (East). Pooled demand is about 14% higher under the proposed routing. With a shared pool, average utilization rises toward roughly 80–85%, where waiting times become sensitive to small changes in arrivals. East, already at 82%, is most exposed to saturation. The 15% utilization gain is only plausible if demand falls, which the table does not show.
- **Rework:** Rework may fall for complex cases if specialist review is consistent, but rises after handovers that lose context. Current rework after handover is 9% (tech), 14% (legal/risk), 18% (cross-team). Shared queues risk more rework if the case notes are poor, because the next actor cannot see the reasoning.
- **Local expertise loss:** North and South run stable local loops and local regional conventions. Removing them reduces tacit knowledge flow. This shows up indirectly as more referrals, longer waits, and higher rework for regionally specific cases, not as a direct metric.
- **Role clarity:** Shared approval and central review separate the "who decided" from "who executed." In the log, the case owner (`org:resource` at the last approval activity) may no longer match the team that delivers customer contact.
- **Resilience to absence:** Local teams had informal cover. Central pooling theoretically improves cover for tech absence, but in practice the cross-team cover currently works poorly because notes are inconsistent. Resilience gains depend on documentation quality.
- **Fairness across teams and segments:** East may benefit most, because pooling relieves its overloaded specialist. North and South may see small losses in service. Customers needing language or regional accommodation may face more handovers, because the shared service may not have local context. Fairness must be tested by segment, not by team average.

# 2. Scenario forecasts

Scenarios are for the first 6–12 months after full change. Numbers are directional, not point estimates; they should be recalibrated with pilot data.

**Optimistic scenario**
Pooling works because capacity is protected, legal/risk gets extra capacity, and a fast "advice desk" preserves early informal consultations.

- **Handover pattern:** Total handovers per case rise only 1–3%. Cross-team handovers fall toward 3 per 100. Tech routing is clearer, with fewer tech  caseworker loops per case.
- **Queue/waiting time:** Tech queue median wait falls to about 2–3h. p90 queue time stays below 1 working day. Legal/risk waits hold near 10h.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median falls modestly (for example, 6.8  6.3d in North). The p90 falls more than the median (for example, 16.4  14d) because the pool absorbs peaks. East improves most (p90 22.8  16–18d).
- **Rework and quality:** Rework falls for high-complexity cases; overall rework approximately flat or down 1–2 points. Quality Check failures on difficult cases decline.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Approval owners are recorded clearly; the shared approval queue has a named accountable approver per decision class.
- **Fairness:** Segment gaps narrow. Regional-accommodation cases have similar rework and wait to the rest.
- **Early warning signs:** None major. Monitor tech utilization staying below 85%.

**Central scenario (most likely on current evidence)**
Pooling reduces East's average waiting time but adds queue time for medium-complexity cases everywhere. Local expertise weakens gradually.

- **Handover pattern:** Total handovers per case up about 5% (343  ~359 per 100). Tech round-trips (tech  caseworker  tech) increase. Approval handovers rise about 17%.
- **Queue/waiting time:** Tech mean wait rises in North and South (for example, from ~3h to ~4–5h), and falls in East. Legal/risk p90 wait rises to 2–3 days.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median roughly stable overall; North/South p90 worsens by 1–3 days. East median improves. The distribution becomes more right-skewed, with more cases in the 20d+ band.
- **Rework and quality:** Overall rework roughly unchanged (±1 point), but after-handover rework for tech and legal/risk remains high. Rework on complex cases in North/South increases slightly because regional knowledge is lost.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** Approval decisions are consistent but ownership of outcomes is split: caseworker owns customer, shared approver owns decision, tech owns advice. Complaints about "nobody owns this" appear in free-text feedback.
- **Fairness implications:** East benefits relative to its prior state, creating a visible inter-team gap reversal. Segments needing accommodation see 10–20% more handovers per case.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue age p95 increasing week over week; reassignment_count per case rising; ping-pong rate rising; growing share of tech referrals without a preceding local assessment (a sign of over-referral to protect against queue delays, or of caseworkers bypassing the local tech).

**Adverse scenario**
The shared tech service becomes a hidden bottleneck. Legal/risk triage and difficult QC consume specialist time. Local advice disappears and cases ping-pong.

- **Handover pattern:** Handovers per case rise 10–15%. Tech  caseworker  tech loops become frequent. Cross-team transfers are replaced by informal workarounds not captured in the log (for example, emailing a colleague), so logged cross-team handovers look better while real coordination worsens.
- **Queue/waiting time:** Shared tech utilization exceeds 90%. Queue wait grows non-linearly: median 1–2 days, p90 over a week. Legal/risk queue becomes a dominant constraint, with waits of 2–3 weeks for some cases.
- **Cycle-time distribution:** Median may still look stable or improve (because simple cases are fast-tracked), but p90/p95 roughly doubles, and a long tail of stuck cases appears.
- **Rework and quality:** Rework rises across teams, especially after tech and approval handovers. Reopened cases increase. Quality Check rework loops grow because the central queue lacks regional context.
- **Role clarity and accountability:** No clear owner for stalled cases. Approvers re-open decisions because implementers lack context. Staff describe "throwing cases over the wall."
- **Fairness implications:** Accommodation and language-requiring cases, and high-value but medium-complexity cases, experience the largest delays. East improves on paper while North/South suffer, and the overall SLA miss rate rises.
- **Early warning signs:** Queue age growing faster than arrivals; legal/risk queue waiting time exceeding the SLA for more than 10% of cases; rising reassignment_count; increasing shares of cases with more than 6 handovers; staff attrition in local tech roles; growing "silent" cases with no activity for more than 5 working days.

# 4. Risks invisible in average cycle time

1. **Tail latency hidden by a stable mean.** Pooling can reduce average wait while lengthening the 90th–95th percentile for complex or legal cases. Averages mix the many simple cases that are fast-tracked with the few stuck ones. Monitor p90/p95 queue time and cycle time per complexity band and per specialty.

2. **High-complexity case degradation.** The restructuring moves complex cases into the most congested queue. These cases are a minority of volume, so they barely move the mean, but they carry most rework (18% after cross-team, 14% after legal/risk) and most of the customer harm. Track complexity-stratified SLA misses.

3. **Local knowledge loss.** Regional conventions reduce rework in complex cases. Losing local tech loops may raise rework slowly, below the noise floor of the monthly average. Track rework by team for complex cases, and handover notes quality.

4. **Fairness across teams and customer segments.** East may improve while North/South lose quality, and the overall average hides the trade-off. Accommodation and language-requiring customers may need more handovers, which lengthens their cycle time without necessarily changing the average. Report all metrics by team and segment, with confidence intervals.

5. **Learning and skill decay.** Junior caseworkers who no longer receive hard cases do not develop technical skill. The effect appears in future rework and escalation, years later, and in average cycle time only if the pool of qualified staff shrinks. Track first-pass autonomy by tenure cohort.

6. **Accountability and ownership ambiguity.** Shared approval can produce consistent decisions while making ownership unclear. Cycle time may even improve because nobody holds the case long enough to notice a stall. Track reassignment_count, the number of distinct resources per case, and "silent" open cases.

7. **Exception handling and informal workarounds.** Staff bypass the formal queue through email, phone, or side conversations. Process logs record the formal path, so handover counts and cross-team transfers look better while coordination cost is hidden. Compare case-note mentions and staff feedback with logged events.

8. **Capacity saturation before visible waiting.** Utilization near 85–90% produces small early changes in queue length that look like noise, then a sudden breakdown. Averages lag the saturation point. Monitor arrival/service ratio and utilization directly.

9. **Survivorship and censoring bias in early averages.** In the first weeks after go-live, cases still open are excluded from cycle-time calculations, so early averages look better than they will be. Queued backlog from the old regime is cleared first, creating a temporary improvement. Use cohort analysis by case start date, not by close date.

10. **Mix shift masquerading as improvement.** If the shared service receives simpler cases in early weeks (because complex ones are still routed locally during transition), averages improve for the wrong reason. Standardize by complexity and specialty.

11. **Resilience illusion.** Pooling appears to protect against absence, but if notes are poor, cross-team cover fails and cases stall. Average metrics in a normal month cannot detect this. Test resilience by analyzing absence periods explicitly.

# 5. Pilot design

**Phase 0: Baseline and instrumentation (weeks 8 to 0)**
- Standardize case-note fields at each handover (minimum: reason, advice requested, advice given, open risks).
- Log informal consultations where possible (tagged events).
- Fix the event-log schema: ensure `org:resource`, `team`, `required_specialty`, `case_complexity`, `customer_segment`, and `handover_reason` are populated for all events.
- Establish baseline distributions, not only means.

**Phase 1: Pilot scope (weeks 1–12)**
- **Pilot unit:** East, with tech pooling and the shared approval queue for medium-value cases, but with local triage retained. Also a limited pilot of the legal/risk shared queue, with added capacity (for example, one additional part-time reviewer) before the routing rule is applied.
- **Not in pilot:** Removal of the supervisor position; full routing of all high-complexity cases.
- **Why East first:** Highest tech utilization and largest expected benefit. This is also a risk: East's benefit may overstate the general effect, so the comparison group matters.

**Phase 2: Second wave (weeks 13–24), conditional on Phase 1 guardrails**
- Extend pooled tech to South, with a separate local advice desk in North to retain local loops, or vice versa. North and South should run on different designs to compare.
- Trial the shared approval queue for medium/high-value cases in one team.

**Phase 3: Decision point (week 25+)**
- Full rollout, modification, or stop, based on evidence (see Section 6).

**Comparison group**
- North (as the stable control for the tech pooling changes) and, where possible, a matched set of East cases from before the pilot.
- Use difference-in-differences: change in East minus change in North, adjusted for complexity mix, specialty, priority, and customer segment.
- Matching: propensity or stratified matching on complexity, priority, required_specialty, and customer segment.

**Pre/post observation window**
- **Pre:** At least 8 weeks (preferably 12) before go-live, to capture seasonality and at least two cycles of specialist rotation.
- **Pilot:** Minimum 12 weeks for flow metrics (queue, handovers, cycle time).
- **Outcome tail:** Additional 12–16 weeks for rework, complaints, reopen rate, and satisfaction, because these lag.
- Analyze by case start cohort to avoid censoring bias.

**Segmentation**
- **Team:** North, South, East.
- **Complexity:** Low / medium / high (`case_complexity`).
- **Specialty:** None / tech / legal / risk / security (`required_specialty`).
- **Customer segment:** Include segments with language or regional accommodation needs.
- **Priority:** Standard / high (for SLA misses).
- Report each segment's sample size; suppress or flag cells with fewer than about 30 cases.

**Guardrail metrics (thresholds are illustrative; set from baseline)**

| Guardrail | Threshold for concern | Action if breached |
|---|---|---|
| Legal/risk p90 queue wait | > 12h, or > 2 working days | Add capacity or pause routing |
| Tech queue age p90 | > 2 working days for 2 weeks | Pause routing of non-urgent cases |
| P95 cycle time, high complexity | Worse than baseline by > 15% | Review routing rules |
| Rework rate, any team/segment | > baseline + 2 points | Investigate by handover type |
| SLA miss rate, high priority | > baseline + 2 points | Escalate and review capacity |
| Reassignment count p90 | Rising > 20% vs baseline | Review ownership rules |
| Silent open cases | > 5% of open cases idle > 5 working days | Mandatory owner assignment |
| Tech utilization | > 85% sustained for 3 weeks | Add capacity or reduce referral scope |
| Customer satisfaction, any segment | Drop > 0.3 points | Segment review |
| Staff attrition or sick leave, tech/caseworkers | Above baseline | Staff review |

**Staff feedback collection**
- Fortnightly 5-item pulse survey (queue visibility, ownership clarity, advice availability, learning, workload).
- Structured retrospectives every four weeks, separately for each team and for specialists.
- Free-text channel with anonymity for concerns about fairness, skills, and workload.
- Specialist-side logs: count of triage requests not in scope, to measure hidden load.
- Interviews with a sample of caseworkers at baseline, week 6, and week 12, focusing on tacit knowledge and informal consultations.

**Rollback or adjustment triggers**
- **Rollback (pause routing for the affected stream):** Any guardrail breached for 3 consecutive weeks *and* not explained by a one-off event; or legal/risk p90 wait over SLA for two consecutive weeks; or a segment-specific rework increase of more than 3 points.
- **Adjust (keep routing, change rules):** Tech queue age rising while utilization stays under 85% (suggests routing rule issue, not capacity); rising over-referral share (add a quick advice desk); rising ping-pong rate (require notes at handover and single accountable owner).
- **Escalate to redesign:** Staff reporting loss of expertise without measurable change in outcomes after 12 weeks; or equity gap widening for accommodation segments.

# 6. Recommendation

**Recommendation: Modify and pilot first. Do not approve full rollout.**

Reasons:
- The management handover table does not support the claim of nominal handover reduction. Total handovers rise by about 5%, and tech demand rises by about 14% while the claimed utilization gain requires lower demand.
- Legal/risk capacity is already at 91–93%. Routing more cases there without added capacity creates a predictable queue.
- The strongest benefit is plausible only in East, while North and South are at risk of losing a well-functioning local loop and regional knowledge.
- Fairness and learning effects are real, but they are invisible in averages and only appear over months.

**Conditional elements that may proceed**
1. Standardize case-note fields at handovers, regardless of structure. This is low risk and a precondition for any shared model.
2. Pool tech in East as a pilot, with local triage retained and a fast advice desk.
3. Add legal/risk capacity before routing additional cases there.
4. Keep the local supervisor role in place until the shared approval queue shows that accountability and quality are maintained.
5. Retain a local tech advice channel in North and South until the pilot shows no regression in complex-case rework.

**Conditions for full rollout**
- Across pilot and control, p90/p95 cycle time does not worsen for any team or complexity band.
- Rework, reopen, and complaint rates do not worsen for any segment, including accommodation segments.
- Tech utilization stays below 85% with arrival/service ratio below 0.9.
- Legal/risk p90 wait is within SLA.
- Staff pulse data show no net loss in ownership clarity or learning.
- Over-referral and round-trip rates are stable or falling.

**What management should not infer from early averages**
- Do not infer benefit from the first 2–4 weeks. Early averages are distorted by backlog clearance, censoring of open cases, and mix shifts.
- Do not infer that stable average cycle time means stable service. Tail, complexity, and segment results can move in opposite directions.
- Do not infer that fewer logged cross-team handovers means better coordination. Informal workarounds may replace them.
- Do not infer that East's improvement generalizes to North and South. East is the most constrained team, so it has the most to gain from pooling.
- Do not infer that higher specialist utilization is a success. Utilization above about 85% usually means queue growth, not productivity.
- Do not infer absence resilience from normal months. Resilience can only be tested during absences.
- Do not infer that fewer tech referrals means fewer unnecessary referrals. It may mean caseworkers are avoiding the queue, which shifts risk into unrecorded decisions.

# Case-level metrics table

      .agg(
          team=("team", "first"),

# Ping-pong detection: A -> B -> A team or resource patterns
def ping_pong(frame):
    frame = frame.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"])
    out = {}
    for cid, g in frame.groupby("case:concept:name"):
        r = g["org:resource"].tolist()
        out[cid] = sum(
            1 for i in range(len(r) - 2)
            if r[i] == r[i + 2] and r[i] != r[i + 1]
        )
    return pd.Series(out, name="pingpong_count")

pp = ping_pong(df)
case_tbl = case_tbl.join(pp)
case_tbl["has_pingpong"] = case_tbl["pingpong_count"] > 0

# Leading indicator: weekly tech arrival/service ratio (requires referral and completion events)

# ---------- 8. Difference-in-differences: East vs North control ----------
def did_estimate(metric_col, treated_team="East", control_team="North"):
    g = case_tbl.groupby(["team", "cohort"])[metric_col].mean().unstack()
    treated_change = g.loc[treated_team, "post"] - g.loc[treated_team, "pre"]
    control_change = g.loc[control_team, "post"] - g.loc[control_team, "pre"]
    return treated_change - control_change

for m in ["cycle_days", "rework", "sla_miss", "reassignments"]:
    print(m, round(did_estimate(m), 3))
