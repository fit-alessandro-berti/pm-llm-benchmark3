# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

**Calculation convention.** All durations below are in minutes. The displayed timestamps are treated as exact minute marks. “Service” means the start-to-end interval under the stated model; if work can be preempted, it is more cautiously interpreted as elapsed service-interval time rather than pure touch time.

For case \(c\), let:

- \(a_c\): arrival;
- \(r_c\): fork release;
- \(s_{ci},e_{ci}\): start and end of branch \(i\in\{A,B,C\}\);
- \(f^s_c,f^e_c\): final activity start and end.

The decomposition is:

\[
U_c=r_c-a_c
\]

\[
Q_{ci}=s_{ci}-r_c,\qquad V_{ci}=e_{ci}-s_{ci}
\]

where \(Q\) is branch queue waiting and \(V\) is branch service. A branch’s release-to-completion offset is:

\[
H_{ci}=Q_{ci}+V_{ci}=e_{ci}-r_c
\]

Under the stated AND-join rule:

\[
J_c=\max_i(e_{ci}),\qquad
L_c=J_c-r_c=\max_i(H_{ci})
\]

where \(J_c\) is `join_ready` and \(L_c\) is elapsed fork-join stage time. The critical set is \(K_c=\{i:e_{ci}=J_c\}\). Ties should be retained as a set rather than broken arbitrarily.

Branch-specific synchronization waiting is:

\[
W_{ci}=J_c-e_{ci}=L_c-H_{ci}
\]

The remaining case-level components are:

\[
D_c=f^s_c-J_c,\qquad
G_c=f^e_c-f^s_c,\qquad
T_c=f^e_c-a_c
\]

where \(D_c\) is downstream queueing under the model assumptions, \(G_c\) is final service, and \(T_c\) is total flow time.

The non-double-counting case identity is therefore:

\[
\boxed{T_c=U_c+L_c+D_c+G_c
      =U_c+\max_i(Q_{ci}+V_{ci})+D_c+G_c}
\]

For any critical branch \(k\in K_c\), \(W_{ck}=0\), so equivalently \(L_c=Q_{ck}+V_{ck}\).

**Why synchronization waits cannot simply be summed.** For every branch,

\[
Q_{ci}+V_{ci}+W_{ci}=L_c.
\]

Thus, summing the complete branch timelines over three branches gives \(3L_c\), not \(L_c\). Moreover, synchronization waits overlap in calendar time.

For example, in F02:

- A: \(0+50+10=60\);
- B: \(20+40+0=60\);
- C: \(5+20+35=60\).

The actual fork-join elapsed time is 60 minutes, not 180. Also, A’s 10-minute synchronization wait lies inside C’s 35-minute wait: their sum is 45 branch-minutes, while the union of those two waiting intervals is only 35 elapsed minutes. Adding the 45 branch-minutes again to \(L_c\) would double count time already inside the fork-join stage.

| Branch | Service: mean [range] | Queue: mean; median [range] | \(H=Q+V\): mean / max | Critical frequency | Mean sync wait | \(L\) when critical: mean / max |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 50.00 [50, 50] | 0.00; 0 [0, 0] | 50.00 / 50 | 2/12, 16.7% | 11.00 | 50.00 / 50 |
| B | 37.50 [30, 45] | 19.17; 20 [5, 30] | 56.67 / 75 | 8/12, 66.7% | 4.33 | 65.00 / 75 |
| C | 20.17 [18, 30] | 10.42; 5 [5, 35] | 30.58 / 57 | 2/12, 16.7% | 30.42 | 56.00 / 57 |

The critical cases are:

- **A:** F01, F11;
- **B:** F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F12;
- **C:** F04, F08.

**Influence assessment**

1. **B is clearly the strongest controller of `join_ready`.**  
   Its mean service is actually lower than A’s, but its queue-plus-service offset is larger on average and has the highest tail: 75 minutes in F07 and F12. B is critical in two-thirds of cases and has the lowest mean synchronization wait, consistent with frequently being the branch for which the others wait.

2. **B also dominates the observed elapsed-time tail.**  
   The five longest fork-join stages—75, 75, 65, 65, and 65 minutes—are all B-critical. All five cases with total flow time of at least 120 minutes are also B-critical. This is descriptive evidence, not proof of causation.

3. **A’s large service time does not translate into equivalent standalone influence.**  
   A has the largest mean service time, 50 minutes, but is critical only twice. Nevertheless, A is latest or second-latest in every observed case and creates a persistent 50-minute release-to-join floor. It therefore becomes important when B or C is accelerated.

4. **C is usually noncritical, but its administrative queue can create tail episodes.**  
   C’s high mean synchronization wait, 30.42 minutes, reflects frequently completing early and waiting. However, C becomes critical through queue-plus-service in F04 and F08. It should not be dismissed merely because it is administrative or has low mean service.

There are no exact observed join ties. The smallest critical margins are five minutes in F01, F08, and F10; F04’s margin is seven minutes. A and C are tied in critical frequency, and C’s mean \(L\) on its two critical occurrences exceeds A’s, although both samples are very small. A defensible ranking is therefore **B first, A next for persistent/structural influence, and C as a second-tier but important tail-specific contributor**—not a statistically sharp A-versus-C ordering.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

**Counterfactual assumptions.** These are mechanical, ceteris paribus scenarios, not causal forecasts:

- For A, its start remains fixed, service falls from 50 to 40 minutes, and B/C completion times remain unchanged.
- For B, its queue is halved, its start moves earlier accordingly, service remains unchanged, and A/C completion times remain unchanged.
- Branch counterfactuals first compute the exact reduction in \(J_c\), denoted \(\Delta J_c\).
- The tabulated A/B **flow saving** additionally assumes that observed \(D_c\) remains unchanged and the final station can feasibly move F earlier by \(\Delta J_c\). Hence \(\Delta T_c=\Delta J_c\).
- If the original final start is instead held fixed, the branch improvement produces **zero total-flow saving**: \(D_c\) grows by \(\Delta J_c\). These are alternative propagation scenarios, not statistical confidence bounds.

| Case | A service 20%: \(\Delta J=\Delta T\) under full propagation | B queue 50%: \(\Delta J=\Delta T\) under full propagation | Final \(D\to0\): downstream-only \(\Delta T\) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| F01 | 5 | 0 | 8 |
| F02 | 0 | 10 | 15 |
| F03 | 0 | 10 | 15 |
| F04 | 0 | 0 | 18 |
| F05 | 0 | 10 | 10 |
| F06 | 0 | 15 | 15 |
| F07 | 0 | 15 | 15 |
| F08 | 0 | 0 | 15 |
| F09 | 0 | 12.5 | 15 |
| F10 | 0 | 5 | 20 |
| F11 | 10 | 0 | 20 |
| F12 | 0 | 15 | 10 |
| **Total case-minutes saved** | **15** | **92.5** | **176** |
| **Mean saving per case** | **1.25** | **7.71** | **14.67** |

**1. Reduce A service by 20%**

- **Direct benefit:** A completes 10 minutes earlier in every case, equivalent to 120 case-minutes of earlier A completions. Only 15 case-minutes reach `join_ready`.
- **F01:** The new A end is 08:50, but B still ends at 08:55. Thus \(J\) moves only from 09:00 to 08:55: a five-minute benefit, with B becoming critical.
- **F11:** The new A end is 11:20, exactly when B ends. \(J\) moves from 11:30 to 11:20, producing the full 10-minute benefit and an A/B tie.
- **Zero-benefit cases:** In the other ten cases, B or C remains critical and \(J\) does not change.
- **Flow-time result:** Under full propagation, total mean flow falls from 115.25 to 114.00 minutes. Under fixed final starts, realized total-flow savings are zero.
- **Secondary effects:** A capacity is released earlier, which could help work outside this sample. On noncritical cases, however, faster A merely completes earlier and waits longer for synchronization. B becomes the next constraint in F01, and downstream congestion can absorb the earlier join.

**2. Reduce B queue delay by 50%**

- **Direct benefit:** Eight cases have a positive `join_ready` saving: F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, and F12.
- **Zero-benefit cases:** F01 and F11 remain A-controlled; F04 and F08 remain C-controlled. In those cases, earlier B completion only increases B’s synchronization waiting.
- **Critical-branch caps:** A becomes critical in F05 and F10, limiting their savings to 10 and 5 minutes. A and B tie in F02 and F06. B remains critical in F03, F07, F09, and F12.
- **Completion versus case impact:** B’s own completion advances by 115 case-minutes in aggregate, but only 92.5 reach \(J\). Fifteen case-minutes occur on noncritical-B cases, and another 7.5 are capped when A takes over.
- **Flow-time result:** Under full propagation, mean total flow falls to 107.54 minutes. Under fixed final starts, it remains unchanged.
- **Secondary effects:** Achieving the queue reduction might require additional B capacity, different priorities, or both. A priority-only change could move waiting to other work rather than create capacity. Earlier B completions also release more cases earlier toward F.

A concrete masking example is F05. The B scenario moves \(J\) from 10:10 to 10:00. However, its assigned F1 resource is occupied by F03 until 10:20, and F05’s logged final start is 10:20. Therefore, the optimistic ten-minute flow saving requires a different feasible final slot or resource; earlier B completion alone does not establish it.

**3. Add final capacity or otherwise eliminate downstream waiting**

The scenario \(f^s_c=J_c\), with final service unchanged, gives \(f^e_c=J_c+20\). Because \(D_c\geq0\), this is an **upper-bound scenario for downstream-only improvement** with branch times fixed:

- total saving: 176 case-minutes;
- mean saving: 14.67 minutes;
- resulting mean total flow: 100.58 minutes;
- largest case-level savings: 20 minutes in F10 and F11.

It is **not** an estimate of what one additional resource would achieve. The logged resource intervals give mixed evidence:

- At \(J\), the assigned resource is still busy in F04, F05, F08, F09, F10, and F11.
- For F05, F08, F09, and F11, \(D\) exactly equals the remaining logged busy time.
- For F04 and F10, \(D\) exceeds that remaining busy time.
- The assigned resource is not busy at \(J\) in F01, F02, F03, F06, F07, and F12, despite positive \(D\).

Thus, some waits are consistent with final-resource contention, while others require an additional explanation such as dispatch policy, reserved capacity, unlogged work, or batching. Capacity-only improvements could have little or no benefit in the latter cases. Unlike branch acceleration, this redesign does not change \(J\) or branch synchronization waits; it changes only the conversion of join readiness into final completion.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**1. Pilot a B release-to-completion redesign, emphasizing queue reduction**

- **Design:** First diagnose why B starts late, then pilot the least speculative mechanism—such as a scheduling/priority change if idle capacity exists, or carefully sized additional capacity if B is genuinely saturated. The timestamps do not justify prescribing a particular number of B resources.
- **Throughput:** Throughput should improve only if B waiting is constraining system output. A priority change without spare capacity may merely reorder queues. The counterfactual supports a potential mean `join_ready` improvement of 7.71 minutes, not a guaranteed throughput increase.
- **Flow-time tail:** Explicitly target the combination of long B queues and long B service seen in F07 and F12, rather than optimizing mean service alone. Track maximum and upper-quantile \(H_B\), \(L\), and total flow.
- **Resource utilization:** Measure B busy/idle time, calendars, and work from other cases before adding capacity. Extra capacity may reduce B utilization and increase cost; higher utilization from tighter scheduling could worsen variability or starve other work.
- **New bottlenecks and synchronization:** A becomes critical or tied in several improved cases, while C remains critical in F04/F08-type cases. Accelerating noncritical B produces no case-level benefit and increases its synchronization wait.
- **Pilot first:** Test on high-\(Q_B\) cases and record \(Q_B,V_B,H_B\), critical-branch frequency, \(J\), \(D\), and feasible final starts. The pilot should include a final-station schedule check so that earlier B completion is not mistaken for realized flow-time savings.

**2. Redesign final dispatch/release policy, adding capacity only where contention is verified**

- **Design:** Separate resource-contention waiting from batching, planned release, and unexplained dispatch delay. Then pilot flexible F1/F2 assignment, revised dispatch rules, or targeted extra capacity during demonstrated contention periods.
- **Throughput:** If F is a true capacity constraint, better dispatch or added capacity can increase completions per hour. If positive \(D\) mainly reflects deliberate release policy or idle assigned resources, capacity additions may have little throughput effect.
- **Flow-time tail:** The \(D\to0\) upper bound suggests substantial potential—up to 20 minutes per case and 14.67 minutes on average—but it will not shorten B’s 75-minute fork-join stages. B-driven tail risk therefore remains.
- **Resource utilization:** Additional F capacity may reduce utilization and make capacity expensive. Conversely, batching can improve utilization while increasing flow time. Current utilization cannot be calculated reliably without resource calendars and unlogged-work data.
- **New bottlenecks and synchronization:** Once F waiting is reduced, B, A’s 50-minute offset, C’s occasional administrative queue, or upstream preparation can become more visible. This redesign leaves branch synchronization waits unchanged, but it can prevent branch improvements from being converted into larger \(D\).
- **Pilot first:** Log final enqueue/ready time, dispatch time, resource availability, queue discipline, and other work on F1/F2. Classify each minute of \(D\), simulate a feasible resource-constrained schedule, and measure realized \(D\), final utilization, total-flow tail, and whether A/B improvements propagate to `final_end`.

A standalone A-service compression is not the first redesign priority: its optimistic standalone mean saving is only 1.25 minutes. It becomes more attractive after B is improved. C’s queue should also remain a monitored, tail-specific contingency rather than being ignored.

for c in time_cols:
    df[c] = pd.to_datetime(
        "2026-05-19 " + df[c].astype(str),
        format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M"
    )

branches = ["A", "B", "C"]

def minutes(delta):
    """Convert a pandas timedelta Series to minutes."""
    return delta.dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

# Custom fork-join decomposition.

    df[f"{i}_queue"] = minutes(df[f"{i}_start"] - df["fork_release"])
    df[f"{i}_service"] = minutes(df[f"{i}_end"] - df[f"{i}_start"])
    df[f"{i}_offset"] = df[f"{i}_queue"] + df[f"{i}_service"]

# join_ready is not read from a log column: it is the max branch completion.
df["join_ready"] = df[[f"{i}_end" for i in branches]].max(axis=1)

# Retain every critical branch. Ties are displayed deterministically in A,B,C

def critical_set(row):
    return tuple(
        i for i in branches if row[f"{i}_end"] == row["join_ready"]
    )

df["critical_set"] = df.apply(critical_set, axis=1)
df["critical_branch"] = df["critical_set"].apply(lambda x: "&".join(x))

df["fork_join_elapsed"] = minutes(df["join_ready"] - df["fork_release"])
for i in branches:
    df[f"{i}_sync"] = minutes(df["join_ready"] - df[f"{i}_end"])

df["downstream_queue"] = minutes(df["final_start"] - df["join_ready"])
df["final_service"] = minutes(df["final_end"] - df["final_start"])
df["total_flow_time"] = minutes(df["final_end"] - df["arrival"])

# Validate both non-double-counting identities.
additive_total = (
    df["upstream_elapsed"]
    + df["fork_join_elapsed"]
    + df["downstream_queue"]
    + df["final_service"]
)
assert (additive_total - df["total_flow_time"]).abs().max() < 1e-9

for i in branches:
    branch_timeline = (
        df[f"{i}_queue"] + df[f"{i}_service"] + df[f"{i}_sync"]
    )
    assert (branch_timeline - df["fork_join_elapsed"]).abs().max() < 1e-9

# Compact case-level output.
case_cols = [
    "case", "upstream_elapsed",
    "A_queue", "A_service",
    "B_queue", "B_service",
    "C_queue", "C_service",
    "fork_join_elapsed", "join_ready", "critical_branch",
    "downstream_queue", "final_service", "total_flow_time"
]
case_out = df[case_cols].copy()
case_out["join_ready"] = case_out["join_ready"].dt.strftime("%H:%M")
case_out["sync_A/B/C"] = df.apply(
    lambda r: "/".join(str(int(r[f"{i}_sync"])) for i in branches),
    axis=1
)
print("CASE DECOMPOSITION (minutes except join_ready)")
print(case_out.round(2).to_string(index=False))

print("\nMEAN OBSERVED COMPONENTS")
print(
    df[[
        "upstream_elapsed", "fork_join_elapsed", "downstream_queue",
        "final_service", "total_flow_time"

)

# Branch comparison.
branch_rows = []
for i in branches:
    is_critical = df["critical_set"].apply(lambda s: i in s)
    critical_L = df.loc[is_critical, "fork_join_elapsed"]
    branch_rows.append({
        "branch": i,
        "service_mean": df[f"{i}_service"].mean(),
        "service_min": df[f"{i}_service"].min(),
        "service_max": df[f"{i}_service"].max(),
        "queue_mean": df[f"{i}_queue"].mean(),
        "queue_median": df[f"{i}_queue"].median(),
        "queue_min": df[f"{i}_queue"].min(),
        "queue_max": df[f"{i}_queue"].max(),
        "offset_mean": df[f"{i}_offset"].mean(),
        "offset_max": df[f"{i}_offset"].max(),
        "critical_n": int(is_critical.sum()),
        "critical_pct": 100 * is_critical.mean(),
        "sync_mean": df[f"{i}_sync"].mean(),

    })

branch_summary = pd.DataFrame(branch_rows)
print("\nBRANCH SUMMARY")
print(branch_summary.round(2).to_string(index=False))

# Counterfactual 1: A service -20%, A_start and B/C ends fixed.
A_end_new = (
    df["A_start"]
    + pd.to_timedelta(0.8 * df["A_service"], unit="m")
)
J_A_new = pd.concat(
    [A_end_new, df["B_end"], df["C_end"]], axis=1

save_A_join = minutes(df["join_ready"] - J_A_new)

# Counterfactual 2: B queue -50%, B service and A/C ends fixed.
# Fractional minutes are retained as arithmetic scenario values, not rounded

B_start_new = (
    df["fork_release"]
    + pd.to_timedelta(0.5 * df["B_queue"], unit="m")

# 7. Limits of inference

- **Causality is not identified.** “Critical” means arithmetically last to complete under the stated join rule. It does not prove that the branch caused congestion or that accelerating it will produce the calculated flow improvement. The counterfactuals are deterministic scenarios with strong ceteris paribus assumptions.
- **Branch capacity and utilization are unknown.** There are no A/B/C resource counts, calendars, resource identifiers, queue disciplines, or competing-work logs. Queue time could reflect capacity shortage, scheduling, batching, priority, or delayed release. Throughput and required capacity cannot be inferred from these 12 cases alone.
- **Start-to-end service need not be active work.** Preemption, pauses, rework, and internal waiting cannot be detected. If present, \(V_{ci}\) is elapsed service-interval time, and a 20% service compression may not be achievable simply by adding resources.
- **Final waiting is not proven to be a queue.** \(D_c\) is a residual under the model. It could include resource contention, planned release, batching, staffing rules, reserved capacity, or unlogged work. F1/F2 assignments and final intervals are insufficient to reconstruct the true queue or simulate an additional resource reliably.
- **The join and downstream definitions depend on the stated rule.** No explicit join event is logged. If F also depends on another hidden condition, or if the AND-join rule is incomplete, the calculated \(J_c\) and \(D_c\) would not have the same interpretation.
- **Upstream elapsed time cannot be decomposed further.** Arrival-to-fork-release may include active preparation, upstream queueing, and scheduling, but those components are not separately observed.
- **Generalization is limited.** These are 12 cases on one day, with no case-mix, seasonality, demand, or quality information. Critical percentages and observed maxima are descriptive, not stable statistical tail estimates.
- **Precision and secondary effects matter.** Source durations use minute-resolution timestamps; half-minute counterfactual results are arithmetic outputs, not extra measurement precision. Real interventions could change case ordering, service times, utilization, rework, and other cases’ waits. Fixed-final-start and full-propagation calculations illustrate masking and propagation, but neither is an identified causal forecast.
