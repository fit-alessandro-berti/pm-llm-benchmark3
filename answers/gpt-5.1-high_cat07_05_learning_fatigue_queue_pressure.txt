# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is best explained by a **combination** of learning, case-mix, queue pressure, fatigue, circadian patterns, batching behavior, and automation interaction, rather than any single mechanism. At the start of a shift, workers confront a backlog and complex carryover from the previous shift: complexity and high-priority share are highest in the 1–5 and 6–15 sequence bins, and Early and Night shifts show particularly high early complexity. Seniors are intentionally routed more difficult cases early. This produces **longer service times and somewhat elevated error/rework** at the beginning of shifts, but these early errors are not catastrophic because they are partly mitigated by experienced staff and targeted quality checks.

As the shift progresses into the 16–30 and 31–50 event range, several things happen together. Workers “warm up” and refine their routines; backlogs of complex carryover cases are cleared; complexity and high-priority share fall; and WIP falls to moderate levels. This produces the **best overall operating point**: service times fall to about 13–13 min and then ~13 min or slightly below, and error rates reach their minimum (around 2.5–2.7%) with moderate rework. At this stage, learning and stabilization are important, but they are **supported** by a friendlier environment: moderately loaded queues, fewer urgent exceptions, and a more regular mix of cases.

Later in the shift, especially after position 50, demand and routing patterns drive WIP and queue length back up. The 51–75 and 76+ bins show rising queues (62 and 88) and continued declines in service time (down to 10.8 min), but error and rework climb sharply (to 7.9% error and 13.7% rework). This regime reflects **queue pressure dominating learning**: workers speed up further in response to high WIP, which is reinforced by implicit or explicit pressure to “clear the queue before the end of the shift.” Juniors and mid-levels are especially vulnerable, with error rates under high WIP later in the shift far higher than early-shift error, even though cases are objectively less complex. Fatigue amplifies this effect: late-shift workers are more tired, making them more likely to cut corners or miss subtle errors when rushing, especially on high-complexity and high-priority cases.

Circadian and shift-structure effects overlay these dynamics. Night shifts handle a smaller but more complex set of urgent exceptions; they show slower service with moderate error, consistent with a combination of lower base alertness and challenging cases. The Early shift confronts the accumulated night backlog (high starting queue, high early complexity) but ends with relatively modest late-shift error, suggesting effective backlog management but also reliance on seniors and more regular daytime support. The Day shift faces the main demand peak, with queues growing from 38 to 75 and error in the last two hours peaking at 7.5% while service time is shortest (10.9 min). This pattern fits a story where **time-of-day demand and staffing levels** interact with learning and fatigue: even if individuals learn and then tire, the external demand pattern (high arrivals during the Day, backlog at Early, exceptions at Night) critically shapes what they see at each point of the shift.

Case-mix and selection biases are central throughout. High-complexity cases and high-priority work are disproportionately assigned early in shifts and to seniors, especially after weekends or nights. Juniors may receive simpler tasks or standardized document reviews, sometimes in batches, which inflates their apparent speed but may mask later rework. Because the early hours are both more complex and more heavily staffed by seniors, it would be wrong to interpret early higher service times or error as a sign of poor performance; they instead reflect **deliberate routing of difficult work**. Conversely, some late-shift work may be easier but processed under extreme WIP and fatigue, so elevated late errors cannot be read as pure inability or laziness.

Batching and workarounds further shape the pattern. Juniors perform sequential batched Document Review late in the Day shift, producing runs of very short service times for similar tasks. Quality Check is often deferred until the end of a shift, creating bursts of corrections in the last 45 minutes. These behaviors shorten apparent service time on individual events but can **push error detection downstream**, where it appears as rework or late corrections. In the aggregated data, this contributes to the rise in late-shift rework even as complexity falls, and it makes some workers and activities look artificially efficient in the moment while shifting quality costs either to future events or to other resources.

Automation through Bot_Risk adds one more layer. The bot performs Risk Classification instantly, but humans correct its outputs more often when queue length exceeds 70, suggesting that under extreme pressure, either more problematic cases are sent through the bot or humans feel compelled to intervene on suspicious bot decisions. Depending on how corrections and downstream rework are recorded, this can make certain resources look worse (because they clean up bot errors) or better (if they delegate risky decisions to the bot and do not correct enough). The result is a **three-way interaction** between automation, human vigilance, and queue pressure, which can amplify or dampen observed learning, fatigue, and selection effects.

Putting this together, service times fall over the shift because of **(a) genuine learning and process stabilization, (b) a shift in case-mix towards less complex and less urgent work, and (c) conscious and unconscious speeding-up under rising queue pressure and batching practices.** Error and rework fall initially as learning and easier work dominate, but they rise later when high WIP and fatigue induce a sharper speed–quality trade-off, especially for juniors and mid-levels working on batched tasks and complex cases that remain unresolved late in the shift. The observed performance is therefore the result of **interacting mechanisms**, not simply “workers get tired” or “workers learn,” and any analysis or intervention that ignores case-mix and queue pressure will misattribute responsibility and suggest misleading fixes.


# 3. Proposed analyses

Below is a set of statistical and process-mining analyses that, taken together, can disentangle learning, fatigue, circadian effects, queue pressure, case-mix, batching, automation interaction, and downstream rework.

**Data preparation (common to analyses)**
- Use `pm4py.format_dataframe` to ensure columns are correctly mapped:
  - `case_id="case:concept:name"`, `activity_key="concept:name"`, `timestamp_key="time:timestamp"`.
- Derive performance fields:
  - Compute per-event service time with `pm4py.get_service_time` (for start/end timestamps).
  - Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate` to obtain waiting time and throughput per case.
  - Use `pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe` to add hour-of-day, weekday/weekend, and time-since-shift-start features (in addition to `minutes_since_shift_start` and `sequence_position_within_shift`).
- Convert to an event log when needed for control-flow analysis:
  - `log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df_formatted)`.

Example preprocessing pseudocode:

```python
import pm4py

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)
df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(df)
df = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(df)

df["service_time"] = pm4py.get_service_time(df)
```

## Within-resource comparisons

- **Goal**: Separate personal learning/fatigue from case-mix and queue effects.
- Approach:
  - For each `org:resource`, analyze how `service_time`, `error_flag`, and `rework_flag` vary with `sequence_position_within_shift` and `minutes_since_shift_start`.
  - Control for `wip_at_start`, `queue_length_at_start`, `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, `shift_id`, and `resource_experience_months`.
  - Use fixed-effects or mixed-effects models (per-resource intercepts) in Python:
    - Linear model for log-service-time; logistic model for error/rework.
  - Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` to subset by resource or resource groups (Seniors, Mid-level, Juniors, Night).
- Expected outcome:
  - Early warm-up: negative coefficient for early sequence positions under low/medium WIP.
  - Late fatigue: positive coefficient for late sequence positions under fixed WIP and complexity, stronger for juniors and night staff.

## Within-shift position effects

- **Goal**: Quantify how performance changes across the shift, net of other covariates.
- Approach:
  - Create sequence bins (e.g., 1–5, 6–15, 16–30, 31–50, 51–75, 76+) using `sequence_position_within_shift`.
  - Compute mean service time, error, rework, complexity, and WIP by bin, stratified by resource group and shift.
  - Use `pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph` to visualize event counts and performance metrics across sequence bins and shifts.
  - Compare patterns **within the same WIP band** to isolate position effects from queue pressure.
- Expected outcome:
  - Confirm early warm-up (1–30), mid-shift plateau (31–50), and late deterioration (51+) while holding WIP and complexity roughly constant.

## WIP/queue stratification

- **Goal**: Quantify the speed–quality trade-off driven by queue pressure.
- Approach:
  - Define WIP bands (e.g., <30, 30–70, >70) and possibly finer bands (e.g., deciles).
  - For each band, compute service time, error, rework by complexity band, resource group, and shift.
  - Use `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` on subsets of the log for low vs high WIP to visualize which paths are most affected by queue pressure.
  - Use `pm4py.view_dotted_chart` colored by `wip_at_start` to see when high-WIP work tends to occur within shifts.
- Expected outcome:
  - Strong monotonic relationship: higher WIP → shorter service time but higher error/rework, particularly for complex cases and juniors/mid-levels.

## Complexity adjustment

- **Goal**: Avoid confounding early high complexity and late low complexity with learning/fatigue.
- Approach:
  - Use `case_complexity_score` to define low/medium/high complexity bands (e.g., quantiles).
  - Within each band, analyze service time and error over sequence position, WIP, and shift.
  - Use `pm4py.cluster_log` to group cases into clusters based on activity sequences and complexity scores, then inspect performance per cluster and resource.
- Expected outcome:
  - Even within fixed complexity bands, early warm-up and late queue-pressure/fatigue patterns should still be observable, but with reduced magnitude compared to unadjusted aggregates.

## Shift and time-of-day controls

- **Goal**: Distinguish circadian/time-of-day effects from shift-structure and case-mix.
- Approach:
  - Use `shift_id` and temporal features from `pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe` (hour, weekday) as covariates in models.
  - Use `pm4py.filter_time_range` to compare performance in the same clock-time window across different shifts (e.g., 10:00–12:00 Early vs Day).
  - Estimate models with shift fixed effects plus WIP, complexity, and experience; test whether shift/time-of-day coefficients remain significant.
- Expected outcome:
  - Residual shift effects after controls, especially poorer performance at biologically challenging times (very early or late hours), but smaller than raw differences.

## Batching detection

- **Goal**: Measure batching and its impact on performance and rework.
- Approach:
  - Apply `pm4py.discover_batches` with focus on `activity_family` = Document Review and Quality Check, and on specific resources (Junior_H, Junior_I).
  - Use `pm4py.view_dotted_chart` to visualize long sequences of the same activity/resource with short service times, especially late in Day and Late shifts.
  - Compare downstream `rework_flag` and error among cases that pass through batched segments vs those processed individually.
- Expected outcome:
  - Clear evidence of batching late in shifts with very short service times; batches associated with elevated downstream rework and corrections, especially when combined with high WIP.

## Resource-experience interactions

- **Goal**: Understand how experience moderates learning, fatigue, and queue pressure.
- Approach:
  - Categorize resources by `resource_experience_months` (e.g., Juniors, Mid-levels, Seniors, Night staff as given).
  - Fit interaction models where WIP, complexity, and sequence position effects differ by experience group.
  - Use `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` to examine each group separately.
- Expected outcome:
  - Seniors show strong speed-up with almost flat error under moderate WIP, and moderate error increase under high WIP.
  - Juniors show both more early-shift difficulty on complex cases and much higher late-shift error under high WIP, indicating combined learning and pressure vulnerability.

## Rework/error downstream analysis

- **Goal**: Track where errors are corrected, how rework propagates across activities/shifts, and which upstream conditions predict rework.
- Approach:
  - Use `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` to identify activities and paths with high rework.
  - Use `pm4py.filter_case_performance` to focus on cases with extreme total service time or many rework steps.
  - Use `pm4py.filter_paths_performance` and `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` to see which paths (e.g., involving Rework Correction or late Quality Check) have elevated performance problems.
  - Model rework probability (per case) as a function of upstream WIP, complexity, shift, batching, and automation involvement (Bot_Risk).
- Expected outcome:
  - Late Quality Check batches and high-WIP segments show disproportionate downstream rework.
  - Automation paths with insufficient human oversight under high WIP may show elevated rework after initial apparent speed gains.

Example path-level performance analysis:

```python
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(log)
pm4py.view_dfg(perf_dfg[0], perf_dfg[1])  # performance and frequency DFG

rework_stats = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
```


# 4. Intervention options

Below, each proposed intervention is evaluated in terms of expected benefit, risk, and monitoring using event-log indicators.

**1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Limits extreme fatigue, especially at the end of long shifts, which should reduce very late-shift error and rework when WIP is high.
  - Encourages better staffing and handover planning instead of “heroic” overtime that leads to error spikes.
- **Risks**:
  - If capacity is not adjusted, caps may increase backlog and WIP at shift boundaries, making the first bins of the next shift even more overloaded and complex.
  - More handovers between resources and shifts can introduce additional coordination errors.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Track error and rework rates in the last two hours of each shift pre- and post-intervention, stratified by WIP and complexity.
  - Monitor `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start` at each shift start and end; ensure caps are not just pushing pressure forward.
  - Observe handover counts (cases changing `org:resource` or `shift_id`) and their associated error/rework rates.

**2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Reduces early-shift cognitive load when workers are still warming up and may face residual backlog; may lower early-shift error on complex work.
  - Allows workers to start with moderate complexity to establish flow before tackling the most challenging tasks.
- **Risks**:
  - Deferring complex cases may concentrate them later in the shift, potentially under higher WIP and fatigue, creating a **worse** speed–quality trade-off.
  - Could undermine the current beneficial practice where seniors handle complex carryover early, when there is more time to correct errors within the shift.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Track `case_complexity_score` distribution by `minutes_since_shift_start` and shift, before and after the policy.
  - Monitor error and rework on high-complexity cases by time-since-shift-start and WIP band.
  - Ensure that the share of high-complexity work processed under high WIP (>70) does not increase.

**3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Creates visibility for supervisors and workers when the system enters the “risk zone” where error and rework rates are highest.
  - Enables real-time interventions (e.g., adding a senior reviewer, temporarily re-routing arrivals, pausing non-urgent work) to prevent quality collapse.
- **Risks**:
  - If interpreted as “you must go faster now,” alerts could perversely encourage more corner-cutting and exacerbate the speed–quality trade-off.
  - Workers may feel surveilled and stressed, especially if alerts are tied to punitive consequences.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Measure the frequency and duration of WIP>70 episodes, and how they change post-alert.
  - Compare service time, error, and rework distributions before vs after alerts, especially in the 30 minutes following an alert.
  - Track which interventions follow alerts (e.g., resource changes, activity mix changes) using event attributes and resource assignment patterns.

**4. Force random assignment of cases to reduce selection bias**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Makes case-mix more comparable across workers, improving fairness of performance comparisons and simplifying statistical analysis.
  - Reduces opportunities to cherry-pick easy or hard cases, potentially improving overall balance and learning opportunities.
- **Risks**:
  - Ignores specialization: seniors are currently used to handle complex cases early, and night staff handle urgent exceptions. Removing targeted routing could **reduce quality on complex or high-risk cases**.
  - May reduce throughput if complex cases are handed to less experienced workers who require more time and generate more rework.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Track the distribution of `case_complexity_score` and priority by `org:resource` before and after randomization.
  - Monitor error and rework on high-complexity and high-priority cases; ensure they do not deteriorate.
  - Evaluate worker-level metrics adjusted for complexity and WIP to verify that randomization genuinely reduces selection bias.

**5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in the final two hours**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Reduces cognitive fatigue and maintains attention in the period where queue pressure and error rates are high.
  - May help juniors and night staff maintain quality under pressure without dramatically increasing average service time.
- **Risks**:
  - If micro-breaks are not accompanied by capacity planning, they may increase WIP and queue length later in the shift, potentially offsetting quality gains.
  - Poorly timed breaks (e.g., during spikes) could create additional bottlenecks.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Compare error and rework in the final two hours before vs after introducing breaks, stratified by WIP and complexity.
  - Track WIP trajectories over the last three hours of shifts to ensure breaks do not systematically push WIP into the >70 zone.
  - Use dotted charts (`pm4py.view_dotted_chart`) to visualize break patterns and surrounding workload.

**6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Reduces end-of-shift workload on Quality Check, potentially smoothing the high-WIP, high-error period and lowering late rework bursts.
  - Allows workers to focus on high-priority and high-risk activities during peak demand, while still preserving quality checks (deferred, not removed).
- **Risks**:
  - Can create large backlog of Quality Check work for the next shift (e.g., Early or Night), increasing their WIP and early complexity.
  - Delaying quality checks may allow some defects to propagate further into downstream processes or customer interactions before detection.
  - If misunderstood, it may drift toward **removing** quality checks, which would be unsafe and is not recommended.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Track timing of Quality Check events relative to case closure, shift boundaries, and WIP levels.
  - Monitor queue and WIP for Quality Check at the start of shifts after implementation.
  - Compare rework rates and downstream corrections by whether Quality Check was same-shift vs deferred, ensuring that deferral does not increase severe or late rework.

**7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - Provides more experienced capacity exactly when queues and error rates peak (late Day shift), which should reduce both WIP and late-shift errors.
  - Seniors can absorb complex or exception cases, coach juniors, and handle problematic bot outputs during peak pressure.
- **Risks**:
  - Cost and scheduling complexity; if mis-timed, extra seniors might be underused.
  - Could inadvertently concentrate complex work even more on seniors, potentially increasing their fatigue if not accompanied by better routing and support.
- **Monitoring**:
  - Track Day-shift queue length and WIP trajectories around demand peaks (e.g., 14:00–18:00) before and after adding seniors.
  - Measure error and rework rates in those periods, stratified by resource group and complexity.
  - Check how the distribution of high-complexity and high-priority cases across resource groups changes with the additional senior capacity.

**8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates**
- **Expected benefit**:
  - In principle, might encourage attention to quality and discourage end-of-shift corner-cutting if metrics were perfectly risk-adjusted and fair.
- **Risks**:
  - In practice, raw late-shift error rates are **heavily confounded** by case-mix, WIP, batching, automation paths, and selection; penalizing on this basis is likely unfair and may target those cleaning up others’ mistakes or handling complex backlog.
  - Creates incentives to game the system (e.g., avoiding complex or risky cases near shift end, avoiding detection of errors, or manipulating logging) rather than genuinely improving quality.
  - May increase stress and queue pressure, further degrading performance and worker well-being.
- **Monitoring (if considered at all, preferably for coaching rather than punishment)**:
  - Use complexity- and WIP-adjusted metrics, never raw rates, and compare within similar case-mix strata.
  - Track changes in routing patterns and case-mix per worker to detect gaming or avoidance of difficult cases.
  - Monitor worker retention, sickness, and complaint indicators; if negative trends emerge, reconsider the policy.
- **Recommendation**:
  - Do **not** use punitive measures based solely on raw late-shift error rates. Instead, use risk-adjusted analytics to identify where support, training, and systemic process improvements are needed.


# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

First, **worker-level conclusions from this event log can easily be misleading**. Resources do not see the same work: seniors are routed complex and urgent cases, juniors handle more standardized tasks and batched document processing, night staff see urgent exceptions, and shift positions face different WIP and priority levels. A simple comparison of raw error rates or service times across workers or shifts ignores this selection and case-mix bias and risks labeling the people taking on the hardest work as “poor performers.” Any assessment should adjust for complexity, priority, WIP, automation involvement, and shift context, and should focus on systemic constraints as much as individual behavior.

Second, **measurement itself is imperfect**. The `error_flag` and `rework_flag` reflect detected and recorded issues, which can depend on how thoroughly different workers and shifts log and correct problems. Workers who diligently create Rework Correction events and trigger Quality Check may look worse than those whose errors are never recorded. Delayed Quality Check and batching can shift error detection to different times and resources, further decoupling observed errors from original causes. Instrumentation and logging practices must be reviewed to avoid systematic under- or over-reporting for particular groups.

Third, **queue pressure and scheduling decisions are largely management-created**, not solely worker-driven. High WIP (>70), heavy backlogs at shift boundaries, and peaks in demand during the Day shift reflect staffing, planning, and intake control decisions. When late-shift error rates rise under extreme WIP, this is often a sign that the system is being run close to or beyond capacity, not that specific workers are negligent. Blaming individuals for structural overload is ethically problematic and may discourage honest reporting of issues or rework.

Fourth, **automation quality and interaction with humans complicate attribution of responsibility**. Bot_Risk may misclassify risk, and humans may have to correct those errors under high pressure. If metrics attribute all downstream errors to the last human who touched the case, they may unfairly penalize the person cleaning up bot mistakes or handling the most complex bot outputs. Evaluations must explicitly consider automation paths, track where bot decisions enter the process, and measure both bot and human contributions to errors and rework.

Finally, **punitive use of error rates is particularly dangerous**. If workers expect to be punished for higher late-shift error rates or rework, they may avoid high-risk cases, hide errors, or resist process improvements that increase transparency (like better quality checks and detailed logging). Ethical and effective performance management should prioritize learning, coaching, system redesign, and workload balancing rather than punishment. Event-log analytics should therefore be framed as tools for **joint diagnosis and continuous improvement**, not for one-sided blame.


# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Design a process-mining dashboard that tracks the interacting mechanisms and supports both operational control and improvement. At minimum, it should include the following indicators (each with drill-down by shift, resource group, WIP band, complexity band, and activity family):

1. **Service time distribution**
   - Definition: Per-event service time (e.g., minutes between `start_timestamp` and `time:timestamp`) and per-case total service time.
   - Display: Histograms and time-series of mean/median service time by `sequence_position_within_shift`, `shift_id`, and resource group; performance DFG (`pm4py.discover_performance_dfg`) with mean service times on arcs.

2. **Waiting time and backlog**
   - Definition: Waiting time from case arrival to activity start (from `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`) and queue-related fields (`wip_at_start`, `queue_length_at_start`).
   - Display: Time-series of average waiting time and WIP, plus distribution of WIP by band (<30, 30–70, >70). Highlight periods when WIP>70 and associated downstream performance.

3. **Work-in-progress (WIP) and queue-pressure indicator**
   - Definition: Proportion of events executed under each WIP band; duration and frequency of WIP>70 episodes.
   - Display: Control chart showing WIP over time per shift; heatmap of error/rework rates by WIP band and complexity band.

4. **Error and rework indicators**
   - Definition: `error_flag` and `rework_flag` rates per activity family, shift, resource group, WIP band, and complexity band; counts of Rework Correction events.
   - Display: Stacked bar charts of error vs rework; path-based visualization using `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` and `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` to highlight high-rework arcs and activities.

5. **Complexity mix and priority mix**
   - Definition: Distribution of `case_complexity_score` (e.g., low/medium/high bands) and `priority` across time-of-day, shift, sequence position, and resource groups.
   - Display: Area charts or stacked bars showing complexity and priority share in the first hour, mid-shift, and last two hours per shift; metrics showing how much high-complexity work is processed under high WIP.

6. **Batching indicators**
   - Definition: Detection of batches (e.g., from `pm4py.discover_batches`) for key activities like Document Review and Quality Check; batch size and occurrence time.
   - Display: Dotted charts with color by batch ID and resource; table of batch statistics (average batch size, mean service time within batches, downstream rework for batched vs non-batched cases), highlighting late-shift batches.

7. **Handover and queue ownership changes**
   - Definition: Number of resource handovers per case, especially across shifts; cases that cross shift boundaries with open WIP or deferred Quality Check.
   - Display: Handover-of-work network showing transitions between resources/roles; metrics of error and rework vs number of handovers and cross-shift transitions. Highlight cases with many handovers or carryover backlog between shifts.

8. **Worker-load fairness and exposure**
   - Definition: For each resource, share of high-complexity and high-priority cases, average WIP level at case start, proportion of work done under WIP>70, and share of rework and bot-correction tasks.
   - Display: Fairness panel with per-resource indicators normalized by complexity and WIP (e.g., complexity-adjusted load index). This helps identify workers disproportionately exposed to difficult work or high queue pressure and supports equitable staffing and training decisions.

9. **Automation interaction metrics**
   - Definition: For cases involving Bot_Risk, measure proportion of bot-assisted cases, human override/correction rate, and downstream rework and error by WIP band and shift.
   - Display: Comparison charts of bot-involved vs non-bot paths; performance DFGs highlighting arcs that include Bot_Risk and subsequent human activities, with error/rework overlays.

10. **Shift health summary**
    - Definition: For each shift, key summary metrics: mean queue at start/end, share of high-complexity cases in the first hour, error and rework in the last two hours, and time spent in high-WIP states.
    - Display: Compact scoreboard per shift with trends over weeks, supporting evaluation of interventions (e.g., added seniors, micro-breaks, adjusted scheduling) and enabling proactive capacity management.

Together, these indicators allow managers and analysts to monitor service time, waiting time, WIP, rework, error rates, complexity mix, batching, handovers, automation behavior, and fairness of workload. Crucially, the dashboard should emphasize **risk-adjusted, context-aware metrics** rather than raw error rates, supporting ethical and effective continuous improvement rather than punitive oversight.
