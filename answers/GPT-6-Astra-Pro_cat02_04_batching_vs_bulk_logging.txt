# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

## Definitions and assumptions

For point-time diagnostics, define

$$
W_i=s_i-a_i,\qquad D_i=c_i-s_i,\qquad L_i=r_i-c_i,
$$

where \(a_i,s_i,c_i,r_i\) denote queue arrival, start, completion, and insertion.

**Assumption A1:** Cross-system timestamps are temporarily treated as comparable for descriptive arithmetic. This is not evidence that their clocks are synchronized.

**Assumption A2:** Recorded intervals are treated as half-open, \([s_i,c_i)\). Touching intervals are adjacent, not overlapping. Zero-duration records contribute no positive-duration overlap.

**Assumption A3:** Queue diagnostics concern only the supplied fragments. They omit any initial backlog, unobserved cases, other activities, and unavailable capacity.

Importantly, \(D_i\) is **recorded case elapsed time**, not automatically active labor. Neither \(\sum_iD_i\) nor the union of account-level intervals establishes utilization without trustworthy lifecycle semantics, resource identity, and an observation/calendar denominator.

## G1: likely operational batching, but distinguish item residence from machine effort

Recorded waits are **6, 5, 4, and 2 minutes**, averaging **4.25 minutes**. Each case has three minutes of recorded elapsed service.

Under the fixture-cycle interpretation, the cell is occupied for **three minutes**, not twelve. The twelve-minute sum represents item-minutes in the fixture. Dividing the cycle among four items gives 0.75 machine-minutes per item as an allocation convention, **not** an individual elapsed service time.

Ignoring batching could therefore overstate machine workload fourfold and incorrectly identify the cell as a capacity bottleneck. Conversely, interpreting the three minutes as four independently concurrent labeling operations could overstate physical parallelism.

Four recorded completions belong to one cycle; that does not establish a sustainable throughput rate. Resource-capacity conformance should recognize the fixture and its item links rather than treat four overlapping rows as four independent uses of a single-capacity machine.

## G2: common starts can move waiting into apparent service

Recorded mean waiting is **12.25 minutes**; mean elapsed service is **17.5 minutes**, with **70 case-minutes** inside a **27-minute** run.

Consider an explicitly hypothetical reconstruction: inspections occur serially in completion order, beginning at 10:00, with each subsequent inspection starting when its predecessor completes. Individual service times would then be **8, 6, 7, and 6 minutes**. Mean waiting would become **23 minutes**, while total active service would be 27 rather than 70 minutes.

Under that reconstruction, copied group starts **underestimate waiting and overestimate service**. They also create false resource concurrency and can shift bottleneck attribution from the queue to inspection processing.

However, a genuine common setup might make 10:00 a valid activity start for all cases. In that interpretation, the remedy is to distinguish shared setup, internal waiting, and individual inspection—not simply to declare the timestamps wrong.

Staggered completion counts remain more informative about output timing than the group starts. A discovered parallel-execution model would not be justified solely by the overlapping intervals.

## G3: common ends can turn post-work delay into service

Recorded waits average **19.5 minutes**. Recorded service durations are **28, 20, 13, and 6 minutes**, summing to **67 minutes** across a 28-minute envelope.

If starts are genuine and work is sequential, another admissible reconstruction has each review finish when the next starts, with the last finishing at 11:30. Service would then total **28 minutes**. The other 39 recorded case-minutes would represent waiting for folder completion/submission rather than active review.

Thus waiting **before first work** may remain approximately valid, while service, labor utilization, and completion-based cycle time are inflated. Physical output may have occurred progressively even though the log shows a burst at 11:30.

Alternatively, all cases may legitimately remain incomplete until a common approval. Then completion-based flow time can be valid while active labor remains overstated. Temporal conformance must distinguish review completion from shared release; the fragments do not establish which milestone the normative process requires.

## G4: reliable serial pattern, unidentified batching intent

Recorded waits are **7, 10, 13, 16, and 19 minutes**; each service interval lasts **four minutes**. The run occupies 20 minutes, without recorded overlap.

If the scanner captures actual starts and finishes, waiting and service have no obvious directional timing bias. Nevertheless, extrapolating the fully occupied 20-minute run to a whole shift would overestimate utilization because the sample was selected around a busy episode.

The increasing waits are compatible with five jobs progressing through one serial server. They do not establish chronic undercapacity, deliberate waiting to form a batch, or a batching policy. Throughput during this run is one item every four minutes; no broader sustainable rate follows.

Serial conformance is plausible under a serial norm, but neither such a norm nor batch-intent requirements are supplied.

## G5: person-level utilization is not identifiable

Recorded waiting averages **15.25 minutes**. The four intervals total **70 case-minutes** within a **30-minute** union.

All four are open from **13:12 to 13:20**. With at most three analysts, they cannot all represent continuous, dedicated, one-analyst-per-case service throughout that overlap. Some combination of paused/open sessions, multitasking, non-attended processing, timestamp error, or different effort semantics is required.

Treating `analyst_pool` as one worker overstates person-level workload and apparent concurrency. Treating all elapsed time as active work remains problematic even after assuming three workers: the apparently plausible aggregate ratio \(70/(3\times30)\) hides the four-way overlap.

The direction of waiting bias is unknown: a start might mean first actual attention, session opening, or assignment. Aggregate output counts can be retained, but individual productivity, capacity bottlenecks, and identity-based conformance cannot be assessed from the shared login.

## G6: inflated waiting and near-zero service can arise together

Recorded waiting ranges from **35 to 175 minutes**, with a **110-minute median**. Every service duration is zero.

Suppose actual work begins at \(u_i\), finishes at \(v_i\), and the form later writes both lifecycle fields as \(b=17{:}00\). Then

$$
W_i^{\text{logged}}=b-a_i
=(u_i-a_i)+(v_i-u_i)+(b-v_i),
\qquad
D_i^{\text{logged}}=b-b=0.
$$

The apparent waiting contains **true waiting, actual work, and post-work reporting delay**, while apparent service collapses to zero.

This can simultaneously produce an enormous apparent queue bottleneck, near-zero measured resource utilization, and a spurious completion burst. The one-minute `recorded_at − complete` difference does not expose the earlier delay because completion itself may have been overwritten.

The competing interpretation matters: if the measured activity is genuinely only the final digital closure, a very short closure duration and prolonged readiness-to-close delay may be legitimate. These records still do not measure earlier ticket-resolution work. No skipped control or noncompliance can be inferred from zero duration alone.

## G7: point-time queue and utilization estimates are invalid

Recorded waits are **2, −1, −4, −7, and −11 minutes**. Their negative values are diagnostic evidence against using the point timestamps as physical starts.

Five fifteen-minute intervals produce **75 case-minutes** inside a fifteen-minute union, suggesting five concurrent cases or 500% single-worker loading if interpreted naively. Neither conclusion is supported.

With floor-rounded starts and ceiling-rounded completions, service is generally overstated and waiting understated. With another rounding rule, even the direction of some errors changes. Completion heaping also creates artificial throughput bursts.

Do not clamp negative waits to zero: that hides the measurement problem and creates a falsely precise distribution. Apparent arrival-order violations and resource-concurrency violations should be treated as unresolved timing questions, not established process nonconformance.

## G8: distinguish service capacity from temporary unavailability

Recorded waits are **38, 43, 48, 54, and 58 minutes**, averaging **48.2 minutes**. Each triage lasts seven minutes; the serial run occupies 35 minutes.

Under the **additional hypotheses** that clocks align and the safety check makes T-H unavailable until 15:35, these waits decompose into:

$$
\text{residual unavailability after arrival}
+3\text{ minutes before the run}
+\text{waiting behind earlier triages}.
$$

The respective average components are **31.2, 3, and 14 minutes**. This is a conditional explanation, not a measured causal attribution.

Ignoring availability could misdiagnose slow triage when the issue is time during which triage cannot occur. Conversely, considering only the service run hides the preceding loss of repair capacity. Safety work may also be genuine resource work rather than idleness.

Output cadence during recovery is well represented conditionally on scanner semantics, but sustained throughput and safety-versus-triage conformance require aligned clocks and availability evidence.

# 4. PM4Py and pandas analysis

The implementation below retains all 38 rows in a master table. Missing or invalid intervals would be reported separately rather than silently imputed. Semantically suspect but structurally valid intervals—including G6’s zero-duration records and G7’s negative waits—remain in raw batch discovery.

PM4Py groups batch candidates by activity and resource; `merge_distance` is measured in seconds, and `min_batch_size` counts constituent events. The returned batch count is not the number of constituent cases. ([GitHub][1])

The start-timestamp property is set explicitly because the public `discover_batches` interface obtains it through log properties rather than a public `start_timestamp_key` argument. Otherwise, a completion-only fallback can invalidate interval-based interpretation. ([GitHub][2])

**Execution status:** The pandas diagnostics were executed and checked. PM4Py was unavailable in the execution environment, so the PM4Py labels following the code are expected results derived from its implementation, not claimed execution results. The script executes all four discovery configurations when PM4Py is installed.

def interval_stats(g: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    """Positive-duration pair overlap; half-open intervals [start, complete)."""
    positive = g.loc[g.elapsed_min.gt(0) & g.interval_eligible]
    spans = list(positive[['start', 'complete']]
                 .itertuples(index=False, name=None))
    pairs = list(combinations(spans, 2))
    overlaps = sum(
        max(a, c) < min(b, d) for (a, b), (c, d) in pairs)
    points = [(s, 1) for s, _ in spans] + [(e, -1) for _, e in spans]
    busy_s = 0.0
    peak = active = 0
    previous = None
    if points:
        changes = (pd.DataFrame(points, columns=['t', 'delta'])
                   .groupby('t').delta.sum())
        for t, delta in changes.items():
            if previous is not None and active > 0:
                busy_s += (t - previous).total_seconds()
            active += int(delta)  # Aggregate ties: adjacency is not overlap.
            peak = max(peak, active)
            previous = t
    return {
        'pair_overlap': overlaps / len(pairs) if pairs else float('nan'),
        'positive_n': len(spans), 'peak_open': peak,
        'union_min': busy_s / 60
    }


def queue_stats(g: pd.DataFrame) -> dict:
    """Signed A(t)-S(t), for fragments only; not a full-queue estimate."""
    events = ([(t, 1) for t in g.queue_arrival.dropna()]
              + [(t, -1) for t in g.start.dropna()])
    balance = pd.Series(dtype=float)
    if events:
        balance = (pd.DataFrame(events, columns=['t', 'delta'])
                   .groupby('t').delta.sum().sort_index().cumsum())
    return {
        'Q_max': max(0, balance.max()) if len(balance) else float('nan'),
        'Q_min': min(0, balance.min()) if len(balance) else float('nan'),
        'arrival_span_min': (
            g.queue_arrival.max() - g.queue_arrival.min()
        ).total_seconds() / 60
    }


def temporal_pattern(g: pd.DataFrame) -> str:
    v = g.loc[g.interval_eligible].sort_values(['start', 'complete', 'case'])
    if len(v) < len(g):
        return 'unresolved lifecycle'
    if len(v) < 2:
        return 'singleton'
    if v.start.nunique() == 1 and v.complete.nunique() == 1:
        return 'Sim'
    if v.start.nunique() == 1:
        return 'Start'
    if v.complete.nunique() == 1:
        return 'End'
    if all(v.complete.iloc[i] == v.start.iloc[i + 1]
           for i in range(len(v) - 1)):
        return 'Seq'
    return ('Overlap' if interval_stats(v)['pair_overlap'] > 0
            else 'Separated')

        ('insertion_inversions', inversions)

            flags.append(f'{name}={count}')

        'quarter_grid': seconds.mod(900).eq(0).mean(),

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

## Trusted identities versus shared accounts

With trusted worker identities, G4 and G8 strongly support serial execution for the identified worker. G2 and G3 instead demand an explanation for overlapping intervals: group boundaries, multitasking, passive time, or inaccurate lifecycle semantics. A worker cannot be assumed to give exclusive attention to every open case.

When identifiers may be shared, overlap can instead reflect several people. That weakens person-level utilization and resource-conformance conclusions, even if activity durations are otherwise accurate.

G5 is especially informative: shared identity is supplied as fact, but **sharing does not resolve the four-way-overlap versus three-analyst constraint**. Even allowing each endpoint to move by up to one minute leaves a common overlap from approximately 13:13 to 13:19. Dedicated continuous service remains incompatible with three analysts under that sensitivity assumption.

G1 should be treated separately: its identifier denotes an automated cell. A human shared-account explanation is less directly supported than ambiguity about fixture-level versus item-level timestamps.

## Exact timestamps versus interval censoring

Documented granularity is not an accuracy guarantee. Any finite uncertainty interval requires an additional assumption about rounding and clock error.

For a timestamp \(t\) and granularity \(\Delta\), possible rounding models include:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{floor rounding: }&t^*\in[t,t+\Delta),\\
\text{ceiling rounding: }&t^*\in(t-\Delta,t],\\
\text{nearest rounding: }&t^*\in[t-\Delta/2,t+\Delta/2].
\end{aligned}
$$

With an unknown choice among these rules, \([t-\Delta,t+\Delta]\) is a conservative **rounding-sensitivity envelope**, not a confidence interval for true execution time. Unknown clock offsets or retrospective entry can exceed it.

For G7, assume—only for a sensitivity construction—that starts are floored and completions are ceiling-rounded. Both of the following physical histories fit the supplied rounded values:

**Serial counterexample:** M01 executes 14:00–14:02, M02 14:02–14:04, M03 14:04–14:07, M04 14:07–14:11, and M05 14:11–14:15. Every case starts after arrival.

**Simultaneous counterexample:** all five execute 14:11–14:15, after the last arrival.

These are hypothetical reconstructions, not observations. They demonstrate that the same G7 timestamps can support incompatible physical models.

Second-level uncertainty is unlikely to overturn the broad three-, four-, and seven-minute durations in G1, G4, and G8, but it can remove exact adjacency or equality. Minute-level censoring cannot repair G6’s missing execution meaning. G8’s cross-system causal attribution additionally requires clock-offset evidence.

## Multiple explanations for the same pattern

The strongest observational equivalences are:

* **Equal starts and ends:** G1 suggests a fixture cycle, G6 a bulk transaction, and G7 rounding.
* **Equal starts with staggered ends:** G2 can describe parallel work, serial work after group opening, or shared setup followed by mixed execution.
* **Staggered starts with equal ends:** G3 can describe genuine synchronized release or deferred completion entry.
* **Adjacent intervals:** G4 and G8 can represent deliberate batches or ordinary serial backlog clearance.

Two further diagnostics prevent false certainty. First, every supplied lifecycle value falls on a whole-minute boundary, including the nominally one-second sources. This is an observed alignment/display feature, not proof that their underlying source resolution is one minute.

Second, G5 has **two reversals in `recorded_at` when sorted by `sequence_no`**. These may reflect reordered delivery between the source and the database, or inconsistent metadata. Source insertion order must not be promoted into physical execution order.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

**Priority 1 — Define and separate execution milestones.** Record actual work start, active-work completion, business-effective completion, user submission, and database insertion as distinct fields. Preserve original values and subsequent corrections with provenance. For G2, add individual inspection starts beside group start. For G3, separate review completion from folder approval/submission. For G6, distinguish earlier substantive work from the final closure transaction. Missing execution times should remain explicitly missing rather than defaulting to submission time.

**Priority 2 — Establish clock and precision provenance.** Store source-system and device identifiers, clock domain, synchronization status, estimated offset and uncertainty, timezone, and ingestion time. Record precision and rounding policy **per field**, including whether the value is floored, rounded, ceiling-adjusted, manually entered, or copied. Preserve raw timestamps beside any normalized version. Resolving G7’s rounding rule and G8’s safety-versus-repair clock offset has immediate analytical value.

**Priority 3 — Separate people, equipment, operational batches, and logging transactions.** Retain privacy-preserving badge/session identifiers alongside shared account names. Capture workstation/equipment identity independently from the operator. Use different identifiers for `operation_batch_id`, `fixture_cycle_id`, and `submission_transaction_id`; one must not stand in for another. Link a shared physical cycle to its constituent items rather than counting its duration repeatedly as independent machine work.

**Priority 4 — Observe capacity and interruptions.** Record workstation capacity, staffed analyst count, fixture occupancy, availability calendars, safety checks, breakdowns, setup, and capacity-restoration events. Add pause/resume events and distinguish “case open,” “worker attending,” and “machine running.” This directly addresses the G5 concurrency contradiction and the G8 availability hypothesis.

**Priority 5 — Validate with a targeted operational sample.** Compare a small set of directly observed executions with source records, concentrating on G2, G3, G5, G6, and G7. Check negative waits, zero durations, account-level overlaps exceeding known capacity, source-versus-database ordering reversals, and endpoint heaping. Publish metric-specific eligibility flags rather than applying a single “clean/dirty case” label.

# 7. Final adjudication

**High-confidence interpretations:** G4 and G8 exhibit serial temporal runs. G2 has group-start semantics. G5 has a confirmed shared-identity problem. G7 has confirmed coarsening. G6 strongly indicates transaction-level lifecycle recording, and G3 strongly suggests deferred or shared completion. G1 is the strongest candidate for a genuine operational batch, although simultaneous individual label application remains unverified.

**Queue-analysis treatment:** Exclude G6 from physical-work waiting/service estimates until the meaning of its closure timestamps is established. Exclude G7 from point-time queue estimates and retain it through explicit interval censoring where defensible. For G2, distinguish waiting before group opening from waiting for individual attention. For G3, retain pre-start waiting only conditionally and separate post-work submission delay. G5’s start-based waiting is provisional; person-level workload is not identifiable. G1, G4, and G8 permit more credible descriptive timing, subject to clock and lifecycle validation.

**Operational validation before performance attribution:** Validate G1’s fixture semantics, G2’s individual starts, G3’s completion milestone, G4’s batching intent, G5’s identities and pauses, G6’s execution milestones, G7’s rounding rule, and G8’s capacity/clock relationship. None of these fragments alone establishes a persistent bottleneck, sustainable throughput, or whole-shift utilization.

**The recommended decision is to retain every cluster but govern its use by metric and measurement semantics.** Do not deduplicate identical timestamps, treat PM4Py categories as physical facts, or launch a capacity intervention solely because a temporal batch pattern was detected.
