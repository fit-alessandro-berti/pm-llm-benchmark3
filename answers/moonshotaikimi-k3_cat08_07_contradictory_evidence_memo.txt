1. **Now:** restore billing-after-activation and permit checks as hard, conformance-monitored controls in all regions, pilot included. Pause survey skipping outside a randomized trial.
2. **Scale:** coordinator ownership — with backup pairing and a utilization cap — and merged validate/schedule, for simple low-risk houses only, via staged regional expansion.
3. **Measure:** 60-day full downstream cost (M4), randomized survey-skip trial by risk stratum (M1), rule validation (M6), conformance monitoring (M7), reason codes and start timestamps (M3, M2).
4. **Gate:** expand beyond simple houses only if, within each segment, first-time-right is non-inferior to baseline, complaints  baseline +1pp, p90  baseline +10%, full 60-day cost  baseline, and billing-before-activation events are zero.

Gate review in 10–12 weeks. The pilot produced a strong hypothesis about *where* R works; E tests it without making customers pay for our uncertainty.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness, low precision.** Fitness asks: can the model replay what we observed? Precision asks: does the model avoid allowing behavior we never observed? Pilot fitness 0.94 means the discovered model replays pilot cases well — many follow the simplified coordinator path. Precision 0.51 means the same model permits a large space of unobserved combinations (skip survey × manual activation × second visit × billing start, in various orders). This is not contradictory; it is the signature of a process that became *less standardized*. The log itself gained behavioral variance, so any model fitting it must be permissive. The alignment findings (billing before activation, installs without surveys, missing permits) confirm the variance is real, and the generalization drop (0.700.56) warns the model overfits the pilot log and predicts future behavior poorly. Practical consequence: the discovered pilot model is unusable as a conformance standard; monitoring must use declared rules (M7), not the discovered model.

**Improved median, worse tail.** The median is set by the typical case; the tail is set by exceptions. R accelerated the typical case (merged validate/schedule, fewer handovers, single owner) while making exceptions more frequent (failed visits 13%19%, second visits 11%18%) and slower (failed-visitsecond-visit delay grew in high-utilization regions; billingcomplaint became a new long-tail relation). The distribution shifted left in the bulk and stretched right at the tail. This is the classic signature of optimizing the happy path while degrading exception handling — and it is why "median improved" must never be read as "process improved."

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience.** Handovers are friction, but they are also redundancy: each transfer shares context and creates a natural backup. Removing them cut transfer losses (cases with 3+ handovers 34%12%) while centralizing the working-together network around coordinators and activation specialists. At 93% utilization there is no slack, and queueing delay grows non-linearly near capacity — visible in the worsened failedsecond-visit delay in high-utilization regions. When a coordinator is absent, cases stall because context lives in one head. The redesign removed what it classified as waste; some of that "waste" was resilience.

**Lower visible cost, higher downstream cost.** The €23 saving is real but measured in a truncated window: it excludes complaint handling after week 4, precisely where R's new costs land (complaints 6.1%9.8%, complaint escalation after billing start as a new edge at 8%). Utilization data show work was moved, not eliminated: survey team idled (78%52%) while the second-visit team saturated (66%92%). The estimated all-in add-on tripled (€14€46 per case), flipping the sign (€198€207) even before the missing weeks are counted. Complaint themes shifted from "waiting for appointment" to "installed but not working" and "billing before activation" — categories with longer-tail costs (disputes, churn, regulatory exposure) that may not appear in any 60-day window.

**Simple houses improve; apartments/old buildings deteriorate.** Heterogeneous treatment effects with clear mechanisms. For simple low-risk houses, surveys rarely change the outcome, so skipping them and merging intake tasks saves time at almost no quality cost (+3pp FTR). For medium-risk apartments, the predictive rule is often wrong, so skipped surveys surface as failed visits and second visits (installactivation worsened for apartments; +12 days p90). For old buildings, the missing step is permit checks (move-on-model: expected permit not observed), producing multi-week stalls (+25 days p90). For business customers, manual activation creates billing disputes. Aggregation hides all of this — and because the pilot oversampled the harmed segments, the *aggregate* worsening likely overstates the national average effect while the *within-segment* harm remains genuine and would recur nationally in absolute volume.

## 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale R nationally.**
- *Advantages:* fastest capture of simple-house gains; visible cost and median improvements are real; no dual-process complexity.
- *Risks:* nationalizes billing-before-activation (compliance-like exposure), a skip rule proven wrong for medium-risk addresses, and missing permit checks; tail, rework, and complaints worsen at scale; coordinator model is already at 93% utilization; relies on confounded, cost-truncated evidence.
- *Reasonable if:* M4 showed net savings including downstream cost, compliance cleared billing-before-activation, and segment harms were shown to be pure case-mix artifacts. None of these holds today.

**B. Roll back to baseline.**
- *Advantages:* immediately stops tail/rework growth; restores permit checks and activation-before-billing; returns to a better-controlled process (precision 0.79).
- *Risks:* forfeits the largest segment's demonstrated gains (6 days, +3pp FTR, 1pp complaints, €23 visible saving); wastes the pilot's learning; baseline itself had 13% SLA breach and a 31-day p90.
- *Reasonable if:* M4 shows downstream costs overwhelm savings even for simple houses, or billing-before-activation is ruled non-compliant *and* cannot be decoupled from the bundle, or attribute completion reveals the segment harms are actually universal.

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation.**
- *Advantages:* keeps the most plausible driver of median/handover gains; removes the two most damaging elements; simpler to operate than E.
- *Risks:* attributes the bundle's gains to one component without evidence (confounding); ignores the 93% utilization and absence-fragility problem; forgoes the merged validate/schedule gains if those, not ownership, drove the simple-house improvement; generates no new evidence.
- *Reasonable if:* speed of decision matters more than learning, compliance demands an immediate stop to post-billing activation, and ownership is paired with resilience fixes (backup pairing, shared case context, utilization cap).

**D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement.**
- *Advantages:* maximizes learning; randomization/stratification resolves the case-mix confound; M1–M8 embed naturally.
- *Risks:* while we experiment, known harms continue — it is hard to justify deliberately billing customers before activation or skipping permits for six more weeks; delays capture of the proven simple-house gains; costs management attention.
- *Reasonable if:* restricted to the genuinely uncertain elements (survey skipping by stratum, ownership model). It is not a defensible posture for elements already shown harmful; those should be paused, not studied further.

**E. Hybrid: deploy R for simple low-risk houses; controls for the rest; measure before wider scale.**
- *Advantages:* captures the demonstrated win now; stops harm where it is concentrated; converts the bundle into testable components; reversible; matches the actual structure of the evidence (heterogeneous effects).
- *Risks:* two process variants to run and govern; eligibility misclassification risk (the intake risk rule is itself unvalidated — mitigate with conservative eligibility until M6 lands); coordinator fragility must be fixed, not inherited; requires discipline to act on pre-agreed thresholds.
- *Reasonable if:* intake segment/risk attributes can be made complete, conformance monitoring is stood up first, and leadership commits to the gate criteria. All achievable.

## 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Alternative E — hybrid targeted deployment with restored controls and a gated measurement program.**

- **Immediate action (weeks 0–2):** Reinstate "billing only after successful activation" and permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings as hard controls in *all* regions, pilot included; stand up conformance monitoring (M7) against these two rules; open a compliance review of billing-before-activation (A5). Begin staged expansion of merged validate/schedule plus coordinator ownership — with backup pairing, shared case context, and an 85% utilization cap — for simple low-risk houses.
- **Pause:** The survey-skip rule for all addresses outside the M1 randomized trial; post-billing activation everywhere; any national scale-up beyond the simple-house segment.
- **Scale:** Coordinator ownership (with the resilience fixes above) and merged intake for simple low-risk houses, expanding region-by-stage rather than big-bang, given the four-region, case-mix-skewed evidence base.
- **Measure next:** M7 (now), M4 (60-day full cost), M1 and M6 (skip rule, causally and observationally), M3 and M2 (reason codes, timestamps), M5 (ownership resilience), M8 (CX split). Complete segment/risk attribute capture at intake as a precondition for eligibility-based routing.
- **Thresholds for broader rollout (pre-registered, gate review ~week 12):** expand to a new segment only if, *within that segment*: first-time-right  baseline  2pp; complaints  baseline + 1pp; p90  baseline + 10%; full 60-day cost per case  baseline; zero billing-before-activation events; permit-check conformance  99% where required; coordinator utilization  85% with a passed absence drill. **Fallbacks:** if M4 shows full cost worse even for simple houses, revert to C; if conformance violations persist after controls are restored, escalate to compliance and freeze all expansion; if M1/M6 show the skip rule unsalvageable, retire it permanently and keep the ownership and merged-intake components.
