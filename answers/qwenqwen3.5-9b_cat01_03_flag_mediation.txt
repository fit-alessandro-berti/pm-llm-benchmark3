**Framework:** We adopt the *Potential Outcomes Framework* (Rubin) extended for mediation (Imai et al.). We distinguish Natural Direct Effects (NDE) where the path `Flag -> Triage -> Cycle` is held constant, and Natural Indirect Effects (NIE) where `Flag -> Routing -> Cycle` varies. However, because `risk_score` is a proxy for `underlying_risk` (unobserved), we treat `risk_score` as a confounder requiring adjustment.

Exposure-induced confounding arises because `flag` is determined by `risk_score`, which correlates with `complexity` and `arrival_load`. These same factors determine `manual_review` and `escalation`. Thus, `flag` is collinear with the mediators. Interference occurs because `manual_queue_wait` is shared; one flagged case's delay increases another's wait time. Timestamp semantics matter: `triage_start` is local clock; if cases span midnight, `residual-to-close` must handle day rollover correctly. Positivity is violated if no unflagged high-complexity cases exist (R04 is the only unflagged non-escalated high complexity case).

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

## 8. Reversal conditions

1.  **Hidden Risk:** If `risk_score` systematically underestimates risk for flagged cases (e.g., algorithm bias), the "delay" is actually necessary due to higher actual risk.
2.  **Queue Interference:** If removing flagged cases from the queue reduces total wait time for unflagged cases by >20%, the mechanism is purely interference (priority lane creates congestion elsewhere).
3.  **Decision Quality:** If flagged cases have a significantly higher error rate (e.g., false approvals) than unflagged cases, the delay is a necessary cost of accuracy, and P3 (Protected Capacity) is essential.
4.  **Timestamp Semantics:** If local clocks are not synchronized across triage and manual review stages, calculated wait times are artifacts rather than real delays.
5.  **Positivity/Lack of Overlap:** If no unflagged cases exist with `High` complexity, we cannot disentangle complexity effects from flag effects (selection bias).
6.  **Reviewer Behavior Change:** If reviewers adapt to the flag by becoming more lenient or stricter (behavioral response), the observed cycle time changes reflect policy-induced behavior rather than process mechanics.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed is that flagged cases have faster triage times (median 13m vs 141m) but longer total cycle times (median 13h vs 8h). This confirms the prompt's premise: the flag accelerates intake but delays closure.

A plausible mechanism is the policy bundle: the flag forces mandatory manual review and escalation, which are sequential bottlenecks shared with the general queue. Unflagged cases often bypass manual review if judged low risk. Thus, the flag acts as a gateway to a longer, higher-quality path.

What remains unidentified is the causal contribution of `underlying_risk` vs `flag` itself. Since `risk_score` is a proxy, we cannot know if the delay is due to the flag or the inherent complexity of the cases it selects. We also cannot quantify interference effects without more granular queue data.

The most useful next evidence comes from a randomized encouragement pilot (P3). By decoupling the flag from the routing decision in a controlled sample, we can isolate the direct effect of the flag on triage speed from the indirect effect on manual review duration. This distinguishes whether the flag is a useful signal or a bureaucratic artifact.
