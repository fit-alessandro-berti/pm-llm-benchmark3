## 1. Decision rule

Use a **gated decision rule: eligibility and evidence first, then urgency and robust expected benefit, then portfolio-level spillover**. Breach probability is neither a sufficient intervention threshold nor an estimate of treatment effect.

### Eligibility and admission gates

A case should receive a swarm only if:

1. **The intervention can still matter:** it is before `Diagnosis Complete`, there is a specific unresolved diagnostic question, and the team can use the result before diagnosis is completed.
2. **A realistic completion path exists:** there is enough time for triage, the 45-minute swarm, and any necessary downstream work. A swarm is not a substitute for missing resolution or validation capacity.
3. **There is actionable evidence:** either the original opening evidence has been reviewed, or there has been a material event or evidence change—such as a new diagnostic result, a usable customer log, or independently verified queue information.
   - A model refresh alone is not new evidence.
   - An absolute probability change of at least 0.05 should trigger human review, but does not by itself justify treatment.
   - An increase caused by imputation requires verification, even if it is large.
4. **Repeat-treatment rules are satisfied:** no second swarm without a material new event and renewed stage/readiness checks.
5. **The case is worth intervention under uncertainty:** preferably,
   \[
   EB_{\text{robust}}
   = e_{\text{low}}V-(€450+B)>0,
   \]
   where \(e_{\text{low}}\) is a conservative credible effect estimate and \(B\) is the stated false-alarm burden.
   - A negative conservative net benefit normally means “prepare and monitor,” not “never eligible.”
   - Serious contractual, regulatory, safety, or customer obligations can override a narrow financial rule, but should trigger explicit escalation and additional resources—not an unjustified swarm.

### Probability adjustments

These adjustments concern the **baseline breach-risk assessment**, not the causal effect of a swarm:

- **SME:** add approximately 0.04–0.09, subject to validation.
- **Strategic Enterprise:** subtract approximately 0.05–0.12, reflecting the post-redesign overprediction.
- **Public Sector:** no deterministic segment adjustment; allow additional uncertainty because sample size is moderate.
- **Non-Native-Language Support:** do not invent a directional correction. Retain the estimate provisionally, widen uncertainty, and require review of interpretation, translated evidence, and communication quality.
- **I-06:** treat its current prediction as provisional until the missing queue feature is checked. Do not mechanically add the Public Sector correction to a possibly artifactual jump.

### Prioritization

Within eligible cases, use tiers rather than a probability-only ranking:

- **Tier A:** verified, ready, time-sensitive, and positive under a conservative effect case.
- **Tier B:** credible next candidates, or cases requiring a bounded evidence/readiness check.
- **Tier C:** active ordinary diagnosis and scheduled re-evaluation, without immediate swarm allocation.
- **Tier D:** presently ineligible or unsuitable for a swarm.

Compare cases using:

- remaining feasible time and deadline severity;
- lower and midpoint treatment-effect estimates;
- case value and false-alarm burden;
- evidence quality and probability of a genuinely modifiable cause;
- probability of actually completing diagnosis before the intervention window closes;
- **marginal harm to the untreated diagnosis queue**.

Portfolio expected harm should include the value-weighted probability increases on the actual six exposed cases. A case with attractive standalone economics should not consume a slot if its credible marginal queue harm exceeds its expected benefit. Mandatory obligations may instead be addressed with supplementary capacity.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy

### Alert lifecycle and timing rules

- **Separate score refreshes from intervention alerts.** Recompute risk internally every 30 minutes, but do not generate a new swarm request merely because the model refreshed.
- **Minimum evidence change:** require a source-linked diagnostic event, usable new evidence, or independently verified process information. A probability movement of at least 0.05 can trigger review; it is not a causal explanation.
- **Pending-request cool-down:** if a material alert receives no swarm, suppress identical automatic requests for **two hours**, while maintaining ordinary diagnosis and deadline escalation.
- **Post-swarm cool-down:** impose at least **four hours before another swarm**, and require a material new event even after that interval. Recheck stage eligibility every time.
- **Urgent escalation:** any verified, stage-eligible case with three hours or less to deadline must receive human review within **15 minutes**, regardless of alert history or value. Suppression must never suppress urgent case-owner attention.
- **Re-entry triggers:** a new diagnostic result, translated/clarified evidence, verified queue recovery, a newly actionable diagnostic question, or release of additional capacity. Time pressure can trigger review, but does not by itself prove a useful treatment effect.
- **Failure feedback:** a swarm that finds no useful diagnostic change should trigger ordinary-care review and root-cause analysis—not automatic repetition with the same case.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks

### Value-based prioritization can starve lower-value cases

A high breach probability plus high financial value can repeatedly attract senior capacity, while lower-value cases receive only routine diagnosis.

**Safeguards:**

- Use the robust-benefit rule **and** a service floor: all verified, stage-eligible cases within three hours of deadline receive review within 15 minutes.
- Record cases that would have met effect/economic tests but were displaced by value, runway, or queue burden. Review their actual breach outcomes and customer harm.
- Do not impose a minimum monetary value for eligibility.
- Provide an incident-command escalation route for low-value but high-obligation cases.
- Review lower-value/high-burden cases automatically when a slot becomes available, rather than leaving their disposition to whichever manager notices them first.

### Non-Native-Language Support faces compound uncertainty

Sparse calibration and uncertain treatment effects can become a self-reinforcing reason for non-selection.

**Safeguards:**

- Provide bilingual case review and evidence-quality validation before assessment, not after a case has waited through several scoring cycles.
- Record whether a language-support flag affected evidence completeness, treatment selection, completion time, or customer validation.
- Do not impose arbitrary risk uplifts or downgrades. Use case-specific evidence and wider uncertainty.
- Measure selection and effect heterogeneity separately for these cases; a group-level prior should be updated when sufficient evidence becomes available.
- Language support itself should be funded separately where possible, so support needs do not automatically consume the scarce senior diagnostic slot.

### Differential calibration can create inconsistent standards

A common probability threshold would systematically underreact to SME underprediction and overreact to Strategic overprediction.

**Safeguards:**

- Maintain segment-specific calibration monitoring and versioned probability adjustments.
- Use adjusted risk for urgency and baseline assessment, but never convert the adjusted probability into a treatment effect.
- Require credible intervals or uncertainty bands around small-sample segment corrections.
- Prevent an uncertain adjustment for one segment from becoming an unexamined permanent offset.

### Selective intervention changes future training labels

Swarms are assigned to unusual, valuable, or unusually modifiable cases. A model trained naively on post-intervention outcomes can learn that those cases would have breached anyway, even though the swarm helped.

**Safeguards:**

- Record pre-intervention predictions, alert state, eligibility, and offer for every case—not only treated cases.
- Retain actual deadline outcomes for selected and unselected cases.
- Do not treat “not selected” as evidence of zero treatment effect.
- Record specialist, timing, reason for selection, and downstream resolution constraints.
- Fit and evaluate prediction models against an explicit pre-intervention snapshot, with workflow-version and policy-version indicators.

### Spillover may fall systematically on untreated groups

The six untreated cases may systematically lack access to senior attention and carry the congestion caused by better-resourced cases.

**Safeguards:**

- Identify the actual exposed six before allocating each wave, including segment and language-support context.
- Track delay, breach, and customer burden for exposed cases, not just risk increases.
- Require explicit portfolio-level harm reporting and incident-manager notification when material delay occurs.
- Trigger mitigation—additional ordinary diagnostic capacity, rescheduling, or pausing marginal swarms—when credible spillover harm exceeds the selected case’s incremental benefit.
- Never use “untreated” as synonymous with “unimportant.”

A practical fairness trigger is an adjusted treatment-rate ratio below 0.80 for a group over a rolling four-week window, or more than 10% of cases in a language-support group being excluded solely for uncertainty. Such a trigger requires independent review; it should not be “fixed” by automatically excluding that group.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan

### Operational monitoring — continuous, reviewed daily

Track for every alert and swarm:

- stage at alert, diagnosis-ready status, and time to deadline;
- time to human review and swarm start;
- actual start/finish time, specialist assigned, and reason for selection;
- whether `Diagnosis Complete` occurred before treatment;
- whether the diagnostic question was resolved or materially changed;
- post-diagnosis resolution and customer-validation blockers;
- repeated alerts, duplicate requests, and repeat-treatment violations;
- false-alarm burden, complaints, and other non-monetary harm;
- per-slot aggregate delay to identified untreated cases.

Proposed service expectations are 100% stage/eligibility documentation, at least 90% of accepted cases started within 15 minutes, and a recorded reason for every suppressed high-priority alert. Two stage violations or repeated unsupervised treatments should prompt an immediate workflow review.

### Predictive monitoring — weekly dashboard, monthly formal review

Measure by segment and language-support context:

- calibration bias, Brier score, log loss, and calibration error;
- false-positive and false-negative rates at the actual operating decisions;
- missing-field rates, imputation prevalence, and distribution drift;
- changes associated with workflow versions and the live queue-feature failure;
- alert volume, persistence, and duplicate-request rates.

Freeze each evaluated prediction at its pre-intervention prefix. Recalibrate when, for example, a segment shows approximately 0.05 absolute bias with a credible interval excluding zero over two successive reviews. Use cross-validated recalibration and hierarchical pooling where samples are small. Do not respond to every small-sample fluctuation.

### Causal-effect estimation — prospective learning plan

The supplied ranges should be treated as **uncertain priors or planning ranges**, not causal confidence intervals.

- When more verified cases are ready than available slots, randomize allocation within the same urgency/readiness tier.
- Preserve mandatory interventions and ordinary care; the learning comparison is between timely swarm and no additional swarm, not treatment versus neglect.
- Record first offer, acceptance, actual start time, specialist, and reason for non-treatment.
- Estimate intention-to-treat effects of an offer and, with appropriate assumptions, effects of actual treatment.
- Measure effects on diagnosis completion, time to resolution, deadline breach, customer validation, and untreated-queue outcomes.
- Estimate heterogeneity by diagnostic event type, time available, baseline slack, specialist, and segment.
- Use randomized allocation as the primary design. Matched-queue comparisons or difference-in-differences can supplement it, but only with explicit attention to common shocks and pre-trend validity.

Update case-level effect estimates only when the evidence is adequate. A credible reduction of roughly 0.05 or more in the conservative effect, or a change in which cases clear the robust-benefit threshold, should trigger portfolio reallocation.

### Capacity and externality monitoring — daily during the intervention window

Measure:

- available versus used slots and reserved-slot idle time;
- time to treatment and number of verified cases waiting;
- senior specialists’ ordinary-work displacement;
- actual, not merely assumed, spillover delay;
- risk and value-weighted harm to the untreated diagnosis cohort.

If a marginal slot’s credible queue harm exceeds its direct expected benefit, defer that slot. If sustained verified demand exceeds capacity, add diagnostic capacity or restrict treatment to stronger-effect cases rather than forcing already-selected cases out of a rank-order queue.

### Policy-change rules

Change the allocation rule—not merely the raw probability cutoff—when:

- stage eligibility or repeat-treatment controls fail;
- the full portfolio’s credible net impact becomes non-positive;
- calibration drift materially changes urgency tiers;
- causal-effect estimates cross the robust-benefit boundary;
- spillover materially exceeds the stated base case;
- fairness safeguards identify repeated exclusion of an evidenced group;
- downstream capacity repeatedly makes diagnosis-focused intervention ineffective.

Log every rule or threshold change with its evidence, owner, effective date, affected cases, and review date. Use a short rolling operational review and a fuller periodic policy review, so the portfolio learns from outcomes without chasing noise or optimizing yesterday’s alert rate.
