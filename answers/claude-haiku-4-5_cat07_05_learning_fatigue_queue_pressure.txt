# 2. Integrated explanation

The data reveals a **multi-mechanism interplay** in which service time and error rates are not driven by a single factor but by competing dynamics that shift dominance across the work period:

## Early Shift (Sequences 1–30): Selection-Driven Complexity Dominance

The earliest work period shows:
- **High mean complexity (0.72)**, inherited from night shift backlog and weekend carryover.
- **High queue at start (46)**, but **low error rate (3.4%)**.
- **Longest service times (18.8 min)**, reflecting genuine case difficulty, not inefficiency.
- **Strongest assignment to senior resources**, who apply expertise to complex cases.

**Mechanism**: Early-shift performance is determined by *case complexity and resource allocation*, not fatigue or learning deficits. Seniors are scheduled early specifically to clear high-complexity backlog. The queue is large in absolute terms, but *relative to case difficulty*, it is managed well. Learning warm-up does occur (18.8  13.6 min from seq 1–5 to 16–30), but it is small compared to the 4.4 min reduction in service time from case complexity normalization (0.72  0.54 complexity; 22.4 min on high-complexity under low WIP, vs. 12.7 min on low-complexity under low WIP).

## Mid-Shift (Sequences 31–50): Stable Learning and Case Normalization

- **Lower complexity (0.50)**, as high-complexity backlog is cleared.
- **Lower queue (31)**, but WIP remains manageable.
- **Lowest service time (12.9 min)** paired with **lowest error rate (2.7%)**.
- **Smallest rework rate (5.0%)**.

**Mechanism**: This is a "Goldilocks" zone. Warm-up is complete, but fatigue has not yet accumulated. Queue pressure is present but not extreme. Case complexity is normalized. This zone represents peak *sustainable* efficiency.

## Late-Shift Transition (Sequences 51–75): Queue Pressure Emerges

- **Service time drops further (11.4 min)**, but **error rate jumps to 4.6%** and **rework spikes to 8.2%**.
- **Queue length nearly *doubles* (from 31 to 62)**, marking the inflection point.
- **Complexity remains low (0.46)**, ruling out case-mix as a driver.

**Mechanism**: Service-time reduction occurs *because* workers are accelerating throughput in response to queue growth. This is **rational queue management under pressure**, not learning. The error-rate jump indicates a **speed-quality tradeoff**. Complexity is not the confound; the mechanism is explicit: workers prioritize throughput to clear the queue, sacrificing quality margins.

*Why do errors not rise earlier if fatigue were the primary driver?* Because sequence 1–30, which includes the same clock times as sequence 51–75 in a single shift, does not show elevated error. The error rise is *conditional on WIP/queue*, not on clock time alone.

## Late Late-Shift (Sequences 76+): Sustained High WIP and Degraded Quality

- **Fastest service time (10.8 min)**, representing aggressive speed.
- **Highest error rate (7.9%)** and **rework rate (13.7%)**.
- **Highest queue (88)**.
- **Lowest complexity (0.43)**, so quality degradation is not justified by case difficulty.

**Mechanism**: This represents exhaustion of the queue-management margin. Workers are moving as fast as they can, but WIP remains high, suggesting demand exceeds sustainable capacity. The combination of very short service time + high error rate on simple cases is a **signature of insufficient time per case**: workers are skipping steps, not thinking deeply, or being interrupted by queue dynamics.

## Shift-Level Patterns Confirm WIP-Driven Error Dynamics

- **Early shift**: Queue starts high (61) but **ends low (42)**, error in last 2 hours is **4.2%**—sustainable closure of backlog.
- **Day shift**: Queue starts moderate (38) but **ends high (75)**, error in last 2 hours is **7.5%**—demand accumulates, queue pressure escalates.
- **Late shift**: Inherits backlog (queue 70), **ends nearly unchanged (66)**, error **6.8%**—dealing with sustained pressure, not recovery.

Error rates in last 2 hours follow queue trajectory, not time-of-day: early shift (queue cleared) has lowest error; day shift (queue grows) has highest; late shift (queue sustained) is intermediate.

## Resource Experience Amplifies Queue-Pressure Effects

- **Seniors**: Service time 15.9  11.8 min (learning), error 2.1% (early low WIP)  2.2% (later low WIP) ** 4.9% (later high WIP)**. Stable baseline, degradation only under extreme pressure.
- **Mid-level**: Service time 16.8  12.1 min, error 2.8%  2.9% (low WIP)  6.8% (high WIP). Faster learning drop, steeper pressure sensitivity.
- **Juniors**: Service time 19.6  13.7 min, error 5.1% (early)  4.4% (later low WIP)  **12.4% (later high WIP)**. Highest baseline error, catastrophic failure under pressure.

**Interpretation**: Juniors benefit from early low-WIP work (error drops to 4.4%), showing they *can* learn and perform. But their error control is fragile under WIP > 70 (12.4%), suggesting limited mental/procedural reserves when speed is demanded. Seniors maintain error discipline even under extreme WIP, implying they have learned or internalized error-avoidance strategies that survive queue pressure.

## Batching and Automation as Symptomatic, Not Causal

- Batching (consecutive short-service-time events by juniors late in shift) correlates with high WIP and late-shift timing, not the reverse. **Batching is a rational worker response** to clear simple-case backlogs when complex work is scarce or when being pulled in multiple directions.
- Bot_Risk instant classification avoids bottleneck, but increases downstream Quality Check load. Corrections spike when queue > 70, not because bot is unreliable, but because **human oversight is deferred** under pressure, then rebounds. This is a **workaround**, not a primary mechanism.

## Circadian Effects Are Present But Secondary

Night shift shows 5.1% error (not dramatically high) and 15.2 min service time (slowest, but only 40% slower than day-shift average 10.9 min, and night shift has 56% high-complexity cases). This suggests **circadian troughs are real** but are **dominated by case-mix effects** (night shift has genuinely more urgent, exception-driven work). Day shift's 7.5% error in last 2 hours (15:00–17:00, classical post-lunch dip) is consistent with circadian effects, but **early shift at identical clock times (12:00–14:00) shows only 4.2% error**, proving that **shift timing and queue trajectory override clock-time effects**.

# 3. Proposed analyses

## 3.1 Within-Resource Comparisons

**Objective**: Isolate learning, fatigue, and queue-pressure effects for each resource by comparing performance on identical case types and complexities under varied WIP and sequence-position conditions.

**PM4Py-oriented approach**:

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd

# Load and format event log
log_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    raw_df, 
    case_id='case:concept:name',
    activity_key='concept:name',
    timestamp_key='time:timestamp'
)

# For each senior resource (Senior_A, Senior_B, Senior_C):
for resource in ['Senior_A', 'Senior_B', 'Senior_C']:
    resource_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        log_df, 'org:resource', [resource]
    )
    
    # Isolate low-complexity cases (< 0.5 complexity score)
    simple_cases = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        resource_log, 'case_complexity_score', 
        lambda x: x < 0.5
    )
    
    # Stratify by WIP bin and sequence position
    for wip_bin in ['<30', '30-70', '>70']:
        if wip_bin == '<30':
            subset = simple_cases[simple_cases['wip_at_start'] < 30]
        elif wip_bin == '30-70':
            subset = simple_cases[(simple_cases['wip_at_start'] >= 30) & 
                                   (simple_cases['wip_at_start'] <= 70)]
        else:
            subset = simple_cases[simple_cases['wip_at_start'] > 70]
        
        # For each sequence-position bin within this WIP stratum
        for seq_bin in ['1-20', '21-40', '41-60', '61+']:
            if seq_bin == '1-20':
                seq_subset = subset[subset['sequence_position_within_shift'] <= 20]
            # ... similar for other bins
            
            # Compute service time and error rate
            service_times = seq_subset.groupby('case:concept:name')['minutes_since_shift_start'].apply(
                lambda x: x.max() - x.min()
            )
            error_rate = seq_subset['error_flag'].mean()
            
            print(f"{resource}, WIP {wip_bin}, Seq {seq_bin}: "
                  f"Service time {service_times.mean():.1f} min, "
                  f"Error rate {error_rate:.1%}")
```

**Expected output**: A matrix showing service time and error rate for each resource × WIP bin × sequence-position combination, on identical complexity level. This isolates learning (should be visible in first 20 events, then plateau) from WIP-driven effects (should be visible across all sequence positions when WIP > 70).

## 3.2 Within-Shift Position Effects (Controlling for Complexity and WIP)

**Objective**: Decompose sequence-position effects into learning, fatigue, and queue-pressure components using regression.

```python
# Create enriched feature set
log_df['log_service_time'] = np.log(log_df['service_time'] + 0.1)
log_df['seq_phase'] = pd.cut(
    log_df['sequence_position_within_shift'],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, 1000],
    labels=['1-5', '6-15', '16-30', '31-50', '51-75', '76+']
)

# Fit mixed-effects model
from statsmodels.formula.api import mixedlm
import statsmodels.api as sm

# Service time model
model_st = mixedlm(
    'log_service_time ~ seq_phase + case_complexity_score + wip_at_start + '
    'queue_length_at_start + resource_experience_months + priority',
    data=log_df,
    groups=log_df['org:resource'],
    re_formula='~seq_phase'  # Allow slope for seq_phase to vary by resource
)
result_st = model_st.fit()
print(result_st.summary())

# Error rate model (logistic)
model_err = mixedlm(
    'error_flag ~ seq_phase + case_complexity_score + wip_at_start + '
    'queue_length_at_start + resource_experience_months + priority',
    data=log_df,
    groups=log_df['org:resource'],
    re_formula='~seq_phase',
    family=sm.families.Binomial()
)
result_err = model_err.fit()
print(result_err.summary())
```

**Interpretation**: 
- Coefficient on `seq_phase` (holding complexity and WIP constant) isolates learning/fatigue.
- Coefficient on `wip_at_start` isolates queue pressure.
- Interaction `seq_phase × wip_at_start` tests whether late-shift errors are purely queue-driven.
- Random slope on `seq_phase` by resource tests whether juniors show different learning curves than seniors.

## 3.3 WIP and Queue Stratification with Complexity Adjustment

**Objective**: Test whether the service-time/error-rate relationship with WIP is confounded by complexity or resource selection.

```python
# Create WIP deciles to avoid arbitrary binning
log_df['wip_decile'] = pd.qcut(log_df['wip_at_start'], q=10, labels=False)

# Stratify analysis
for complexity_band in ['low', 'mid', 'high']:
    if complexity_band == 'low':
        c_subset = log_df[log_df['case_complexity_score'] < 0.33]
    elif complexity_band == 'mid':
        c_subset = log_df[(log_df['case_complexity_score'] >= 0.33) & 
                          (log_df['case_complexity_score'] < 0.67)]
    else:
        c_subset = log_df[log_df['case_complexity_score'] >= 0.67]
    
    # Plot service time and error rate across WIP deciles
    wip_performance = c_subset.groupby('wip_decile').agg({
        'service_time': ['mean', 'std'],
        'error_flag': 'mean',
        'rework_flag': 'mean',
        'wip_at_start': 'mean'
    }).reset_index()
    
    print(f"\n{complexity_band.upper()} COMPLEXITY:")
    print(wip_performance)
    
    # Fit polynomial trend (service time may decrease then plateau)
    from scipy.stats import linregress
    
    # Linear model
    slope_st, intercept_st, r_value_st, p_value_st, std_err_st = linregress(
        c_subset['wip_at_start'], c_subset['service_time']
    )
    
    slope_err, intercept_err, r_value_err, p_value_err, std_err_err = linregress(
        c_subset['wip_at_start'], c_subset['error_flag']
    )
    
    print(f"Service time vs. WIP: slope {slope_st:.4f} (p={p_value_st:.3f}), "
          f"R² {r_value_st**2:.3f}")
    print(f"Error rate vs. WIP: slope {slope_err:.4f} (p={p_value_err:.3f}), "
          f"R² {r_value_err**2:.3f}")
```

**Expected output**: Three tables (low, mid, high complexity), each showing WIP decile vs. service time and error rate. If WIP is the true driver, all three should show inverse service-time trend and positive error trend, independent of complexity.

## 3.4 Shift and Time-of-Day Controls

**Objective**: Test whether errors rise late in shift due to fatigue, circadian effects, or queue dynamics.

```python
# Extract time-of-day features
log_df = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(log_df)

# Add hour-of-day (clock time)
log_df['hour_of_day'] = pd.to_datetime(log_df['time:timestamp']).dt.hour

# Create within-shift time buckets
log_df['shift_hour'] = (
    pd.to_datetime(log_df['time:timestamp']) - 
    pd.to_datetime(log_df['shift_start_time'])

log_df['shift_phase'] = pd.cut(
    log_df['shift_hour'],
    bins=[0, 2, 4, 6, 8],
    labels=['0-2h', '2-4h', '4-6h', '6-8h']
)

# Compare performance at same clock time across different shifts
for hour in range(6, 22):
    performance_by_shift = log_df[log_df['hour_of_day'] == hour].groupby('shift_id').agg({
        'service_time': 'mean',
        'error_flag': 'mean',
        'wip_at_start': 'mean',
        'case_complexity_score': 'mean',
        'org:resource': 'count'  # Event count
    }).reset_index()
    
    print(f"\nClock hour {hour}:00–{hour+1}:00")
    print(performance_by_shift)

# Compare same shift phase (hours into shift) across shifts
for phase in ['0-2h', '2-4h', '4-6h', '6-8h']:
    perf_by_shift_phase = log_df[log_df['shift_phase'] == phase].groupby('shift_id').agg({
        'service_time': 'mean',
        'error_flag': 'mean',
        'wip_at_start': 'mean',
        'case_complexity_score': 'mean',
    })
    
    print(f"\nShift phase {phase}:")
    print(perf_by_shift_phase)

# Test interaction of shift phase and WIP
model_shift = mixedlm(
    'error_flag ~ shift_phase + wip_at_start + shift_phase:wip_at_start + '
    'case_complexity_score + resource_experience_months',
    data=log_df,
    groups=log_df['org:resource']
)
result_shift = model_shift.fit()
print("\nShift phase + WIP interaction model:")
print(result_shift.summary())
```

**Interpretation**:
- If errors are high at 15:00 regardless of shift, it is circadian.
- If errors are high in shift hour 4–6 regardless of shift, it is fatigue or learned response.
- If errors correlate with WIP at a given clock time across shifts, WIP is the driver.

## 3.5 Batching Detection and Characterization

**Objective**: Quantify batching, identify when it occurs, and test whether it is a cause or symptom of performance issues.

```python
# Use PM4Py's batch discovery
from pm4py.algo.discovery.batching import algorithm as batching_discovery

batches = batching_discovery.discover_batches(
    log_df,
    case_id_key='case:concept:name',
    activity_key='concept:name',
    resource_key='org:resource',
    timestamp_key='time:timestamp'
)

print(f"Number of batches discovered: {len(batches)}")

# Analyze batch characteristics
batch_stats = []
for batch in batches:
    batch_size = len(batch)
    resource = batch.iloc[0]['org:resource']
    activity = batch.iloc[0]['concept:name']
    mean_service_time = batch['service_time'].mean()
    error_rate = batch['error_flag'].mean()
    mean_wip = batch['wip_at_start'].mean()
    mean_complexity = batch['case_complexity_score'].mean()
    
    batch_stats.append({
        'resource': resource,
        'activity': activity,
        'batch_size': batch_size,
        'mean_service_time': mean_service_time,
        'error_rate': error_rate,
        'mean_wip': mean_wip,
        'mean_complexity': mean_complexity
    })

batch_df = pd.DataFrame(batch_stats)

# Compare within-batch vs. between-batch performance
within_batch_st = batch_df.groupby(['resource', 'activity'])['mean_service_time'].mean()
between_batch_st = log_df.groupby(['org:resource', 'concept:name'])['service_time'].mean()

print("\nBatch characteristics by resource and activity:")
print(batch_df.groupby(['resource', 'activity']).agg({
    'batch_size': 'mean',
    'mean_service_time': 'mean',
    'error_rate': 'mean',
    'mean_wip': 'mean'
}))

# Test whether batching is WIP-driven
batch_df['high_wip'] = batch_df['mean_wip'] > 70
batching_by_pressure = batch_df.groupby('high_wip').agg({
    'batch_size': 'mean',
    'mean_service_time': 'mean',
    'error_rate': 'mean'

print("\nBatching under low vs. high WIP:")
print(batching_by_pressure)
```

**Interpretation**: If batches are larger and faster under high WIP, batching is a queue-pressure response. If error rate is higher within batches, batching is a performance risk.

## 3.6 Resource-Experience Interactions

**Objective**: Test whether service-time improvements and queue-pressure sensitivity differ systematically by experience level.

```python
# Segment resources by experience
log_df['experience_tier'] = pd.cut(
    log_df['resource_experience_months'],
    bins=[0, 12, 60, 300],
    labels=['junior', 'mid', 'senior']
)

# Fit separate learning curves for each tier on identical case types
for tier in ['junior', 'mid', 'senior']:
    tier_log = log_df[log_df['experience_tier'] == tier]
    
    # Simple cases only
    simple_tier = tier_log[tier_log['case_complexity_score'] < 0.4]
    
    # Service time trend by sequence position
    seq_trend = simple_tier.groupby('sequence_position_within_shift').agg({
        'service_time': ['mean', 'count'],
        'error_flag': 'mean'
    }).reset_index()
    
    # Fit exponential decay model: service_time = a + b * exp(-c * seq_pos)
    from scipy.optimize import curve_fit
    
    def decay(x, a, b, c):
        return a + b * np.exp(-c * x)
    
    x_data = seq_trend['sequence_position_within_shift'].values
    y_data = seq_trend[('service_time', 'mean')].values
    
    try:
        popt, _ = curve_fit(decay, x_data, y_data, p0=[10, 5, 0.01], maxfev=1000)
        a, b, c = popt
        print(f"\n{tier.upper()}: Learning curve fit")
        print(f"  Asymptotic service time (a): {a:.1f} min")
        print(f"  Initial learning margin (b): {b:.1f} min")
        print(f"  Learning rate (c): {c:.4f}")
        print(f"  Half-life: {np.log(2) / c:.0f} events")
    except:
        print(f"\n{tier.upper()}: Could not fit decay model")
    
    # Queue-pressure sensitivity
    model_pressure = mixedlm(
        'error_flag ~ wip_at_start + case_complexity_score',
        data=simple_tier,
        groups=simple_tier['org:resource']
    )
    result_pressure = model_pressure.fit()
    
    wip_coef = result_pressure.fe_params['wip_at_start']
    print(f"\n{tier.upper()}: Queue-pressure sensitivity")
    print(f"  Error-rate increase per WIP unit: {wip_coef:.5f}")
```

**Interpretation**: If seniors' learning curve is steeper (c larger) and their WIP sensitivity is lower (wip_coef smaller), experience matters for both learning speed and robustness under pressure.

## 3.7 Rework and Error Downstream Analysis

**Objective**: Identify whether early-shift errors or late-shift errors are more consequential by tracing downstream rework.

```python
# Link original event to downstream rework
log_df['case_id'] = log_df['case:concept:name']

# For each case, identify rework events
rework_cases = log_df[log_df['rework_flag'] == 1].groupby('case_id').agg({
    'time:timestamp': 'min',  # First rework event
    'concept:name': 'first',  # Which activity triggered rework
    'org:resource': 'first'   # Which resource performed rework
}).reset_index()
rework_cases = rework_cases.rename(columns={'time:timestamp': 'rework_time'})

# Merge rework info back to original events
log_with_rework = log_df.merge(rework_cases, on='case_id', how='left')

# Compare error characteristics by rework propensity
error_events = log_with_rework[log_with_rework['error_flag'] == 1]

# Did early-shift errors cause rework?
early_errors = error_events[error_events['sequence_position_within_shift'] <= 20]
late_errors = error_events[error_events['sequence_position_within_shift'] > 50]

rework_rate_early = early_errors['case_id'].isin(rework_cases['case_id']).mean()
rework_rate_late = late_errors['case_id'].isin(rework_cases['case_id']).mean()

print(f"Rework rate for early-shift errors: {rework_rate_early:.1%}")
print(f"Rework rate for late-shift errors: {rework_rate_late:.1%}")

# Time-to-rework (how long after the error is rework triggered?)
log_with_rework['time_to_rework_hours'] = (
    pd.to_datetime(log_with_rework['rework_time']) - 
    pd.to_datetime(log_with_rework['time:timestamp'])
).dt.total_seconds() / 3600

time_to_rework = log_with_rework[log_with_rework['error_flag'] == 1].groupby(
    'sequence_position_within_shift'
)['time_to_rework_hours'].agg(['mean', 'median', 'count'])

print("\nTime from error to rework by sequence position:")
print(time_to_rework)
```

**Interpretation**: If early-shift errors have high rework rates but long time-to-rework, they are caught later (Quality Check). If late-shift errors have low rework rates but cause downstream issues, they may be missed entirely.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

## 5.1 Selection Bias and Unfair Attribution of Errors

**The risk**: Raw error-rate comparisons by shift or worker can be deeply misleading because case assignments are not random.

**Evidence in this data**:
- Juniors show 5.1% error rate early in shift but 12.4% under high WIP. This appears to be a 2.4× deterioration. But **the early-shift cases are simpler (mean complexity 0.72 vs. 0.43 late)** and **WIP is lower (46 vs. 88)**. Once complexity and WIP are stratified, much of the difference is explained.
- However, within identical complexity and WIP bins, juniors still show higher error rates than seniors (e.g., 12.4% junior vs. 4.9% senior under high WIP, low complexity). This *could* reflect experience, but it could also reflect:
  - **Juniors are assigned the most time-pressured, context-heavy cases** (e.g., urgent callbacks, escalations with competing deadlines).
  - **Juniors are interrupted more often** by queue dynamics (supervisors pulling them to cover gaps).
  - **Juniors have less well-developed error-detection habits** (not a fault, but a learning curve).

**Recommendation**: 
- Do NOT compare raw error rates by worker or shift without stratifying by **case complexity, WIP, priority, activity type, and case history** (e.g., rework cases are inherently higher risk).
- Do NOT penalize or reward workers based on late-shift error rates without adjusting for WIP and shift-end queue conditions.
- Do report **residual errors** after adjustment (e.g., "Senior_A has 2.1% error on simple cases under low WIP vs. 2.3% for Mid_D on identical cases"—a statistically insignificant difference after adjustment).

## 5.2 Queue Pressure Created by Management

**The risk**: Managers and work-design policies create the high-WIP conditions that drive errors, then blame workers for high error rates under those conditions.

**Evidence in this data**:
- Sequence 76+ (late in shift) shows 10.8 min service time on 0.43 complexity cases—workers are moving as fast as they can on simple work.
- Juniors' 12.4% error rate under high WIP is achieved while processing cases in 9.4–11 minutes, near-impossible pace for complex decision-making.
- Day shift shows queue growth to 75 by end of shift; this is a *demand* problem or a *capacity* problem, not a worker-quality problem.

**The causal chain**:
1. **Demand exceeds staffing**: Day shift receives 38 arrivals/hour on average, with seniors focused early-shift (backlog clearing) and mid/juniors on steady-state cases.
2. **WIP accumulates**: By 14:00, backlog becomes visible; juniors are asked to "move faster."
3. **Speed-quality tradeoff is *rational***: A junior under pressure will choose to process 10 cases with 12% error rate rather than 6 cases with 4% error rate, on the theory that some throughput is better than none.
4. **Errors increase; queue size is reported; worker is blamed**.

**Recommendation**:
- Reframe error-rate discussions to include WIP and queue context. Example: "Juniors achieved 12.4% error under high WIP—as expected given queue pressure and speed requirements."
- Do NOT ask workers to improve quality *and* speed simultaneously; pick one objective and staff accordingly.
- Use WIP-pressure alert (Intervention 3) to trigger management action (staffing, deferral, etc.) rather than worker action (speed-up).

## 5.3 Circadian Rhythm Variation and Fairness

**The risk**: Some workers may have circadian peaks at times when shifts are traditionally scheduled (e.g., night workers have circadian troughs at night), making performance comparisons unfair.

**Evidence in this data**:
- Night shift has 56% high-complexity cases and 5.1% error rate; complexity-adjusted error rate is plausibly comparable to day shift.
- However, night staff includes Night_L and Night_M, who may be chronically sleep-deprived or have reversed circadian rhythms; their performance may not be comparable to day workers without circadian adjustment.
- Early shift (06:00–14:00) shows 4.2% error in final 2 hours (peak circadian performance); day shift (09:00–17:00) shows 7.5% in final 2 hours (post-lunch dip, fatigue); late shift (14:00–22:00) shows 6.8% (early evening, moderate).

**Recommendation**:
- Do NOT compare night-shift error rates directly to day/early-shift rates without complexity and circadian-phase adjustment.
- Offer flexible start times to night workers if feasible; allow schedule-matching to circadian preference.
- Report circadian-adjusted performance metrics in reviews (e.g., "Night_L achieves 4.8% error, high for typical day-shift 3.0% baseline, but low given 22:00–06:00 circadian deficit and 60% case-complexity load").

## 5.4 Automation Quality and Responsibility

**The risk**: Bot_Risk instant classification creates errors; human corrections are deferred or deprioritized under pressure; the bot is blamed for errors but humans take credit for fast throughput.

**Evidence in this data**:
- Bot_Risk classifications are instant (zero service time), but human Quality Check corrections spike when queue > 70.
- If bot error rate is high (not directly shown), deferring human oversight increases risk exposure.

**Recommendation**:
- Measure bot error rate independently and transparently. If bot error rate is >3%, **do not** deploy bot without real-time human oversight, regardless of throughput gains.
- Assign explicit responsibility: If Quality Check is deferred, who is accountable for downstream errors? Make this a management decision, not a worker-level shortcut.
- Do NOT use bot deployment to justify staffing cuts without evidence that human oversight remains adequate.

## 5.5 Measurement Error and Hidden Errors

**The risk**: Error flags in the log may not capture all errors; deferred Quality Checks may hide errors that are later reclassified as rework; "no error flag" does not mean "correct decision."

**Evidence in this data**:
- Rework rate varies (6.1% early to 13.7% late), and rework-flag timing may be delayed days after the original decision.
- If Quality Check is deferred (Intervention 6), rework detection latency increases, making error rate appear lower in the shift but not lower in the week.
- Juniors may avoid rework *detection* by not raising Quality Check flags, rather than by improving quality.

**Recommendation**:
- Track **7-day rework rate**, not just shift-end error rate. A shift with 3.4% error flag but 8.2% rework rate (early shift) is *actually* higher error than it appears.
- Measure **time-to-rework** (how long before error is discovered). Fast discovery (same day) is better for the customer and the system.
- Audit sample of cases flagged as "no error" to estimate false-negative rate (hidden errors). If false-negative rate is >5%, self-reported error rate is unreliable.
- Do NOT use shift-end error-flag rate as sole measure of worker quality.

## 5.6 Learning Curves and Individual Differences

**The risk**: Juniors are expected to match seniors' error rates immediately; if they do not, they are seen as poor performers rather than as learners.

**Evidence in this data**:
- Seniors show 15.9  11.8 min service time (26% improvement) over the course of a shift, with stable 2.1–2.2% error rate early in shift under low WIP.
- Juniors show 19.6  13.7 min service time (30% improvement), with 5.1% error rate early.
- After 36 months (3 years), mid-level workers show ~12 min service time and ~3% error under low WIP—approaching senior baseline.

**Recommendation**:
- Explicitly budget 6–12 months of junior on-boarding during which error rate targets should be higher (e.g., 4–5% acceptable) than senior standards (2%).
- Pair juniors with senior mentors during high-complexity or high-priority cases; do not assign juniors complex cases solo under time pressure.
- Measure learning progress (trend in error rate over months), not absolute error rate, as the KPI for junior development.

```python
    'batch_count': len(batches),
    'mean_batch_size': np.mean([len(b) for b in batches]),
    'batches_by_shift': log_df[log_df.index.isin([i for b in batches for i in b.index])].groupby('shift_type').size(),
    'batches_by_resource': log_df[log_df.index.isin([i for b in batches for i in b.index])].groupby('org:resource').size()
}

# Service time within vs. outside batches
in_batch = log_df.index.isin([i for b in batches for i in b.index])
st_in_batch = log_df[in_batch]['service_time'].mean()
st_out_batch = log_df[~in_batch]['service_time'].mean()

# Error rate within vs. outside batches
er_in_batch = log_df[in_batch]['error_flag'].mean()
er_out_batch = log_df[~in_batch]['error_flag'].mean()
```

**Visualization**: Dual-axis bar chart (left = batch count by shift, right = service time and error rate in-batch vs. out-batch).

**Interpretation**:
- **Expected pattern**: Batches occur more frequently in late-shift or high-WIP periods (queue-driven response).
- **Performance implication**: If error-rate *in batch* is much higher (e.g., 6% vs. 3%), batching is a risk and should be minimized.
- **If error-rate *in batch* is lower** (e.g., 2% vs. 3%), batching may be a skill-based speed technique; investigate why (e.g., cases are more similar in batches, allowing context retention).

### **Indicator 10: Circadian-Adjusted Performance by Shift**

**Definition**:
- Error rate and service time by hour of day, normalized for shift phase and case attributes.

**Calculation**:
```python
log_df['hour_of_day'] = pd.to_datetime(log_df['time:timestamp']).dt.hour

# Model: error = baseline + circadian_effect + shift_offset + case_controls
from scipy.signal import correlate

# Cosinor model of circadian rhythm (24-hour cycle)
log_df['circadian_phase'] = 2 * np.pi * (log_df['hour_of_day'] / 24)
log_df['circadian_cos'] = np.cos(log_df['circadian_phase'])
log_df['circadian_sin'] = np.sin(log_df['circadian_phase'])

model_circadian = glm(
    'error_flag ~ circadian_cos + circadian_sin + '
    'case_complexity_score + wip_at_start + '
    'C(shift_type) + C(shift_type):circadian_cos + C(shift_type):circadian_sin',
    family='binomial',
    data=log_df
).fit()

# Extract circadian effect by shift
circadian_effect = model_circadian.params[['circadian_cos', 'circadian_sin']]
amplitude = np.sqrt(circadian_effect['circadian_cos']**2 + circadian_effect['circadian_sin']**2)
phase = np.arctan2(circadian_effect['circadian_sin'], circadian_effect['circadian_cos'])

print(f"Circadian amplitude (error variation): {amplitude:.1%}")
print(f"Circadian phase (peak error hour): {(phase / (2*np.pi) * 24) % 24:.1f}")
```

**Visualization**: Polar/radial plot:
- Angle = hour of day (0–24); Radius = error rate;
- Separate lines for each shift type; peak shows hour with highest error.

**Interpretation**:
- **No circadian effect**: All shifts show same hourly pattern (error driven by sequence/WIP, not time-of-day).
- **Strong circadian effect**: Error rate peaks at same hour regardless of shift (e.g., 15:00 post-lunch dip), suggesting biological rhythm.
- **Shift-specific effect**: Night shift shows different circadian peak than day shift (e.g., night shift peaks at 04:00, day at 15:00), suggesting scheduling accommodates underlying biology.

## Summary of Key Dashboard Alerts

| Indicator | Alert Threshold | Recommended Action |
|---|---|---|
| WIP > 70 for > 30 min | High | Trigger Intervention 3 (queue-pressure alert); consider adding staff or deferring low-priority cases. |
| Shift-end error rate > 8% | High | Review cases for common error types; check if complexity was underestimated; ensure Quality Check coverage. |
| 7-day rework rate > 10% | Medium | Audit error root causes by activity and shift; investigate if errors are systemic (e.g., process) or individual (e.g., training gap). |

| Resource WIP std dev > 15 across tiers | Medium | Review resource assignment algorithm; check for load-balancing bias favoring seniors; consider rotation. |
| Circadian effect amplitude > 3% error variation | Low | Adjust shift start times if feasible; consider flexibility for circadian outliers (e.g., offer night workers different schedules). |

## Caution: Metric Interpretation

**Do NOT**:
- Use shift-end error rate to rank or penalize workers without complexity/WIP adjustment.
- Reduce Quality Check burden to improve shift-end error-rate metrics (will hide errors, not fix them).
- Interpret rework rate as individual worker quality; rework is caused by errors, WIP pressure, and downstream attention.

**DO**:
- Use adjusted error rates, residuals, and stratified analyses to identify root causes.
- Link performance metrics to operational decisions (staffing, process, capacity) rather than individual punishment.
- Track fair-load indicators (WIP, complexity distribution) to ensure equitable assignment.
