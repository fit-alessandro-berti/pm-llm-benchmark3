# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

## High fitness but low precision

Fitness measures whether the discovered model can replay the observed event log. Precision measures whether the model is too permissive in allowing behavior that was not observed or is undesirable.

The pilot has high fitness because many cases now follow the intended simplified coordinator path. But it has low precision because the observed process contains many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, failed visit, second visit, billing start, and complaint escalation. The alignment diagnostics reinforce this: billing before activation, repeated installation visits without surveys, and missing permit checks are frequent deviations.

**Strategic meaning:** the pilot is not merely “different”; it is less tightly controlled. High fitness alone is not evidence that the redesign is ready for scale.

## Improved median but worse tail duration

Median cycle time improved because simple cases moved faster through merged validation/scheduling and fewer handovers. The median is dominated by the typical case, and the pilot had a large share of simple cases that benefited.

The tail worsened because complex cases encountered exceptions: wrong survey skips, missing permits, failed visits, manual activation, and billing before activation. The temporal profile shows that installation-to-activation worsened for apartments and that billing-to-complaint became a new long-tail relation. The DFG shows higher frequencies of failed visits, second visits, manual activation, and complaint escalation after billing.

**Strategic meaning:** the redesign creates a faster core path but weakens controls where cases are complex. Median improvement is not sufficient for a decision when p90/p95, complaints, and rework worsen.

## Fewer handovers but lower resilience

The redesign reduced formal handovers from 34% of cases with 3+ handovers to 12%. That supports accountability and speed. But the social/resource findings show that work became centralized around coordinators. Coordinator utilization reached 93%, and cases stalled when coordinators were absent because context was not shared.

**Strategic meaning:** handover count is a structural metric, not a resilience metric. A process can have fewer handovers and still be more fragile if knowledge and ownership are overly concentrated.

## Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

Visible cost before complaint handling improved from EUR 184 to EUR 161. But complaints, second visits, manual activation, and billing-before-activation all increased. The estimated cost including complaint/revisit handling worsened from EUR 198 to EUR 207, and the estimate is incomplete because downstream complaint handling after week 4 was excluded.

**Strategic meaning:** the pilot may be moving cost downstream rather than eliminating it. A decision based only on visible cost would be misleading.

## Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings

Simple low-risk houses benefited: median duration fell by 6 days, p90 improved, first-time-right improved, and complaints fell. These cases likely need fewer surveys, permits, and coordination steps.

Apartments, medium-risk addresses, and old permit-sensitive buildings worsened. The survey-skip rule was often wrong for apartments, permit checks were missing for older buildings, and installation-to-activation delays increased. These segments need more control, not less.

**Strategic meaning:** the redesign should not be treated as one intervention with one effect. It is beneficial for some segments and harmful for others.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Option E — hybrid targeted deployment with controlled measurement.**

## Immediate action

1. **Create a segment gate.**
   - Use Redesign R only for **simple low-risk houses**.
   - Use baseline or controlled baseline-plus-monitoring for apartments, medium-risk addresses, permit-sensitive older buildings, and business cases with billing disputes.

2. **Implement conformance monitoring immediately.**
   - Monitor **billing start before successful activation test**.
   - Monitor **missing permit checks** for permit-sensitive buildings.
   - Escalate violations as operational incidents.

3. **Protect coordinator capacity.**
   - Set a coordinator utilization cap, for example **85%**.
   - Require backup or shared-team ownership when a coordinator is absent.
   - Avoid assigning complex cases to overloaded coordinators until resilience is proven.

## What to pause

- **Pause national scaling of Redesign R.**
- **Pause survey skipping** for medium-risk addresses, apartments, older buildings, and any permit-sensitive case until M6/M1 provide evidence.
- **Pause billing before successful activation test**, except where a controlled exception is explicitly approved, monitored, and reversible.
- **Pause expansion into new regions** until segment-level thresholds are defined and measured.

## What to scale

- Scale the **merged address validation/appointment scheduling** step for simple low-risk houses where it improved speed.
- Scale **coordinator ownership for simple low-risk houses**, but only with utilization caps and absence coverage.
- Scale **conformance monitoring and segment routing**, because these are required safeguards for any further expansion.

## What to measure next

Priority order:

1. **M7:** conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and missing permits.
2. **M6:** validation of the building-risk predictive rule.
3. **M1:** randomized survey-skip experiment by risk stratum.
4. **M4:** 60-day downstream cost including complaints and revisits.
5. **M2:** start timestamps for installation, activation, and manual activation.
6. **M3:** structured reason codes for failures, manual activation, complaints, and second visits.
7. **M5:** coordinator ownership versus shared-team ownership under absence.
8. **M8:** separated customer-experience measures for appointment speed and working service after installation.

## Thresholds for broader rollout

Broader rollout should require evidence, not just management confidence.

For **simple low-risk houses**, continue or expand only if:

- Median cycle time remains better than baseline for that segment.
- p90 duration is not worse than baseline for that segment.
- First-time-right and complaint rate are at least as good as baseline.
- SLA breaches are not worse.
- Unauthorized billing before activation is effectively zero.

For **apartments, medium-risk addresses, and older buildings**, expand only if:

- Randomized or strongly controlled evidence shows survey skipping does not increase second visits, complaints, or tail duration.
- Permit compliance is near-perfect for permit-sensitive buildings.
- Case-mix-adjusted p90/p95 duration is no worse than baseline.
- First-time-right returns to at least baseline levels.
- Complaint rate returns to at least baseline levels.
- Manual activation and second visits return to at least baseline levels.
- 60-day full cost is no worse than baseline.
- Coordinator utilization remains below the agreed cap and absence coverage prevents stalling.
- The discovered process model becomes less permissive, with precision returning toward or above baseline levels and conformance violations rare.

If these thresholds are not met, management should keep the affected segments on the baseline process or roll back the harmful elements. The defensible path is not “all in” or “all out”; it is **selective scaling where evidence is strong, containment where evidence is weak, and disciplined learning where evidence is missing.**
