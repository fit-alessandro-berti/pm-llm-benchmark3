## 1. Analytical assumptions

A **case notion** is the object instance used to partition and order events for a particular analytical question. It is not an assertion that the selected object is the ontologically “real” case. For example, `O3`, `P3`, `D2`, and `V2` are all legitimate units, but they support different claims.

I use the terms operationally:

- **Convergence** is many-to-one at the source-graph level: several business entities participate in one event or object. Examples are `e16`, which involves `O1`, `O2`, `O3`, `P2`, `P3`, and `D2`, and `V2`, which bills `O2` and `O3`.
- **Divergence** is one-to-many after projection: one source event is copied into several traces, or one business episode is split across several object lifecycles. Thus `e16` is a convergent source event but becomes divergent when copied into the `O1`, `O2`, and `O3` traces.

An event can therefore exhibit both phenomena. Copied shared events are **both evidence and duplication**. They are evidence because the related object genuinely participates; they are duplication because the same source event identifier does not represent a new execution. I would retain source event identifiers and report both “event executions” and “object-event involvements.”

The supplied direct E2O relations are the baseline. I would not silently propagate relations through the O2O graph. A separately named derived view may use typed propagation, such as allocating `M2` to items through `M2  V2  O2/O3  items`, but unrestricted transitive closure is unsafe: it can connect `O1` to `O4` through `I12  P2  D2  P3  I31  O3  I32  P4  I41`, although those orders never share a package or delivery.

A short lifecycle is not automatically missing data. `D1` legitimately has only Dispatch and Confirm Delivery, whereas `M2` is a one-event payment trace whose context must come from its relations.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Cross-order package `P3` in the Order flattening.**  
   `e13` is one Pack Package event related to `O2`, `O3`, `I21`, `I31`, and `P3`, but it is copied into both the `O2` and `O3` traces. A DFG frequency of two means two order-pack involvements, not two physical packing executions. This is harmful for resource or activity-volume measurement, but informative if the measure is order exposure to shared packing.

2. **Cross-order package `P4` in the Order flattening.**  
   `e14` is copied into `O3` and `O4`. For `O3`, it follows `e13`, correctly indicating that `I31` and `I32` are split across `P3` and `P4`; for `O4`, the same physical event represents its `I41` content. Treating each copy as a separately executed packing operation is harmful, while the two package involvements of `O3` are informative.

3. **Mixed-order content hidden by the Package flattening.**  
   `P3` has the sequence `e13  e16  e24`, and `P4` has `e14  e17  e27`. Both look as simple as `P1`, even though `P3` combines `O2/O3` and `P4` combines `O3/O4`. The resulting single DFG variant is useful for handling compliance but harmful for studying how mixed-order packages create coupling.

4. **Consolidated delivery `D2` copied across orders.**  
   `e16` and `e24` each enter the `O1`, `O2`, and `O3` traces. One physical dispatch and one physical confirmation therefore produce three order-case rows each. Moreover, the copies have different local contexts: `e16` follows `e12` for `O1`, `e13` for `O2`, and `e14` for `O3`. This inflates Dispatch and Confirm Delivery frequencies and can suggest that complete orders were consolidated, although only `I12/P2`, `I21/P3`, and `I31/P3` are involved.

5. **Consolidated delivery hidden by the Delivery flattening.**  
   The `D2` trace is only `e16  e24`, identical in activity structure to `D1` and `D3`. Its 4-hour-55-minute duration is a valid route-performance measure, but the trace hides that `D2` carries two packages and touches three orders. This is informative for transport SLA and harmful for capacity or risk analysis.

6. **Consolidated invoice `V2` copied across orders.**  
   `e19`, `e22`, and `e25` are copied into both `O2` and `O3`. Thus two actual payment events become four order-payment involvements, and each order trace appears to have two Record Payment events. This is informative for allocating financial exposure, but harmful if interpreted as two separate invoices or four separate payment transactions.

7. **Partial payments in the Invoice flattening.**  
   The `V2` trace is `e19  e22  e25`, correctly showing two collections. Because both activities are named Record Payment, a plain DFG can make the second payment look like repetition or rework. Retaining the qualifiers `partially_settles` for `M2` and `completes_settlement` for `M3` makes the pattern informative rather than anomalous.

8. **Near-singleton Payment lifecycles.**  
   `M1`, `M2`, `M3`, and `M4` each form a one-event trace. The DFG has no edges, and the fact that `M2` precedes `M3` in the settlement of `V2` disappears from the lifecycle model. This is acceptable for a cash-event audit but severely harmful for end-to-end discovery.

9. **Uniform but truncated Item lifecycles.**  
   Every item has Add Item  Pack Package  Dispatch  Confirm Delivery. However, `e16` and `e24` are each copied to `I12`, `I21`, and `I31`, so one dispatch and one confirmation each generate three item rows. The universal item DFG is useful for checking line handling, but it hides order creation, closure, invoicing, payment, and package consolidation.

10. **Split fulfilment mistaken for repetition.**  
    In the Order flattening, `O1` has `e15/e16` and `e23/e24`, while `O3` has `e16/e17` and `e24/e27`. These repetitions reveal genuine split fulfilment, but the shared nature of `e16`, `e24`, `e17`, and `e27` can make them look like duplicate whole-order dispatches. Package and Delivery views are needed to distinguish order completion from physical movement.

11. **Order-level performance frontier masks component timing.**  
    For `O3`, `I31/P3` is confirmed at `e24` on 3 February, while `I32/P4` is confirmed at `e27` on 4 February; the final `V2` payment occurs at `e25`, before `e27`. The order trace correctly waits for the latest component, but attributing the full delay to every item or package would be misleading. This effect is context-dependent: appropriate for an order promise, inappropriate for package-level SLA.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

I would create all six flattened logs once for comparison, but retain them with different statuses:

- **Order:** primary customer-order log. It can safely answer when an order was created, closed, invoiced, paid, and last confirmed, and whether fulfilment was split. It cannot safely report unique physical execution counts.
- **Item:** line-level handling log. It can test whether every item follows Add Item  Pack Package  Dispatch  Confirm Delivery.
- **Package:** physical-unit log. It can compare package handling and isolate `P3/P4`, but mixed-order composition must be retained as an annotation.
- **Delivery:** route-SLA log. It is appropriate for `D1`, `D2`, and `D3` dispatch-to-confirmation duration.
- **Invoice:** billing and collection log. It is the safest traditional view of `V2` and its two instalments.
- **Payment:** useful mainly as an event inventory or reconciliation extract; process discovery on four singleton traces adds little.

Questions about shared execution, convergence, divergence, object multiplicity, and cross-type causality require object-centric representations:

- An **OC-DFG** should distinguish event frequency, unique-object frequency, and total object involvement.
- An **OTG** should expose the `contains`, `packs`, `carries`, `bills`, and settlement paths.
- An **ET–OT graph** should show that Dispatch involves Order, Item, Package, and Delivery, while Record Payment involves Order, Invoice, and Payment.
- An **object interaction summary** should quantify the coupling caused by `e13`, `e14`, `e16`, `e19`, `e22`, `e24`, `e25`, and `e27`.
- Equivalent-execution clustering can test whether order lifecycles remain equivalent after abstraction, but it must not be used to erase source-event identity.

Contradictions should first be treated as denominator mismatches. For Dispatch, there are 3 unique source events, 6 order involvements, 6 item involvements, 4 package involvements, and 3 delivery involvements. All five numbers can be correct. Likewise, Record Payment has 4 unique events but 6 order-case copies. A flattened edge means “next visible event for this object,” not necessarily a causal edge between physical executions. Direct E2O evidence should take precedence over inferred propagation, and every reported metric should carry its unit.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**Decision: B. Use a primary lead object plus explicitly secondary lifecycles.**

- **Decision criteria:** business accountability, direct event coverage, interpretability, preservation of completion semantics, control of duplication, and the ability to explain physical and financial consolidation.
- **Strongest supporting evidence:** Order is the only candidate directly related to all 31 source events and has a natural create-to-close lifecycle. It captures the operational and financial interleaving of `O1` through `O4`, including split fulfilment and the effects of `V2`.
- **Strongest counterargument:** Order flattening produces 42 event rows from 31 source events. Shared events such as `e16/e24` can make line-level participation look like whole-order execution, while `V2` payments are duplicated across `O2` and `O3`.
- **Residual risk:** order-level reporting may overstate activity executions, hide which item caused a delay, and obscure the independent status of `P3`, `D2`, or `V2`.
- **Why not A or C:** Order is defensible as the primary customer-facing denominator, so rejecting any lead would lose useful reporting structure. It is not defensible as the sole lifecycle because the physical and financial objects answer materially different questions.
- **Conditions for changing the decision:** use Delivery as lead for transport optimization, Package for warehouse operations, or Invoice for collections. I would move to position C if copied events could not retain source identifiers, if order involvement were no longer the main accountability unit, or if network-level consolidation were more important than customer-order completion.

This choice is not based on Order having the longest or most numerous traces; it is based on direct coverage and business accountability, with secondary lifecycles controlling its distortions.

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Copied shared-event treatment.**  
   Compare raw multiplicity, inverse-multiplicity weighting, and a unique-source-event report. For example, give `e16` weight \(1/3\) in the Order flattening and \(1/2\) in the Package flattening. Simply deleting copies can create artificial adjacency, such as connecting the event before `e16` directly to the event after it, so weighted frequencies are preferable to sequence deletion.

2. **Direct E2O versus explicitly propagated relations.**  
   Keep the supplied E2O baseline, then create clearly named derived views. One view could propagate order creation and closure to contained items; another could propagate `M2/M3` through `V2` and `O2/O3` to their items. Compare the resulting lifecycle lengths, edge frequencies, and trace variants. Reject unrestricted closure because it can connect `O1` to `O4` through the `D2/P3/O3/P4` chain.

3. **Lead-object sensitivity.**  
   Ask the same completion question under Order, Item, Package, Delivery, Invoice, and Payment. For `O3`, `P3` completes at `e24`, `P4` at `e27`, `V2` at `e25`, and the order closes at `e30`. If a conclusion changes, report it as perspective-dependent rather than selecting the longest trace.

4. **OC-DFG denominator comparison.**  
   Compare unique events, unique objects, and total object relations. Globally, the snapshot has 31 unique events, 24 objects, and 95 direct E2O involvements. For `e16`, the respective event and object counts are 1 and 9; at activity level, Dispatch has 3 unique events but 6 order involvements, 6 item involvements, 4 package involvements, and 3 delivery involvements.

5. **Activity and qualifier granularity.**  
   Rerun the financial views with Record Payment refined by `partially_settles` and `completes_settlement`. Similarly mark `e13`, `e14`, `e16`, and `e19` as shared or consolidated. This tests whether apparent rework, variants, or log-skeleton violations are merely label-granularity artifacts.

6. **Lifecycle-endpoint sensitivity.**
