# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

## Mathematical Formulation and Indexing
Let $\mathcal{C} = \{F01, F02, \dots, F12\}$ denote the set of observed process cases, indexed by $c$.
Let $\mathcal{B} = \{A, B, C\}$ denote the set of parallel branches released at the fork, where:
- $A$: Laboratory Assay (technical work)
- $B$: Machine Stress Test (technical work)
- $C$: Documentation Review (administrative work)

Each case $c$ is characterized by the following primary event timestamps (accurate to one minute on 2026-05-19):
- $t_{arr}(c)$: Overall process arrival timestamp.
- $t_{fork}(c)$: Fork release timestamp (earliest time all branch work is authorized to start).
- $t_{start, i}(c), t_{end, i}(c)$: Activity start and completion timestamps for branch $i \in \mathcal{B}$.
- $t_{start, F}(c), t_{end, F}(c)$: Activity start and completion timestamps for the final activity $F$ (Release Decision).

The delay and service components are defined formally as follows:

1. **Upstream Elapsed Time ($T_{upstream}(c)$)**:
   The duration from initial case arrival to the fork release point:
   $$T_{upstream}(c) = t_{fork}(c) - t_{arr}(c)$$

2. **Branch-Specific Queue Waiting ($W_i^{queue}(c)$)**:
   The delay experienced by branch $i$ between fork release and its actual execution start:
   $$W_i^{queue}(c) = t_{start, i}(c) - t_{fork}(c) \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{B}$$

3. **Branch Active Service Time ($S_i(c)$)**:
   The net active execution duration of branch $i$:
   $$S_i(c) = t_{end, i}(c) - t_{start, i}(c) \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{B}$$

4. **Total Branch Elapsed Duration ($E_i(c)$)**:
   The total elapsed lead time of branch $i$ from fork release to completion:
   $$E_i(c) = t_{end, i}(c) - t_{fork}(c) = W_i^{queue}(c) + S_i(c) \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{B}$$

5. **Join-Ready Timestamp ($t_{join\_ready}(c)$)**:
   Under the AND-join semantics, the final station may start only when all parallel branches have completed:
   $$t_{join\_ready}(c) = \max_{i \in \mathcal{B}} t_{end, i}(c) = t_{fork}(c) + \max_{i \in \mathcal{B}} E_i(c)$$

6. **Elapsed Fork-Join Stage Time ($T_{FJ}(c)$)**:
   The total elapsed wall-clock time required for the entire parallel stage to clear:
   $$T_{FJ}(c) = t_{join\_ready}(c) - t_{fork}(c) = \max_{i \in \mathcal{B}} E_i(c) = \max_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \left( W_i^{queue}(c) + S_i(c) \right)$$

7. **Branch-Specific Synchronization Waiting ($W_i^{sync}(c)$)**:
   The idle waiting time experienced by branch $i$ after completing service while waiting for the slowest branch:
   $$W_i^{sync}(c) = t_{join\_ready}(c) - t_{end, i}(c) = T_{FJ}(c) - E_i(c) \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{B}$$
   By definition, $W_i^{sync}(c) \ge 0$, and for at least one branch $i^*$, $W_{i^*}^{sync}(c) = 0$.

8. **Critical Branch ($\mathcal{B}^*(c)$)**:
   The branch (or set of branches in case of ties) that finishes last and therefore defines $t_{join\_ready}(c)$:
   $$\mathcal{B}^*(c) = \left\{ i \in \mathcal{B} \;\middle|\; t_{end, i}(c) = t_{join\_ready}(c) \right\} = \arg\max_{i \in \mathcal{B}} E_i(c)$$

9. **Downstream Queueing Before $F$ ($W_{downstream}(c)$)**:
   The waiting time from synchronization readiness to the start of the final decision station:
   $$W_{downstream}(c) = t_{start, F}(c) - t_{join\_ready}(c)$$

10. **Final Service Time ($S_{final}(c)$)**:
    The active execution duration of activity $F$:
    $$S_{final}(c) = t_{end, F}(c) - t_{start, F}(c)$$

11. **Total Case Flow Time ($T_{flow}(c)$)**:
    The complete end-to-end cycle time of the case:
    $$T_{flow}(c) = t_{end, F}(c) - t_{arr}(c)$$

---

## Conservation Law and Non-Double-Counting Proof

For every branch $i \in \mathcal{B}$, the internal branch duration decomposes into queueing, service, and synchronization wait:
$$W_i^{queue}(c) + S_i(c) + W_i^{sync}(c) = E_i(c) + \left( T_{FJ}(c) - E_i(c) \right) = T_{FJ}(c)$$

The total case flow time $T_{flow}(c)$ decomposes strictly additively along the critical path without double counting:
$$T_{flow}(c) = T_{upstream}(c) + T_{FJ}(c) + W_{downstream}(c) + S_{final}(c)$$
Substituting $T_{FJ}(c)$:
$$T_{flow}(c) = T_{upstream}(c) + \max_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \left( W_i^{queue}(c) + S_i(c) \right) + W_{downstream}(c) + S_{final}(c)$$

### Why Summing Branch Synchronization Waits Double Counts Elapsed Time
In a parallel fork-join construct with $|\mathcal{B}| = 3$ branches, wall-clock time elapses concurrently across all three branches. If an analyst attempts to compute stage delay or total case delay by summing all branch queue times, service times, and synchronization waits across all branches:
$$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \left( W_i^{queue}(c) + S_i(c) + W_i^{sync}(c) \right) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} T_{FJ}(c) = 3 \cdot T_{FJ}(c)$$
This would **triple-count** the elapsed wall-clock duration of the parallel stage.

Similarly, summing the synchronization waits across branches yields:
$$\sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} W_i^{sync}(c) = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} \left( T_{FJ}(c) - E_i(c) \right) = 3 \cdot T_{FJ}(c) - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{B}} E_i(c)$$
Synchronization wait $W_i^{sync}(c)$ is **internal slack** (idle buffer) on a non-critical branch. It measures how much earlier branch $i$ finished relative to the critical bottleneck branch $i^*$. Because this waiting occurs concurrently with the ongoing execution of the critical branch, it consumes zero additional wall-clock time for the case. In cycle-time accounting, the elapsed time of the fork-join stage is uniquely determined by the critical branch ($E_{crit}(c) = T_{FJ}(c)$ where $W_{crit}^{sync}(c) = 0$). Adding non-zero synchronization waits into total flow time would erroneously treat concurrent idle time as sequential additive processing time.

## Analytical Findings: Service Time vs. Critical Influence

### 1. The Fallacy of Mean Service Time
A naive examination of activity durations would suggest that **Branch A (Laboratory Assay)** is the dominant bottleneck because its mean service time ($50.00\text{ min}$) is $33.3\%$ higher than Branch B ($37.50\text{ min}$) and $147.9\%$ higher than Branch C ($20.17\text{ min}$). 
However, **Branch B (Machine Stress Test) is the critical branch in 66.7% of all cases** (8 out of 12). Branch A is critical in only 16.7% of cases (2 out of 12).

### 2. Queue Delay plus Service Drives the Critical Path
Branch A operates with infinite/ample capacity (zero queue delay in every case, $W_A^{queue} = 0$), resulting in a strictly deterministic elapsed duration of exactly $50\text{ min}$.
Branch B experiences substantial queue accumulation ($\overline{W_B^{queue}} = 19.17\text{ min}$, peaking at $30\text{ min}$) alongside variable service ($30 - 45\text{ min}$). Consequently, Branch B's total elapsed duration averages $56.67\text{ min}$ and reaches up to $75.00\text{ min}$ (in F07 and F12). Whenever $E_B > 50\text{ min}$, Branch B unilaterally dictates the join-ready time.

### 3. Synchronization Slack and Tail Dynamics
- **Branch C (Documentation Review)** is typically fast ($E_C \approx 23 - 25\text{ min}$ in 8 cases), spending an average of $30.42\text{ min}$ in synchronization waiting (peaking at $52\text{ min}$ in F07 and F12). However, in cases F04 and F08, acute queue spikes ($W_C^{queue} = 35\text{ min}$ and $25\text{ min}$) push $E_C$ to $57\text{ min}$ and $55\text{ min}$, making Branch C the critical bottleneck in those two instances.
- **Branch A** finishes and waits in synchronization for an average of $11.00\text{ min}$ (and up to $25.00\text{ min}$) in 10 out of 12 cases.

### 4. Analysis of Ties and Near-Ties
In this empirical dataset, there are **no exact numerical ties** for the maximum completion time ($t_{join\_ready}$). However, several cases represent **near-ties** where the margin between the critical branch and the runner-up is $\le 10\text{ min}$:
- **Case F01**: Branch A ($50\text{ min}$) edges out Branch B ($45\text{ min}$) by 5 minutes.
- **Case F05**: Branch B ($60\text{ min}$) finishes 10 minutes after Branch A ($50\text{ min}$).
- **Case F08**: Branch C ($55\text{ min}$) finishes 5 minutes after Branch A ($50\text{ min}$) and 20 minutes after Branch B ($35\text{ min}$).
- **Case F10**: Branch B ($55\text{ min}$) finishes 5 minutes after Branch A ($50\text{ min}$).
- **Case F11**: Branch A ($50\text{ min}$) finishes 10 minutes after Branch B ($40\text{ min}$).

These near-ties demonstrate the "shifting bottleneck" phenomenon: as soon as the primary critical branch is slightly accelerated, the runner-up branch immediately becomes critical, limiting the realized system-level gain.

## Detailed Evaluation of Counterfactuals

### Counterfactual 1: Reducing Branch A Service Time by 20% ($S_A: 50 \to 40\text{ min}$)
- **Mechanics**: $E_A'(c) = 0 + 40 = 40\text{ min}$. $T_{FJ}'(c) = \max(40, E_B(c), E_C(c))$.
- **Direct Benefits**:
  - **F01**: $E_A$ drops from 50 to 40 min. However, $E_B = 45\text{ min}$ and $E_C = 28\text{ min}$. The new stage time is $\max(40, 45, 28) = 45\text{ min}$, yielding a **5.0 min saving** (not 10 min, because Branch B becomes critical at 45 min).
  - **F11**: $E_A$ drops from 50 to 40 min. Since $E_B = 40\text{ min}$ and $E_C = 23\text{ min}$, new stage time is $\max(40, 40, 23) = 40\text{ min}$, yielding a **10.0 min saving** (A and B tie).
  - Aggregate stage savings: **15.0 minutes total** (mean: **1.25 min/case**).
- **Cases with Zero Benefit (Masking Effect)**:
  - In **10 out of 12 cases (83.3%)**, this intervention delivers **0.0 minutes** of flow-time reduction. In F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F12, Branch B takes between 55 and 75 min; in F04 and F08, Branch C takes 57 and 55 min. Accelerating Branch A merely causes Laboratory Assay to finish 10 minutes earlier, increasing Branch A's synchronization wait ($W_A^{sync}$) from $11.00\text{ min}$ to $19.75\text{ min}$ on average.
- **Secondary Effects**: High capital expenditure on assay automation would be almost entirely wasted without addressing upstream/parallel delays in Branch B.

### Counterfactual 2: Reducing Branch B Queue Delay by 50% ($W_B^{queue} \to 0.5 \times W_B^{queue}$)
- **Mechanics**: $W_B^{queue \prime}(c) = 0.5 \times W_B^{queue}(c)$, $E_B'(c) = W_B^{queue \prime}(c) + S_B(c)$.
- **Direct Benefits**:
  - Positively impacts **7 out of 12 cases (58.3%)**, generating **92.5 minutes total savings** (mean: **7.71 min/case**).
  - Major tail compression: In F07 and F12, stage time drops from $75\text{ min}$ to $60\text{ min}$ (15 min reduction per case).
  - In F05, $E_B$ drops from 60 to 47.5 min; but because $E_A = 50\text{ min}$, the new stage time is capped at 50 min (saving 10 min, while Branch A becomes the new bottleneck).
  - In F10, $E_B$ drops from 55 to 45 min; capped by $E_A = 50\text{ min}$ (saving 5 min, Branch A becomes bottleneck).
- **Cases with Zero Benefit**:
  - In F01 and F11 (where A is critical) and F04 and F08 (where C is critical), reducing B queue yields **0.0 min** case savings.
- **Secondary Effects**: Shifts the dominant system bottleneck from Branch B to Branch A. Once B queue is halved, Branch A becomes critical or co-critical in 6 of 12 cases.

### Counterfactual 3: Adding Final-Station Capacity / Eliminating Downstream Queueing
- **Mechanics**: $W_{downstream}'(c) = 0$, so $T_{flow}'(c) = T_{upstream}(c) + T_{FJ}(c) + S_{final}(c)$.
- **Direct Benefits**:
  - Downstream queueing is in series with the fork-join block. Therefore, unlike parallel branches, **every single minute of downstream queue reduction translates 1-to-1 into total flow time reduction across 100% of cases**.
  - Total time savings: **176.0 minutes** across 12 cases (mean: **14.67 min/case**, range: $8 - 20\text{ min}$).
- **Masking vs. Release Policy Nuance**:
  - Crucially, downstream delay is **not** primarily caused by server overutilization: in cases F01, F03, F04, F06, F07, F10, and F12, the assigned resource (F1 or F2) was **idle** when the case reached $join\_ready$. The downstream delay is therefore driven by static release policies, fixed batch inspection windows, or transfer batching. Eliminating this procedural lag recovers 14.67 min/case without buying any new machinery.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Recommendation 1: De-bottlenecking and Concurrency Leveling for Branch B (Machine Stress Test)

### Description of Intervention
Expand effective capacity and eliminate queue buildup at Station B by:
1. Implementing a **Single-Minute Exchange of Die (SMED)** protocol to reduce fixture setup/changeover time between test cycles.
2. Cross-training an additional technician or adding a secondary testing fixture to enable 2-server parallel processing during peak arrival windows (09:00 - 11:30).
3. Instituting dynamic FIFO dispatching to prevent queue starvation and staging delays.

### Impact Analysis
- **Throughput**: Increases overall system maximum throughput from ~1 case every 20-25 min to the theoretical limit dictated by arrival spacing (15 min).
- **Flow-Time Tail**: Compresses the extreme tail of fork-join duration: P95 stage time drops from $75\text{ min}$ down to $50\text{ min}$, eliminating up to $25\text{ min}$ of variance per case.
- **Resource Utilization**: Eliminates artificial idle starvation on Branch A (where high-value assay results sit waiting for stress tests) and levels Machine B utilization.
- **Synchronization Effects**: Mean synchronization wait across the process drops significantly; Branch A idle wait drops from $11.00\text{ min}$ toward zero.
- **Potential New Bottlenecks**: As Branch B elapsed time falls below $50\text{ min}$, **Branch A (Laboratory Assay) becomes the immutable structural floor** ($50\text{ min}$). Further improvements in B beyond that threshold yield zero return unless A is addressed concurrently.
- **What to Pilot / Measure First**: Conduct a 1-week time study of Station B changeover and fixture load/unload times. Measure the distribution of setup time vs. net test run time to verify whether queueing is caused by equipment availability or operator multitasking.

## Recommendation 2: Dynamic Resource Pooling and Continuous "Pull" Dispatch at Final Decision Station (F)

### Description of Intervention
Transition Station F from a rigid, static odd/even resource allocation and batch-release schedule to a **dynamically pooled FIFO queue with real-time event-driven pull**:
1. Pool resources F1 and F2 into a shared server group where whichever resource frees up first pulls the next available case immediately upon $t_{join\_ready}$.
2. Eliminate fixed-interval release batching and manual handover delays, enabling electronic notification and instantaneous case handover upon completion of the last parallel branch.

### Impact Analysis
- **Throughput**: Prevents artificial server starvation (such as resource F1 sitting idle for 32 minutes between 09:28 and 10:00 while case F03 waited 15 minutes in queue).
- **Flow-Time Tail**: Delivers a direct, deterministic reduction in mean total flow time of **14.67 minutes per case** across all 12 cases.
- **Resource Utilization**: Balances workload evenly between F1 and F2, avoiding workload imbalances caused by uneven branch durations.
- **Synchronization Effects**: Decouples the completion of the parallel stage from arbitrary clock-time inspection windows, directly coupling fork-join completion to final sign-off.
- **Potential New Bottlenecks**: If Station F latency is removed, cases flow directly to dispatch. The upstream preparation step ($20\text{ min}$) and the fork-join stage ($50\text{ min}$) become the sole determinants of cycle time.
- **What to Pilot / Measure First**: Pilot an automated digital trigger that alerts F1/F2 the moment all 3 branch signatures are logged in the LIMS/ERP. Measure the "handover lag" (time between join-ready and first touch by F1/F2) for 50 consecutive cases.

# 7. Limits of inference

While event-log timestamp decomposition provides valuable structural clarity regarding fork-join execution, several critical systemic properties cannot be identified from these observational timestamps alone without external domain evidence:

### 1. Causality vs. Correlation in Queue Waiting Times
Observing that Branch B experienced a $30\text{ min}$ queue wait (e.g., in F06, F07, F12) does not reveal the underlying root cause. Timestamps alone cannot determine whether the queue delay was caused by:
- Physical machine unreadiness (e.g., ongoing test cycles on unlogged earlier batches, warm-up, calibration, or maintenance).
- Operator unavailability (e.g., technician shared across multiple stations or assigned to administrative duties).
- Upstream transfer delay (physical transport of test specimens from upstream preparation to Station B).
- Deliberate batching policies (e.g., holding specimens until a minimum batch size is reached).

### 2. Branch Resource Capacity and Server Counts
- **Branch A (Laboratory Assay)**: Timestamps show up to 4 cases executing simultaneously with exactly 0 minutes of queue delay (e.g., at 08:55, F01, F02, F03, and F04 are all actively in assay). While this proves that Branch A possesses multi-server capacity ($\ge 4$ concurrent units), we cannot infer whether capacity is dedicated, pooled, or infinitely scalable without resource master data.
- **Branch C (Documentation Review)**: Usually exhibits minimal queueing ($5\text{ min}$) and short service ($18-20\text{ min}$), but exhibits isolated queue spikes ($35\text{ min}$ in F04, $25\text{ min}$ in F08). We cannot identify whether this reflects reviewer multitasking, document re-submissions, missing paperwork, or intermittent reviewer absence.

### 3. Work Preemption, Interruptions, and True Active Service
The logged data records only gross interval brackets ($t_{start}$ to $t_{end}$). Under standard process-mining assumptions, the resource is presumed to be actively working 100% of the recorded duration. If a technician was interrupted, paused a test, or multi-tasked during Branch B or C, the true service time is overestimated and internal waiting time is misclassified as active service.

### 4. Downstream Delay: True Queueing vs. Procedural Release Policy
A vital finding from the data is that in cases **F01, F03, F04, F06, F07, F10, and F12**, the assigned final resource (F1 or F2) was **completely idle** when $t_{join\_ready}$ occurred, yet the final activity did not begin for another $8 - 20\text{ minutes}$. 
- In F03, F1 was idle from 09:28 to 10:00 (32 min idle), yet F03 (ready at 09:45) waited 15 min until 10:00.
- In F06, F2 finished at 10:30, yet F06 (ready at 10:30) waited 15 min until 10:45.
- In F07, F1 finished at 10:40, yet F07 (ready at 10:55) waited 15 min until 11:10.
- In F12, F2 finished at 12:00, yet F12 (ready at 12:10) waited 10 min until 12:20.

This empirical pattern proves that $W_{downstream}$ is **not a classical $M/M/c$ queue caused by server congestion**. Instead, it represents either:
- A fixed **schedule-slot policy** (e.g., Release Decisions scheduled only on the hour or half-hour: 10:00, 10:45, 11:10, 11:40, 12:20).
- A **batch physical transport / cooling / decontamination window** required before final inspection.
- A **manual notification / document sign-off lag**.
Inferring that adding final-station servers (e.g., adding F3) will reduce flow time without reforming this procedural dispatch policy is a dangerous modeling error.

### 5. Sample Size and Temporal Scope
The dataset comprises $N=12$ cases observed over a single 4.5-hour morning window (07:55 - 12:40). It cannot capture diurnal shifts, operator fatigue, weekly volume cycles, or long-term steady-state behavior. Any structural intervention must be validated against a statistically representative multi-week dataset.
