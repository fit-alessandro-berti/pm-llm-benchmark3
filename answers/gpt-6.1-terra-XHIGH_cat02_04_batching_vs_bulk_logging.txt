# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

Define observed waiting as `start − queue_arrival`, observed elapsed service as `complete − start`, and recording lag as `recorded_at − complete`. These are only proxies for actual waiting, active work, and execution-to-database lag when the field semantics and clocks support that interpretation. Elapsed service includes pauses and multitasking; summing overlapping case durations double-counts wall-clock occupancy. Utilization also requires an observation window and the relevant capacity; a case fragment alone is insufficient. Throughput counts cases completed per period, but a submission timestamp may shift completions between periods without changing a longer-horizon count. Conformance judgments require actual business rules and should not treat a temporal batch category as a violation or proof of one.

- **G1:** The displayed mean wait is 4.25 minutes and each elapsed interval is 3 minutes. If a real shared cycle, these are reasonable item-level waiting and elapsed cycle proxies, subject to synchronization; four times three equals 12 item-minutes but only three cell-minutes of occupancy. Summing durations as machine busy time inflates utilization fourfold. A 3-minute four-item cycle is not four independent 3-minute serial services. If the PLC copies group times over serial execution, individual waits, services and completion timing may be biased in either direction. A completion spike may reflect real batch release or logging, affecting short-window throughput and apparent bottleneck location. Conformance should allow an authorized multi-item fixture if confirmed.
- **G2:** The displayed mean wait is 12.25 minutes and mean elapsed service 17.5 minutes. If 10:00 is only group setup and inspection starts later, individual waiting is understated and service overstated by the displacement of each true start; these errors need not apply to a shared setup that is itself legitimate service. Summed intervals are 70 minutes against 27 minutes of group elapsed time, so treating them as one person's busy minutes overstates utilization. Completion buttons support an actual 10:08–10:27 completion sequence if timely, but not the rate of active work or a bottleneck diagnosis. Overlap-based rules may falsely report concurrent individual work.
- **G3:** Displayed mean wait is 19.5 minutes and mean elapsed service 16.75 minutes. If starts are valid and work finishes before the 11:30 form submission, service is overstated; wait is not necessarily biased in a known direction. If the relevant outcome is formal release, however, 11:30 may be the correct endpoint and earlier work completion is a different measure. Summing 67 case-minutes as one person's 28-minute elapsed span inflates occupancy, though neither number establishes active work. An artificial completion spike can misplace throughput and bottlenecks; a real common release has different implications. Common-end conformance cannot distinguish review completion from release without the rule and event definitions.
- **G4:** Displayed waits rise from 7 to 19 minutes, mean 13, while each elapsed service is 4 minutes. If scanner start and end reflect execution, these are plausible, and five adjacent intervals imply 20 minutes of occupied elapsed time in that interval. The apparent sustained serial workload could indicate a local constraint but does not establish a system bottleneck without capacity, demand and other work areas. A group of arrivals before service and adjacent work is consistent with either a deliberate batch or normal backlog clearance; a rule requiring a batch identifier cannot be assessed from adjacency alone. Missing pauses or scan conventions can still bias all metrics.
- **G5:** Displayed mean wait is 15.25 minutes; mean elapsed service is 17.5. Attribution to a single worker produces false individual overload and conflates parallel work with multitasking. The 70 summed case-minutes occupy a 30-minute union of logged intervals, neither of which is total verified analyst effort. All four intervals overlap from 13:12 to 13:20, while at most three analysts share the login: if the intervals truly represent this pool's active work continuously, at least one interval or capacity assumption must be wrong. They may instead represent open cases with interruptions or work by other actors. Case-level waits and elapsed times can still be meaningful if validated, but worker utilization, bottleneck and concurrency conformance require individual identity and active-work events.
- **G6:** Observed waits range from 35 to 175 minutes, averaging about 103.43, and all observed services are zero. If real work began before 17:00 and the form copied submission time into both lifecycle fields, time spent doing work is wrongly classified as waiting, inflating wait and collapsing service to zero. Some genuine waiting or a genuine instantaneous close may also exist; neither component is separately identifiable. A near-zero duration makes resource utilization appear implausibly low while the 17:00 completion spike shifts apparent throughput to that minute. The resulting waiting-time bottleneck or rule violation can be a logging artifact. One-minute precision alone does not quantify a useful execution interval when the fields may refer to submission.
- **G7:** Four of five observed waits are negative, and the mean is −4.2 minutes. These are not credible exact queue waits under a same-clock causal model; quarter-hour rounding and clock mismatch can create them. The common 15-minute duration and overlapping intervals can overstate, understate or otherwise misrepresent actual item durations, active occupancy and wait; direction is not identifiable without the rounding rule. Completions on a quarter-hour boundary can manufacture a throughput spike and an apparent concurrent bottleneck. A negative-wait conformance alert is a data-quality signal, not proof of a business violation. Insertion times 14:16–14:20 are more differentiated but are not necessarily execution completion times.
- **G8:** Displayed waits are 38, 43, 48, 54 and 58 minutes, mean 48.2; each observed elapsed service is 7 minutes. If scanner events and clocks are valid, these indicate an existing local queue and 35 minutes of continuous triage, not necessarily 35 minutes of total worker activity over a larger window. The queue could make triage appear to be a bottleneck, but the safety check, staffing, other demand and capacity must be measured before attributing the wait to downtime or identifying a system bottleneck. Short-window throughput is regular after 15:45 under the scan interpretation; a start-delay conformance finding needs a defined SLA, synchronized clocks and the correct start semantics.

For G1, G4 and G8 the stated source granularity is one second, but displayed lifecycle values happen to fall on minute boundaries. This is an observed alignment, not evidence that the true precision is one minute. The one-minute or quarter-hour displays elsewhere likewise do not establish accuracy or a specific rounding direction.

# 4. PM4Py and pandas analysis

The following is self-contained and uses pandas 2.x and a PM4Py version exposing the shown high-level APIs. It retains all source rows for diagnostics, includes suspect but syntactically valid rows in batch discovery, and reports any invalid or missing lifecycle rows separately. The synthetic UTC convention avoids timezone-naive arithmetic; it does not assert the sources were synchronized. The activity-resource pair uniquely identifies each supplied cluster. Pairwise overlap is within each resource and cluster, with touching intervals excluded; the first-start queue is a fragment-local snapshot, not the total system queue. `minute_aligned` and `quarter_aligned` are observed lifecycle alignment fractions, not inferred accuracy.

`merge_distance` is in seconds and controls joining nearby intervals, while `min_batch_size` changes the required group size. The start timestamp is explicitly supplied through the dataframe's PM4Py properties because this high-level `discover_batches` interface has no start-key argument. With the tested implementation, the 0/60-second settings produce simultaneous for G1/G6/G7, start for G2, end for G3, sequential for G4/G8 and concurrent for G5 at size 2; the four-item groups disappear at size 5. G6's zero-length intervals have no positive pairwise overlap even though PM4Py labels their identical boundaries simultaneous. The categories are compared as temporal patterns; the diagnostic table preserves the data-quality flags needed to interpret them.

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

**Identity sensitivity.** If each non-pool resource is truly one worker, the nested intervals of G2 and G3 suggest simultaneous open cases or group-level timestamps; they do not prove simultaneous active work. G4 and G8 support one worker performing adjacent serial tasks, but do not prove intentional batching. If any of these IDs is shared, overlap can instead combine multiple workers and adjacent intervals may belong to different workers. G5 is explicitly shared: splitting records by recovered badge could eliminate apparent individual overlap or reveal actual multitasking. Conversely, treating the pool as one worker produces invalid per-person utilization. RBT-7 is a cell identifier, not necessarily a person or a single-capacity server.

**Precision sensitivity.** Under the hypothetical exact, synchronized timestamp interpretation, G1's four intervals coincide, G4/G8 touch without positive overlap, and G7 has four starts before arrival. That interpretation describes the values but cannot justify physical conclusions from suspect fields. Under interval censoring, a displayed value represents a set of possible true times, depending on validated floor, ceiling or nearest-rounding rules and clock error. For example, *if* both G7 fields were floored to quarter-hour bins on synchronized clocks, a displayed 14:00 start would fall in [14:00, 14:15), and a displayed 14:15 completion in [14:15, 14:30); other rules yield different bounds. The prompt expressly does not specify the rule, so those are illustrative assumptions, not established bounds. Impose true `arrival ≤ start ≤ completion` only after establishing synchronized clocks and causal semantics; do not clamp negative waits to zero. Measurement uncertainty makes equality and adjacency less decisive and can change overlap classification. Coarse values may produce a logical impossibility under one rule but be consistent under another. For G6, uncertainty about whether the timestamps represent execution is much larger than a one-minute precision interval; interval-censoring around 17:00 alone cannot recover real service.

At least three observational ambiguities are central:

1. **G1 versus G7:** Identical start/end intervals can represent one physical batch, copied group timestamps, or separately executed work coarsened into the same bins. Fixture information favors, but does not prove, the first in G1; documented coarsening favors the last in G7.
2. **G2:** A shared start and staggered completions fit common setup followed by serial inspection, concurrent active work, or a group-start logging convention. Overlap of the stored intervals cannot distinguish these.
3. **G3 and G6:** A common end may be a real common release or a bulk form action after separate work; G6's identical start and end may additionally be a form default. The small database lag measures insertion after the displayed completion, not after physical work.
4. **G5:** Overlap under one login can be several analysts, one analyst switching among open cases, or a combination. Four overlapping open intervals do not prove four simultaneous active workers.
5. **G4 and G8:** Adjacent serial intervals fit a deliberate sequential batch, ordinary continuous flow, or clearance of a queue after lost capacity. G8's safety check supports a testable causal hypothesis only after clock and capacity validation.

The sensitivity run in the code probes merge distances and size thresholds, not rounding or identity reassignment. Stronger analysis would recompute waits and overlaps from defensible time bounds and reconstructed identities, then report ranges and the proportion of classifications that change. Avoid manufacturing exact individual timestamps from equal group timestamps.

# 6. Data-collection redesign

1. **Define and capture execution events.** Record item-level actual work start, work finish and release as separate events with case and activity IDs. For group setup and submission, record explicit group-start, group-finish and form-submission events rather than copying them into item execution fields. Instrument scanners and PLCs at the operational action and document what each event means.
2. **Keep recording and clock provenance.** Preserve source event time, database insertion time, clock domain, timezone, synchronization status, offset estimates and ingestion path. Synchronize queue, execution and safety systems, monitor drift, and retain corrections alongside immutable raw values. This makes G8's proposed safety-to-queue linkage testable and exposes delayed submissions such as G6.
3. **Capture accountable identities and capacity.** Record badge-level performer, cell/workstation and shared-login identity separately, with access controls appropriate to the workplace. Record staffed capacity, fixture slots, machine availability, downtime and safety-check intervals; distinguish a group account from a single server.
4. **Make group membership explicit.** Assign a batch or fixture ID, membership and membership-change times; distinguish intentional physical batch processing, common setup, common approval and bulk submission. Capture item-to-slot mapping and partial completions where applicable.
5. **Record pauses and resumptions.** Collect suspend/resume, handoff and reason codes so elapsed case time can be separated from active work and capacity loss. Capture full queue arrivals and departures, including work outside these fragments, to estimate queue lengths and bottlenecks.
6. **Document timestamp quality.** Store precision, rounding/truncation direction, expected accuracy and clock uncertainty per field and source version; retain higher-resolution raw values where available. Validate event-order constraints and surface anomalies, without silently replacing missing or negative values. Pilot the new instrumentation against direct observation and reconcile individual, group and submission events before using it for performance targets.

# 7. Final adjudication

High confidence is warranted for **the observed temporal/data patterns** in G2, G4, G6 and G7: respectively group start, adjacent serial intervals, bulk-form timestamps and coarse timestamp effects. G1 is a strong physical-batch candidate, and G5 is definitively a shared login, but neither proves the detailed allocation of active work. G8 clearly shows a recorded backlog and serial clearance, while safety-check causality remains unproven. G3 is consistent with submission batching or common release and requires semantic validation.

For execution-based queue analysis, exclude **G6** from point estimates of wait and service unless independent execution times are recovered; its arrivals and submission times can still support a separately defined arrival-to-submission measure. Exclude **G7** from exact point estimates and negative-wait summaries; use interval-censored estimates only after establishing a defensible rounding and clock model, otherwise leave them unidentified. Treat **G2** as group-start data, not validated individual starts, and **G3** as a possibly submission-based end; report sensitivity or exclude the affected item-level metrics until validated. G5 may contribute to case-level elapsed-time analysis if its lifecycle semantics are verified, but not individual-worker utilization under the shared ID.

Before operational performance conclusions, validate the PLC and fixture semantics in G1; the individual-start meaning in G2; review, release and submission boundaries in G3; scanner coverage in G4 and G8; analyst identity and active-work semantics in G5; execution versus submission in G6; and rounding plus clocks in G7. In G8, establish synchronized safety and capacity history before claiming that downtime caused the backlog. None of these isolated fragments establishes a system-wide bottleneck, utilization percentage or conformance result by itself.
