# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

For case \(c\) and branch \(i\in\{A,B,C\}\), let:

- \(a_c\): process arrival
- \(r_c\): fork release
- \(s_{ci},e_{ci}\): branch start and completion
- \(f^s_c,f^e_c\): final-activity start and completion

Define:

\[
U_c=r_c-a_c
\]

as upstream elapsed time before fork release.

For each branch:

\[
Q_{ci}=s_{ci}-r_c,\qquad
S_{ci}=e_{ci}-s_{ci},\qquad
L_{ci}=Q_{ci}+S_{ci}=e_{ci}-r_c.
\]

Here \(Q_{ci}\) is branch queue waiting, \(S_{ci}\) active service, and \(L_{ci}\) elapsed branch latency from release through completion.

Under the stated AND-join rule:

\[
J_c=\max_i e_{ci}
\]

is `join_ready`, and the fork-join stage elapsed time is:

\[
P_c=J_c-r_c=\max_i L_{ci}.
\]

A branch is critical if \(e_{ci}=J_c\). Ties should be retained as ties rather than assigned arbitrarily for analysis.

Branch-specific synchronization waiting is:

\[
W^{sync}_{ci}=J_c-e_{ci}=P_c-L_{ci}.
\]

It describes how long an already completed branch waits for the other branches. It is diagnostically useful, but synchronization waits are concurrent rather than additive components of case elapsed time. In particular,

\[
\sum_i W^{sync}_{ci}
=\sum_i(J_c-e_{ci})
=3J_c-\sum_i e_{ci},
\]

which is generally not a distinct elapsed interval on the case timeline. For F02, for example, A waits 10 minutes and C waits 35 minutes, but the fork-join stage is 60 minutes—not \(60+10+35\). Adding all synchronization waits to the critical-path decomposition would count overlapping clock time more than once.

Downstream queueing and final service are:

\[
D_c=f^s_c-J_c,\qquad F_c=f^e_c-f^s_c.
\]

Total case flow time is:

\[
T_c=f^e_c-a_c
    =U_c+P_c+D_c+F_c
    =U_c+\max_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci})+D_c+F_c.
\]

This is the non-double-counting decomposition. The individual branch queues, services, and synchronization waits explain the parallel stage, but they must not all be summed into total flow time.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

“Completion latency” below is queue plus service from fork release. Tail is the observed maximum, appropriate for this small 12-case sample rather than a stable population-tail estimate.

| branch | mean service | service range | mean queue | queue max | mean completion latency | latency max | critical cases | critical frequency | mean sync wait |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 50.0 | 50–50 | 0.0 | 0 | 50.0 | 50 | 2 | 16.7% | 11.0 |
| B | 37.1 | 30–45 | 17.5 | 30 | 54.6 | 75 | 8 | 66.7% | 4.3 |
| C | 20.2 | 18–30 | 10.4 | 35 | 30.6 | 57 | 2 | 16.7% | 30.4 |

There are no observed critical-branch ties. Mean fork-join elapsed is 61.0 minutes; its observed maximum is 75 minutes. Mean downstream queueing is 14.7 minutes, and final service is uniformly 20 minutes.

### Influence ranking

1. **B is the strongest observed controller.** It is critical in 8 of 12 cases, has the largest mean completion latency (54.6 minutes), and reaches the joint-highest latency tail of 75 minutes. Its influence comes from queue plus service: its mean service is lower than A’s, but its 17.5-minute mean queue pushes it past A frequently.
2. **A is the stable baseline constraint.** A has the largest mean service (50 minutes), but not the highest completion influence because it has no observed queue and is critical only twice. Nevertheless, every branch redesign must beat A’s fixed 50-minute latency before producing a case-level improvement.
3. **C is usually noncritical but episodically important.** Its mean completion latency is low, yet C controls F04 and F08 because of queues of 35 and 25 minutes. C’s mean synchronization wait of 30.4 minutes shows substantial early completion in most cases, but does not rule out an administrative queue tail.

A useful measure of a branch’s **observed removable critical excess** is:

\[
E_{ci}=\max(0,L_{ci}-\max_{j\ne i}L_{cj}).
\]

Summed over cases, the observed excess is approximately:

- A: 15 minutes
- B: 130 minutes
- C: 12 minutes

This is an upper-bound-like diagnostic for branch-specific acceleration with all other completion times held fixed. It reinforces B’s ranking, while showing that A and C are closer to one another in aggregate critical excess than their mean services suggest.

The observed maximum join-ready contribution is shared by B’s F07/F12 latency of 75 minutes. A’s maximum is 50; C’s is 57. Thus B dominates both average completion influence and the observed fork-join tail.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

The numerical counterfactuals below are deterministic, case-local calculations. They hold arrivals, other branch timings, downstream final starts, and service calendars fixed unless explicitly changed. That makes them first-order estimates, not queueing-system simulations.

## Counterfactual 1: reduce A service time by 20%

A service falls from 50 to 40 minutes, with A start unchanged.

- Direct join improvement occurs only for F01 and F11, where A is currently critical.
- F01 saves 5 minutes at the join because B then finishes at 08:55.
- F11 saves 10 minutes because B then finishes at 11:20.
- The other 10 cases save zero at the join because B or C remains critical.
- Aggregate potential join saving: **15 minutes**, or **1.25 minutes per case**.
- Maximum per-case potential saving: **10 minutes**.

If historical `final_start` is held fixed, these 15 minutes merely increase downstream waiting and observed `final_end` does not move. If F can pull work forward whenever capacity is available, some or all of the 15 minutes may become total-flow savings. Therefore the realized benefit is bounded between 0 and 15 aggregate minutes in this static timestamp model.

Secondary effects may include lower A utilization and earlier A resource availability, but also more A synchronization waiting. The redesign does little for the 75-minute fork-join tail because F07 and F12 are B-critical.

## Counterfactual 2: reduce B queue delay by 50%

B start is moved halfway from its observed start toward `fork_release`; B service duration is unchanged.

| cases | join saving |
|---|---:|
| F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, F12 | 10–15 minutes each |
| F02 | 10 minutes |
| F01, F04, F08, F11 | 0 |

Per-case savings are:

- F02 10
- F03 10
- F05 10
- F06 15
- F07 15
- F09 15
- F10 5
- F12 15
- all others 0

Aggregate potential join saving is **95 minutes**, or **7.92 minutes per case**; maximum is **15 minutes**. Eight cases benefit and four have zero benefit because A or C remains—or already was—critical.

Some improved cases switch criticality to A:

- F03, F05, and F10 become A-critical.
- F07, F09, and F12 remain B-critical after the 50% queue reduction.
- F06 becomes an A/B tie under exact minute arithmetic.
- F02 remains B-critical.

This is evidence of an emerging A bottleneck. As above, if final starts cannot move, total-flow savings are masked and become extra downstream queueing. If the final station can pull cases earlier, up to 95 aggregate minutes could translate to flow-time improvement.

Likely secondary effects include increased B resource pressure earlier in the day and possible displacement of queueing to other cases. Because B resource identities and capacity are not supplied, the feasibility of halving every B queue cannot be established.

## Counterfactual 3: eliminate observed downstream queueing

Assume final capacity or dispatch redesign permits F to start at `join_ready`, with the same 20-minute service time and no new contention.

- Every case benefits because every observed downstream queue is positive.
- Per-case savings equal the observed downstream queues: 8–20 minutes.
- Aggregate saving: **176 minutes**
- Mean saving: **14.67 minutes per case**
- Maximum saving: **20 minutes**
- Mean flow time would fall from **115.25** to **100.58 minutes** under this idealized assumption.

Unlike the branch counterfactuals, this intervention directly changes final starts and therefore produces modeled total-flow savings. However, “eliminate all downstream queueing” is an optimistic upper bound. With two final resources and overlapping demand, simply adding one resource does not mechanically imply zero waiting; a resource-calendar simulation would be needed.

Secondary effects include lower final utilization, more idle capacity between arrivals, and greater exposure of B as the dominant remaining bottleneck. If observed delays are caused by a release policy rather than resource congestion, capacity investment may have little effect and dispatch-policy change would be the relevant intervention.

### Cross-counterfactual comparison

| counterfactual | cases with positive direct benefit | aggregate potential saving | mean per case | maximum |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A service 20% | 2/12 | 15 min | 1.25 | 10 |
| B queue 50% | 8/12 | 95 min | 7.92 | 15 |
| Eliminate downstream queue | 12/12 | 176 min | 14.67 | 20 |

For branch changes, “saving” is potential join advancement; realized final-completion saving depends on final-stage pull-forward. The final-stage scenario directly advances completion by assumption.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

## Recommendation 1: pilot B queue reduction rather than faster B processing

B’s mean service is only 37.1 minutes, below A’s 50, but queue plus service makes B critical in two-thirds of cases. A focused pilot should investigate earlier dispatch to B, reservation at fork release, workload leveling, or removal of pre-start dependencies.

- **Throughput:** Reduced B queue could release cases to the join sooner and may improve throughput if B waiting reflects avoidable scheduling loss.
- **Flow-time tail:** This targets the 75-minute fork-join tail in F07 and F12; the modeled 50% queue reduction saves 15 minutes in each.
- **Utilization:** Earlier starts may raise or redistribute B utilization. If B is already capacity-constrained, queue reduction without capacity or sequencing changes may only move waits between cases.
- **New bottleneck:** A becomes critical in several improved cases, while C remains an episodic constraint.
- **Synchronization:** A and C synchronization waits should generally fall when B is late, but aggressive B acceleration can instead cause B to wait for A.
- **Pilot/measurement first:** Capture B resource identity, availability calendars, worklist position, dispatch decisions, interruptions, and competing workload. Pilot on a subset of releases and measure join-ready advancement—not merely B service time.

## Recommendation 2: test final-stage pull/dispatch policy before purchasing capacity

The observed downstream queue averages 14.7 minutes and applies to every case, making it the largest modeled immediate opportunity. However, the two-resource final station’s actual availability is not reconstructible from these cases alone.

- **Throughput:** If F1/F2 are genuinely saturated, added capacity or schedule changes may increase throughput. If the delay is policy-driven, a pull rule that starts the next join-ready case may achieve gains without capital expenditure.
- **Flow-time tail:** Removing observed downstream waits would cut 8–20 minutes from every case and reduce the maximum observed flow time from 130 to 115 minutes under the idealized calculation.
- **Utilization:** Extra capacity may reduce utilization and create idle time; a dispatch-policy improvement could retain utilization while reducing avoidable gaps.
- **New bottleneck:** Once downstream waits fall, B’s fork-join delays dominate more visibly. A may then become the next constraint after B improvements.
- **Synchronization:** Final redesign does not change branch synchronization directly, but it prevents earlier join completion from being absorbed into a longer final queue.
- **Pilot/measurement first:** Record F1/F2 availability, setup/changeover, breaks, eligibility constraints, priority rules, non-study workload, and the time a case becomes dispatchable. Trial a join-ready pull queue before adding permanent capacity.

A broad A speed-up is not among the first two recommendations: despite A’s largest mean service, the modeled 20% reduction helps only two cases and does not address the observed tail.

"""
df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(raw))
date = "2026-05-19 "
time_cols = [c for c in df.columns if c not in ("case", "final_resource")]
for col in time_cols:
    df[col] = pd.to_datetime(date + df[col], format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M")

def mins(delta):
    return delta.dt.total_seconds() / 60

branches = ["A", "B", "C"]
df["upstream_elapsed"] = mins(df["fork_release"] - df["arrival"])

for b in branches:
    df[f"{b}_queue"] = mins(df[f"{b}_start"] - df["fork_release"])
    df[f"{b}_service"] = mins(df[f"{b}_end"] - df[f"{b}_start"])
    df[f"{b}_latency"] = mins(df[f"{b}_end"] - df["fork_release"])

df["join_ready"] = df[[f"{b}_end" for b in branches]].max(axis=1)
df["fork_join_elapsed"] = mins(df["join_ready"] - df["fork_release"])
for b in branches:
    df[f"{b}_sync_wait"] = mins(df["join_ready"] - df[f"{b}_end"])

# Preserve analytical ties as strings such as "A/B".
df["critical_branch"] = df.apply(
    lambda r: "/".join(b for b in branches if r[f"{b}_end"] == r["join_ready"]),
    axis=1
)
# Deterministic single-label version, if required by downstream software:
# ties are broken in lexical branch order A, B, C.
df["critical_branch_deterministic"] = df.apply(
    lambda r: next(b for b in branches if r[f"{b}_end"] == r["join_ready"]),
    axis=1
)

df["downstream_queue"] = mins(df["final_start"] - df["join_ready"])
df["final_service"] = mins(df["final_end"] - df["final_start"])
df["total_flow_time"] = mins(df["final_end"] - df["arrival"])

case_cols = [
    "case", "upstream_elapsed",
    "A_queue", "A_service", "B_queue", "B_service", "C_queue", "C_service",
    "join_ready", "critical_branch", "downstream_queue",
    "final_service", "total_flow_time"
]
case_out = df[case_cols].copy()
case_out["join_ready"] = case_out["join_ready"].dt.strftime("%H:%M")
print("\nCASE DECOMPOSITION")
print(case_out.to_string(index=False))

summary_rows = []
for b in branches:
    summary_rows.append({
        "branch": b,
        "mean_service": df[f"{b}_service"].mean(),
        "max_service": df[f"{b}_service"].max(),
        "mean_queue": df[f"{b}_queue"].mean(),
        "max_queue": df[f"{b}_queue"].max(),
        "mean_latency": df[f"{b}_latency"].mean(),
        "max_latency": df[f"{b}_latency"].max(),
        "critical_count": df["critical_branch"].str.split("/").apply(
            lambda x: b in x
        ).sum(),
        "mean_sync_wait": df[f"{b}_sync_wait"].mean()
    })

print(pd.DataFrame(summary_rows).round(2).to_string(index=False))

def evaluate_branch_cf(name, new_ends):
    """Potential join savings; historical final starts are not moved."""
    ends = pd.DataFrame({b: new_ends.get(b, df[f"{b}_end"]) for b in branches})
    new_join = ends.max(axis=1)
    saving = mins(df["join_ready"] - new_join).clip(lower=0)
    crit = ends.apply(
        lambda r: "/".join(sorted([b for b in branches if r[b] == r.max()])),
        axis=1
    )
    return pd.DataFrame({
        "case": df["case"], "scenario": name,
        "old_join": df["join_ready"], "new_join": new_join,
        "new_critical": crit, "saving_min": saving
    })

# CF2: B queue -50%, preserving B service.

b_new_end = b_new_start + (df["B_end"] - df["B_start"])

# CF3: idealized zero downstream queue: F begins at join_ready.
cf_f = pd.DataFrame({
    "case": df["case"],
    "scenario": "zero downstream queue",
    "old_join": df["join_ready"],
    "new_join": df["join_ready"],
    "new_critical": df["critical_branch"],
    "saving_min": df["downstream_queue"]
})

cf_all = pd.concat([cf_a, cf_b, cf_f], ignore_index=True)
print("\nCOUNTERFACTUAL PER-CASE SAVINGS")
print(cf_all[["case", "scenario", "new_critical", "saving_min"]]
      .to_string(index=False))
print("\nCOUNTERFACTUAL AGGREGATES")
print(cf_all.groupby("scenario")["saving_min"]
      .agg(cases_positive=lambda x: (x > 0).sum(),

# Build one interval event per branch/final activity: 12 cases x 4 = 48 rows.
events = []
for _, r in df.iterrows():

# 7. Limits of inference

- **Causality is not identified.** The timestamps establish associations and arithmetic criticality, not why queues arose or whether a proposed intervention would cause the calculated improvement.
- **Branch resource capacity is unknown.** No A, B, or C resource identities, calendars, parallel capacities, breaks, or competing workloads are supplied. Queue delay cannot be attributed confidently to understaffing, batching, priority rules, or upstream information readiness.
- **Preemption cannot be observed.** Each branch has one start and one end. “Service” is elapsed interval time and may include interruptions, pauses, setup, or inactive residence. It is not proven to be continuous active labor or machine processing.
- **Final waiting may not be congestion.** Calling `final_start - join_ready` downstream queueing relies on the AND-join and immediate-eligibility assumptions. The interval could instead reflect release windows, batching, priority policy, missing approvals, resource eligibility, planned schedules, or unlogged work.
- **The two-resource final system is incomplete.** Only final resources for these 12 cases are shown. Other cases, downtime, shifts, and setup requirements are unknown, so utilization and the capacity benefit of a third resource cannot be calculated reliably.
- **Counterfactual queue interactions are omitted.** Moving one branch or final activity earlier may conflict with another case’s resource use. The estimates preserve other timestamps and therefore do not constitute a resource-constrained simulation.
- **Timestamp resolution matters.** Data are accurate to one minute. Near-ties within a minute may be observationally indistinguishable, even though no exact recorded critical ties occur.
- **The sample is small and covers one morning.** Means and maxima are descriptive of these 12 cases, not stable long-run distributions. Tail claims refer only to the observed tail.
- **Fork semantics are assumed from the scenario.** The analysis treats `fork_release` as simultaneous permission for all branches and `join_ready` as the maximum branch end. Hidden branch prerequisites or alternative join rules would invalidate the decomposition.
- **Throughput cannot be estimated directly.** Case arrivals, branch completions, and final completions over this limited window do not provide a steady-state throughput or utilization model.
