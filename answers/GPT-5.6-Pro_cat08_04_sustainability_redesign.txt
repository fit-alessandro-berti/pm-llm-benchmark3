## 1. System boundary and assumptions

The environmental boundary should be an **operational lifecycle boundary from `Receive order` through delivery, closure, or cancellation**, extended far enough to capture consequences created by the process: production and packaging rework, repeat shipments, returns where available, and items produced in batches but not shipped. It is not a complete product lifecycle assessment. The evidence supports measuring operational electricity, transport emissions, material scrap, packaging material, rework, and obsolescence, while using service, quality, and resilience as binding constraints. 

| Boundary dimension     | Included                                                                                                                                            | Outside the current evidence or still uncertain                                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Process span           | Order receipt, availability decisions, picking, production, batch waiting, inspection, packaging, shipping, confirmation, closure, and cancellation | Customer use of the spare part and its end-of-life                                                                                                                      |
| Energy                 | Logged `energy_kwh` for CNC, heat treatment, inspection, emergency production, rework, and any other metered events                                 | Embodied energy of machines and facilities; unmetered warehouse energy                                                                                                  |
| Transport              | Logged `transport_kgco2e` for parcel ground, parcel air, consolidated truck, and dedicated van, including additional shipment legs when represented | Carrier vehicle manufacture; customer collection trips; empty van returns unless separately obtained                                                                    |
| Materials              | `material_scrap_kg`, `package_material_kg`, packaging rework, and produced items that become obsolete                                               | Full embodied carbon and toxicity of different alloys and packaging materials unless emission factors are added                                                         |
| Quality consequences   | Production rework, packaging rework, and damage-related returns where return events are available                                                   | Unrecorded field failures, warranty replacements, and customer-side disposal                                                                                            |
| Service and resilience | Cycle time, promised lead time, late delivery, cancellation, route availability, emergency capacity, and segment-specific service outcomes          | The economic or environmental cost of customer downtime; it must therefore be treated as a service constraint rather than claimed as a quantified environmental benefit |

The primary functional unit should eventually be **one conforming line item successfully delivered**, supplemented by an order-level view for customer service. The present log is flattened by order even though orders contain one or more line items. Consequently, an order with five parts is not directly comparable with a single-part order on energy, scrap, package mass, or transport intensity. Until line-item quantities are restored, results should be reported both per order and within sufficiently homogeneous product or order-size groups.

The following assumptions govern the recommendations:

1. **Environmental dimensions should not be collapsed prematurely into one score.** Kilowatt-hours, kilograms of scrap, packaging mass, and transport kgCO2e should remain visible separately unless credible energy and material lifecycle factors are supplied.

2. **Service, quality, and resilience are feasibility constraints.** A lower-impact alternative is eligible only when it is sufficiently likely to meet the promise, complies with Q1, and preserves R1. Among eligible alternatives, management can then choose the environmentally preferable one.

3. **The SP1 threshold is applied prospectively.** The relevant probability is the on-time probability estimated at the routing decision using information then available—not a retrospective judgment after the outcome is known.

4. **A missing SP2 justification is a governance failure, not proof that the van was operationally unnecessary.** The 31% missing-event finding should trigger better decision logging and review, not automatic condemnation of every affected shipment.

5. **Observed path differences are not automatically causal.** For example, CNC_B’s 18% rework rate versus CNC_A’s 9% may partly reflect different parts, urgency, operators, or queue conditions. The difference is actionable enough for a controlled routing pilot, but not enough to route every job to CNC_A immediately.

6. **Rework and obsolescence are part of the environmental outcome.** A nominally efficient large batch is not environmentally efficient if it creates unshipped items, rework, late orders that require air freight, or cancellations followed by replacement orders.

7. **The listed DFG is evidence about frequent paths, not a complete flow balance.** Its endpoint and intermediate counts do not fully reconcile to all 12,000 orders, and partial shipments may generate multiple shipment events. Case-level and line-item-level reconstruction must precede precise impact attribution.

## 2. Sustainability-related process indicators

Indicators should be calculated by `case_type`, `customer_type`, product or part family, route, machine group, batch-size band, expedite status, and relevant path. Median and p90 outcomes are needed alongside averages because the main risks occur in the tail.

| Indicator                                             | Definition and process relevance                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Delivered-unit energy intensity**                   | Total `energy_kwh` divided by conforming delivered items. Until item quantities are available, use energy per order within homogeneous order-size groups. Break down by CNC, heat treatment, inspection, emergency production, and rework.                                    |
| **Rework energy share**                               | Energy consumed after entry into `Rework production`, repeated CNC or inspection activities, or packaging rework, divided by total energy. This exposes energy hidden inside apparently normal completed orders.                                                              |
| **Transport carbon intensity**                        | Total `transport_kgco2e` per delivered item and per completed order, including every leg of a partial shipment and any return leg represented in the log.                                                                                                                     |
| **Avoidable high-carbon shipment rate**               | Share of air or dedicated-van shipments for which a lower-impact route had an estimated on-time probability above 85% and no valid exception was logged. Report SP1 air cases separately from SP2 dedicated-van cases.                                                        |
| **Material yield and scrap intensity**                | Good delivered output divided by material input where input is available; otherwise `material_scrap_kg` per good delivered item. Segment by machine, part family, rework status, and emergency status.                                                                        |
| **Packaging intensity and packaging-rework burden**   | `package_material_kg` per successful delivery, plus the package mass discarded or replaced through `Rework packaging`. Separate standard and reinforced packaging and identify route/geometry incompatibilities.                                                              |
| **First-pass completion rate**                        | Share of orders reaching delivery without production rework, packaging rework, repeat shipment caused by failure, or damage return. This is the principal combined quality and environmental-yield indicator.                                                                 |
| **Batch eco-service frontier**                        | For every `batch_id`, jointly report batch size, energy per produced item, energy per shipped item, median/p90 batch wait, late-delivery rate, and obsolescence. The distinction between produced and shipped items prevents large obsolete batches from appearing efficient. |
| **Wait-cap violation rate**                           | Share of CNC and heat-treatment cases held beyond the segment-specific maximum batching wait. Report separately for Regular, Service-contract, and true Critical-downtime cases.                                                                                              |
| **Obsolescence rate and burden**                      | Share of batch-produced items not shipped within 14 days, together with their associated energy, scrap, and any eventual disposal or reprocessing. The observed 6% rate should not be hidden within production efficiency.                                                    |
| **Service guardrail set**                             | Median and p90 cycle time, late-delivery rate, cancellation due to delay, and promise-date adherence by segment. Overall averages are insufficient because Critical-downtime and Service-contract cases have different constraints.                                           |
| **Partial-shipment burden**                           | Orders and line items receiving multiple outbound legs, extra package material, incremental transport kgCO2e, backorder time, cancellation rate, and customer contacts.                                                                                                       |
| **Quality indicators**                                | Machine- and part-specific first-pass yield, inspection escape rate, production-rework loops, packaging damage returns, and Q1 conformance.                                                                                                                                   |
| **Resilience indicators**                             | R1 compliance, number of independently available routes for Service-contract cases, carrier and machine concentration, disruption-period service performance, shared-lane utilization, and recovery time after route or machine loss.                                         |
| **On-time, first-pass, lower-impact completion rate** | Share of orders that meet the promise, avoid rework/return, satisfy Q1/R1, and use the lowest-impact feasible route or production option. This prevents a team from improving one environmental metric by degrading the rest of the process.                                  |

## 3. Intervention options

### 3.1 S1 and S4 — Slack-aware shipping recommender with priority picking

**Target path or segment.** Standard-stock Service-contract orders, especially `Pick from stock -> Package standard -> Ship air`, and the 22% of this segment violating SP1.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** At availability check, calculate remaining promise slack using current pick queues, packaging time, route reliability, cut-off times, customer segment, and disruption status. Where ground still has an on-time probability above 85%, use priority picking followed by ground rather than consuming the slack in the warehouse and then recovering with air. The evidence suggests a large transport opportunity: the selected stock-air path averages 18.5 kgCO2e, compared with 3.6 for the stock-ground path, and air is approximately four to six times more carbon intensive per order.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Priority picking can retain much of the service benefit without the transport burden. Air remains available when slack is genuinely insufficient or the ground network is disrupted. R1 requires an alternative route, so the change must not become exclusive dependence on one ground carrier. The major risk is a poorly calibrated on-time prediction that systematically underestimates ground delays.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Log the decision timestamp, routes considered, predicted arrival distribution for each route, priority-pick recommendation, selected route, actual arrival, disruption state, and override reason. Monitor calibration by segment and lane, SP1 violations, emissions avoided, and late deliveries among orders diverted from air.

### 3.2 S5 — Twice-daily consolidation for eligible stock orders

**Target path or segment.** Non-urgent Standard-stock Regular orders and Service-contract stock orders with more than the required consolidation and transport slack.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Route eligible packages to two scheduled consolidated-truck departures instead of separate parcel-ground consignments. The observed transport reduction is approximately 35–55% relative to individual ground parcels.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Consolidation introduces a documented 12–24-hour wait. It is therefore suitable only when the wait fits within the promise with a buffer. A missed departure must not automatically trigger air; the decision engine should compare the next consolidation departure with ordinary ground. Ground parcel service should remain available as the R1 fallback during consolidation or carrier disruption.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Record consolidation eligibility, arrival at consolidation queue, planned and actual departure, truck utilization, package count, consolidation waiting time, fallback route, transport kgCO2e, and final lateness. Compare eligible treated orders with equivalent parcel-ground orders.

### 3.3 S2 — Dynamic CNC and heat-treatment batching with segment-specific wait caps

**Target path or segment.** `Plan production -> Batch wait -> CNC production` and subsequent heat-treatment batching, primarily for Made-to-order orders.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Replace a fixed “wait until the preferred batch size” rule with a release policy that considers batch compatibility, current batch size, machine energy curve, remaining due-date slack, inspection capacity, and obsolescence risk. Release a batch when either its energy-efficient target is reached or its segment-specific maximum wait expires. True Critical-downtime cases should not wait for an energy target.

The energy opportunity is real: CNC_A falls from 2.8 kWh per item at batch size 1–3 to 1.3 at size 12–20. But the associated median wait rises from 4 to 38 hours, so batch size alone is the wrong objective.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Proper caps should retain much of the energy benefit while preventing extreme waiting. Service-contract Made-to-order orders need shorter caps than Regular orders. Heat-treatment batches need particularly conservative controls because batch waiting is associated with 1.7 times higher late-delivery risk. An immediate-release route must remain for urgent and disruption cases.

**Required process-mining evidence.** For each batch decision, record compatible jobs available, batch size, target and maximum wait, release reason, due-date slack, energy, machine setup, downstream inspection queue, lateness, and items not shipped within 14 days. Evaluate energy per shipped—not merely produced—item.

### 3.4 S3 — Due-date-aware routing from CNC_B to CNC_A

**Target path or segment.** Made-to-order part and machine combinations currently routed to CNC_B that exhibit high production rework, particularly variants resembling `CNC_B -> Inspection -> Rework production -> ground`.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Prefer CNC_A when its queue, capability, and due-date slack allow. The principal benefit is avoiding rework energy and scrap rather than assuming that CNC_A is intrinsically more energy efficient for every job.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** The raw rework difference—18% for CNC_B versus 9% for CNC_A—suggests quality potential, but CNC_A congestion could increase cycle time and cause later expediting. CNC_B should remain qualified as an alternative machine for urgent work, disruption recovery, and parts for which it performs well. Routing should therefore be part-family-specific rather than a blanket transfer.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Capture machine eligibility, queue length, due-date slack, part family, geometry or complexity, operator, chosen machine, routing reason, first-pass yield, scrap, energy, and downstream delays. Use matched or randomized comparisons within eligible part families to separate machine effect from case mix.

### 3.5 S7 — Risk-based inspection quality gate and protected inspection capacity

**Target path or segment.** High-rework machine/part combinations, rushed inspections, and the `Inspection -> Rework production -> CNC production` loop.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Add a risk-based inspection gate for combinations with high historical rework or defect escape. Reserve sufficient inspection capacity so that urgent cases do not receive a nominal but ineffective rushed inspection. The gate should intensify checks only where expected avoided rework exceeds the extra inspection burden.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Inspection time may rise for targeted jobs, but preventing CNC rework, packaging failures, and customer returns should reduce tail duration, energy, and material loss. The danger is moving the bottleneck into `inspection_cell`; a blanket gate would be counterproductive.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Monitor inspection queue and service time, risk-gate activation, defect detection stage, inspection escapes, rework loops, energy and scrap after inspection, return events, and late delivery. Compare end-to-end time, not only inspection duration.

### 3.6 S6 — Packaging-route compatibility check with selective Q1 enforcement

**Target path or segment.** `Package standard/reinforced -> Rework packaging`, Made-to-order parts over 8 kg, and route/geometry combinations associated with packaging damage.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Before packaging begins, evaluate part weight, geometry, fragility, selected route, handling conditions, and Q1. Made-to-order parts over 8 kg receive reinforced packaging unless inspection has produced a valid low-damage-risk certificate. This prevents both package replacement and damage-related returns.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Correct packaging should reduce rework and delays. Reinforced packaging lowers damage-related returns by 40% for heavy Made-to-order parts but doubles package material, so universal reinforcement would merely displace the impact from returns to packaging. The correct policy is selective reinforcement plus geometry-compatible standard packaging where certified.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Log package recommendation, route, part weight and geometry, inspection certificate, actual package type, material mass, packaging rework, damage return, and Q1 override. Analyze material per successful delivery, not package mass alone.

### 3.7 S8 and S9 — Separate true downtime from precautionary urgency and introduce shared emergency lanes

**Target path or segment.** Critical-downtime Service-contract orders, especially `Emergency production -> reinforced pack -> dedicated van`, which has excellent service but an average 52 kgCO2e transport burden.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** At order receipt, divide the current Critical-downtime label into at least:

* **True downtime:** equipment is stopped or imminent safety/production loss is verified.
* **Precautionary urgent:** rapid delivery is desirable, but immediate dedicated transport is not essential.

For true downtime, retain emergency production and dedicated vans when no shared option can meet the service requirement. For compatible destinations and departure windows, use shared emergency courier lanes. Precautionary cases can use stock/ground, priority ground, or shared lanes.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** A blanket van ban is unacceptable: the dedicated-van path has 14-hour median duration and 3% late delivery, whereas the lower-emission stock/ground exception has 23-hour median duration and 9% late delivery. Misclassification is therefore the principal service risk. Shared lanes can improve resilience if they add capacity, but can weaken it if all emergency work is concentrated in one shared provider.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Record urgency category, operational justification, customer asset status, route candidates, predicted delivery, van occupancy, distance, empty or repositioning distance, shared-lane departure, actual delivery, and override. Monitor SP2 separately for missing documentation and for retrospectively questionable use.

### 3.8 S11 — Reduce avoidable partial shipments through line-item-aware availability decisions

**Target path or segment.** `Pick from stock -> Partial shipment -> Backorder wait`, particularly the selected variant with 79-hour median duration, 24% late delivery, 7.2 kgCO2e, and a second outbound shipment.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** At availability check, compare:

* the incremental service value of shipping available lines immediately;
* expected backorder duration;
* the extra transport and package material from a second shipment;
* line criticality and customer type;
* probability that waiting permits a complete on-time shipment.

Hold for complete shipment when the entire order can still meet its promise. Split only when the first shipment materially avoids downtime, a contractual breach, or another documented harm.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Avoiding unnecessary splits reduces transport, packaging, handling, and customer contacts. But eliminating all splits would harm orders containing a critical available item. The decision must therefore be line-item-aware; the current flattened order log is insufficient for full automation.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Add line item, quantity, availability, criticality, expected replenishment, shipment membership, package mass, route leg, delivery time per line, customer choice, cancellation, and reorder events. Evaluate both order-complete time and first-useful-item delivery.

### 3.9 S10 — Carbon-aware promise-date negotiation for Regular Made-to-order customers

**Target path or segment.** Regular Made-to-order orders, which have 94-hour median duration, 180-hour p90 duration, and an 18% late-delivery rate.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Offer a small set of explicit promise windows, including a lower-impact window that provides enough slack for efficient CNC or heat-treatment batching and ground or consolidated transport. The customer—not the internal operation—chooses the longer promise.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Against the newly accepted promise, reliability may improve because the process no longer depends on heroic expediting. This should not be the default for Service-contract or true Critical-downtime customers. Poorly designed incentives could cause customers to submit orders earlier, split orders, or label routine work urgent.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Record promise options shown, estimated environmental differences, customer selection, revised promise, actual batch and route, final lateness, cancellation, reorder, and customer complaint. Compare environmental and behavioral outcomes, not just acceptance of the green option.

### 3.10 S12 — Sustainability and quality exception event log

**Target path or segment.** Every SP1, SP2, or Q1 decision, particularly air, dedicated-van, standard packaging for heavy Made-to-order parts, and disruptions involving partial shipments or emergency production.

**Mechanism of environmental improvement.** Insert an explicit decision event containing the rule evaluated, alternatives considered, predicted service outcomes, selected action, reason code, approver or system actor, disruption context, and expiry. This does not directly reduce emissions, but it prevents policy drift and reveals which exceptions should be redesigned.

**Likely service, quality, and resilience effect.** Legitimate exceptions remain available. The intervention improves accountability without forcing employees to choose an inappropriate low-impact option. It also avoids interpreting the discovered model’s low precision as proof of noncompliance.

**Required process-mining evidence.** Monitor exception frequency, completeness, actor and site concentration, repeat reasons, predicted versus actual service, environmental cost of overrides, and whether exceptions become permanent unofficial routes.

## 5. Rebound and displacement risks

| Risk                                                      | How the apparent improvement can worsen the wider process                                                                                                                                                       | Required control                                                                                                                 |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Batching-to-expedite rebound**                          | Larger CNC or heat-treatment batches lower energy per produced item but create waiting. Late cases may then require air, emergency production, or dedicated vans, displacing production savings into transport. | Optimize total order outcome, use wait caps, and include subsequent expedite emissions in batch evaluation.                      |
| **Batch obsolescence displacement**                       | A large batch can look efficient while 6% of batch-produced items remain unshipped after 14 days. Energy and material are shifted into inventory or disposal rather than avoided.                               | Measure energy per shipped item, forecast error, inventory age, and final disposition.                                           |
| **Ground or consolidation recovery loop**                 | Replacing air with a lower-carbon route can backfire if a missed cutoff leads to a later air shipment, duplicate parcel, customer cancellation, or emergency replacement.                                       | Make the route decision using conservative arrival distributions and track all later legs attributable to the original decision. |
| **Packaging-material rebound**                            | Applying reinforced packaging broadly would reduce some damage returns but double packaging material even for low-risk parts.                                                                                   | Enforce Q1 selectively using weight, geometry, route, and inspection certification.                                              |
| **Inspection bottleneck displacement**                    | More inspection can reduce production rework but lengthen the inspection queue. Teams may then rush inspections again or use high-carbon transport to recover time.                                             | Protect capacity, activate the gate only for high-risk combinations, and monitor end-to-end effects.                             |
| **Hidden rework displacement**                            | Pressure to reduce recorded production rework can cause marginal parts to pass inspection and fail during packaging or at the customer, moving waste outside the measured factory loop.                         | Monitor return and warranty events and use first-pass-to-customer quality, not merely the local rework rate.                     |
| **CNC_A concentration risk**                              | Routing too much work to the lower-rework machine may overload CNC_A or make it a single point of failure. A later breakdown could force CNC_B rush jobs and air transport.                                     | Maintain dual qualification, queue limits, and machine-resilience metrics.                                                       |
| **Partial-shipment service displacement**                 | Holding an order to avoid a second shipment may leave a customer without an immediately usable critical part. The customer may place duplicate orders or hold additional safety stock.                          | Decide at line-item level and retain splits when the first shipment materially prevents downtime.                                |
| **Shared-emergency-lane rebound**                         | Combining urgent deliveries reduces empty van travel, but waiting for another consignment or making detours may breach a true downtime SLA.                                                                     | Use hard latest-departure rules and preserve dedicated service when sharing is not feasible.                                     |
| **Customer-behavior rebound**                             | Carbon-aware promise choices may lead customers to order earlier than needed, split demand across orders, select shorter promises strategically, or overstate urgency.                                          | Monitor order frequency, size, cancellations, urgency changes, and duplicate demand at customer level.                           |
| **Urgency-classification gaming**                         | Employees or customers may classify precautionary cases as true downtime to obtain emergency production or a dedicated van.                                                                                     | Require auditable reason codes and review repeated classifications by customer, site, and decision maker.                        |
| **Resilience displacement through carrier concentration** | Consolidation or ground optimization can reduce emissions while reducing the number of viable carriers and routes, violating R1 during disruption.                                                              | Measure alternative-route availability and conduct disruption scenarios before increasing route concentration.                   |
| **Boundary displacement**                                 | Prepositioning more stock could reduce logged emergency transport but increase upstream production, storage, and obsolescence outside the present event-log boundary.                                           | Add inventory age, disposal, replenishment, and warehouse-energy data before claiming a lifecycle benefit.                       |

## 6. Balanced recommendation

Management should implement a **constrained, segment-specific redesign package**, beginning immediately with high-confidence controls and using targeted pilots for interventions whose net effects are uncertain. It should neither impose a blanket low-emission policy nor preserve the status quo. The strongest opportunities occur in avoidable Service-contract air freight, high-rework Made-to-order paths, packaging incompatibility, unnecessary split shipments, and precautionary cases currently treated as true downtime. 

The central decision rule should be:

1. **Classify the case and establish hard constraints.** Determine customer segment, true operational urgency, Q1 packaging obligations, R1 route requirements, and the current promise.
2. **Generate feasible alternatives.** Consider production release time, machine, inspection plan, package, transport mode, and whether to split or consolidate.
3. **Reject alternatives that create unacceptable service, quality, or resilience risk.** In particular, do not select a lower-impact route under SP1 unless its calibrated on-time probability exceeds 85%.
4. **Among the remaining alternatives, prefer Pareto-efficient choices.** Minimize energy, transport kgCO2e, scrap, packaging, expected rework, and expected obsolescence without hiding one dimension inside another.
5. **Require an explicit exception event for deviations.**

A segment-specific operating policy should be adopted:

| Segment                                     | Recommended default                                                                                                                                                                   | Protected exceptions                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Standard-stock Regular**                  | Standard packaging; consolidated truck when the 12–24h wait safely fits; parcel ground otherwise; avoid unnecessary partial shipment                                                  | Air for genuinely insufficient slack or disruption; split when a critical line has immediate value                    |
| **Standard-stock Service-contract**         | Priority pick plus ground whenever the calibrated on-time probability exceeds 85%; maintain a secondary carrier                                                                       | Air where slack is insufficient, ground reliability is degraded, or contractual service cannot otherwise be protected |
| **Made-to-order Regular**                   | Dynamic batching with the longest segment-appropriate cap; CNC_A when qualified and not congested; risk-based inspection; ground or consolidation; offer carbon-aware promise windows | Immediate production or faster transport when chosen promise would otherwise fail                                     |
| **Made-to-order Service-contract**          | Shorter batching caps; selective CNC_A routing; protected inspection capacity; Q1-compliant packaging; ground where feasible                                                          | Immediate CNC, air, or other expediting when required by the service promise                                          |
| **True Critical-downtime Service-contract** | No waiting for energy-optimal batches; emergency production where necessary; shared emergency lane only when it meets the hard latest arrival                                         | Dedicated van and air remain valid, with SP2 justification and an alternative disruption route                        |
| **Precautionary urgent Service-contract**   | Priority stock, ground, or shared emergency lane; normal or shortened production caps depending on slack                                                                              | Dedicated van only after an explicit assessment shows that shared or ground service is insufficient                   |

The implementation sequence should be:

**First, deploy the no-regret controls.** These are the S12 exception event, packaging-route compatibility and Q1 validation, shadow-mode SP1 route recommendations, and the true-downtime versus precautionary classification. They address known policy and information defects without removing legitimate emergency options.

**Second, pilot the operational trade-offs.** Test dynamic batching, selective CNC_A routing, the inspection gate, consolidation departures, partial-shipment rules, and shared emergency lanes on narrowly defined eligible populations. These interventions have promising environmental mechanisms but can create queues or service displacement if scaled indiscriminately.

**Third, scale only the combinations that improve the whole order outcome.** A production-energy saving should not qualify for scale if it is offset by more air freight, obsolescence, rework, or late deliveries. Conversely, a small increase in inspection effort or protective package material can be justified when it materially reduces repeat production and returns.

Management should explicitly reject four simplistic alternatives:

* no blanket prohibition on air or dedicated vans;
* no universal target for maximum batch size;
* no universal reinforced-packaging rule;
* no transfer of all Made-to-order production to CNC_A.

The recommended package reduces impact primarily by eliminating **avoidable high-carbon and failure-generating work**, rather than by delaying every order. That is why it is more compatible with service, quality, and resilience than an across-the-board carbon-minimization policy.

## 7. Validation and governance plan

### 7.1 Strengthen the event log before judging outcomes

The decision log should add:

* decision timestamp and actor;
* alternatives considered;
* predicted cycle-time or arrival distribution for each alternative;
* promise slack at decision time;
* selected production, packaging, and transport option;
* SP1, SP2, or Q1 rule result;
* override reason, approver, and disruption context;
* criticality subclassification;
* batching target, maximum wait, and release reason;
* machine eligibility and queue snapshot;
* inspection-gate result;
* consolidation departure and vehicle utilization;
* shipment-level route legs;
* return, replacement, and final disposition events.

The flattened order representation should be augmented with line-item and shipment identifiers. An object-centric representation linking orders, items, production batches, packages, and shipments would be preferable. Without that, partial shipments, package material, and batch obsolescence cannot be allocated reliably.

Dedicated-van analysis also needs actual distance, occupied distance, empty or repositioning distance, and whether multiple urgent consignments shared the journey. A route label alone is insufficient for evaluating S8.

### 7.2 Use staged and controlled pilots

Begin with a pre-intervention baseline stratified by segment, lane, machine/part family, site, and disruption status. Avoid comparing an ordinary post-period with an unusually disrupted pre-period.

Where operationally feasible:

* randomize or stagger the route recommender among SP1-eligible orders;
* pilot consolidation by lane or warehouse departure;
* test alternative batch caps within compatible part families;
* compare CNC routing only among jobs genuinely eligible for both machines;
* activate inspection gates for selected high-risk combinations;
* introduce shared emergency lanes by geography and time window.

Because orders in the same production batch or truck departure are not independent, statistical analysis should cluster by `batch_id`, departure, lane, or relevant operational unit. Outcome windows must be long enough to observe 14-day obsolescence, returns, and replacement shipments.

### 7.3 Monitor total effects and segment-level guardrails

For every intervention, compare:

* energy per conforming delivered item;
* transport kgCO2e including fallback, partial, return, and replacement legs;
* scrap and packaging material;
* first-pass completion;
* batch obsolescence;
* median and p90 cycle time;
* late delivery;
* cancellation due to delay;
* rework and return;
* R1 route availability and disruption performance.

The primary environmental result should be reported only alongside service and quality outcomes. For example, an air-reduction figure should be accompanied by the diverted orders’ late rate, cancellation rate, and any later emergency shipment.

Management should predefine segment-specific non-inferiority margins for late delivery, p90 cycle time, returns, and resilience before examining pilot results. The prompt does not provide defensible numerical margins, so they should be agreed with operations, service-contract owners, quality, and risk—not selected after results are known.

### 7.4 Implement explicit conformance monitoring

**SP1 monitoring.** At each transport decision, reconstruct whether ground or consolidated truck had an estimated on-time probability above 85%. Flag air only when such an alternative existed and no accepted exception was recorded. Audit the prediction model’s calibration separately for Standard-stock Regular, Standard-stock Service-contract, Made-to-order, and disruption periods.

**SP2 monitoring.** Require a justification event before dedicated-van dispatch, containing the urgency class, operational reason, alternatives evaluated, and approver or system decision. Report:

* dedicated vans with complete justification;
* vans with missing justification;
* vans with a justification that appears inconsistent with the subsequent evidence;
* justified vans whose service outcome still suggests the routing policy needs revision.

This separates documentation quality from substantive necessity.

**Q1 monitoring.** For every Made-to-order part over 8 kg, verify that reinforced packaging occurred or that a valid inspection certificate preceded the standard-package event. Link violations to packaging rework, damage return, replacement shipment, and package material. Because the current finding is correlational, the audit should distinguish part geometry and route effects before attributing all returns to the violation.

Given the discovered model’s low precision around emergency and partial-shipment combinations, these policies should be checked as **decision-level declarative rules**, not solely through alignment against one highly permissive end-to-end model. Separate process views should be maintained by segment and disruption regime.

### 7.5 Validate the prediction components

The green route recommender and dynamic batching policy depend on predictions. Their governance should include:

* calibration curves and proper scoring measures by segment and lane;
* comparison of predicted and actual p50/p90 arrival;
* drift detection after carrier, customer-mix, or seasonal changes;
* explicit treatment of censored or still-open orders;
* monitoring of recommendation acceptance and overrides;
* shadow predictions for alternatives not selected;
* periodic retraining without using post-decision information that was unavailable at routing time.

An 85% threshold has little value if “85%” cases arrive on time only 70% of the time in a particular segment.

### 7.6 Establish exception governance

Each exception should have a limited set of reason codes, such as verified downtime, safety risk, carrier disruption, missed upstream commitment, customer-requested split, unavailable qualified machine, or quality containment. Free text may supplement but should not replace structured reasons.

A cross-functional review group covering operations, service management, quality, logistics, resilience, and sustainability should review:

* high-carbon exceptions by environmental burden, not merely count;
* repeat exceptions by customer, planner, part family, and site;
* patterns where batching causes later expediting;
* routes or machines becoming single points of failure;
* Q1 exceptions and downstream returns;
* recommendations frequently overridden by experienced staff.

Overrides should not automatically be treated as employee noncompliance. A concentration of well-founded overrides may indicate that the model or policy is wrong.

### 7.7 Use explicit scale, revise, and stop decisions

Scale an intervention only when it shows a credible reduction in at least one major environmental burden and no unacceptable deterioration in the relevant segment’s service, quality, or resilience guardrails.

Revise rather than abandon an intervention when the mechanism is sound but execution causes a localized bottleneck—for example, an inspection gate that reduces rework but overloads the inspection cell.

Stop or roll back when environmental savings depend on:

* increased Critical-downtime lateness;
* higher damage returns or Q1 failures;
* erosion of R1 route alternatives;
* systematic increases in cancellation or duplicate shipments;
* larger unshipped batch inventories;
* displacement into unlogged returns, emergency production, or customer reorders.

This governance structure allows management to act on the clear opportunities now while reserving broad rollout for process changes that improve the **complete, successfully delivered order**, rather than merely one attractive local metric.
