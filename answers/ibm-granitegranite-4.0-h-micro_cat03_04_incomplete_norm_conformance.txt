### Open-world vs. closed-world assumptions

- **Closed-world assumption**: Relations explicitly marked as forbidden or mandatory within the norm’s scope are assumed not to occur outside those cases. This leads to stricter compliance checks for known rules but may overlook legitimate exceptions or missing data.
- **Open-world assumption**: Relations not covered by the norm are considered permissible or unknown. This approach allows for flexibility and accommodates real-world complexities where norms cannot cover every possible scenario. In the observed data, many relations fall into this category, such as `Sensor Heartbeat -> Invoice` or `Order -> RiskCase`.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG

- **What it can establish**: The OC-DFG model captures the intended sequential flow of events for each object type. It confirms that most mandatory relationships are observed, such as `Order: Create Order -> Validate Order` and `Package: Pack Package -> Dispatch Package`. This establishes the baseline expected behavior for the system.
- **What it cannot establish**: It does not account for variations in process flow, such as manual compliance reviews or unapplied cash scenarios, which are expected to be typical but not strictly defined. Therefore, while it confirms the presence of key relationships, it may miss subtle deviations or exceptions.
- **Interpretation of counts and scopes**: The high counts for core relationships (e.g., `Order: Create Order -> Validate Order` occurring 98 out of 100 times) indicate robust adherence to the norm. However, the lower counts for less frequently observed relationships (e.g., `Order: Reopen Order -> Close Order`) highlight areas where the norm does not fully capture the business process.
- **Interpretation of abstraction mismatch**: The norm’s abstraction level is higher than observed in some cases (e.g., `Order: Create Order -> Manual Compliance Review` instead of `Create Order -> Validate Order`). This discrepancy suggests that the norm assumes a higher-level abstraction than the actual implementation, leading to potential misinterpretations if taken at face value.

### OTG

- **What it can establish**: The OTG model provides a comprehensive view of all allowed interactions between object types, including both mandatory and typical relationships. It confirms the prevalence of core interactions like `Order: Add Item` and `Package: Pack Package -> Dispatch Package`.
- **What it cannot establish**: It does not specify the absence of certain interactions, such as direct customer or risk case associations with invoices, which are legitimate but not modeled. Therefore, it cannot identify unexpected but unregulated behaviors.
- **Interpretation of counts and scopes**: The high weight for core interactions (e.g., `Order: Add Item` with 1450 occurrences) validates the norm’s coverage of typical processes. However, the lower weights for less frequently observed interactions (e.g., `Order: Reopen Order -> Close Order` with 3 occurrences) indicate potential gaps in the norm’s coverage.
- **Interpretation of abstraction mismatch**: The OTG captures both direct and indirect relationships, which can lead to abstraction mismatches. For example, the norm’s explicit edges may not reflect the underlying business processes that involve more complex interactions, such as `Order -> Customer`.

### ET–OT

- **What it can establish**: The ET–OT model captures the end-to-end flow of events from creation to completion, providing a holistic view of the system’s behavior. It confirms the majority of expected event sequences, such as `Create Order -> Record Payment`.
- **What it cannot establish**: It does not account for missing mandatory relationships or unregulated behaviors that are not part of the norm. For example, the absence of `Order: Cancel Order -> Dispatch Package` is flagged as forbidden, but the observed data includes actual instances of this sequence.
- **Interpretation of counts and scopes**: The observed weights for core event sequences (e.g., `Create Order` with 100 occurrences) confirm the norm’s coverage of typical event flows. However, the lower weights for less frequently observed sequences (e.g., `Sensor Heartbeat -> Invoice` with 1 occurrence) highlight areas where the norm may be incomplete.
- **Interpretation of abstraction mismatch**: The ET–OT model abstracts the sequence of events without detailing the underlying business logic. This can lead to confusion when interpreting events that do not fit neatly into the norm’s defined edges, such as `Invoice -> Manual Compliance Review`.

### Danger of interpreting “eventually expected” business rules as directly-follows rules

The norm’s reliance on “eventually expected” business rules can lead to misinterpretation if treated as directly-follows rules. For instance, `Order: Create Order -> Validate Order` is marked as mandatory, but the observed data shows that this transition does not always occur directly. Instead, there may be intermediate steps (e.g., `Manual Compliance Review`) that are not captured by the norm. Treating the norm as a strictly direct-follows rule could lead to false positives in compliance checks, overlooking legitimate process variations. Therefore, it is crucial to maintain a nuanced understanding of the norm’s abstraction level and its implications for observed behavior.
