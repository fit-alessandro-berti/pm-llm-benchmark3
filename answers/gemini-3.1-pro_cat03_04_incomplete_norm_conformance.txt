## 1. Conformance semantics

To evaluate object-centric deviations against a deliberately incomplete normative specification, we must distinguish deviations by mapping them to structural intent, abstraction limitations, and real-world system realities.

- **Forbidden behavior**: This represents relations that are explicitly disallowed in the norm (e.g., dispatching a canceled order, co-birthing orders and payments). An occurrence of forbidden behavior indicates either a severe process violation or a critical system control failure.
- **Missing mandatory behavior**: This occurs when a relation expected by the norm is absent. However, classifying it as a strict violation requires evaluating stated scope conditions and abstractions. A missing mandatory edge might represent a true skipped step, a data loss event, or simply an abstraction mismatch (e.g., an intermediate event obscuring a directly-follows relation).
- **Unexpected but unregulated behavior**: These are behaviors not documented in the norm but entirely allowable under open-world assumptions. Examples include standard intermediate steps like issuing an invoice before closure, or objects naturally completing their lifecycles simultaneously (codeath). These represent true business variability or benign operational facts, not compliance failures.
- **Typical-pattern absence**: This is a deviation from a "typical" norm, which acts as a guideline rather than a strict constraint. The absence of a typical pattern (e.g., a missing payment link due to a write-off) indicates a valid alternative path or exception, even if the system fails to label the exception clearly.
- **Likely data-quality error**: These are behaviors that conflict with physical or logical reality, often evidenced by mismatched identifiers across domains. Examples include an industrial sensor heartbeat linking to a financial invoice due to a numeric ID collision, or orphaned records failing to link to their parent objects.
- **Abstraction mismatch**: This occurs when there is a discrepancy between the normative rule's business intent and the graph's mathematical structural expression. For example, a business rule that an order is "eventually validated" is broken at the graph level if evaluated as a strict "directly-follows" constraint interrupted by a compliance review. Similarly, a structural edge might be missing because the relationship is captured in an event attribute instead.
- **Insufficient evidence**: These are deviations where current data attributes or process scopes (e.g., whether a package is for "external shipment") are unknown in the aggregated summary, preventing a definitive classification.

**Open-world versus closed-world assumptions:**
The interpretation hinges on the stated constraint that the norm is closed-world *only* for explicitly forbidden relations and for mandatory columns within their stated scope. Therefore, the mere absence of an edge from the norm (e.g., codeath, customer interactions, reopening orders) does not imply a violation. Under open-world assumptions, what is not forbidden is permitted. We must not flag unmodeled interactions as noncompliant; we strictly flag only what is explicitly forbidden and what is mandatory but demonstrably missing within its defined scope.

## 3. Detailed reasoning by model family

### OC-DFG (Object-Centric Directly-Follows Graph)
The OC-DFG can establish the immediate sequential succession of events within the lifecycle of a specific object type. It is excellent for capturing exact sequential counts, such as confirming that 98 out of 100 `Orders` move strictly from `Create Order` to `Validate Order`. 
However, the OC-DFG cannot establish long-term eventual relationships if intermediate events occur, nor can it map object-to-object structural relationships (like codeath) or interactions occurring across multiple objects without a defined sequence.
Interpreting exact counts in the OC-DFG requires considering scope. For example, N4 mandates `Pack Package -> Dispatch Package` specifically for *external* packages. The 198 observed external packages might mean 2 internal packages legitimately lack the dispatch relation, requiring scope confirmation rather than immediate penalty.
A major danger lies in interpreting an "eventually expected" business rule as a directly-follows rule. N1 mandates `Create Order -> Validate Order`. The summary notes that the directly-follows abstraction may be too strict because a `Manual Compliance Review` event might intervene. A relation can be conforming at the business abstraction level ("the order was eventually validated") but suspicious at the mathematical OC-DFG level ("creation was not directly followed by validation"). This abstraction mismatch generates false positives if not recognized.

### OTG (Object Type Graph)
The OTG establishes the existence of static, structural relationships between objects, such as interaction, cobirth, and codeath, irrespective of the dynamic event flow. 
It cannot establish the specific sequence in which these objects interacted, the event types that triggered their relationship, or the multiplicity of events over time. 
Counts and weights in the OTG represent distinct object pairs interacting, not event occurrences. For example, a weight of 1450 for `(Order, object_interaction, Item)` confirms the mandatory T1 norm is satisfied in volume across the system. In contrast, the 1 cobirth for `Order` and `Payment` (T6) is a clear structural violation, revealing an illicit simultaneous lifecycle origin regardless of how the sequential process unfolded.
The OTG reveals structural facts hidden in sequential DFGs. The `(Package, object_codeath, Delivery)` relation is an unregulated but entirely normal lifecycle synchronization. The OTG correctly highlights this as a benign structural truth, whereas a sequential graph might only show two disconnected end-events, obscuring the physical reality that both objects reached their terminus together.

### ET–OT (Event Type to Object Type Graph)
The ET–OT establishes the types of objects that participate in specific event types, validating the basic ontological mapping of the process. 
It cannot establish the temporal ordering of events, nor does it guarantee specific instances of objects interacting—only that the event type is capable of touching those object types in aggregate.
By comparing edge weights between object types for the same event type, we can identify precise completeness gaps. For instance, `Add Item` relates to `Item` 1450 times but to `Order` only 1448 times. This delta of 2 exposes a mandatory gap (E2), pointing to orphaned data. Similarly, `Record Payment` relates to 93 `Payments` but 92 `Invoices`, exposing the E8 gap.
A relation might conform structurally but be suspicious contextually, or vice versa. The missing E6 relation (where 2 `Dispatch Package` events lack a `Delivery` edge) appears non-conforming on the ET-OT graph. However, this is perfectly benign when we realize the `Delivery` information is safely embedded in an event attribute (a carrier reference). The business rule is satisfied, even if the graph structure is technically missing an edge.

## 5. PM4Py conformance code sketch

```python
import pm4py

# Assume ocel is the observed log
# norm_ocdfg, norm_otg, norm_etot are normative dictionaries/tuples

# 1. Discover the raw observed graphs
obs_ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
obs_otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
obs_etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)

# 2. Run standard PM4Py conformance checking
# These functions typically return diagnostic structures highlighting missing/extra edges
# We DO NOT treat these raw outputs as a complete business interpretation.
diag_ocdfg = pm4py.conformance_ocdfg(ocel, norm_ocdfg)
diag_otg = pm4py.conformance_otg(ocel, norm_otg)
diag_etot = pm4py.conformance_etot(ocel, norm_etot)

# 3. Define external metadata for semantic classification
# Mandatory/Forbidden/Typical metadata must be maintained OUTSIDE the bare graphs
norm_semantics = {
    'ocdfg': {
        ('Order', 'Create Order', 'Validate Order'): 'mandatory',
        ('Order', 'Cancel Order', 'Dispatch Package'): 'forbidden',
        ('Package', 'Pack Package', 'Dispatch Package'): 'mandatory',
        ('Invoice', 'Issue Invoice', 'Record Payment'): 'typical',
    },
    'otg': {
        ('Order', 'object_cobirth', 'Payment'): 'forbidden',
        ('Order', 'object_interaction', 'Item'): 'mandatory',
    },
    'etot': {
        ('Sensor Heartbeat', 'Invoice'): 'forbidden',
        ('Add Item', 'Order'): 'mandatory',
        ('Dispatch Package', 'Delivery'): 'mandatory',
        ('Record Payment', 'Invoice'): 'mandatory',
    }
}

def semantically_classify_diagnostics(diagnostics, semantics_map):
    """
    Applies a semantic classification layer on top of PM4Py diagnostics
    without overwriting the raw structural output.
    """
    classified_results = []
    # Note: diagnostic structure varies by PM4Py version; assume a list of edge violations
    for violation in diagnostics:
        edge = violation['edge']
        violation_type = violation['type'] # e.g., 'missing_edge', 'extra_edge'
        
        # Look up semantic status from our external metadata map
        status = semantics_map.get(edge, 'unspecified')
        
        # Determine deviation class based on open/closed world and semantics
        if violation_type == 'extra_edge':
            if status == 'forbidden':
                classification = 'forbidden behavior'
            else:
                classification = 'unexpected but unregulated'
        elif violation_type == 'missing_edge':
            if status == 'mandatory':
                classification = 'missing mandatory behavior'
            elif status == 'typical':
                classification = 'typical-pattern absence'
            else:
                classification = 'insufficient evidence'
                
        classified_results.append({
            'raw_diagnostic': violation,
            'semantic_status': status,
            'classification': classification
        })
    return classified_results

# 4. Layer semantic classification on top of raw diagnostics
semantic_ocdfg = semantically_classify_diagnostics(diag_ocdfg, norm_semantics['ocdfg'])
semantic_otg = semantically_classify_diagnostics(diag_otg, norm_semantics['otg'])
semantic_etot = semantically_classify_diagnostics(diag_etot, norm_semantics['etot'])
```

## 6. Norm-completeness assessment

1. **N1 Abstraction (Create Order -> Validate Order):**
   - **Operational risk:** Maintaining this as a directly-follows constraint risks a high rate of false-positive violations when benign intermediate events (like `Manual Compliance Review`) occur, leading to alert fatigue.
   - **Proposed clarification:** Alter the normative rule from a strictly directly-follows constraint to an eventually-follows constraint, or explicitly model allowed intermediate compliance events.
   - **Status:** Mandatory (redefined as eventually-follows).

2. **Delivery relation in Dispatch Package (E6):**
   - **Operational risk:** The ET-OT norm flags missing structural edges, but the delivery data is actually captured in an event attribute (carrier reference). This risks redundant data entry or misinterpreting log completeness.
   - **Proposed clarification:** Specify that the `Delivery` relation rule can be satisfied either by an explicit ET-OT edge or a recognized event attribute.
   - **Status:** Mandatory (with flexible structural definition).

3. **Unapplied Cash Labeling (E8):**
   - **Operational risk:** Finance practice allows unapplied cash, but the system fails to explicitly label it, causing recurring false-positive ET-OT gaps for `Invoice` links.
   - **Proposed clarification:** Mandate that unapplied cash events MUST carry a specific attribute or event type subtype to formally exempt them from E8.
   - **Status:** Mandatory.

4. **External versus Internal Packages (N4):**
   - **Operational risk:** The norm applies to "externally shipped" packages, but it is unclear if this scope is globally deducible from the graph summary. This creates false positives for legitimate internal shipments.
   - **Proposed clarification:** Introduce a package routing attribute or a distinct `Pack Internal Package` event type to clearly delineate scope boundaries.
   - **Status:** Explicitly unspecified (for internal), Mandatory (for external).

5. **Customer and RiskCase Interactions (T4, T5, E9):**
   - **Operational risk:** Leaving these interactions explicitly unspecified means critical compliance and customer management steps are completely unmonitored by the main conformance process, creating blind spots.
   - **Proposed clarification:** Formally adopt standard paths for `RiskCase` and `Customer` involvement into the norm, even if governed elsewhere, to track contextual volume.
   - **Status:** Typical.

6. **Codeath Relations (e.g., Package and Delivery):**
   - **Operational risk:** Missing lifecycle synchronization facts in the norm leads to classifying benign simultaneous closures as "unexpected," obscuring valid system behavior.
   - **Proposed clarification:** Define allowable codeath relationships for objects that are known to share lifecycle terminations.
   - **Status:** Typical.
