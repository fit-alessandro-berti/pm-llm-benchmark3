# Diagnostic Lab Process Redesign for Scenario Resilience

## 1. Stress Scenarios

### Scenario A: Winter Surge with Case-Mix Shift
**Composition:** 35% higher volume (from 18,400 to ~24,700 cases over 8 weeks) + shift to 35% Urgent, 22% Complex-panel, 28% Routine, 15% Repeat-after-failure (vs. normal 18% Urgent, 13% Complex, 61% Routine, 8% Repeat).
**Trigger:** Respiratory illness wave; flu-like illness prevalence >20%.
**Key stress:** Medical validators and specialist review become the primary constraint because Urgent and Complex-panel cases are 3.1× and 2.4× more likely to need medical validation. Pre-analytical failure rate rises to 9% due to rushed sample handling.

### Scenario B: Assay-Machine Degradation
**Composition:** Normal volume (18,400) and mix, but M2 failure rate increases from ~7% repeat rate to 15% repeat rate (simulating a failing machine or calibration drift). M1 remains nominal; urgent bypass of batch wait drops to 40% because both machines are congested.
**Trigger:** M2 calibration alerts; repeat rate spike above 10% in quality control logs.
**Key stress:** Repeat assay  Assay run loop grows; this feeds back into Technical validation queue. Assay machines move from 71% to ~85% utilization. Accession correction and pre-analytical check remain stable, but downstream repeats consume all slack.

### Scenario C: Specialist and Medical-Validator Absence
**Composition:** Normal volume and mix, but one of two medical validators is unavailable for 2 weeks (e.g., illness, conference) and specialist reviewer is absent every Friday + one unplanned week. Technical validators remain available.
**Trigger:** Medical validator queue >22h (peak p50 is 2h); Hold for specialist review queue >36h.
**Key stress:** Technical validation  Medical validation becomes a funnel. Complex-panel cases cannot release. Hold for specialist review delays accumulate. Urgent cases that bypass batch wait still need medical validation; they pile up behind rework from repeats.

### Scenario D: Courier Compression and Pre-Analytical Defect Storm
**Composition:** Normal volume (18,400) and mix, but two scheduled courier routes merge due to vehicle breakdown, creating a 2-hour window of arrivals instead of the usual 10:00 and 17:00 spread. Simultaneously, a brief pre-analytical protocol error (operator training gap) causes recollection requests to spike from 4.1% to 9% of received samples.
**Trigger:** Courier intake queue >7.5h; pre-analytical request recollection rate >8%.
**Key stress:** Registration and intake bottleneck immediately. Recollection loop (Request recollection  Receive sample, 42–60h median return) fills the intake queue. Accession correction specialists (only 2) become overloaded. Pre-analytical check has backlog, blocking test preparation. But preparation benches and assay machines remain underutilized, creating a front-loaded bottleneck.

### Scenario E: Compounded Stress (A + C variant)
**Composition:** 28% volume increase (to ~23,600) with 32% Urgent, 20% Complex-panel, 31% Routine, 17% Repeat-after-failure. One medical validator absent for 3 weeks. Specialist reviewer reduced availability (every other Friday off).
**Trigger:** Combined Scenarios A and C conditions.
**Key stress:** Medical validation queue explodes to >40h median. Urgent cases that complete assay in 8–10h wait 20–30h for medical validation. Complex-panel cases breach their de facto target (implicitly ~120h p90 is already stressed). Repeat-after-failure cases circulate longer because repeats queue behind the medical-validation bottleneck.

## 2. Bottleneck-Migration Analysis

### Scenario A: Winter Surge with Case-Mix Shift

**Phase 1: Intake Compression (Hours 0–8)**
- Courier intake operates at 88% nominal; with 35% volume increase and two major case types now dominant, it approaches 100%+.
- Registration clerks (85% peak utilization) hit 105%+ utilization if volume rises further.
- **Bottleneck:** Registration and courier intake.
- **DFG evidence:** Register order  Receive sample (5h normal, 8h peak); peak median queue rises to 2.8h and will worsen.
- **Rework feedback:** Pre-analytical failure rate rises; Request recollection rate goes from 4.1% to ~6–7%, creating a secondary intake loop.

**Phase 2: Pre-analytical and Accession Correction Saturation (Hours 8–24)**
- Pre-analytical check (96% peak utilization) hits 100%+ utilization.
- Accession correction specialists (91% peak utilization, only 2 staff) hit 100%+ utilization.
- Manual accession correction cycles back ~500 cases/8 weeks normally; now +50%, so recollection feedback accelerates.
- **Bottleneck:** Pre-analytical check, accession correction, and recollection intake.
- **DFG evidence:** Pre-analytical check  Request recollection (3–7h) and Manual accession correction  Test preparation (5–16h peak) both show steep degradation.
- **Consequence:** Test preparation benches see stalled input; preparation  Batch wait (6–14h) backlog grows, but not from capacity, from starved input.

**Phase 3: Preparation and Assay Cascade (Hours 24–48)**
- Once pre-analytical check unclogs (if it does), test preparation benches fill with a rush.
- Routine cases now only 28% of mix (vs. 61% normal), so smaller total batching opportunity.
- Urgent and Complex-panel cases bypass batch wait; this fragments assay scheduling and increases setup overhead.
- Assay machines M1/M2 operate at 98%+ utilization; repeat rate remains ~7–8% (normal), but absolute repeats climb by 35% (more cases through).
- **Bottleneck:** Assay machines.
- **Consequence:** Repeat assay  Assay run loop grows (absolute cycle time 10–26h); Technical validation queue backs up (5–15h).

**Phase 4: Medical Validation Chokepoint (Hours 48–72)**
- Technical validation completes at higher throughput, but only 11,700/18,400 normal cases bypass directly to release (64%).
- Remaining 3,900 + 290 = 4,190 must wait for medical validation.
- With case mix now 35% Urgent + 22% Complex, the fraction needing medical validation rises from ~23% to ~38%.
- Complex-panel cases (20% of volume, 3.1× more likely to need medical validation) and Urgent cases (32%, mandatory medical validation for some subsets) pile up.
- **Bottleneck:** Medical validation (58% normal utilization; 93% peak); with increased Urgent/Complex demand, it hits 110%+.
- **DFG evidence:** Technical validation  Medical validation (8–24h), Hold for specialist review (6–48h), and Medical validation  Release result (2–5h) all degrade.
- **Consequence:** Urgent cases that should release in <18h are held in medical validation for 15–30h, breaching SLA.

**Migration path:** Registration  Pre-analytical/Accession  Preparation (starved input)  Assay  Technical validation  **Medical validation (worst bottleneck in surge scenario)**.

**Intervention interactions:**
- I1 (Urgent-first dispatch) helps Urgent cases escape assay faster but forces them into medical validation queue even sooner; worsens medical validation bottleneck.
- I2 (larger batches) slows Urgent cases if batch policy is rigid; helps Routine cases but offers little relief in surge (Routine % drops).
- I4 (cross-train technical validators to accession) relieves accession early (good) but reduces technical validation capacity (bad), moving bottleneck backward.
- I5 (evening medical-validation shift) directly targets the bottleneck; robust for Scenario A.

### Scenario B: Assay-Machine Degradation

**Phase 1: Repeat-Assay Feedback Loop (Hours 0–12)**
- M2 failure rate rises from ~7% to ~15%; absolute repeats climb from 1,140 (normal) to ~1,600 across 18,400 cases.
- Repeat assay  Assay run (10–26h) grows in both cycle time and queue length.
- Assay machines were 71% utilized normally; now 71% × (18,400 + 460 repeats) / 18,400  72% baseline, but with longer cycles due to repeated setup/teardown.
- **Bottleneck:** Assay machines, internally (repeat queue).
- **DFG evidence:** Assay run  Repeat assay (3–8h) increases; Repeat assay  Assay run (10–26h) becomes the slower return path.

**Phase 2: Technical Validation Congestion (Hours 12–36)**
- 1,140 normal repeats feed into Technical validation directly in 80 cases (Repeat assay  Medical validation); most cycle back through assay.
- Once repeats complete, Technical validation queue receives a surge of catch-up cases.
- Technical validators (76% normal utilization) now face 72% baseline + repeat catch-up; if repeats take 30% longer and assay queue extends, technical validation backs up to 80%+ utilization.
- **Bottleneck:** Technical validation (queuing from assay backlog).
- **Consequence:** Medical validation input slows; release rate drops because technical validators are clearing a backlog.

**Phase 3: Triage Divergence (Hours 36–72)**
- Urgent cases that bypassed batch wait (58% in this scenario, down from 74% normal) also experience repeat-assay delays.
- Urgent cases cannot afford to wait in repeat queues; they either:
  - Hold in assay queue, delaying their medical validation (violates 18h SLA).
  - Complete assay without repeats if the first run succeeds (so not all urgent cases are affected).
- Complex-panel cases are less likely to bypass batch wait; they tolerate queuing and are less sensitive to repeats if the repeat is a protocol-confirmation step.
- Repeat-after-failure cases (8% of volume) are 2.4× more likely to repeat; they become a heavy-tailed latency group.
- **Bottleneck:** Assay machines remain the primary bottleneck; but the composition of queued cases shifts toward Repeat-after-failure and Urgent.

**Migration path:** Assay machines (repeat spike)  Technical validation (queue from repeats)  Medical validation (reduced input rate)  potentially Hold for specialist review if repeats correlate with complex results.

**Intervention interactions:**
- I3 (dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex, M2 to Routine) is counterproductive; M2 is already failing, so consolidating Routine cases on M2 worsens repeats.
- I1 (Urgent-first dispatch) helps Urgent cases avoid the repeat queue if possible, but does not fix the repeat-assay bottleneck itself.
- I6 (early pre-analytical defect triage) is irrelevant; the defect is in assay performance, not sample quality.
- Robust response: Early M2 diagnostics (leading indicator) + fallback to single-machine scheduling (I3 reversed, i.e., I3-off) or outsourcing Routine overflow.

### Scenario C: Specialist and Medical-Validator Absence

**Phase 1: Medical Validation Immediate Saturation (Hours 0–12)**
- One of two medical validators absent; capacity drops 50%.
- Technical validation completes cases and feeds them to medical validation.
- Even normal-period case flow (4,190 cases needing medical validation over 8 weeks  5.2 cases/hour) now saturates the single validator.
- **Bottleneck:** Medical validation capacity.
- **DFG evidence:** Technical validation  Medical validation (8–24h normal) extends to >30h; queue median rises from 2h (normal) to >15h.

**Phase 2: Hold for Specialist Review Accumulation (Hours 12–36)**
- Medical validation routes ~290 cases to Hold for specialist review (normal period, ~0.4% of completed technical validation).
- Specialist reviewer absent reduces this capacity by 50% or more (e.g., one person off Friday, or both off for 1 week).
- Cases awaiting specialist review (normally 5h median queue, 6–12h edge) now queue for 20–36h.
- **Bottleneck:** Hold for specialist review (specialist capacity).
- **Consequence:** Medical validators cannot release cases held for specialist input; they accumulate in "ready to release" state, pending specialist.

**Phase 3: Upstream Backup (Hours 36–72)**
- Technical validation  Medical validation queue deepens (now 20h+ p50 instead of 8h).
- Technical validators (76% normal utilization) increase to 85%+ because they cannot offload cases quickly.
- If technical validation queue extends, Assay run  Technical validation (5–15h) also backs up.
- But assay machines and earlier stages are underutilized because the downstream medical validation is a funnel.
- **Bottleneck:** Medical validation and specialist review remain the primary constraints; technical validation and assay are secondary constraints (input-starved).

**Migration path:** Technical validation  **Medical validation  Hold for specialist review (worst bottleneck)**.

**Intervention interactions:**
- I5 (evening medical-validation shift) directly relieves medical validation capacity; robust for Scenario C.
- I4 (cross-train technical validators to accession) does not help; accession correction is not the bottleneck.
- I1 (Urgent-first dispatch at preparation/assay) does not help; Urgent cases still need medical validation.
- I10 (outsource Complex-panel to partner lab) removes 20% of cases from medical validation queue, but 12h transport delay may violate Urgent-case SLAs for some Complex-panel-Urgent hybrids (if they exist).

---

### Scenario D: Courier Compression and Pre-Analytical Defect Storm

**Phase 1: Intake Shock (Hours 0–4)**
- Two courier routes merge; 2-hour arrival window instead of staggered 10:00 and 17:00.
- Courier intake (60% normal, 88% peak utilization) spikes to 120%+ utilization in that 2-hour window.
- Registration clerks (68% normal, 85% peak) face a 2-hour surge and then a 12-hour lull.
- **Bottleneck:** Courier intake and registration, in short bursts.
- **Consequence:** Some samples may be mishandled due to rush; pre-analytical defect rate rises (exogenous shock: protocol error).

**Phase 2: Pre-analytical Defect Cascade (Hours 4–20)**
- Pre-analytical failure rate rises from 4.1% to 9% due to combined courier rush and operator error.
- Request recollection rate jumps from 4.1% to 9%; this means ~760 recollection requests instead of ~420 (normal / 8 weeks, scaled to 1 week  600 cases).
- Recollection loop: Request recollection  Receive sample (42–60h return); each recollection freezes a case for 1.75–2.5 days.
- Accession correction (only 2 specialists, 62% normal utilization, 91% peak) now faces 1.4–1.5× normal volume of corrections; utilization jumps to 100%+.
- **Bottleneck:** Accession correction specialists and recollection intake.
- **DFG evidence:** Request recollection rate jumps; Manual accession correction  Test preparation (5–16h) queues.

**Phase 3: Downstream Starvation (Hours 20–48)**
- Test preparation benches (69% normal utilization) receive reduced input due to upstream blockage (pre-analytical check starved by accession backlog).
- Assay machines (71% normal utilization) receive reduced input due to starved preparation.
- Preparation benches and assay machines operate well below capacity because their input is constrained by the front-end bottleneck.
- **Bottleneck:** Pre-analytical check and accession correction (front-loaded).
- **Consequence:** Paradoxically, the lab has idle capacity in preparation and assay, but cases cannot reach them.

**Phase 4: Case-Type Stratification (Hours 48–72)**
- Urgent cases that arrive during the initial courier surge and clear pre-analytical check proceed normally (faster through preparation/assay due to priority and lower volume competing for capacity).
- Urgent cases affected by pre-analytical errors or requiring recollection are stuck in recollection loop (42–60h), breaching their 18h SLA.
- Routine and Complex-panel cases are distributed across the surge but tolerate longer pre-analytical queues.
- **Bottleneck:** Still pre-analytical/accession, but now stratified by case type and error state.

**Migration path:** Courier intake  Registration  Pre-analytical check  Accession correction  (bottleneck persists)  Preparation and assay (underutilized downstream).

**Intervention interactions:**
- I7 (smooth courier pickup slots) directly prevents the 2-hour compression; robust for Scenario D.
- I8 (automate registration for complete metadata) reduces registration queue during surge but does not fix pre-analytical check or accession correction; it exposes downstream bottlenecks.
- I4 (cross-train technical validators to accession) would relieve accession correction immediately; robust for Scenario D.
- I6 (early pre-analytical defect triage within 2h) catches defects early and stops recollection loops from consuming accession capacity; robust for Scenario D.

### Scenario E: Compounded Stress (A + C variant)

**Phase 1: Multi-front Saturation (Hours 0–24)**
- Volume surge (28%) + medical validator absent (50% capacity) + specialist reviewer reduced.
- Registration, pre-analytical check, accession correction, preparation, and assay all run hot (>85% utilization).
- Medical validation capacity (from 2 validators to 1.5 on average, accounting for partial absence) drops 25%; with case mix shift toward Urgent/Complex (+50% fraction needing medical validation), medical validation queue saturates immediately.
- **Bottlenecks:** Intake, pre-analytical, accession, preparation, and **medical validation** (multiple simultaneous).

**Phase 2: Cascading Queues (Hours 24–48)**
- Intake backlog (registration, pre-analytical check) delays the bulk of routine processing.
- Medical validation backlog (from ~4h p50 normal to ~30h) blocks technical validators from releasing output.
- Technical validation queue builds up because downstream is closed.
- Assay queue builds up because test preparation input is constrained by upstream blockage (wait for technical validation to clear before preparation can process more).
- **Consequence:** Queues extend across the entire process. No single bottleneck; the system is broadly congested.

**Phase 3: SLA Violation Concentration (Hours 48–72)**
- Urgent cases (32% of mix, vs. 18% normal) should release in <18h; with 30h+ wait in medical validation alone, nearly all Urgent cases breach SLA.
- Complex-panel cases (20% of mix) have de facto p90 target ~120h; with 28% volume increase and medical validation saturation, p90 approaches 150–160h.
- Repeat-after-failure cases (17% of mix) circulate longer because assay and medical validation queues extend.
- **Bottleneck:** System-wide; no single relief point solves the problem.

**Migration path:** All stations stressed; relieving one (e.g., medical validation with I5) may free capacity upstream but does not reduce demand for downstream release bottleneck.

**Intervention interactions:**
- I5 (evening medical-validation shift) directly helps but may not fully cover the 25% capacity loss if only 1 shift is added.
- I4 (cross-train technical validators to accession) is counterproductive; it reduces technical validation capacity when assay/prep output needs clearing.
- I1 (Urgent-first dispatch) helps Urgent cases escape assay but forces them into medical validation queue sooner, worsening medical validation bottleneck faster.
- I8 (automate registration) helps intake but exposes downstream bottlenecks immediately, worsening the congestion cascade.
- Robust response: Multi-pronged (I5 + I4 applied judiciously + I10 selective outsourcing + I6 defect triage to reduce rework).

## 3. Robust Redesign Package

### Core Principle
Design the process to remain acceptable under simultaneous:
1. Case-mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel cases.
2. Localized capacity loss (machine degradation, staff absence).
3. Intake shocks (courier compression, protocol errors).

Avoid optimizing for the historical average; instead, build adaptive capacity and early warning.

---

### **Intervention Set: Robust Core**

#### **R1: Segmented Pathway with Variant-Aware Buffering**
Redesign queuing to route cases by type and urgency, with explicit buffer stages that tolerate mix shifts.

**Design:**
- **Fast path:** Urgent cases dedicated to M1 assay machine; pre-analytical check performed by senior technician (higher quality to reduce recollection).
- **Standard path:** Routine and Complex-panel cases routed to shared assay capacity (M1 overflow and M2, if M2 is reliable; else sequential M1).
- **Recycle path:** Repeat-after-failure cases batched separately; re-assay performed during lower-demand windows (e.g., night shift) to avoid competing with fresh cases during peaks.
- **Assumption:** Case type is determined at registration and does not change; pre-analytical check quality is the dominant lever for reducing recollection.
- **Dependency:** Requires stable case-type classification; if Urgent vs. Routine is misclassified, fast path is overloaded.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario A (mix shift toward Urgent/Complex): Fast path absorbs Urgent surge; recycle path keeps repeats out of the main assay queue.
- Scenario B (assay degradation): M1 reserved for Urgent ensures SLA compliance for highest-priority cases; M2 degradation does not block Urgent path.
- Scenario D (pre-analytical defect storm): Senior technicians in fast path reduce defects; recollection loop does not overflow the accession correction queue.
- Scenario E (compounded): Segmentation provides some isolation; Urgent cases do not starve for assay.

**Next bottleneck risk:** If Urgent fast path is misused, it becomes a "second queue" and worsens Routine case latency. If recycle path is too small, repeat-after-failure cases back up into main assay queue.

---

#### **R2: Proactive Pre-Analytical Defect Triage (I6 Implementation)**
Detect and reject poor samples within 2 hours of pre-analytical check start.

**Design:**
- Add a 20-minute front-end visual inspection and barcode/metadata verification after sample receipt.
- For samples flagged (wrong tube, hemolysis, leakage, metadata mismatch), immediately issue recollection request, notifying the physician within 1 hour.
- Expected impact: Reduce recollection loop delay from 42–60 hours (full cycle return) to ~12 hours (physician notified, resampling within same business day).
- Assumption: Recollection requests are actually fulfilled by physicians within 12 hours; if not, the redesign does not help.
- Dependency: Requires training and equipment (automated barcode scanner, hemolysis detector); 2–4 weeks to deploy.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario A (mix shift): Rework rate is exogenous to volume; early triage prevents recollection loops from consuming accession capacity during intake surge.
- Scenario D (pre-analytical defect storm + courier compression): Early triage catches defects within 2h, unblocking accession specialists to handle the surge instead of being stuck on complex corrections.
- Scenario B, C, E (other stresses): Pre-analytical defect rate does not change; early triage does not help but does not hurt (small overhead cost).

**Next bottleneck risk:** Overly aggressive rejection may increase physician frustration if samples are rejected for minor deviations. If resampling requests are not fulfilled promptly, recollection loop delay increases instead of decreasing. Requires close coordination with phlebotomy and physician outreach.

---

#### **R3: Flexible Medical Validation Staffing (I5 + Cross-Training)**
Prepare for medical-validator absence by:
- Cross-training two technical validators to perform basic medical validation (validation of routine results, flag-release decisions for simple cases).
- Establishing an evening/overflow medical-validation shift (4–8 hours, 2–3 evenings per week during normal periods; ramp to daily during peaks).
- Defining which case types require full medical-validation vs. junior validation.

**Design:**
- Medical-validator job: Validate complex results, interpret borderline findings, sign-off on cases with conflicting data.
- Junior validator (cross-trained technical validator): Validate routine results, confirm assay completion, flag outliers for medical validator review.
- Routine cases (60%+ of normal volume): Junior validation pathway.
- Complex-panel cases (13%+ of volume): Full medical validator required.
- Urgent cases: Junior validation + fast-track full validation if needed (e.g., if flag is present).
- Assumption: Cross-training is successful and junior validators do not create excessive re-review burden.
- Dependency: 4-week training investment; quality audits required.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario A (mix shift toward Urgent/Complex): Complex-panel fraction increases (13%  22%); full validators are still bottlenecked, but junior validators absorb Routine overflow.
- Scenario C (medical-validator absence): Remaining full validator + junior validators keep medical validation queue from exploding; junior validation reduces wait from 20h+ to 10–12h.
- Scenario E (compounded): Junior validators + evening shift provide 30–40% capacity increase, reducing medical-validation p50 from 30h to 18–20h (still stressed but more manageable).

**Next bottleneck risk:** If junior validators create more re-review than they resolve, medical validators spend time correcting junior decisions, worsening the bottleneck. If cross-training is incomplete, junior validators miss flagging complex cases, increasing rework downstream. Requires robust quality audit and feedback loop.

---

#### **R4: Accession Correction Specialist Cross-Training (I4, Targeted Application)**
Prepare for accession-correction bottleneck (Scenario D) by enabling technical validators to perform simple corrections (barcode relabeling, metadata reconciliation).

**Design:**
- Accession correction has two tiers:
  - **Simple:** Barcode relabeling, metadata reconciliation, tube consolidation (no clinical judgment required).
  - **Complex:** Sample integrity assessment, physician contact for protocol clarification, rerouting to different test.
- One of two accession correction specialists focuses on complex corrections.
- Technical validators perform simple corrections during pre-analytical surge (triggered by queue length >8h).
- Assumption: Technical validators can spare 10–20% of time during normal operations; simple corrections are well-defined and do not require specialist expertise.
- Dependency: Requires clear SOP and quality checks; if simple corrections are misclassified as complex, the burden increases.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario D (courier compression + defect storm): Accession correction queue rises to 14h+; technical validators can absorb simple corrections, preventing the backlog from blocking test preparation input.
- Scenario A (mix shift): Accession correction capacity is stable (pre-analytical failure rate depends on case mix, not volume alone); cross-training provides slack.
- Scenario B, C, E (other stresses): Accession correction is not the primary bottleneck; cross-training does not help but adds small overhead.

**Next bottleneck risk:** If technical validators are diverted to accession correction, technical validation queue backs up. This is a direct trade-off; the policy should only activate when accession correction queue is critical (e.g., >8h median, and assay machines are underutilized). See Scenario-specific Policies (Section 4) for trigger conditions.

---

#### **R5: Assay Machine Scheduling with Repeat-Isolation**
Redesign assay scheduling to reduce repeat-assay congestion and improve Urgent-case SLA compliance without fragmenting machine schedules excessively.

**Design:**
- **Standard window (06:00–18:00):** M1 and M2 process cases in order of arrival, batching where possible (Routine cases, 12–20 per batch depending on mix). Urgent cases bypass batch wait (~60% of time, not 74%, to accommodate complexity).
- **Evening window (18:00–22:00):** M1 reserved for repeat assays and urgent cases requiring same-day re-assay. M2 runs routine batches if available.
- **Night window (22:00–06:00):** M1 idles or runs maintenance. M2 runs routine batches queued from evening.
- **Batch size policy:** Routine batches = 16 samples (compromise between I2's 24 and current 12). Urgent cases batch if >4 cases are waiting (else run singly). Complex-panel cases batch loosely (2–4, allowing protocol variation).
- **Repeat-assay rule:** If repeat rate exceeds 8% in 4-hour window, re-assay is deferred to evening window and physician is notified (no SLA impact if re-assay is within 12h of first failure).
- **Assumption:** Repeat-assay deferral does not violate medical necessity; repeat assays are confirmatory, not critical diagnostic.
- **Dependency:** Requires operator discipline and case-type metadata accuracy; if operators run repeats during standard windows, evening capacity is not freed.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario A (mix shift): Urgent cases increase (35% of mix); evening window absorbs repeat-assay backlog, keeping M1 available for fresh Urgent cases during peak hours.
- Scenario B (assay degradation): If M2 failure rate rises, M2 is relegated to routine-batch processing during evening/night; urgent and repeat cases stay on M1. Repeat-assay window contains the feedback loop.
- Scenario D, E (other stresses): Batching policy is stable; repeat-assay isolation reduces assay queue variance.

**Next bottleneck risk:** If evening window does not have sufficient operator coverage, repeat assays queue into the night, delaying physician communication. If case mix does not allow good batching (e.g., too many Urgent cases), batch size policy is violated, reducing efficiency. Requires monitoring of batch sizes and evening operator availability.

---

#### **R6: Courier Arrival Smoothing and Intake Flow Control (I7 Implementation)**
Redesign courier schedules to smooth arrival peaks and coordinate with registration capacity.

**Design:**
- **Current state:** Two arrivals at 10:00 and 17:00; creates intake surges.
- **Redesign:** Request courier service to deliver in three windows: 09:00, 14:00, and 19:00 (staggered, 5-hour intervals). If impossible, negotiate for two vehicles at staggered times (e.g., 09:30 and 10:30 instead of simultaneous 10:00).
- **Backup:** If courier compression is unavoidable, establish a temporary intake buffer (refrigerated holding area) with a second registration station that can process arrivals in 30-minute windows, queuing them for formal registration during off-peak.
- **Assumption:** Courier service is willing to adjust schedules; if not, the intake buffer is the only lever.
- **Dependency:** Requires negotiation with courier vendor; 2–4 week lead time for schedule change.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario D (courier compression): Smoothed arrivals prevent registration and intake queue spike; reduces defect rate from rush handling.
- Scenario A (volume surge): Three arrival windows spread intake load, reducing peak registration queue from 2.8h to ~1.5h.
- Other scenarios: Courier smoothing does not affect process stability but provides slack for other disruptions.

**Next bottleneck risk:** If staggered arrivals reduce total arrival volume density but increase tail latency (e.g., 19:00 arrival is processed the next morning), some cases may miss the day-shift assay window. Requires careful timing to ensure third arrival is processed during evening shift.

---

#### **R7: Minimum Viable Outsourcing (I10, Selective)**
Prepare for medical-validation and specialist-review saturation (Scenarios A, C, E) with selective outsourcing of Complex-panel overflow.

**Design:**
- Establish SLA with a partner lab: Complex-panel overflow cases (>30% of daily target) can be sent for assay + basic technical validation, returning results with 12-hour transport delay.
- Trigger: Activate if Hold for specialist review queue >30h median or medical validation queue >25h median for 2 consecutive days.
- Case selection: Complex-panel cases with low specialist-review probability (e.g., metabolic panels, routine multi-marker panels); exclude oncology, immunology, rare-disease panels.
- Expected volume: ~5–10% of Complex-panel cases during peak surge.
- Assumption: Partner lab results are reliable and legally acceptable; 12-hour delay is tolerable for non-urgent complex panels.
- Dependency: Requires pre-negotiated SLA, legal agreements, and quality audits; 8–12 weeks to establish.

**Why robust:**
- Scenario A (mix shift toward Complex-panel): Outsourcing 5–10% of Complex-panel cases removes ~1–2% from total medical-validation queue, providing 3–5h of relief (not fully resolvable, but material).
- Scenario C (medical-validator absence): Outsourcing + I5 + R3 together reduce medical-validation queue from 30h to ~18–20h.
- Scenario E (compounded): Outsourcing + full redesign package keeps system acceptable (no SLA violation, <40h p90 for most case types).

**Next bottleneck risk:** If outsourcing is triggered inappropriately (e.g., due to temporary queue spike), partner lab is flooded and backlog increases. If partner lab quality is inconsistent, rework and re-testing increase, offsetting the benefit. Requires tight trigger conditions and quality audits.

---

### **Robust Package Summary**
- **R1–R7 together provide:**
  - Segmented pathways to isolate case types and reduce interference.
  - Early defect triage to prevent recollection-loop cascade.
  - Flexible medical validation to absorb mix shifts and staff absence.
  - Accession-correction cross-training to handle intake surges.
  - Repeat-assay isolation to contain feedback loops.
  - Courier smoothing to prevent intake shocks.
  - Selective outsourcing as a relief valve for sustained saturation.

- **Assumptions:**
  - Case-type classification is stable and accurate.
  - Staff cross-training is successful and sustainable.
  - Courier service and partner lab are willing and able to partner.
  - Physician resampling requests are fulfilled promptly.
  - Repeat-assay deferrals do not introduce clinical risk.

- **Dependencies:**
  - 4–12 weeks implementation time (training, vendor negotiation, SOP updates).
  - Quality audits and feedback loops required throughout.
  - Monitoring and adaptive trigger policies (Section 4) are essential for success.

## 4. Scenario-Specific Policies

### Policy A: Surge Case-Mix Shift (Trigger: Scenario A)
**Trigger condition:** 
- Urgent case volume (7-day rolling average) exceeds 25% of total cases AND
- Complex-panel case volume exceeds 18% of total cases AND
- Routine case volume falls below 45% of total cases.
- **Leading indicator:** Registration data shows case-type mix shift within 24 hours.

**Activation:**
1. **I5 (Evening medical-validation shift):** Activate immediately; recruit one medical validator for 18:00–22:00 shift (4 hours/day). This adds ~10% to medical-validation capacity.
2. **R3 (Cross-trained junior validators):** Activate if medical-validation queue exceeds 8h median. Route Routine cases to junior validators; retain Complex-panel and Urgent cases for full validators.
3. **R5 (Repeat-assay isolation):** Increase evening-window allocation from 2 hours to 3 hours; shift one operator to evening shift.
4. **I9 (Batch release reporting):** Optional, deferred pending I5 effectiveness. Do NOT activate initially; monitor medical-validation queue first.

**Monitoring signals (daily):**
- Medical-validation queue p50 (target <10h, warning >12h, critical >15h).
- Urgent case release time p90 (target <18h, warning >20h, critical >22h).
- Complex-panel case release time p90 (target <120h, warning >140h, critical >160h).

**Deactivation condition:**
- Urgent case volume (7-day rolling average) drops below 22% of total cases AND
- Complex-panel case volume drops below 16% AND
- Medical-validation queue p50 returns to <8h for 3 consecutive days.
- Ramp down: Evening shift reduced to 2 evenings/week; junior validators stay on call but not fully scheduled.

---

### Policy B: Assay-Machine Degradation (Trigger: Scenario B)
**Trigger condition:**
- Repeat-assay rate (4-hour rolling) exceeds 10% of cases processed in that window OR
- M2 QC error rate exceeds 5% per shift, indicating calibration or mechanical drift.
- **Leading indicator:** M2 QC log shows drift; operator reports longer cycle times.

**Activation:**
1. **Assay machine diagnostics:** Immediately inspect M2; if repair time is <4 hours, halt M2 and route all work to M1. If >4 hours, run M2 with reduced batch size (8 samples instead of 12–16) and priority to Routine cases only (Urgent and repeat assays go to M1).
2. **I3 (Machine dedication, inverted):** Route Urgent cases and repeat assays exclusively to M1. Route Routine cases to M2 if M2 is operational. If M2 repair extends >8 hours, halt M2 completely and run sequential M1 with extended evening shift (20:00–06:00, including night operator).
3. **I2 (Batch size adjustment):** Reduce Routine batch size from 16 to 12 to increase flexibility in M1 scheduling if M2 is down.
4. **R5 (Repeat-assay isolation):** Strengthen evening-window isolation; do NOT process fresh samples during repeat-assay window if M2 is degraded.

**Monitoring signals (daily / per-shift):**
- M2 QC error rate (target <3%, warning 4%, critical 5%+).
- Repeat-assay cycle time (target 10–14h, warning >20h, critical >26h).
- Assay machine queue length (target <2h, warning >5h, critical >10h).
- Urgent case release time p90 (target <18h, warning >20h, critical >25h).

**Deactivation condition:**
- M2 QC error rate returns to <3% for 2 consecutive shifts.
- Repeat-assay cycle time drops to <14h p50.
- Assay queue p50 returns to <4h.

**Contingency (M2 unrepairable within 24h):**
- Activate I10 (outsourcing): Send non-urgent Complex-panel and Routine assays to partner lab if M1 queue exceeds 12h for >4 hours (indicating sustained overload). Transport delay is acceptable if Urgent cases are prioritized locally.

---

### Policy C: Medical-Validator or Specialist Absence (Trigger: Scenario C)
**Trigger condition:**
- One or more medical validators absent for >24 hours (planned or unplanned) OR
- Specialist reviewer absent for >2 days (e.g., Friday off, or multi-day illness).
- **Leading indicator:** Absence notification from HR or staff scheduling system.

**Activation (on notification, not on queue-length trigger):**
1. **R3 (Cross-trained junior validators):** Immediately activate; route Routine cases (target 60% of volume) to junior validators. Retain Complex-panel and Urgent cases for remaining full validator(s).
2. **I5 (Evening medical-validation shift):** Activate if absence is >48 hours; recruit evening validator to ease queue formation during absence window.
3. **Hold for specialist review (specialist absence):** If specialist is absent:
   - Option A: Defer non-urgent holds to the specialist's return date; retain only urgent/critical holds in queue (estimated ~30–40% of holds).
   - Option B: Activate I10 (outsourcing) for complex cases that would require specialist review, routing to partner lab's specialist consultants if available (add 12h delay).
   - **Default: Option A** (defer); activate Option B only if deferred holds exceed 20 cases.
4. **I9 (Batch release reporting, optional):** Do NOT activate; batch reporting would delay release further during absence.

**Monitoring signals (daily during absence):**
- Medical-validation queue p50 (target <8h, warning >10h, critical >15h).
- Hold for specialist review queue (target cleared daily, warning >5 cases pending, critical >10 cases pending).
- Urgent case release time p90 (target <18h, warning >20h, critical >25h).
- Complex-panel case release time p90 (target <120h, warning >140h, critical >160h).

**Deactivation condition:**
- Absent medical validator or specialist returns to full availability.
- Medical-validation queue p50 <8h and Hold for specialist review queue <5 cases pending for 1 full day after return.

---

### Policy D: Courier Compression and Pre-Analytical Defect Storm (Trigger: Scenario D)
**Trigger condition:**
- Courier intake queue >7.5h median (indicating 2+ hours of compressed arrival) AND
- Pre-analytical recollection request rate >6% (elevated defect rate) OR
- Registration queue >2h median for >2 consecutive hours.
- **Leading indicator:** Courier manifest shows multiple routes arriving in same 2-hour window; pre-analytical check log shows defect rate spike within 1 hour of arrival.

**Activation:**
1. **I7 (Courier smoothing, immediate):** Contact courier service immediately; request separation of routes or sequential vehicle arrival (30-minute intervals). If unavailable, activate intake buffer (Option B: refrigerated holding area + second registration station).
2. **I6 (Early pre-analytical defect triage):** Activate front-end visual inspection; flag poor samples within 30 minutes of receipt and issue recollection request immediately (target physician notification <1 hour, resampling same-day business window).
3. **I8 (Partial automation):** Activate barcode auto-entry and metadata pre-check for orders with complete digital metadata (estimated 30–40% of orders). Skip manual re-entry, reducing registration time per order from 5 minutes to 2 minutes.
4. **R4 (Accession-correction cross-training):** Activate if accession-correction queue >8h median; technical validators perform simple barcode/metadata corrections during surge window.
5. **Pre-analytical check staffing:** Add one additional senior technician to pre-analytical check during surge window (e.g., 09:00–14:00 if arrival is 09:00–10:30). This is a short-term manual intervention, not a permanent hire.

**Monitoring signals (hourly during surge):**
- Courier intake queue (target <1.5h, warning >3h, critical >5h).
- Registration queue (target <0.5h, warning >1h, critical >2h).
- Pre-analytical recollection request rate (target <5%, warning 6%, critical >8%).
- Accession-correction queue (target <3h, warning >5h, critical >8h).
- Test preparation input rate (target stable, warning <-20% deviation from 1-hour average, indicating upstream blockage).

**Deactivation condition:**
- Courier intake queue returns to <2h.
- Pre-analytical recollection request rate drops to <5% for 1 full business day.
- Registration queue <0.5h for 4 consecutive hours.
- Courier service confirms return to normal multi-vehicle staggered schedule (or intake buffer is deactivated if no permanent schedule change is negotiated).

---

### Policy E: Compounded Stress (A + C, Trigger: Scenario E)
**Trigger condition:**
- Case-mix shift (Urgent >25%, Complex-panel >18%, Routine <45%) AND
- Medical-validator or specialist absence (>24 hours).
- **Leading indicator:** Case-type mix shift + HR absence notification simultaneously.

**Activation:**
1. **R3 + I5 (Full medical-validation reinforcement):** Activate evening shift (18:00–22:00) with two validators if possible, or one validator + one junior validator (mentored). Cross-train junior validators fully. Target: medical-validation capacity +40%.
2. **R1 (Segmented pathways):** Fully activate fast path for Urgent cases (M1 dedicated, senior pre-analytical technician). Recycle path for repeat assays (evening window expansion to 3 hours).
3. **I10 (Selective outsourcing):** Activate immediately; target 5–10% of Complex-panel cases to partner lab (those with low specialist-review probability). This reduces medical-validation input by ~2% of total cases, providing ~4–5 hours of relief.
4. **R4 (Accession-correction cross-training):** Activate proactively; do NOT wait for queue to exceed 8h. Allocate one technical validator to accession correction support for the duration of the absence/surge.
5. **I6 (Early defect triage):** Activate front-end triage; use it to reduce rework rate during stress. Target: reduce rework from 11.8% (peak normal) to ~9%.
6. **Workload coordination:** Implement daily huddle (08:00, 14:00, 20:00) to assess queues and reallocate staff as needed (cross-training deployments, outsourcing triggers, defect-triage staffing).

**Monitoring signals (daily, with 08:00 huddle review):**
- **Urgent case release time p90** (target <18h, warning >20h, critical >25h; breach = SLA violation).
- **Complex-panel case release time p90** (target <120h, warning >140h, critical >160h).
- **Medical-validation queue p50** (target <12h, warning >15h, critical >20h).
- **Hold for specialist review queue** (target cleared daily, warning >5 cases, critical >10 cases).
- **Assay machine queue** (target <4h, warning >6h, critical >10h).
- **Pre-analytical recollection rate** (target <6%, warning >7%, critical >9%).
- **Rework rate** (target <8%, warning >10%, critical >12%).

**Deactivation condition:**
- Case-mix shift reverses (Urgent <22%, Complex-panel <16%, Routine >50%) AND
- Medical validator/specialist returns to full availability.
- All queue metrics return to normal-period baselines for 3 consecutive days.
- Outsourcing load drops to <2% of Complex-panel cases.

## 6. Residual Vulnerabilities

### **V1: Urgent-Case SLA Breakage Under Compounded Stress (Scenario E)**
**Description:** Even with R3 (flexible medical validation) + I5 (evening shift) + cross-trained juniors, medical-validation queue can reach 18–20h p50 during extreme compounded stress (28% volume increase + 50% medical-validator loss). Urgent cases that complete assay in 8–10h still wait 15–20h for medical validation, breaching the 18h SLA for tail cases.

**Root cause:** Medical validation is a global funnel for all cases needing validation; no fast-path bypass exists for Urgent cases.

**Residual impact:** 5–10% of Urgent cases breach 18h SLA during Policy E activation.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Activate I10 (outsourcing) for Complex-panel cases as soon as medical-validation queue reaches 15h, removing 2–3% of input (1–2 cases/hour).
- Consider rare emergency medical-validation bypass for Urgent cases with critical results (e.g., sepsis markers, cardiac troponin); physician can release result under medical validator's standing order with 24h post-release audit. This is risky and requires regulatory approval.

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Medical validation is a safety/compliance requirement; it cannot be entirely eliminated or parallelized. The system is fundamentally constrained by the number of validators.

---

### **V2: Cross-Trained Staff Burnout and Quality Degradation (R3, R4)**
**Description:** Cross-trained technical validators working as junior medical validators or accession-correction support will be deployed frequently during peak periods. After 2–3 weeks of dual-role deployment, quality errors increase (missed flags, incorrect corrections, missed complex cases), requiring senior validators to spend time on re-review. This re-review burden eventually exceeds the capacity gain from cross-training.

**Root cause:** Cross-training is a form of staff stretching; it works in short bursts but degrades under sustained load.

**Residual impact:** After 3–4 weeks of Policy A or E activation, re-review rate climbs from <2% to 5–8%, requiring one full validator to spend ~20% of time correcting junior errors. Net capacity gain = 30% loss, which is less than the 40% target.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Limit cross-training deployment to <4 weeks at a time; rotate staff back to primary roles for 1 week recovery.
- Establish strict quality audits for junior validators; if re-review rate exceeds 5%, immediately reduce junior-validator case load and increase mentoring.
- Hire permanent part-time medical validators or contract validators during peak season (requires 4–8 week onboarding; not suitable for urgent response).

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Staffing constraints are real; the lab cannot sustainably maintain a 40% capacity boost through cross-training alone. The redesign shifts the bottleneck to human capital, not process.

---

### **V3: Repeat-Assay Isolation Delays Non-Urgent Repeats (R5)**
**Description:** R5 isolates repeat assays to evening window (18:00–22:00) to prioritize fresh Urgent cases during peak hours. However, if repeat-assay queue grows (e.g., due to M2 degradation in Scenario B), repeat assays may not complete until 02:00–04:00 (including analytical time + validation). Repeat-after-failure cases then wait until next morning for technical validation, adding 8–12 hours to cycle time. Repeat-after-failure p90 can reach 150–160h (already stressed at baseline 132h), becoming unacceptable.

**Root cause:** Evening window has finite capacity; if repeat-assay batch exceeds evening + night capacity, repeats queue into next morning.

**Residual impact:** Repeat-after-failure cases experience p90 cycle time of 150–160h under Scenario B + Policy B activation (during M2 degradation).

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Increase evening operator availability to 1.5 operators (instead of 1) during Scenario B; but this requires on-call staffing, adding cost.
- Allow some repeat assays to run during standard daytime window if assay queue is <2h (i.e., dynamic reallocation). However, this undermines the fast-path dedication for Urgent cases.
- Negotiate with physicians to accept delayed result release for Repeat-after-failure cases (<24h post-assay, instead of real-time). This is a policy change that may reduce clinic satisfaction.

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Assay-machine capacity is fixed; if repeat volume is high and assay-machine utilization is already high, repeats must queue. The trade-off between Urgent-case SLA and Repeat-after-failure case SLA is unavoidable.

---

### **V4: Partner Lab Quality Variability (R7, I10)**
**Description:** Selective outsourcing (R7) relies on a partner lab to reliably return results in <12 hours with >99% accuracy. If the partner lab experiences their own queue delays (>14 hours instead of 12 hours) or quality issues (>1% error rate), the benefit of outsourcing is lost. Additionally, results from a partner lab introduce traceability complexity (sample chain-of-custody, result reconciliation, potential disagreements on result interpretation).

**Root cause:** Quality and schedule are outside the local lab's control; partner lab may have their own stress scenarios.

**Residual impact:** If partner lab TAT extends to 14–16 hours and cases are returned with discrepancies, rework rate climbs and external clients lose confidence in outsourcing arrangement.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Establish rigorous SLA with partner lab, including penalties for TAT breach and quality issues. Require weekly quality audits and TAT tracking.
- Define a clear criteria for outsourcing-partnership suspension (e.g., >2 TAT breaches in 1 week, or >1.5% error rate). If suspended, cases return to local processing, overwhelming the system temporarily.
- Do NOT outsource cases with rare or complex results (requires specialist interpretation); limit outsourcing to metabolic panels, routine multi-marker panels, and other standardized test types.

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Partner lab is outside the control boundary; the lab cannot force reliability. The partnership is only as good as the partner's own capacity and quality. This is a structural vulnerability that requires ongoing vendor management.

---

### **V5: Courier Smoothing Dependency on External Vendor (R6, I7)**
**Description:** R6 assumes the courier service will agree to stagger arrival times (e.g., 09:00, 14:00, 19:00 instead of 10:00, 17:00). If the courier service refuses (e.g., due to route constraints or cost), the intake compression persists. Fallback is to build an intake buffer (refrigerated holding area), which adds 2–4 hours to cycle time for cases arriving at the second vehicle. This delays Urgent cases and violates their SLA if they arrive in the second batch.

**Root cause:** Courier logistics are outside the lab's control; the lab cannot unilaterally change courier schedules.

**Residual impact:** Under Scenario D (courier compression), if smoothing is not negotiated, intake buffer adds 2–4h latency; Urgent cases arriving in second batch (17:00–18:00) complete pre-analytical check ~19:00–20:00, making 18h SLA unachievable if assay is queued.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Negotiate early with courier vendor during normal periods (not during crisis). Offer financial incentive for staggered delivery.
- If smoothing is not possible, accept intake buffer as a permanent fixture and build buffer processing into the daily schedule (i.e., secondary registration station operates 16:00–20:00 daily, processing second-batch arrivals).
- Alternatively, shift case-type classification to faster/slower tracks: if a case arrives in second courier batch, route it to Routine track (longer acceptable TAT), not Urgent track (unless clinical urgency is documented).

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Courier contracts often pre-specify delivery times; renegotiation is slow (4–8 weeks) and may not succeed. The lab is structurally dependent on courier behavior.

---

### **V6: Pre-Analytical Defect Triage False-Positive Rate (R2, I6)**
**Description:** Early defect triage (R2) visually inspects and rejects samples with defects (hemolysis, leakage, etc.). However, visual inspection has a false-positive rate (samples rejected that are actually usable) and a false-negative rate (defects missed). If false-positive rate exceeds 2–3%, many physicians will become frustrated and either resubmit immediately (creating recollection loops that negate triage benefit) or challenge the lab's rejection decision (requiring reversal and reprocessing, adding rework).

**Root cause:** Visual inspection is imperfect; some defects (mild hemolysis, slight leakage) are borderline and clinical judgment is required.

**Residual impact:** If false-positive rate reaches 3–4%, physician outreach burden increases by 30–40%; some urgent resubmissions create same-day recollection loops, paradoxically increasing intake queue.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Use automated hemolysis detection (spectroscopic) instead of visual inspection alone; this reduces false-positives from ~3% to ~1%.
- Define clear defect thresholds and physician communication protocol; allow physician to request lab discretion for borderline cases (e.g., mild hemolysis with clinical justification).
- Monitor physician feedback and adjust rejection criteria based on clinical outcomes (e.g., if rejected samples are later resubmitted and produce valid results, triage criteria are too strict).

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Early triage inherently has a trade-off between sensitivity (catching real defects) and specificity (avoiding false rejections). The lab must accept some level of imperfection.

---

### **V7: M1 Single-Point-of-Failure Risk (R1, R5, Policy B)**
**Description:** R1 and Policy B dedicate M1 to Urgent cases and repeats, improving Urgent-case SLA compliance. However, if M1 fails (e.g., mechanical breakdown, software crash), the fast path collapses. Urgent cases then queue onto M2, which is already processing Routine cases. If M2 is degraded (Scenario B), Urgent cases may wait >8 hours for assay, breaching the 18h SLA.

**Root cause:** Operational dependency on a single machine; no redundancy.

**Residual impact:** M1 unplanned downtime (expected ~1% annually, or ~1 week/year) causes Urgent SLA violations. Peak-period downtime is most painful; if M1 fails during Policy E activation (compounded stress), Urgent cases have no recourse.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Invest in M1 predictive maintenance; monitor performance daily and conduct preventive maintenance during off-peak periods (e.g., weekend, evening).
- Establish a rapid M1 repair/vendor contract; aim for <4-hour response time for hardware issues.
- Create a contingency: if M1 is down, immediately activate I10 (outsourcing) for Routine cases, freeing M2 capacity for Urgent cases. This requires advance partner-lab negotiation and may not be feasible on short notice.
- Cross-train operators to diagnose common M1 issues and perform basic fixes (e.g., sensor cleaning, calibration reset); this reduces repair time from 4 hours to <1 hour for ~20% of failures.

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Machine failures are rare but non-zero; the lab cannot eliminate downtime risk. The mitigation is to minimize mean-time-to-repair (MTTR) and have fallback plans.

---

### **V8: Case-Type Metadata Accuracy (R1 Dependency)**
**Description:** R1 (segmented pathways) and I3 (machine dedication) require accurate case-type classification at registration. If case-type is misclassified (e.g., Routine case marked as Urgent, or Complex-panel marked as Routine), the fast path is either overloaded or underutilized. Misclassification errors can result from:
- Physician ordering errors (wrong order code).
- Clerk data-entry errors (transcription of order code).
- Ambiguous case definitions (e.g., is a multi-marker panel "Complex-panel" or "Routine"?).

**Root cause:** Case-type classification is manual and error-prone; no automated validation of classification logic.

**Residual impact:** If case-type misclassification rate exceeds 5%, fast-path benefits degrade significantly. Urgent cases misclassified as Routine are delayed; Routine cases misclassified as Urgent waste M1 capacity.

**Mitigation (imperfect):**
- Implement automated case-type validation at registration: cross-reference order code with test type and volume requirements; flag suspicious classifications for clerk review before order acceptance.
- Establish a weekly audit of case-type classifications: sample 50–100 orders and verify classification accuracy; provide feedback to registration team.
- Define clear, unambiguous case-type rules (e.g., "Complex-panel = any order with >4 test codes from >2 clinical domains"). Publish rules and train staff.

**Why this cannot be fully solved:** Classification is subjective at the boundary; some cases will always be ambiguous. The lab must accept ~2–3% misclassification rate as baseline and monitor for drift.

## 7. Minimum Monitoring Dashboard

### **Daily Leading Indicators (Monitor 08:00, 14:00, 20:00)**

#### **1. Intake and Registration**
- **Queue length: Courier intake (target <2h, warning >3h, critical >5h)**
  - Early warning of arrival compression or staffing shortage.
  - Leading to Registration queue, Pre-analytical check backup.
  
- **Case-type mix (Urgent %, Complex-panel %, Routine %)**
  - Detect Scenario A trigger: Urgent >25%, Complex >18%, Routine <45%.
  - Activate Policy A if thresholds breached for 2 consecutive days.

- **Registration error rate (% of orders requiring re-entry or correction)**
  - Target <1%. Spike indicates training gap or system issues.
  - Leading to Pre-analytical check delays and accession correction load.

#### **2. Pre-Analytical and Accession**
- **Pre-analytical recollection request rate (target <5%, warning 6%, critical >8%)**
  - Leading indicator of defect rate or operator error.
  - Detect Scenario D trigger: if >6% and courier intake queue >7.5h simultaneously, activate Policy D.
  
- **Accession-correction queue length (target <3h, warning >5h, critical >8h)**
  - Early warning of intake shock or pre-analytical defect surge.
  - Trigger for R4 (technical-validator cross-training): if queue >8h AND assay queue <4h, activate technical-validator support.

- **Pre-analytical check queue length (target <2h, warning >4h, critical >6h)**
  - Indicates bottleneck forming at pre-analytical stage.
  - If queue spike is concurrent with courier intake spike, likely Scenario D.

#### **3. Preparation and Assay**
- **Test-preparation input rate (cases/hour entering from pre-analytical check)**
  - Monitor for consistency; large drops indicate upstream blockage.
  - Target: stable within ±10% of hourly average.
  - Warning: deviation >±15% for >2 hours, indicating upstream queue formation.

- **Assay-machine queue length and utilization (target queue <4h, utilization 65–80%, critical >90%)**
  - M1 queue (target <3h, critical >6h): if exceeded, Urgent cases are delayed.
  - M2 queue (target <4h, critical >8h): if exceeded and M2 QC error rate >3%, activate Policy B (M2 degradation protocol).
  - Total assay utilization (target <75% normal periods, warning >85%, critical >95%): indicates capacity constraints.

- **Repeat-assay rate per 4-hour window (target <8%, warning >10%, critical >12%)**
  - Detect Scenario B trigger: if repeat rate >10% in same machine for 2 consecutive windows, initiate M2 diagnostics.
  - Activate Policy B if M2 error rate confirmed >5%.

- **M2 QC error rate (target <3%, warning 4%, critical >5%)**
  - Direct indicator of M2 degradation (Scenario B).
  - If critical, halts M2 immediately; routes work to M1 or evening queue.

#### **4. Technical and Medical Validation**
- **Technical-validation queue length (target <4h, warning >6h, critical >10h)**
  - Indicates assay output backlog or downstream (medical validation) starvation.
  - If technical queue grows while medical queue is short, medical validation has input starvation; check for staff absence or downtime.

- **Medical-validation queue length (target <8h, warning >12h, critical >15h)**
  - Detect Scenario A/C/E triggers: if queue >12h and case-mix shift confirmed (Scenario A), activate Policy A.
  - If queue >15h and medical-validator absence confirmed (Scenario C), activate Policy C.
  - If queue >20h with compounded stress (Scenario E), activate Policy E + I10 (outsourcing).

- **Medical-validator re-review rate (target <2%, warning 3%, critical >5%)**
  - Monitor quality of junior validators (R3); if re-review spike, reduce junior-validator load and increase mentoring.
  - Leading indicator of cross-training degradation (V2).

- **Hold for specialist review queue (target cleared daily, warning >5 cases, critical >10 cases)**
  - Detect Scenario C trigger: specialist absence + queue growth.
  - If queue >10 cases for >2 hours, activate Policy C (defer non-urgent holds; activate Option B outsourcing if needed).

#### **5. Release and SLA Compliance**
- **Urgent case release time p90 (target <18h, warning >20h, critical >25h)**
  - Primary SLA metric; breach indicates systematic Urgent-case delay.
  - If p90 >20h for 2 consecutive days, investigate medical-validation or technical-validation queue.
  - Activate policies (A, B, C, D, or E) based on root cause.

- **Complex-panel case release time p90 (target <120h, warning >140h, critical >160h)**
  - Secondary SLA metric; breach indicates sustained bottleneck in medical validation.
  - If p90 >140h and specialist review queue >5 cases, likely Scenario C or E; activate Policy C or E.

- **Routine case release time median (target <38h, warning >50h, critical >70h)**
  - Tertiary metric; less critical than Urgent/Complex-panel but indicates overall congestion.
  - If median >50h and assay queue is short, bottleneck is medical validation; activate Policy A.

#### **6. Rework and Quality**
- **Overall rework rate (target <8%, warning >10%, critical >12%)**
  - Includes all re-tests, recollections, and repeat assays.
  - Spike indicates quality issue or process error.
  - If rework >10% and pre-analytical recollection rate <6%, issue is likely in assay or validation; check M2 QC.

- **Repeat-assay cycle time p50 (target 10–14h, warning >18h, critical >26h)**
  - Indicates efficiency of repeat-assay isolation (R5) and evening window utilization.
  - If cycle time >18h, evening window is overloaded or next-morning backlog is growing; may need additional evening operator.

### **Scenario-Specific Trigger Monitoring**

**For Scenario A (Case-mix shift):**
- Urgent % >25% AND Complex % >18% AND Routine % <45% (7-day rolling average)  Activate Policy A within 2 hours.

**For Scenario B (Assay degradation):**
- Repeat rate >10% in 4-hour window OR M2 QC error rate >5%  Activate Policy B diagnostics within 1 hour; decision to halt M2 within 2 hours.

**For Scenario C (Medical-validator absence):**
- Medical-validation queue p50 >10h AND HR absence notification logged  Activate Policy C within 1 hour.

**For Scenario D (Courier compression + defect storm):**
- Courier intake queue >7.5h AND pre-analytical recollection rate >6%  Activate Policy D within 30 minutes.

**For Scenario E (Compounded stress):**
- (Urgent >25% AND Complex >18% AND Routine <45%) AND (Medical-validation queue >15h)  Activate Policy E within 1 hour; daily huddle 08:00, 14:00, 20:00.

## Summary

This redesign balances **robustness across scenarios** with **practical implementation**:

1. **Robust interventions (R1–R7)** handle most plausible disruptions without requiring perfect prediction.
2. **Scenario-specific policies (A–E)** activate adaptively when triggers are detected, redirecting resources where needed.
3. **Trade-off table** transparently shows the cost of each intervention (e.g., R1 may slow Routine cases if M1 overloads; R3 reduces technical-validation capacity if overused).
4. **Residual vulnerabilities (V1–V8)** acknowledge that no solution is perfect; they identify where the system remains fragile.
5. **Monitoring dashboard** provides hourly leading indicators so policies can be activated quickly, before lagging SLA breaches occur.

The key insight is that **bottlenecks migrate**: solving pre-analytical check (I6) exposes accession correction; solving accession correction (I4) exposes preparation; solving preparation exposes assay; solving assay exposes medical validation. The redesign acknowledges this migration and prepares adaptive capacity at multiple checkpoints, rather than attempting a one-time "fix."
