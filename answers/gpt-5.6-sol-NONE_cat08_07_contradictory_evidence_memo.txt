# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Fiber Operations Executive Committee  
**Decision:** Adopt **Alternative E**, combined with the controlled learning elements of **D**: deploy the redesigned path only for simple, low-risk houses; retain coordinator ownership more broadly with safeguards; immediately suspend survey skipping for medium-risk and permit-sensitive properties and prohibit billing before a successful activation test.

Redesign R is neither a general success nor a general failure. It improved the typical customer journey: median duration fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, visible cost declined, and cases with three or more handovers dropped from 34% to 12%. Simple low-risk houses received both faster and better service, including an eight-day p90 improvement, a three-point increase in first-time-right installation, and fewer complaints.

Those gains do not justify national scaling of R as currently designed. Overall p90 and p95 duration rose sharply; first-time-right performance fell from 82% to 76%; second visits, manual activations, complaints, and SLA breaches increased. Estimated full cost is also higher, although incomplete. Older buildings and medium-risk apartments account for the clearest harm. The alignments and temporal profiles reinforce this concern: billing is frequently started before successful activation, expected permit checks are absent, repeat visits occur without prior surveys, and post-billing complaints form a new long-tail relationship.

The process-mining results should not be read as evidence that the pilot is controlled merely because fitness rose to 0.94. Precision fell to 0.51 and generalization to 0.56. The model reproduces observed cases but permits many combinations of skipped checks, manual activation, revisits, and billing. This is consistent with a fast dominant path plus a proliferating exception system.

The most defensible next step is therefore **selective exploitation plus controlled learning**:

1. **Scale the combined validation/scheduling path for simple, low-risk houses**, where outcomes improved across central and tail measures.
2. **Preserve coordinator ownership provisionally**, because fewer handovers and clearer ownership appear valuable, but cap utilization, establish backup ownership, and restore shared case context. At 93% utilization, absence and exception demand can create queues.
3. **Pause survey skipping** for medium-risk apartments and old or permit-sensitive buildings pending randomized, risk-stratified validation.
4. **Stop post-billing activation as a routine path.** Require a successful activation test before billing, with a narrowly governed exception process.
5. **Restore mandatory permit checks** for permit-sensitive properties and trigger surveys after failed visits or specified risk signals.
6. **Run a controlled regional experiment** that separates the effects of coordinator ownership, task merging, survey skipping, and activation timing.

The principal trade-off is speed and lower front-end effort versus reliability, tail risk, and downstream cost. Management should not optimize the median by shifting work and harm beyond billing start.

The first measurement priorities are full 60-day downstream cost, structured exception reason codes, validation of the building-risk rule, and randomized testing of survey skipping. Start timestamps should also be added so active processing time can be distinguished from waiting.

Broader rollout should occur only when risk-adjusted evidence shows no material deterioration in p90/p95 duration, first-time-right installation, complaints, SLA breaches, or full downstream cost—and no uncontrolled billing-before-activation or missing-permit behavior. Until then, R should be treated as a segment-specific operating model, not the national default.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### High fitness but low precision

Fitness asks whether the model can reproduce observed traces. The pilot’s dominant coordinator path and flexible exception paths allow it to replay 94% of observed behavior. Precision asks whether the model permits much behavior that was not observed. Combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, repeat visits, missing permits, and billing timing make the model overly permissive, hence precision of 0.51. Lower generalization adds uncertainty that the six-week behavior will hold elsewhere.

The alignments identify specific deviations rather than merely a poor score: billing before successful activation, repeat installation without survey, missing permit checks, and post-billing complaints.

### Improved median but worse tail duration

Simple cases move quickly through combined validation/scheduling and direct installation, shifting the median downward. Difficult cases experience failed visits, manual activation, revisits, permit problems, and overloaded coordinators. These relatively fewer cases drive p90 and p95 upward. Completion-only timestamps limit separation of work time from queueing, but the temporal profile points to apartment activation delays and slower failed-visit recovery.

### Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Single ownership removes transfers and repeated context-setting. However, it centralizes knowledge and decisions. At 93% utilization, coordinators have little buffer for exceptions; absence can stall cases because context is not shared. The social networks therefore show both simplification and concentration. Fewer handovers are not equivalent to robust collaboration.

### Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost

Survey effort and front-end coordination declined, reducing measured pre-complaint cost. Rework then increased through failed visits, second visits, manual activation, disputes, and complaints. Because complaint costs are missing after week 4, the €207 estimate is uncertain, but the direction is consistent with observed failure demand. The redesign may have shifted cost across process and measurement boundaries.

### Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings

Simple houses have predictable installation requirements, making task merging and survey omission relatively safe. Apartments and old buildings have shared infrastructure, access, technical, and permit dependencies. A risk rule that misclassifies these cases removes information precisely where it has greater value. The same standardization can therefore be beneficial in one segment and hazardous in another.

# 4. Decision alternatives

| Alternative | Advantages | Risks | Reasonable when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Captures median, handover, and visible-cost gains quickly; operational consistency | Scales worse tails, rework, complaints, billing-before-activation, permit omissions, and capacity bottlenecks | Only after corrected controls show non-inferior reliability, tail, and full-cost outcomes across risk strata |
| **B. Roll back fully** | Quickly reduces exposure to unsafe survey and activation changes; returns to a more precise known process | Discards strong gains for simple houses and the potential value of coordinator accountability | If immediate control fixes are infeasible or customer/compliance exposure is judged intolerable |
| **C. Scale coordinator ownership only** | Preserves accountability while removing the two riskiest design components | Coordinator effect is not experimentally isolated; concentration and 93% utilization may still worsen resilience | If utilization caps, backup ownership, shared records, and exception escalation are implemented |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment** | Best causal identification; can separate ownership, survey, and activation effects | Delays some benefits; requires disciplined randomization, instrumentation, and sufficient sample size | Essential for medium-risk segments and useful where uncertainty could reverse the rollout decision |
| **E. Segment-specific hybrid** | Captures demonstrated low-risk benefits while limiting exposure and generating evidence | More complex routing; depends on accurate risk classification and conformance controls | Best current option, provided uncertain cases default to the safer path and experiments continue |

# 5. Value-of-information analysis

| Rank | Measure / experiment | Uncertainty resolved | How it could change the decision |
|---:|---|---|---|
| **1** | **M4: Full downstream cost for 60 days** | Whether visible savings survive complaints, revisits, credits, and recovery work | If total cost remains lower with acceptable quality, expansion becomes more attractive; if materially higher, restrict R further |
| **2** | **M6: Validate building-risk rule** | Whether safe segmentation is reliable enough to support E | Strong discrimination supports selective deployment; weak calibration requires broader survey retention or a new rule |
| **3** | **M1: Randomized survey skipping by risk stratum** | Causal benefit and harm of skipping surveys, separate from case mix | Could justify skipping in validated low-risk strata or show that even some “simple” cases need surveys |
| **4** | **M3: Structured exception reason codes** | Why failed visits, manual activations, complaints, and second visits increased | Identifies whether errors arise from risk rules, access, permits, skills, customers, or network defects, enabling targeted controls |
| **5** | **M7: Conformance monitoring** | Incidence and concentration of billing-before-activation and absent permit checks | Supports immediate containment; persistent deviations would block wider rollout even if speed improves |
| **6** | **M5: Coordinator versus shared-team ownership under absence** | Whether ownership benefits can coexist with resilience and lower key-person risk | Could support C more broadly or show that pooled/backup ownership is necessary |
| **7** | **M2: Instrument start timestamps** | Whether tail growth is active work, waiting, resource queues, or logging structure | Changes staffing and workflow remedies, especially for activation and second-visit delays; less likely by itself to reverse segmentation |
| **8** | **M8: Split customer experience measures** | Whether customers value faster appointments despite worse working-service outcomes | Refines service design and weighting of outcomes, but should not override activation reliability or compliance controls |

These rankings reflect **decision value**, not implementation sequence. M7 is relatively easy and should begin immediately despite ranking fifth. M2 should likewise be implemented early because instrumentation becomes more valuable over time.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: Choose E, supported by a controlled experiment under D.**

- **Immediate action:** Enforce successful activation before billing; restore permit checks for permit-sensitive buildings; add backup coordinator ownership, shared case context, utilization triggers, and conformance alerts.
- **Pause:** Survey skipping for medium-risk apartments, old buildings, and uncertain-risk cases; routine post-billing activation; any national rollout of R as a single package.
- **Scale:** Combined validation/scheduling and the redesigned low-risk path for verified simple houses. Continue coordinator ownership where capacity, backup, and escalation safeguards exist.
- **Measure next:** Prioritize M4, M6, M1, and M3; begin M7 and M2 immediately; test resilience through M5 and disaggregate customer experience through M8.
- **Threshold for broader rollout:** Require a sufficiently powered, risk-adjusted controlled trial showing:
  - no material deterioration in p90 or p95 duration;
  - first-time-right, 30-day complaints, and SLA breaches at least non-inferior to baseline;
  - full 60-day cost no higher than baseline;
  - billing-before-successful-activation and missing mandatory permits reduced to a predefined, audited near-zero exception rate;
  - coordinator and second-visit capacity below agreed queue-risk limits; and
  - benefits replicated across regions with acceptable model precision and stable conformance.

Broader rollout should be by validated segment and component—not by scaling the current redesign bundle unchanged.
