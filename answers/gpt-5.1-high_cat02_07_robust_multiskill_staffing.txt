# 1. Assumptions, objectives, and decision variables

## 1.1 Core modelling assumptions

I use only the information in the prompt and make the following explicit assumptions.

- **A1 – Service-time distributions from ranges**  
  - For each activity, the “ordinary” and “complex-tail” ranges are interpreted as **uniform distributions** on the given bounds.  
  - The effective service-time distribution is a **two-point mixture**: with probability \(1-p_\text{tail}\) draw from the ordinary uniform; with probability \(p_\text{tail}\) draw from the complex-tail uniform.  
  - For capacity calculations I use **means** of these mixtures (minutes, before resource multipliers):
    - Intake: ordinary mean \(= 6.5\), tail mean \(= 12\), tail \(p = 0.08\)  
      \(\Rightarrow E[T_\text{Intake}] \approx 0.92\cdot 6.5 + 0.08\cdot 12 \approx 6.9\).
    - Mechanical: \(E[T_M] \approx 26.3\).
    - Electrical: \(E[T_E] \approx 32.8\).
    - Software: \(E[T_S] \approx 38.5\).
    - Validation: \(E[T_V] \approx 15.7\).
  - For tail / SLA reasoning I treat complex-tail draws as **approximate 90–95th percentile** cases.

- **A2 – Workday, capacity and breaks**  
  - Each resource’s **paid shift** is 8 hours with a 30-minute break ⇒ **7.5 hours = 450 minutes** of nominal working time per day.  
  - Overtime up to 30 minutes per resource is available but treated as an **emergency buffer**, not part of planned capacity.  
  - The process day considered is 08:00–20:00 (the demand table’s horizon). Activities may spill slightly beyond shift ends into allowed overtime.

- **A3 – Skill families and diagnostic structure**
  - I treat diagnostic skills in three families: **Mechanical (M)**, **Electrical (E)**, **Software (S)**.  
  - Case types:
    - M: Intake → Mechanical → Validation.
    - E: Intake → Electrical → Validation.
    - S: Intake → Software → Validation.
    - ME: Intake → Mechanical + Electrical → Validation.
    - ES: Intake → Electrical + Software → Validation.
  - For offered-load analysis, **ME and ES always consume both branches** (M+E or E+S). Parallel vs sequential affects **cycle time but not total diagnostic work**, so capacity calculations use “1 unit of work per required branch”.

- **A4 – Resource capabilities and speed factors**
  - Speed multipliers are applied **multiplicatively** to sampled base service times.  
    Example: an E-task of base 30 minutes on Ada (multiplier 1.35) takes 40.5 minutes.
  - For capacity by skill I compute a resource’s **base-time capacity** as \(450 / m_{r,a}\) minutes-equivalent for activity \(a\) with multiplier \(m_{r,a}\).

- **A5 – Handover, cross-team, and setup delays**
  - If a case switches resource between consecutive activities (e.g., Intake → Diagnostic, or Mechanical → Validation), I add **7 minutes** of pure delay (no resource occupied).
  - If a case moves between “mechanical team” and “electrical/software team” and the **same cross-skilled resource does not retain the case**, I add an extra **5-minute** delay.  
    - Mechanical team: resources when executing Mechanical.  
    - Electrical/software team: resources when executing Electrical or Software.
  - If a resource changes diagnostic **skill family** (M vs E/S) between consecutive diagnostic tasks on different cases, I add **5 minutes of setup** to the second case’s start.
  - For capacity, I assume handover + setup add roughly **15–25% extra non-productive time** per case under heavy pooling; under strict specialization this overhead is much lower (often only one or two handovers per case).

- **A6 – ME/ES parallelism vs sequential execution**
  - **Sequential mode**: branches are performed one after the other, usually on the **same cross-skilled resource** where possible to avoid cross-team penalties and extra handovers. Critical path time is approximately \(T_M + T_E\) (or \(T_E + T_S\)).  
  - **Parallel mode**: the two branches are scheduled on (possibly different) resources; the diagnostic completion time is \(\max(T_1, T_2)\), but we incur **8 minutes of coordination delay** before Validation.  
  - For capacity, parallel vs sequential does not change the **total work**, but it changes **when** work is demanded from different skills and the case’s **sojourn time**.

- **A7 – Preemption for P1**
  - P1 jobs **may preempt** an ongoing lower-priority diagnostic activity (M/E/S only, not Intake/Validation).  
  - A preempted task, when resumed, incurs a **restart penalty**: 10% of its remaining active service time.  
  - Each case is preempted **at most once**. I assume controllers prefer to preempt **P3, then P2** work, and only when no idle qualified resource is available for P1.

- **A8 – Demand interpretation**
  - The “expected_arrivals” per 2-hour block are treated as **Poisson means**; for offered load I use the expectations, not stochastic realization.  
  - Priority mixes (P1/P2/P3) and type mixes (M/E/S/ME/ES) are **independent** given a block.  
  - Across the 6 blocks, total expected daily arrivals in the base scenario:
    - \(7 + 9 + 7 + 10 + 6 + 3 = 42\) cases/day.

- **A9 – Queueing model**
  - I conceptually model each skill family as an **\(M/G/c\)**-type queue with time-varying arrivals and fixed staff.  
  - Calculations are approximate: I use **utilisation-based heuristics** (e.g., Kingman-style) and qualitative judgments rather than exact formulas.
  - The discrete-event simulation scaffold in Section 8 uses simplified rules consistent with the above assumptions; any omitted mechanism is called out explicitly in comments.

## 1.2 Approximate offered-load numbers (base case)

Using the block-level type mixes, I compute total expected **diagnostic task counts** over the whole day:

- Total cases per diagnostic type (approximate, from the given table):
  - M-only: ≈ 9.8 cases.
  - E-only: ≈ 11.1 cases.
  - S-only: ≈ 7.9 cases.
  - ME: ≈ 8.3 cases.
  - ES: ≈ 5.0 cases.

From that:

- **Mechanical tasks** (M + ME):  
  \(N_M \approx 9.8 + 8.3 \approx 18.1\).
- **Electrical tasks** (E + ME + ES):  
  \(N_E \approx 11.1 + 8.3 + 5.0 \approx 24.3\).
- **Software tasks** (S + ES):  
  \(N_S \approx 7.9 + 5.0 \approx 12.9\).

Total base-time diagnostic minutes per day (before multipliers, using A1 means):

- Mechanical: \(18.1 \times 26.3 \approx 476\) minutes.
- Electrical: \(24.3 \times 32.8 \approx 797\) minutes.
- Software: \(12.9 \times 38.5 \approx 495\) minutes.
- Intake: \(42 \times 6.9 \approx 292\) minutes.
- Validation: \(42 \times 15.7 \approx 661\) minutes.

So total base-time service demand is roughly:

\[
292 (\text{Intake}) + 476 (M) + 797 (E) + 495 (S) + 661 (V)
\approx 2720 \text{ minutes} \approx 45.3 \text{ hours}.
\]

Total nominal resource time per day is \(6 \times 7.5 = 45\) hours, not counting overtime. Thus:

- The system is **near saturation** in base conditions, *before* accounting for:
  - multipliers \(>1\) (slower resources),
  - handovers/cross-team/setup/coordination overheads,
  - stochastic variability and peaks by block.
- Real effective utilisation will therefore be **close to or slightly above 90%** under any reasonable routing, meaning **non-trivial queues and long tails** are expected without careful design.

## 1.3 Objectives

I use two complementary objective formulations.

- **O1 – SLA- and tail-focused service objective**
  - Minimise a weighted combination of:
    - \(E[T_\text{cycle}^{(p)}]\): mean end-to-end cycle time by priority \(p \in \{\text{P1,P2,P3}\}\).
    - A tail measure such as \(P90\) or \(P95\) of the cycle time for each priority.
    - SLA shortfalls:  
      \(\max(0, 0.9 - P(\text{complete within target}_p))\).
  - A simple scalarisation:
    \[
    J_\text{SLA} = w_1 \cdot \text{P1\_tail} + w_2 \cdot \text{P2\_tail} +
    w_3 \cdot \text{P1\_SLA shortfall} + w_4 \cdot \text{P2\_SLA shortfall},
    \]
    with \(w_1, w_3\) > \(w_2, w_4\) to strongly favour P1 and P2.

- **O2 – Robustness and fairness objective**
  - Consider all scenarios \(s \in \{\text{base, U1, …, U5}\}\) and policies \(k\), and define:
    - \(\rho_{r,s,k}\): utilisation of resource \(r\).
    - \(\rho_{\max,s,k} = \max_r \rho_{r,s,k}\).
    - A **fairness penalty** \(F_{s,k}\), e.g. Gini or simple spread measure:
      \(F_{s,k} = \rho_{\max,s,k} - \rho_{\text{median},s,k}\).
    - A robustness loss combining maximal utilisation and SLA risk:
      \[
      L_{s,k} = \alpha \cdot \rho_{\max,s,k} +
                \beta \cdot (\text{P1\_SLA shortfall})_s +
                \gamma \cdot (\text{P2\_SLA shortfall})_s.
      \]
  - The robust objective is then:
    \[
    J_\text{robust}(k) = \max_{s} L_{s,k} + \delta \cdot \max_{s} F_{s,k},
    \]
    i.e. **minimise the worst-case load & SLA stress** across scenarios, subject to fairness.

Robustness here means “**no single plausible scenario makes the system fragile**”; a slightly worse base-case mean delay is acceptable if the **worst-case tail and utilisation** are much better.

## 1.4 Decision variables and policy levers

Across policies I vary the following decision variables:

- **Staffing and eligibility**
  - Which resources are assigned as **primary** workers for M vs E vs S vs Intake vs Validation in each 2-hour block.
  - Which cross-skilled resources (Ada, Deepa, Farah, Chen, Emil) are allowed to **overflow** into secondary skill families.

- **Queue structure and dispatch rules**
  - Number and type of queues:
    - Separate queues by skill (Mechanical queue, Electrical queue, Software queue, Intake queue, Validation queue).
    - Within each queue, jobs ordered by **priority then arrival** or by **priority then expected remaining processing time (ERPT)**.
  - Whether queues are **strictly separated** (fixed routing) or shared under pooling.

- **Parallel vs sequential diagnostic branching**
  - Policy for ME/ES:
    - Always sequential on one cross-skilled resource when feasible.
    - Always parallel when two skill families are simultaneously free.
    - **Threshold-based parallelisation**: parallel only when current estimated utilisation of both skill families is below a threshold or when P1.

- **Preemption rules**
  - Which lower-priority cases are eligible for preemption (P3 only vs P2+P3).
  - Whether preemption occurs on **arrival of any P1** or only when P1 queue exceeds a limit (e.g., >1 P1 waiting or waiting > 5 minutes).

- **P1 capacity reservation**
  - Fraction of capacity per skill family reserved for P1/P2 during busy blocks (e.g., 20–30%), with rules for when P2/P3 can use that capacity.

- **Handover minimisation vs flexibility**
  - Whether we prioritise “**stickiness**” (same resource keeps a case whenever possible) versus “**shortest expected completion**” (move case to any faster available resource even if it causes handovers).

These levers define the concrete policies compared in Section 3.


# 2. Capacity and offered-load analysis

## 2.1 Stage-level offered load in the base scenario

Using A2–A4 and the mixture means from A1:

- **Intake load**  
  - Demand: ≈ 292 base minutes/day.  
  - All six resources can do Intake; their base-time capacities:
    - Ada (1.00): 450
    - Ben (1.10): ≈ 409
    - Chen (1.10): ≈ 409
    - Deepa (1.00): 450
    - Emil (1.10): ≈ 409
    - Farah (1.00): 450  
    Sum ≈ 2,577 base minutes.  
  - If everyone were dedicated to Intake, utilisation would be ≈ 11%; in reality Intake is a **small portion of the day**, but a short blockage (e.g., U5 outage) can quickly build a sizeable queue because all cases must pass through Intake.

- **Validation load**  
  - Demand: ≈ 661 base minutes/day.  
  - All six can do Validation; base-time capacities:
    - Ada (1.15): ≈ 391
    - Ben (1.00): 450
    - Chen (1.10): ≈ 409
    - Deepa (1.00): 450
    - Emil (1.00): 450
    - Farah (1.20): 375  
    Sum ≈ 2,525 base minutes ⇒ Validation is **not a nominal bottleneck** but can become one if too few validators are available late in the day.

- **Mechanical load**
  - Demand: ≈ 476 base minutes/day.  
  - Mechanical-capable resources and their base-time capacities:
    - Ada (1.00): 450
    - Ben (1.00): 450
    - Deepa (1.25): 360
    - Farah (1.40): ≈ 321  
    Sum ≈ 1,581 base minutes.  
  - If all four focused solely on Mechanical, average utilisation would be:
    \(\rho_M \approx 476 / 1581 \approx 0.30\).  
    Mechanical work is **not globally capacity-limiting**; its bottleneck behaviour comes from **time-of-day peaks** and interaction with electrical/software tasks for ME cases.

- **Electrical load**
  - Demand: ≈ 797 base minutes/day.  
  - Electrical-capable resources:
    - Ada (1.35): ≈ 333
    - Chen (1.00): 450
    - Deepa (1.00): 450
    - Emil (1.30): ≈ 346
    - Farah (1.40): ≈ 321  
    Sum ≈ 1,900 base minutes ⇒ \(\rho_E \approx 797 / 1900 \approx 0.42\).
  - Electrical is heavier than Mechanical in aggregate and is a **likely bottleneck family** in midday blocks and in U2.

- **Software load**
  - Demand: ≈ 495 base minutes/day.  
  - Software-capable resources:
    - Chen (1.25): 360
    - Emil (1.00): 450
    - Farah (1.40): ≈ 321  
    Sum ≈ 1,131 base minutes ⇒ \(\rho_S \approx 495 / 1131 \approx 0.44\).
  - Software has **fewer qualified resources** but lower total demand than Electrical; it is vulnerable in shocks that increase S/ES demand or remove Chen/Emil.

Overall, ignoring overheads, **diagnostic families individually have comfortable nominal headroom**. The near-saturation at system level arises because:

- The same people must cover **Intake, Diagnostics, and Validation**.
- Cross-skill workers are pulled in multiple directions.
- Handover, setup, parallelisation coordination, and preemption create **non-productive time**.

With a conservative allowance of ~20% extra non-productive time in a highly pooled policy, the effective demand easily rises from ~45.3 to **54+ hours**, well above the 45–48 hours of available capacity. This is why policy design is crucial.

## 2.2 Time-block and priority patterns (base scenario)

From the block table:

- Daily arrivals: 42 cases, but concentrated in **10:00–16:00** (9 + 7 + 10 = 26 cases; ≈ 62% of demand).
- P1 shares:
  - Morning blocks: 10–18% P1.
  - Afternoon/early evening: 10–18% P1, but with fewer total arrivals.
  - This implies **P1 arrivals are clustered in busy hours**, particularly 10:00–16:00.
- ME/ES mixes:
  - ME is 20–25% of cases in daytime blocks; ES is 10–15%.  
  - Thus, a significant fraction of work involves **two diagnostic branches**, stressing coordination.

Given the shifts:

- **Farah** is only available 08:00–12:00 but has the broadest skill set (M/E/S, albeit slow). This is very valuable in **morning surges**.
- **Emil** arrives at 12:00 and can cover E/S into the evening, important when E/S incidents escalate.
- Lunch breaks stagger between 12:00–13:00; capacity around midday dips slightly.

The **likely base-case bottlenecks** are therefore:

- **Electrical and Software** diagnostics around 12:00–16:00, especially for ME/ES cases.
- **Validation** late in the day if many long-tail diagnostics complete near 16:00–20:00 and few staff remain on-duty.
- **Intake** is only transiently a bottleneck (short surges), not structurally.

## 2.3 Scenario-by-scenario qualitative capacity impact

I now assess how uncertainty scenarios U1–U5 reshape these bottlenecks.

- **U1 – Morning surge**
  - 08:00–12:00 arrivals multiply by 1.7, P1 share in those blocks rises by 8 percentage points.
  - Result:
    - Morning Intake load grows by 70%; Farah and Ada/Ben become critical.
    - Diagnostic load is **pulled forward**: more M/E/S branches starting earlier.
    - Many more **P1 ME/ES** cases in the morning; executing them sequentially on a single resource risks breaching the 120-minute target.
  - Bottlenecks:
    - **Intake and E/S diagnostics** from 08:00–12:00.
    - If not enough morning E/S capacity is protected for P1, P1 SLA will erode sharply.

- **U2 – Electrical/software incident (12:00–16:00)**
  - E and ES demand doubles from 12:00–16:00; proportions renormalised in those blocks.
  - Result:
    - Mechanical load is unchanged, but **Electrical and Software minutes roughly double** midday.
    - ME and ES cases consume more time on E/S branches; many cases will have E/S on the critical path.
  - Bottlenecks:
    - **Electrical and Software** are now near or above full utilisation in those hours, even if all qualified resources are fully used.
    - Without pooling, Chen and Emil become severe bottlenecks; even with pooling, P2/P3 tails grow.

- **U3 – Chen absent**
  - Full-day absence of Chen removes:
    - 450 base minutes of E capacity and 360 base minutes of S capacity.
    - Some Intake and Validation capacity in a central time window.
  - New capacities:
    - Electrical: loses 450 of 1,900 base minutes ⇒ capacity drops ~24%; \(\rho_E\) rises from ~0.42 to ~0.55.
    - Software: loses 360 of 1,131 base minutes ⇒ capacity drops ~32%; \(\rho_S\) rises from ~0.44 to ~0.73.
  - Bottlenecks:
    - **Software** becomes critical, especially in ME/ES and S-only cases.
    - E work must be redistributed to Ada, Deepa, Emil, and Farah, increasing cross-team handovers and setup.

- **U4 – Long-tail day**
  - Complex-tail probabilities for all **diagnostic activities** double; Intake and Validation distributions unchanged.
  - New mixture means (approx.):
    - Mechanical: tail \(p\) doubles from 0.15 to 0.30  
      \(E[T_M] \approx 0.7\cdot 23 + 0.3\cdot 45 \approx 29.6\) (≈ +13%).
    - Electrical: tail \(p\) 0.18 → 0.36  
      \(E[T_E] \approx 0.64\cdot 28.5 + 0.36\cdot 52.5 \approx 37.6\) (≈ +15%).
    - Software: tail \(p\) 0.20 → 0.40  
      \(E[T_S] \approx 0.6\cdot 32.5 + 0.4\cdot 62.5 \approx 44.5\) (≈ +16%).
  - Total diagnostic base minutes increase by roughly 14–16%, pushing system-wide utilisation well beyond 1 unless work is deferred or staffing increased.
  - Bottlenecks:
    - **All three diagnostic families** become tighter; long jobs now dominate P90/P95.
    - P1 SLA becomes difficult even with strong preemption if P1 has long-tail diagnostics.

- **U5 – Combined stress**
  - U1 (morning surge) + U3 (Chen absent) + a 30-minute Intake outage (09:30–10:00).
  - Effects:
    - Huge early-morning **Intake backlog**: arrivals accumulate during the outage, then must be cleared quickly.
    - Loss of Chen amplifies **E/S shortage**, especially for P1/ME/ES cases.
    - Many P2/P3 jobs risk being pushed late into the day, where Validation capacity might be constrained and overtime may be exhausted.
  - Bottlenecks:
    - Early: Intake, E/S diagnostics.
    - Late: Validation and remaining E/S jobs, with high risk of **P2/P3 SLA violations and overtime**.

In summary, **Electrical and Software diagnostics** are the most fragile components, especially in scenarios U2–U5. Any robust policy must:

- Protect **P1 access to fast E/S capacity**.
- Allow controlled **cross-skill overflow** from Mechanical-capable staff during E/S surges.
- Limit coordination and setup penalties so that extra flexibility actually buys performance.


# 3. Candidate policies

I define three concrete, implementable policies consistent with the “policy space” list.

## 3.1 Policy A – Strict specialization with fixed queues

**High-level idea:** minimise handovers and setup by giving each diagnostic family its own mostly fixed team and queue, with limited cross-skill use.

- **Queue structure**
  - Separate queues for:
    - Intake (one joint queue).
    - Mechanical diagnostics.
    - Electrical diagnostics.
    - Software diagnostics.
    - Validation (one joint queue).
  - Within each diagnostic queue, jobs are ordered by **priority (P1 → P2 → P3)** then **arrival time to that queue** (FIFO within priority).

- **Staffing / eligibility**
  - **Intake:** primarily Ada, Ben, Deepa, Farah 08:00–12:00; Chen & Emil cover more Intake during 12:00–13:00 as Farah leaves and others go to lunch. Intake work is limited to ~1–2 simultaneous resources; others focus on diagnostics.
  - **Mechanical queue:**  
    - Primary: Ada, Ben, Deepa.  
    - Farah used only when mechanical queue length exceeds a threshold (e.g., >4 cases).
  - **Electrical queue:**  
    - Primary: Chen, Deepa, Emil.  
    - Ada and Farah only used if electrical queue length exceeds threshold.
  - **Software queue:**  
    - Primary: Chen, Emil.  
    - Farah used if software queue length exceeds threshold and before 12:00.
  - **Validation:** primarily Ben, Deepa, Emil; Ada and Chen help only when no open diagnostic P1/P2 work.

- **Routing rules**
  - **M-only cases:** Intake → Mechanical queue → Validation.
  - **E-only cases:** Intake → Electrical queue → Validation.
  - **S-only cases:** Intake → Software queue → Validation.
  - **ME cases:**  
    - Always executed **sequentially**: Mechanical branch first, Electrical second.  
    - Prefer to keep both branches on a **single cross-skilled resource** if one exists and is in the mechanical team (Ada or Deepa) to avoid mechanical↔electrical cross-team penalty.
  - **ES cases:**  
    - Executed **sequentially**, Electrical then Software.  
    - Prefer Chen or Emil for both branches, again to avoid cross-team penalties.

- **Preemption**
  - P1 can **preempt ongoing P3** work in the *same diagnostic queue only* if:
    - No qualified resource is idle.
    - The P1 job has waited more than, say, **5 minutes** in that queue.
  - Preemption of P2 is allowed only in U1/U5 “surge mode”; otherwise P2 is not preempted.

- **ME/ES parallelisation**
  - **Disabled by design** (sequential only). The focus is on simplicity and low coordination overhead.

- **Handover & setup minimisation**
  - Strong preference for:  
    - Same resource for Intake + Diagnostic + Validation when feasible (especially for P2/P3 M-only and E-only).  
    - Same resource to complete both branches of ME/ES when cross-skilled.
  - Diagnostic resources are kept in one skill family (M vs E/S) for **blocks of time** (e.g., 2-hour windows) to avoid frequent 5-minute setup penalties.

**Expected behaviour:** low handover/setup overhead but **high vulnerability** to E/S overload and poor robustness under U2–U5.

## 3.2 Policy B – Full pooling with transparent dispatch

**High-level idea:** treat all qualified staff as a pooled team; any resource with the right skill can take the next most urgent job, using an “expected remaining processing time” variant of priority dispatch.

- **Queue structure**
  - Per-activity, **pooled queues** shared by all eligible resources:
    - One Intake queue.
    - One pooled Mechanical queue.
    - One pooled Electrical queue.
    - One pooled Software queue.
    - One pooled Validation queue.
  - Each queue orders jobs by:
    1. Priority (P1 > P2 > P3).
    2. Increasing **expected remaining processing time** (ERPT) of the case, approximated as:
       - For a case at activity \(a\):  
         \(ERPT = E[T_a] + \sum_{a' \text{ remaining}} E[T_{a'}]\) plus an allowance for expected waits.

- **Staffing / eligibility**
  - Any resource with a skill multiplier defined (≠‘-’) for a given activity is fully eligible to work on that activity at any time.
  - No hard partition by team; the system uses cross-skilling aggressively to smooth queues.

- **Routing rules**
  - Cases move strictly by process flow; upon becoming ready for an activity, they join that activity’s queue.  
  - When a resource is free, it:
    - Looks at the queue(s) it is qualified for.
    - Picks the job with highest priority and smallest ERPT across those queues.
  - **ME and ES cases:**
    - If, when the first branch is ready, there is also an idle resource in the other required skill family, the system launches **both branches in parallel** on different resources.
    - Otherwise branches are executed sequentially on however many resources become available first.

- **Preemption**
  - For diagnostics (M/E/S):
    - On arrival of a P1 job that cannot find an idle qualified resource, the dispatcher **preempts** a running P3 task in the same skill family with the **largest remaining processing time**.  
    - If no P3 task is running, it may preempt a P2 with the largest remaining time.  
    - Preempted tasks go back to the front of their queue at the same priority; their remaining time is inflated by 10% per rule.
  - Intake and Validation are not preempted to avoid chaotic “thrashing” at the process boundaries.

- **ME/ES parallelisation**
  - Enabled aggressively whenever two qualified resources from different families are free within a short look-ahead window (e.g., next 5 minutes).  
  - The 8-minute coordination overhead is always applied before Validation.

- **Handover & setup**
  - Handover delay (7 minutes) applies whenever the resource changes between activities; in practice this occurs **frequently** because pooling prioritises speed over continuity.
  - Cross-team penalty (5 minutes) occurs whenever ME/ES jobs move between mechanical and E/S teams on different resources.
  - Resources regularly switch between mechanical and electrical/software queues, incurring quite a few 5-minute family-change setups.

**Expected behaviour:** very good average delays and P1 protection in many scenarios, but **high non-productive overhead**, more complex to reason about, and potential fairness issues as the fastest cross-skilled staff take a disproportionate share of urgent work.

## 3.3 Policy C – Hybrid with reserved P1 capacity and controlled cross-skill overflow

**High-level idea:** start from specialisation, add light pooling and dynamic reservations to get robustness without exploding coordination overhead.

- **Queue structure**
  - Separate queues for each diagnostic family, as in Policy A:
    - Mechanical queue, Electrical queue, Software queue.
  - Single Intake and Validation queues.
  - Within diagnostic queues:
    - Primary order: priority (P1 > P2 > P3).
    - Secondary order: **age** (FIFO) with a **maximum waiting-time cap** per class to prevent P3 starvation (e.g., escalate P3 jobs that waited >8 hours).

- **Staffing / eligibility by block (example tuning)**
  - **08:00–10:00**
    - Intake: Ada + Farah (primary), Ben for spillover.
    - Mechanical: Ben (primary), Ada (secondary), Deepa joins at 10:00.
    - Electrical: Chen (primary), Farah overflow when idle.
    - Software: Chen + Farah (for S/ES priority jobs).
  - **10:00–14:00**
    - Reserve at least one cross-skilled resource **primarily for P1/P2 E/S** (e.g., Chen before lunch, Emil after 12:00).
    - Mechanical: Ben + Ada + Deepa; overflow into E when electrical queue exceeds a threshold.
  - **14:00–20:00**
    - As demand shifts towards late Validation, Ben + Emil + Deepa focus more on Validation; Ada/Chen/Emil continue E/S and ME/ES.

  The exact schedule can be tuned, but the principle is: each block has **primary assignments** plus defined **overflow rules**.

- **P1 capacity reservation**
  - For each diagnostic queue, a fraction (e.g., **25–30%**) of capacity for cross-skilled resources is **logically reserved** for P1 jobs:
    - If P1 queue length > 0, that reserved capacity works P1 only.
    - P2 may use that capacity when P1 queue is empty but must release it when a P1 arrives (non-preemptive for P2 in normal mode).
    - P3 cannot use reserved capacity except in off-peak hours or when explicitly allowed (e.g., 18:00–20:00).

- **Routing and ME/ES parallelisation**
  - **M, E, S-only:** similar to Policy A (one branch).
  - **ME/ES diagnostics:**
    - For **P1**:
      - If both relevant families have utilisation below ~80% and at least one fast resource in each family is available within a short horizon, execute **branches in parallel** (with 8-minute coordination overhead).
      - Otherwise, execute **sequentially on a single cross-skilled resource** whenever possible (Ada/Deepa for ME; Chen/Emil for ES) to avoid extra handovers and cross-team penalties.
    - For **P2/P3**:
      - Default is **sequential** on a single cross-skilled resource (if available) focusing on the family that is less loaded first.
      - Parallelisation is only allowed when both family queues are short (e.g., queue length ≤1) and total utilisation <70%, to avoid overloading in busy periods.

- **Preemption**
  - P1 is allowed to **preempt P3** work in the same family; preempting P2 is allowed only when:
    - P1 waiting time exceeds 10 minutes, or
    - P1 queue length exceeds 2.
  - A simple “preemption quota” (e.g., 1 preemption per resource per hour) is used to avoid excessive thrashing.

- **Handover & setup minimisation**
  - Preference ordering for each new task:
    1. Same resource as previous activity for that case (to avoid handover).
    2. A resource that has been working in the same skill family (to avoid 5-minute setup).
    3. Fastest available cross-skilled resource.
  - This yields **fewer handovers** than full pooling while still allowing flexible overflow when queues grow.

**Expected behaviour:** P1 protection via reservations; better fairness and lower non-productive overhead than Policy B; more robust to U1–U5 than Policy A due to structured cross-skill overflow.


# 4. Scenario comparison

## 4.1 Qualitative metric scales

To keep the comparison auditable yet concise, I use the following **qualitative-to-quantitative scales** for the requested metrics (all delays are end-to-end Intake→Validation across all priorities unless otherwise stated):

  - *VL*: < 1 hour  
  - *L*: 1–2 hours  
  - *M*: 2–6 hours  
  - *H*: 6–12 hours  
  - *VH*: > 12 hours

- **p90_delay** (rough 90th percentile)
  - *L*: < 4 hours  
  - *M*: 4–10 hours  
  - *H*: 10–18 hours  
  - *VH*: > 18 hours

- **P1_SLA, P2_SLA** (fraction of cases meeting the 120 min / 8 h targets)
  - Given as approximate ranges: e.g., “0.93–0.97” means 93–97%.

  - *L*: < 0.75  
  - *M*: 0.75–0.90  
  - *H*: 0.90–0.98  
  - *VH*: > 0.98

  - *Good*: difference ≤ 0.15.  
  - *Medium*: difference 0.15–0.30.  
  - *Poor*: difference > 0.30.

  - *Low*: typically ≤ 1 handover/setup per case.  
  - *Medium*: 1–2 handovers/setups per case on average.  
  - *High*: > 2 per case or frequent family switching.

- **resilience**
  - *Low*: small changes (e.g., U1 or U2 alone) cause large SLA failures or require emergency overtime.  
  - *Medium*: some SLA degradation but still mostly operational without major workarounds.  
  - *High*: maintains P1 SLA near or above 90% and avoids extreme tails across all scenarios with limited overtime.

- **main_failure_mode**
  - Short textual description of the primary way the policy fails in that scenario.

# 5. Robust recommendation

## 5.1 Primary recommendation: Policy C (Hybrid with reserved P1 capacity and controlled overflow)

Under objectives O1 and O2, Policy C is the **best compromise**:

- **Robust across scenarios:**  
  - Maintains P1 SLA near or above 90% in all U1–U5 with reasonable parameter choices.  
  - Keeps maximal utilisation near or below 0.97 outside the most extreme peaks in U5, whereas Policies A and B often run at ≈1.0 in multiple scenarios.
- **Fairness and sustainability:**  
  - By design, primary assignments and modest overflow caps prevent a small subset of fast cross-skilled staff from being permanently overloaded.  
  - Mechanical staff are not permanently sacrificed to E/S surges; overflow is **threshold-based** and time-bounded.
- **Controlled coordination overhead:**  
  - Fewer handovers and skill-family switches than full pooling.  
  - Parallelisation is used selectively (P1 and light-load conditions), so the 8-minute coordination overhead is applied where it most improves SLA.
- **Operational interpretability:**  
  - Rules like “reserve 25% of E/S capacity for P1 in 10:00–16:00” and “allow Mechanical to help Electrical only when the E queue exceeds 3 cases” are explainable, auditable, and can be parameterised in a routing engine.

In robust terms, Policy C **minimises the worst-case SLA and utilisation losses** while maintaining decent fairness.

## 5.2 Surge-mode fallback: temporary shift towards Policy B

I recommend defining an explicit **“surge mode”** which temporarily moves Policy C closer to Policy B (full pooling) when the system is under extreme stress, e.g.:

- Trigger surge mode if any of the following hold for more than 15–30 minutes:
  - P1 queue length in any diagnostic family ≥ 3.
  - Estimated P1 breach probability (from a simple online predictor) exceeds 15% in the next hour.
  - Measured utilisation for E or S exceeds 97% for a rolling 30-minute window.
- Surge-mode behaviours:
  - Relax P1 capacity reservations to allow **more aggressive preemption and pooling**, similar to Policy B.
  - Allow more ME/ES parallelisation, even for some P2 cases.
  - Temporarily allow P2-preemption by P1 with fewer constraints.

Surge mode should have **clear exit rules** (e.g., P1 queues have cleared and utilisation has fallen below 90% for 30 minutes), reverting to the normal hybrid regime to restore fairness and reduce operational stress.

## 5.3 When another policy may be preferable

- **Policy A (Specialization)** may be acceptable if:
  - U1–U5 are known to be **very rare**, and
  - Management prioritises **simplicity and minimal coordination overhead** above robustness, e.g., in a small shop with strong informal coordination.
- **Policy B (Pooled)** might be chosen if:
  - The primary concern is **throughput and mean delay**, and staff are comfortable with higher variability in workload and workflow, or
  - Automation can manage complex handover/preemption logic transparently, and fairness can be monitored and mitigated through **rotations and staffing adjustments**.

However, given the explicit uncertainty scenarios U1–U5 and fairness constraints (90% utilisation ceiling), Policy C remains the **dominant robust choice**.


# 6. Secondary effects and implementation risks

Key risks and side-effects to consider when implementing the recommended hybrid policy:

- **Gaming of estimates and priority inflation**
  - If estimated complexity or priority influences routing, teams may be tempted—consciously or not—to upgrade cases to P1 or “complex” to gain faster service.
  - Mitigation:  
    - Use **objective classification rules** based on observable features where possible.  
    - Track the distribution of priorities over time; sudden shifts should trigger review.

- **Starvation of P3 work**
  - With strong P1 reservations and preemption, P3 can be pushed to late evening, particularly in U1, U2, and U5.
  - Mitigation in Policy C:
    - Explicit maximum waiting-time rules for P3 (e.g., escalate P3 waiting > 12 hours to be treated as P2).  
    - Configurable “P3 protection windows” during calmer hours (e.g., 16:00–18:00) where P3 must receive some share of capacity.

- **Skill erosion and over-specialisation**
  - Over time, if Ada/Deepa mainly do Mechanical and Chen/Emil mainly E/S, some cross-skilling may erode.
  - Mitigation:
    - Plan **training shifts** with deliberate exposure to secondary skills.  
    - Use routing logic that occasionally assigns “stretch” work in low-load periods.

- **Excessive preemption and cognitive load**
  - Aggressive preemption (as in Policy B or surge mode) can:
    - Increase restart penalties and coordination costs.
    - Increase cognitive switching load on staff.
  - Mitigation:
    - Keep a **cap on preemptions per hour per resource**.  
    - Use preemption only when predicted P1 SLA is actually at risk, not just on every P1 arrival.

- **Coordination overhead and new bottlenecks at Validation**
  - Parallel ME/ES branches and heavy pooling can create many handovers, driving up the 7-minute and 5-minute penalties.
  - Validation may become a surprise bottleneck if many long-tail diagnostics finish simultaneously in late afternoon.
  - Mitigation:
    - Use **stickiness heuristics**: same resource for Intake + Diagnostics + Validation when feasible.  
    - Temporarily shift some diagnostic staff to Validation in the last two hours when diagnostic queues are short and many cases are diagnostic-complete.

- **Fairness and human interpretability**
  - Policies must remain **understandable** to frontline supervisors and staff:
    - If dispatch rules are too opaque, staff may distrust them and create informal workarounds.
  - Mitigation:
    - Provide simple, visible rules (e.g., “Chen always reserves 25% of her time for P1 E/S in 10:00–16:00”) and dashboards showing why the router chose certain assignments.


# 7. Monitoring and process-mining design

## 7.1 Event attributes to log

To monitor and refine these policies, the event log should capture at least:

- **Case-level attributes**
  - `case:concept:name`: unique incident ID.
  - `case:priority`: P1 / P2 / P3.
  - `case:type`: M, E, S, ME, ES.
  - `case:scenario_flag`: base, U1, U2, U3, U4, U5 (for simulations and what-if analyses).
  - `case:policy_version`: A, B, C, or specific parameter set.
  - `case:parallel_mode`: none / ME_parallel / ES_parallel (derived from events).

- **Event-level attributes**
  - `concept:name`: activity name (`Intake`, `Mechanical`, `Electrical`, `Software`, `Validation`, plus possibly synthetic events like `Handover`, `Setup`, `Preemption`).
  - `org:resource`: resource executing the activity.
  - `org:role` or `skill_family`: mechanical, electrical, software, intake/validation.
  - `start_timestamp`: start time of the activity (interval log).
  - `time:timestamp`: completion time of the activity.
  - `queue:entry_time`: when the case entered the queue for that activity.
  - `queue:eligible_resources`: a coded representation (e.g., list of resource IDs or a skill tag) of resources that were eligible at assignment time.
  - `routing:decision_rule`: e.g., “specialized”, “pooled_ERPT”, “hybrid_reserved”.
  - `routing:preempted`: boolean flag, and `routing:preempted_by_priority`.
  - `handover:kind`: `resource_change`, `team_change`, `skill_family_change`.
  - `handover:delay_minutes`: delay incurred due to handover/setup.
  - `branch:id`: identifier linking ME/ES parallel branches to their parent case.

With these attributes, we can reconstruct:

- Queue waiting times per activity and per priority.
- Actual service-times per resource and activity.
- Preemption frequency and its impact.
- Handover and setup networks between resources and teams.

## 7.2 Monitoring indicators

Key indicators for ongoing operations:

- **SLA and tail indicators**
  - P1, P2, P3 cycle time distributions (mean, median, P90, P95).
  - SLA attainment for each priority, by time of day and by case type (M/E/S/ME/ES).

- **Utilisation and fairness**
  - Per-resource utilisation (from event durations), broken down by activity family.
  - Fairness metrics (spread between max and median utilisation; Gini coefficient).
  - Percentage of time each resource spends in each skill family (to monitor skill erosion).

- **Queueing and preemption**
  - Average and tail queueing time by activity and priority.
  - Number of preemptions per hour, per resource, per priority pair (e.g., P1→P3).
  - Fraction of work affected by restarts and the induced extra service time.

- **Handover and setup burden**
  - Average number of resource changes per case.
  - Average and total minutes lost to handover and setup events.
  - Identification of “oscillation patterns” where cases bounce between teams.

## 7.3 PM4Py analyses for drift and bottleneck migration

Using the simulated or real event log (see Section 8), PM4Py can be used as follows:

- **Formatting and basic performance metrics**
  - Use `pm4py.format_dataframe(df, case_id='case:concept:name', activity_key='concept:name', timestamp_key='time:timestamp')` to standardise the log.
  - Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df, parameters=...)` to compute **case-level service and waiting times**, possibly per stage:
    - E.g., compute waiting time specifically for `Mechanical` vs `Electrical` vs `Software` queues.
  - Use `pm4py.get_service_time(df, parameters=...)` to get **activity- and resource-specific service-time distributions**, identify drift (e.g., slower Software tasks).

- **Handover-of-work network**
  - Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df, parameters=...)` to:
    - Quantify edges between resources/teams (who hands over to whom).
    - Monitor whether hybrid policy C indeed reduces cross-team edges compared with full pooling.
    - Detect emerging bottlenecks where many paths converge on specific validators or E/S experts.

- **Process model and conformance**
  - Discover a simple Directly-Follows Graph (DFG) or Petri net with `pm4py.discover_dfg` or `pm4py.discover_petri_net_*` to:
    - Validate that routing patterns (e.g., ME/ES parallel vs sequential) match the intended policy.  
    - Detect unexpected loops (e.g., repeated diagnostics due to mis-triage).

- **Scenario and policy comparison**
  - Use case attributes (`case:scenario_flag`, `case:policy_version`) to **filter logs** and compare:
    - base vs U1–U5 for a fixed policy.
    - Policy A vs B vs C for the same demand pattern (e.g., in simulation).
  - This reveals how bottlenecks migrate (Intake vs Diagnostics vs Validation) and whether the hybrid policy behaves as expected under stress.

# Scenario modifiers
SCENARIOS = {
    "base": {},
    "U1": {"morning_multiplier": 1.7, "morning_P1_delta": 8},
    "U2": {"E_ES_double_12_16": True},
    "U3": {"chen_absent": True},
    "U4": {"long_tail_multiplier": 2.0},
    "U5": {"morning_multiplier": 1.7, "morning_P1_delta": 8, "chen_absent": True, "intake_outage": ("09:30", "10:00")},
}


def apply_scenario_to_blocks(scenario: str) -> List[dict]:
    """
    Return a list of block dicts adjusted for the given scenario.
    Uses expectation-level adjustments (no Poisson sampling).
    """
    cfg = SCENARIOS.get(scenario, {})
    blocks = []
    for b in BLOCKS:
        b2 = dict(b)
        # U1 and U5: morning surge
        if "morning_multiplier" in cfg and b["name"] in ("08:00-10:00", "10:00-12:00"):
            b2["expected_arrivals"] *= cfg["morning_multiplier"]
            b2["P1_pct"] = min(100, b2["P1_pct"] + cfg.get("morning_P1_delta", 0))
            # Renormalise P2/P3 down proportionally
            remaining = 100 - b2["P1_pct"]
            p2_share = b["P2_pct"] / (b["P2_pct"] + b["P3_pct"])
            b2["P2_pct"] = remaining * p2_share
            b2["P3_pct"] = remaining * (1 - p2_share)
        # U2: double E and ES demand in 12:00–16:00
        if cfg.get("E_ES_double_12_16") and b["name"] in ("12:00-14:00", "14:00-16:00"):
            e = b2["E_pct"]
            es = b2["ES_pct"]
            m = b2["M_pct"]
            s = b2["S_pct"]
            me = b2["ME_pct"]
            e *= 2
            es *= 2
            total = m + e + s + me + es
            b2["M_pct"] = m * 100.0 / total
            b2["E_pct"] = e * 100.0 / total
            b2["S_pct"] = s * 100.0 / total
            b2["ME_pct"] = me * 100.0 / total
            b2["ES_pct"] = es * 100.0 / total
        blocks.append(b2)
    return blocks


def offered_load_by_skill(scenario: str, long_tail_multiplier: float = 1.0) -> Dict[str, float]:
    """
    Approximate base-time minutes of work per diagnostic skill (M/E/S) and Intake/Validation
    under a scenario by aggregating expectations from the block table.
    This is a capacity approximation, not a full queueing simulation.
    """
    blocks = apply_scenario_to_blocks(scenario)

    # Precompute mean service times for diagnostics under long-tail scenarios
    rng = random.Random(1234)

    def mean_service(activity: str, n_samples: int = 2000) -> float:
        vals = [sample_service_time(activity, rng, long_tail_multiplier=long_tail_multiplier) for _ in range(n_samples)]
        return sum(vals) / len(vals)

    m_mean = mean_service("Mechanical")
    e_mean = mean_service("Electrical")
    s_mean = mean_service("Software")
    intake_mean = mean_service("Intake")
    val_mean = mean_service("Validation")

    total_M = total_E = total_S = total_Intake = total_Val = 0.0

    for b in blocks:
        lam = b["expected_arrivals"]
        m = lam * b["M_pct"] / 100.0
        e = lam * b["E_pct"] / 100.0
        s = lam * b["S_pct"] / 100.0
        me = lam * b["ME_pct"] / 100.0
        es = lam * b["ES_pct"] / 100.0

        total_Intake += lam * intake_mean
        total_Val += lam * val_mean

        total_M += (m + me) * m_mean
        total_E += (e + me + es) * e_mean
        total_S += (s + es) * s_mean

    return {
        "Intake": total_Intake,
        "Mechanical": total_M,
        "Electrical": total_E,
        "Software": total_S,
        "Validation": total_Val,
    }


def capacity_by_skill(chen_absent: bool = False) -> Dict[str, float]:
    """
    Approximate base-time capacity in minutes per skill family and Intake/Validation,
    using 450 minutes of planned work per resource.
    """
    capacity = {"Intake": 0.0, "Mechanical": 0.0, "Electrical": 0.0, "Software": 0.0, "Validation": 0.0}
    for rname, res in RESOURCES.items():
        if chen_absent and rname == "Chen":
            continue
        for act in capacity.keys():
            mult = res.multipliers.get(act)
            if mult is None:
                continue
            # Base-time minutes capacity: work_minutes / mult
            capacity[act] += res.work_minutes / mult
    return capacity


def utilisation_estimates(scenario: str) -> Dict[str, float]:
    """
    Compute rough utilisation per skill family and Intake/Validation by comparing offered load
    to base-time capacity, under the scenario.
    """
    cfg = SCENARIOS.get(scenario, {})
    long_mult = cfg.get("long_tail_multiplier", 1.0)
    loads = offered_load_by_skill(scenario, long_tail_multiplier=long_mult)
    caps = capacity_by_skill(chen_absent=cfg.get("chen_absent", False))
    util = {}
    for act in loads:
        util[act] = loads[act] / max(1.0, caps[act])
    return util

# 8.2 Simple policy scoring

POLICIES = ["A_specialized", "B_pooled", "C_hybrid"]


def rough_delay_metric(utilisation: float) -> Tuple[float, float]:
    """
    Map an overall utilisation level to approximate mean and p90 delay multipliers,
    using a simple monotone function inspired by queueing theory. This is deliberately
    rough and used only for policy ranking, not exact prediction.
    """
    if utilisation < 0.7:
        mean_mult = 0.5
        p90_mult = 1.0
    elif utilisation < 0.85:
        mean_mult = 1.0
        p90_mult = 2.0
    elif utilisation < 0.95:
        mean_mult = 2.0
        p90_mult = 4.0
    else:
        mean_mult = 4.0
        p90_mult = 8.0
    return mean_mult, p90_mult


def policy_adjustments(policy: str, scenario: str) -> Dict[str, float]:
    """
    Encode qualitative policy differences as small multiplicative adjustments.
    For example, pooling improves delays but increases overhead; hybrid aims
    for robustness. This is a *scaffold*, not a calibrated model.
    """
    # Base multipliers for (mean_delay, p90_delay, P1_SLA_shortfall, P2_SLA_shortfall)
    if policy == "A_specialized":
        return {"mean": 1.2, "p90": 1.2, "P1_short": 1.2, "P2_short": 1.3}
    if policy == "B_pooled":
        return {"mean": 0.9, "p90": 0.95, "P1_short": 0.9, "P2_short": 0.95}
    # Hybrid default
    return {"mean": 1.0, "p90": 0.95, "P1_short": 0.9, "P2_short": 0.9}


def scenario_severity(scenario: str) -> float:
    """Rough severity score for U1–U5 to modulate SLA risk."""
    if scenario == "base":
        return 1.0
    if scenario in ("U1", "U2", "U3", "U4"):
        return 1.3
    if scenario == "U5":
        return 1.7
    return 1.0


def evaluate_policies() -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Produce a compact comparison table of policy vs scenario metrics using the
    capacity-based utilisation estimates and qualitative policy adjustments.
    This does *not* implement exact discrete-event simulation or preemption;
    it is a transparent approximation consistent with the reasoning in Section 4.
    """
    rows = []
    for scenario in SCENARIOS.keys():
        util = utilisation_estimates(scenario)
        # Use the maximum utilisation over diagnostic skills as a driver
        diag_util = max(util["Mechanical"], util["Electrical"], util["Software"])
        mean_mult, p90_mult = rough_delay_metric(diag_util)
        base_P1_short = max(0.0, diag_util - 0.85)  # simple heuristic
        base_P2_short = max(0.0, diag_util - 0.9)
        sev = scenario_severity(scenario)
        for policy in POLICIES:
            adj = policy_adjustments(policy, scenario)
            mean_delay_hours = 3.0 * mean_mult * adj["mean"]  # 3h nominal baseline
            p90_delay_hours = 8.0 * p90_mult * adj["p90"]  # 8h nominal baseline
            P1_short = base_P1_short * sev * adj["P1_short"]
            P2_short = base_P2_short * sev * adj["P2_short"]
            P1_SLA = max(0.0, 0.95 - P1_short)  # optimistic base SLA ~95%
            P2_SLA = max(0.0, 0.90 - P2_short)
            rows.append(
                {
                    "policy": policy,
                    "scenario": scenario,
                    "max_diag_util": diag_util,
                    "mean_delay_h": mean_delay_hours,
                    "p90_delay_h": p90_delay_hours,
                    "P1_SLA": P1_SLA,
                    "P2_SLA": P2_SLA,
                }
            )
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def simulate_single_case(case_id: int, scenario: str, rng: random.Random) -> List[dict]:
    """
    Highly simplified case simulation: generates Intake, one or two diagnostic
    activities, and Validation. This does NOT implement detailed resource
    competition or preemption; it focuses on explicit representation of:
    - handover delays (7 minutes when resource changes),
    - cross-team penalties (5 minutes when moving between mechanical vs E/S),
    - optional ME/ES parallel branches for policy 'B_pooled' and 'C_hybrid' (stubbed),
    - simple queueing by shifting start times.

    This scaffold is meant to generate a realistic-looking interval event log,
    not to perfectly match the analytical metrics above.
    """
    # For simplicity we ignore block structure and sample a uniform arrival in the day.
    day_start = time_to_minutes("08:00")
    day_end = time_to_minutes("20:00")
    arrival = rng.uniform(day_start, day_end - 60)
    priority = rng.choices(["P1", "P2", "P3"], weights=[0.12, 0.55, 0.33])[0]
    ctype = rng.choices(["M", "E", "S", "ME", "ES"], weights=[0.23, 0.26, 0.19, 0.20, 0.12])[0]

    events = []

    def add_event(activity: str, start: float, end: float, resource: str) -> None:
        events.append(
            {
                "case:concept:name": f"case_{case_id}",
                "concept:name": activity,
                "org:resource": resource,
                "start_timestamp": pd.Timestamp("2024-01-01") + pd.Timedelta(minutes=start),
                "time:timestamp": pd.Timestamp("2024-01-01") + pd.Timedelta(minutes=end),
                "case:priority": priority,
                "case:type": ctype,
                "scenario": scenario,
            }
        )

    # Intake: assign to a simple rotating set of Intake-capable resources
    intake_res = ["Ada", "Ben", "Chen", "Deepa", "Emil", "Farah"]
    r_intake = rng.choice(intake_res)
    t_intake = sample_service_time("Intake", rng, long_tail_multiplier=1.0)
    start = arrival
    end = start + t_intake * RESOURCES[r_intake].multipliers["Intake"]
    add_event("Intake", start, end, r_intake)

    # Diagnostic structure
    diag_acts: List[str]
    if ctype == "M":
        diag_acts = ["Mechanical"]
    elif ctype == "E":
        diag_acts = ["Electrical"]
    elif ctype == "S":
        diag_acts = ["Software"]
    elif ctype == "ME":
        diag_acts = ["Mechanical", "Electrical"]
    else:
        diag_acts = ["Electrical", "Software"]

    last_end = end
    last_res = r_intake
    last_team = "intake"

    for act in diag_acts:
        # Choose a resource with a defined multiplier for this diagnostic
        eligible = [r for r, res in RESOURCES.items() if res.multipliers.get(act) is not None]
        r_diag = rng.choice(eligible)

        base_service = sample_service_time(act, rng, long_tail_multiplier=1.0)
        mult = RESOURCES[r_diag].multipliers[act]
        service = base_service * mult

        # Add handover delay if resource changes
        start = last_end
        if r_diag != last_res:
            start += 7.0
            # Cross-team penalty between Mechanical vs E/S
            if (last_team == "M" and act in ("Electrical", "Software")) or (
                last_team in ("E", "S") and act == "Mechanical"
            ):
                start += 5.0

        end = start + service
        add_event(act, start, end, r_diag)
        last_end = end
        last_res = r_diag
        last_team = "M" if act == "Mechanical" else ("S" if act == "Software" else "E")

    # Validation, assigned randomly to any capable resource
    valid_res = [r for r, res in RESOURCES.items() if res.multipliers.get("Validation") is not None]
    r_val = rng.choice(valid_res)
    t_val = sample_service_time("Validation", rng, long_tail_multiplier=1.0)
    service_val = t_val * RESOURCES[r_val].multipliers["Validation"]
    start = last_end
    if r_val != last_res:
        start += 7.0
    end = start + service_val
    add_event("Validation", start, end, r_val)

    return events


def generate_event_log(num_cases: int, scenario: str, seed: int = 123) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    Generate a synthetic interval event log for a given scenario. This log is not a
    full resource-constrained DES result, but it respects the process structure and
    explicitly records handover-related delays as part of activity start times.
    """
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    all_events: List[dict] = []
    for i in range(num_cases):
        all_events.extend(simulate_single_case(i, scenario, rng))
    df = pd.DataFrame(all_events)
    # Compute simple queue-entry times (here set equal to previous activity end for illustration)
    df["queue:entry_time"] = df["start_timestamp"]  # in a full DES, this would differ
    return df


def run_pm4py_analyses() -> None:
    """
    Demonstrate PM4Py usage:
    - format the dataframe
    - compute service and waiting times
    - discover a handover-of-work network

    df = generate_event_log(num_cases=200, scenario="base", seed=42)

if __name__ == "__main__":

# 9. Limits and validation plan

## 9.1 Limits of the current analysis

The analysis is intentionally high-level and relies on **expected arrivals and service-time ranges** plus simple queueing heuristics. Key limitations:

- **Distributional assumptions**
  - Real service times may be far from uniform (e.g., log-normal or heavy-tailed), and tails may differ by resource and case type.
  - Dependencies between activities (e.g., ME cases systematically taking longer) are not fully captured.

- **Arrival patterns and non-stationarity**
  - The 2-hour arrival blocks may hide **within-block peaks** and day-of-week or seasonal patterns.
  - The scenarios U1–U5 are stylised; real shocks may combine features differently (e.g., partial absences, multi-day outages).

- **Resource behaviour**
  - Human factors such as multi-tasking inefficiency, context-switch penalties beyond the 5-minute setup, learning curves, and fatigue are only approximated.
  - The analysis assumes staff follow dispatch rules exactly; in practice, there may be informal overrides.

- **Preemption and parallelisation**
  - The qualitative reasoning incorporates preemption and ME/ES parallelisation, but the simple capacity-based metrics and Python scaffold do not fully model all their dynamic effects.
  - Especially under U4, detailed discrete-event simulation would be needed to correctly quantify tails and restarts.

Therefore, the numbers in Section 4 should be seen as **scenario-comparison heuristics**, not precise forecasts.

## 9.2 Pilot and backtest design

To validate and refine the policies before a full rollout:

- **Step 1 – Historical backtest (if logs exist)**
  - Apply process mining to existing incident logs to:
    - Estimate true service-time distributions by activity, resource, and case type.
    - Reconstruct historical queueing and handover patterns.
  - Use the Python scaffold as a starting point to build a **discrete-event simulator** calibrated to historical data (including preemptions and ME/ES behaviour).
  - Run **counterfactual simulations** of Policies A, B, and C on historical demand sequences to compare:
    - P1/P2 SLA, tails, and utilisation.
    - Handover and setup overhead.

- **Step 2 – Safe pilot of Policy C**
  - Select a subset of hours (e.g., **10:00–16:00 on selected weekdays**) and a subset of teams to run the hybrid policy while:
    - Keeping a **hard safety constraint**: P1 must always be dispatched to the fastest available qualified resource; manual overrides allowed.
    - Limiting the number of concurrent ME/ES parallel cases to avoid overwhelming E/S when learning.
  - Monitor:
    - P1 and P2 cycle times.
    - P3 backlog and any signs of starvation.
    - Staff workload distribution and feedback on interpretability.

- **Step 3 – Stress-test drills**
  - Use simulations of U1–U5 to devise **table-top drills** where supervisors react to synthetic surges and absences:
    - Practise activating and deactivating **surge mode** (Policy C → B).
    - Evaluate whether triggers and thresholds are understandable and actionable.

- **Step 4 – Incremental rollout with guardrails**
  - Gradually extend Policy C to more hours and teams while:
    - Continuously monitoring PM4Py-based indicators (SLA, utilisation, handovers).
    - Keeping **roll-back procedures**: if P1 SLA dips below an agreed threshold for a sustained period, automatically revert to a safer fallback (e.g., a more conservative variant of Policy A or B).

This validation path ensures that the chosen hybrid policy is not only theoretically robust across base and U1–U5 scenarios but also **empirically safe, understandable, and sustainable** in day-to-day operations.
