# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

- **Handover-of-work network**: The handover network shows who passes work to whom in sequence. Mira has very high out-degree, in-degree, and betweenness, with strong edges from `Bot_Rules`, `Temp_1`, and `Temp_2` into her, and from her to adjusters and specialists. This implies she sits on many exception paths between automation/temporaries on the front end and adjusters/specialists on the back end, especially when information is missing, a claim is reopened, or a complaint is raised. What it does **not** imply is that she personally spends much time on each case, that every exception must pass through her, or that she is the sole owner of those cases; it only shows she is a structural routing hub in the observed sequences.

- **Working-together network**: The working-together network captures which resources co-occur on the same cases and how often. Mira’s co-case rates with adjusters and team leads (4–8%) are much lower than the strong intra-team pairs (around 20–27%), indicating that she is rarely part of the “core team” that works through an entire claim. This fits with her short “routing / reopen / clarification” activities: she appears briefly on many difficult cases, then disappears while adjusters and team leads continue the work. The network therefore suggests that she is episodically, not continuously, involved. It does **not** mean that she is unimportant; it merely indicates that she is not a long-term co-worker on most cases.

- **Subcontracting network**: The subcontracting network looks for A–B–A patterns, where a resource sends work out and later resumes it within a few events. Mira’s strong patterns with `Omar_Medical` and `Priya_Legal` show that she often “subcontracts” to specialists and then resumes control, e.g., Request Missing Information → Medical Review → Assign Specialist/Reopen or Escalate Complaint → Legal Review → Reopen/Assign. High subcontracting betweenness suggests she orchestrates short specialist consultations rather than handing off ownership. This does **not** prove that she makes the substantive medical or legal decisions, only that she coordinates the request–response loop and uses specialists as temporary service providers.

- **Performance/time statistics**: Mira’s median service time per event is modest (4.2 minutes), but she touches 22% of all claims and a much higher share of reopened claims (64%) and complaints (71%). The absence experiment shows that when she is missing, queues and tail cycle times increase for exception cases but not for ordinary low-value claims. Together, this implies that her own tasks are quick but that she sits on the critical path for many exceptional cases; delays likely arise from queueing before she acts or from repeated re-entries, not from long handling times per event. The statistics do **not** prove that she is always a bottleneck, only that her availability strongly affects the tail of the distribution for difficult cases.

- **Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity**: Activity-based similarity and organizational role discovery would likely place Mira in a distinct “exception desk / coordinator” role, separate from adjusters (damage assessment and negotiation), bots (ingestion and rules), and vendors (`Vendor_Verify`, `Vendor_Pay`). Her activity mix (Request Missing Information, Reopen Claim, Escalate Complaint, Assign Specialist) and high share of exception_flag=True cases distinguish her from formal team leads and from temporaries. This does **not** tell us whether her role is formally defined in the organization chart, nor whether her discretion is tightly controlled; it only indicates that, in the event data, she behaves as a specialized routing and exception-coordination role.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

## 2.1 Formal coordinator / exception desk

- **Mechanism**: Mira operates as a centralized exception desk that receives outputs from `Bot_Rules` and temporaries when cases are unusual, incomplete, reopened, or escalated. She verifies what is missing, triggers `Request Missing Information`, reopens claims, assigns appropriate adjusters or specialists, and routes complaints to legal or to team leads.

- **Supporting evidence**:
  - High handover in-degree from `Bot_Rules`, `Temp_1`, and `Temp_2`, all explicitly linked to exception handling and closure exceptions.
  - Activity profile dominated by routing, missing-information, reopen, and complaint-related tasks.
  - Strong subcontracting loops with medical and legal specialists, consistent with a coordinator requesting expert input and then resuming control.
  - She touches a minority of total claims (22%) but a majority of reopened claims and complaints, aligning with an exception-focused desk.

- **Contradicting or limiting evidence**:
  - Routine North/South assignment from `Alice_TeamLead` and `Bruno_TeamLead` to adjusters happens without Mira, so she does not coordinate all work.
  - Working-together centrality is low; she is not embedded as a day-to-day team member in most cases.
  - Some exception_flag=True cases apparently bypass her, so exception handling is not fully centralized.

- **Observable predictions**:
  - In filtered logs where `exception_flag=False`, Mira’s presence should be rare, and her centrality should drop sharply.
  - In variants with `Reopen Claim` or `Escalate Complaint`, she should appear early in the variant and around the transition to specialist involvement.
  - New exception types (e.g., new channels or regions) would first route into her queue until automation and team leads are updated.

## 2.2 Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency

- **Mechanism**: Because many exception flows (from bots, temporaries, and specialists) converge on Mira before proceeding, her availability and queue length determine how fast difficult cases move. When she is overloaded or absent, exception cases wait in her queue, stretching the tail of cycle times even if other resources are free.

- **Supporting evidence**:
  - Very high handover betweenness (0.77) and subcontracting betweenness (0.69), indicating that many paths between resources pass through her.
  - Case count percentile of 0.91 and involvement in most reopened and complaint cases, meaning she is on a large share of the hardest work.
  - During her six-day absence, 90th percentile cycle time for exception_flag=True cases jumped from 8.4 to 13.9 days, while ordinary low-value claims were unaffected, consistent with a single-point bottleneck for exceptions.

- **Contradicting or limiting evidence**:
  - Her per-event median service time is low, suggesting she works quickly when she does process a case; the bottleneck may be queueing, not slow handling.
  - Exception_flag=True cases can still be processed (albeit slowly) in her absence, so there are alternative, if less efficient, paths.
  - Other high-wait components, like `Vendor_Verify` and `Vendor_Pay`, also contribute substantial waiting time, so she is not the only source of delay.

- **Observable predictions**:
  - Queue length and waiting time before Mira’s events should correlate strongly with delays and SLA breaches for exception cases.
  - Time-of-day or vacation periods with reduced Mira capacity should show spikes in waiting time on arcs leading into her.
  - Introducing a second coordinator or codifying some of her routing rules into `Bot_Rules` should reduce tail cycle times for exception_flag=True cases.

## 2.3 Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker

- **Mechanism**: Mira acts as a broker across organizational boundaries: between automated routing (`Bot_Rules`), temporaries, two regional teams (North and South), external vendors, and in-house specialists. She interprets non-standard situations and decides which team, specialist, or channel should take the next step.

- **Supporting evidence**:
  - Inputs from `Bot_Rules`, `Temp_1`, and `Temp_2` and outputs to adjusters in both teams and to specialists indicate cross-boundary routing.
  - High handover out-degree centrality and in-degree centrality, with edges to and from almost every human specialist role.
  - Adjusters’ qualitative description of her as “the person who knows where hard cases should go” is typical of a boundary-spanning broker.
  - High share of reopened and complaint cases suggests she connects front-line handling with escalation paths (legal, medical, complaint resolution).

- **Contradicting or limiting evidence**:
  - Co-case rates with team leads are modest (4–5%), so she may be a broker between systems and queues more than between human teams.
  - We see explicit subcontracting with specialists but not with vendors; vendor coordination may be handled more by temporaries or bots.
  - Only 22% of all cases involve her, so many straightforward cross-boundary cases may be handled through standard routing without her.

- **Observable predictions**:
  - Cases spanning multiple regions, channels, or high `amount_band` values should show a higher probability of involving Mira.
  - Changes in routing rules or new vendors should initially increase her involvement until new boundaries are stabilized.
  - Network analysis by region or channel would show Mira as a more central node in cross-region or cross-channel subgraphs than in purely local ones.

## 2.4 Quality gatekeeper / compliance buffer

- **Mechanism**: Mira functions as a human quality and compliance gate in front of specialists and settlement. She checks for missing documentation, potential fraud or coverage issues, and fairness concerns before allowing a claim to advance, especially for complaints and reopened cases.

- **Supporting evidence**:
  - Dominance of `Request Missing Information`, `Reopen Claim`, and `Escalate Complaint` in her activity profile suggests a focus on quality and remediation.
  - The compliance reviewer explicitly worries that she is both detecting problems and selecting specialists, which matches a powerful gatekeeper role.
  - Her involvement with a high proportion of exception_flag=True cases implies she is often where regulatory or quality concerns are surfaced and addressed.
  - Her absence leads to growing queues of problematic cases, consistent with unresolved quality checks accumulating.

- **Contradicting or limiting evidence**:
  - The process owner downplays her as “not a real team lead, just a queue cleaner,” indicating that management may not recognize or formalize her gatekeeping authority.
  - Some quality-related delays also stem from `Vendor_Verify` and `Vendor_Pay`, so not all compliance work is concentrated on her.
  - We do not see explicit “approve” or “reject” activities under her name; she may flag issues but not make final decisions.

- **Observable predictions**:
  - Cases passing through Mira should exhibit fewer late-stage escalations or regulatory findings than similarly risky cases that bypass her.
  - Introducing clearer checklists or automated pre-checks for documentation should reduce her workload on simple quality problems while leaving complex cases still routed to her.
  - If her gatekeeper role is formalized, we would expect new log attributes (e.g., quality codes) to be attached to her events.

# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk**: High betweenness and concentration of reopened and complaint cases around Mira, combined with the observed increase in 90th-percentile cycle time during her absence, indicate a real but targeted bottleneck risk. The risk is concentrated in exceptional and complex claims; ordinary low-value claims remain largely unaffected. Treating her role as a bottleneck in need of support, rather than as a performance problem, is important.

- **Coordination risk**: Because Mira connects bots, temporaries, adjusters, specialists, and escalation paths, misalignments between her routing decisions and automated rules (`Bot_Rules`, vendor processes) can lead to duplicated work, ping-ponging, or missed handovers. Her low working-together co-occurrence means that coordination is mostly via brief handovers and subcontracting loops, not through stable team collaboration, which can make cross-team understanding fragile.

- **Key-person risk**: The six-day absence experiment is strong evidence of key-person dependency for exception handling. Knowledge of where to send hard cases, how to interpret borderline rules, and how to reopen or escalate efficiently appears to reside heavily with Mira. Without clear documentation or trained backups, the organization is vulnerable to further absences, turnover, or overload.

- **Compliance/control risk**: Having the same person both detect issues (missing information, potential non-compliance) and select the next specialist can create uncontrolled discretion and potential bias, even if no wrongdoing is observed. The current networks suggest limited transparency around why particular specialists or paths are chosen. In a regulated environment, this calls for better documentation, audit trails, and decision criteria rather than immediate removal of the role.

- **Workload fairness risk**: Mira’s portfolio is skewed toward stressful, exceptional, and complaint-driven work, while bots, temporaries, and vendors handle simpler or more routine steps. Adjusters and team leads may perceive her either as offloading complexity or as a dumping ground for problematic cases. If exception queues grow, her workload and stress may become disproportionate, and downstream specialists might receive bursts of complex work, affecting fairness and burnout.

- **Data-quality risk**: Because many of Mira’s events are short routing or clarification steps, log designers might under-specify attributes (e.g., reasons for rerouting, criteria for escalation), making her behavior hard to interpret. There is also a risk that some automated or system-level routing is attributed to her user ID, inflating her apparent centrality. Poorly differentiated logging around her steps can mislead analyses and hide whether the real issues lie in automation configuration, vendor delays, or human discretion.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

- **Handover-of-work network analyses**: Use `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` on separate subsets of the log (e.g., `exception_flag=True` vs `False`, by `case_type`, by `region`). Compare Mira’s in/out-degree and betweenness across these slices to see where she is structurally necessary versus optional. Analyze time-filtered networks before, during, and after her absence to quantify how routing paths reconfigured and which resources partially substituted for her.

- **Working-together network analyses**: Apply `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` on subsets such as reopened cases, complaints (`Escalate Complaint` present), or high `amount_band` claims. Check whether Mira becomes more central in co-case terms for the most complex portfolios, and whether particular adjusters or specialists repeatedly co-occur with her. This can reveal informal “micro-teams” for difficult cases that the overall network averages obscure.

- **Subcontracting network analyses**: Using `pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network` (with different n values), examine A–B–A patterns centered on Mira versus team leads. Compare how often she resumes work after medical, legal, or vendor steps relative to other resources. This can distinguish true coordination loops (Mira maintaining ownership) from simple handoffs where adjusters or leads take over after specialist input.

- **Activity-based resource similarity / organizational role discovery**: Run `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` or `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` to cluster resources based on activity profiles. Inspect which cluster Mira falls into and how far it is (in similarity space) from bots, temporaries, adjusters, team leads, and vendors. If she forms a singleton or very small cluster, that supports the idea of a unique coordination role; if she clusters with team leads or temporaries, her function may be closer to those roles than assumed.

- **Filtering by exception_flag, activity, variants, or time ranges**: With `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` and `pm4py.filter_variants`, construct focused logs: only `exception_flag=True`, only cases with `Reopen Claim` or `Escalate Complaint`, or only high-priority or high-amount claims. Compare variants with and without Mira, looking at where in the sequence she appears and which paths bypass her. Use time-window filters to replicate the absence period and other peak times to see how behavior changes under stress.

- **Service and waiting-time analysis**: Using `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`, decompose case cycle times into active service by each resource versus waiting between events. Specifically measure waiting time before and after Mira’s events, and before/after key automated and vendor steps (`Bot_Rules`, `Vendor_Verify`, `Vendor_Pay`). This allows distinguishing between genuine human processing bottlenecks and external or automation-induced delays, and testing whether adding capacity around Mira would meaningfully improve tail performance.

# 5. Recommendation

- **What should be changed immediately**:
  - Explicitly define Mira’s role as an exception/triage coordinator, including written routing and escalation criteria, to reduce undocumented discretion.
  - Introduce at least one trained backup (possibly a senior adjuster or team lead) who can perform the same coordination tasks, and update access rights and worklists accordingly.
  - Enhance logging around her events (e.g., reason codes for rerouting, escalation type, chosen specialist) to improve auditability and enable more precise analyses.

- **What should be monitored**:
  - Queue lengths and waiting times into and out of Mira’s activities, segmented by `exception_flag`, `priority`, `amount_band`, and `region`.
  - The distribution of specialist and team-lead selections made after her interventions, to detect potential bias or overloading of particular experts.
  - Tail cycle times (e.g., 80th/90th percentiles) for exception and complaint cases as configuration changes are made to `Bot_Rules` and vendor processes.
  - Workload balance between Mira, temporaries, adjusters, and bots on exception handling, to ensure that human judgment is used where needed but not over-concentrated.

- **What should not be changed until more evidence is available**:
  - Do not remove or radically decentralize Mira’s coordination role without first designing and validating an alternative mechanism (e.g., rule-based routing plus multiple coordinators) that can handle reopened, complaint, and cross-boundary cases.
  - Avoid assuming that her high centrality is purely negative; the absence experiment suggests that she provides valuable stability for complex work.
  - Postpone major changes to vendor or automation interfaces that currently rely on her as a human buffer until the above analyses clarify which delays are due to external partners versus internal coordination.

# 6. Short executive summary

In this claims process, Mira_Coordinator appears as a central hub for exceptional cases rather than an ordinary team lead. Handover and subcontracting networks show that automation, temporaries, and specialists all route complex, reopened, or complaint-driven claims through her for triage, missing-information follow‑up, and specialist selection. Her own activities are short and routing‑oriented, which explains why she looks peripheral in the working‑together network despite being structurally important. The six‑day absence, where queues and tail cycle times for exception cases grew while routine claims stayed stable, indicates both useful coordination and potential bottleneck or key‑person risk. I recommend treating Mira as a formally defined exception desk and boundary‑spanning broker, not as an ad‑hoc “queue cleaner.” Management should document her triage rules, add at least one trained backup, and monitor waiting times and complaint rates around her steps before considering any deeper redesign or automation.
