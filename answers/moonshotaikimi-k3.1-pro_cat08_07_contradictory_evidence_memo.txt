## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Operations Committee  
**Decision:** Whether to scale, modify, or withdraw Redesign R

**Recommendation: choose E, with D embedded in it.** Do not scale R nationally and do not roll back the entire redesign. Instead, permit a controlled expansion for verifiably simple, low-risk houses, using only the lower-risk elements of R, while containing the risky features and running controlled regional comparisons for the remaining segments.

R offers substantial potential value: median cycle time fell by 3.4 days, cases with three or more handovers fell from 34% to 12%, visible cost declined by EUR 23 per case, and simple houses improved on median duration, p90 duration, first-time-right, and complaints. A wholesale rollback would sacrifice these gains without establishing which design element produced them.

However, the pilot also bought speed alongside a materially worse exception path. p90 and p95 duration increased by 7.5 and 17 days respectively; first-time-right fell six percentage points; complaints, manual activation, second visits, and SLA breaches increased. Estimated cost including downstream handling worsened, although that estimate is incomplete. These outcomes cannot be dismissed as aggregate case-mix effects: apartments and old buildings deteriorated within their own segments.

The evidence does not yet establish causality. The pilot was shorter, covered different regions and more complex buildings, and had incomplete early segment/risk attributes. R also changed several things simultaneously. Nevertheless, the alignments, DFG changes, temporal deviations, and resource evidence provide sufficiently consistent warning signals to act before perfect causal evidence is available.

Accordingly, management should immediately:

- Stop national deployment and suspend predictive survey skipping outside an approved experiment.
- Reinstate required permit checks and successful activation before billing as the default; audit and remediate affected pilot cases.
- Preserve merged validation/scheduling and accountable case ownership only in a controlled simple-house pathway, with normal survey policy, shared case context, backup ownership, and capacity headroom.
- Add near-term coordinator and second-visit capacity where utilization is constraining recovery.
- Run stratified experiments rather than attributing the pilot result to coordinator ownership alone.

The trade-off is deliberate: we forgo some possible short-term speed and survey savings in exchange for lower customer exposure and better information. Broader rollout should require concurrent-control evidence by segment, acceptable tail and rework performance, complete 60-day economics, mandatory-control conformance, and demonstrated resilience during coordinator absence. Median improvement alone is not a rollout gate.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness concerns how well the model replays observed traces; precision concerns how much additional, unobserved behavior the model allows. A model can therefore replay the common coordinator path very well while remaining excessively permissive about combinations of skipped surveys, repeat visits, manual activation, and billing. Frequent alignments can coexist with high aggregate fitness when most common traces replay well. Low precision is not itself a rework or compliance rate, and high fitness is not evidence of customer outcomes or future scalability. The fall in generalization to 0.56 adds further caution about transferring the model to new cases or regions.

**Improved median but worse tail.** The median primarily reflects the common case; it is relatively insensitive to the worst 5–10%. Merged validation/scheduling can accelerate straightforward orders while a minority encounter failed visits, permit delays, manual activation, or slow second-visit recovery. Those exceptions strongly affect p90 and p95 without necessarily reversing the median. The new BillingComplaint long-tail relation and increased Failed visitSecond visit delay are consistent with this explanation. Case mix may amplify the aggregate effect, but within-segment deterioration shows that case mix is not a complete explanation.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Handover count measures transfers between actors, not redundancy, shared knowledge, or spare capacity. A coordinator can reduce fragmented accountability while becoming a single point of dependency. Centralization in the working-together network, stalls during absence, and 93% utilization illustrate the difference. A process can have fewer interfaces and still be less able to absorb absence or demand spikes.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.** Survey and coordination savings can reduce cost recorded before complaint handling while failed visits, manual activation, second visits, and billing disputes create work elsewhere or later. The utilization shift from survey teams to second-visit teams is consistent with capacity displacement, although it does not prove a cash-cost transfer. Because downstream cost capture is incomplete, neither the EUR 23 visible saving nor the EUR 9 estimated total-cost deterioration should be treated as the final business case.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments and old buildings.** R’s components have different effects by risk. Task merging appears beneficial where validation and scheduling are genuinely simple. By contrast, an incorrect survey-skip decision can remove precisely the information needed to prevent a failed visit, while permit-sensitive buildings require controls that a simplified path may bypass. Thus, treatment heterogeneity and aggregate case mix operate together. The overall result is not a weighted average of segment medians, and strong simple-house performance should not be used to offset harm in other segments.

## 5. Value-of-information analysis

The ranking below reflects **ability to change the pending scale/contain/rollback decision**, adjusted for cost, delay, and whether management would act on the result. It is not a strict implementation sequence; inexpensive instrumentation should proceed in parallel.

| Rank | Measurement | Uncertainty resolved and how it could change the decision |
|---:|---|---|
| **1** | **M7 — Conformance monitoring for billing/activation and permit absence** | Establishes whether these are isolated exceptions or systemic, ongoing exposure and whether mandatory controls are effective. Widespread severe violations could trigger immediate rollback of affected paths; contained deviations would support continuing the guarded experiment. Monitoring does not replace immediate remediation or prove that R caused every deviation. |
| **2** | **M4 — Full downstream cost for 60 days after billing** | Determines whether the EUR 23 visible saving survives rework, complaint handling, and delayed activation costs. If the simple-house pathway remains economically attractive with acceptable outcomes, expansion gains support. If its apparent saving disappears, the case for even limited deployment weakens substantially. Start now on existing cohorts because of the follow-up lag. |
| **3** | **M5 — Coordinator versus shared-team ownership under absence** | Tests the main component candidate for scaling and the resilience hypothesis directly. If shared ownership retains speed while avoiding absence stalls, choose that model rather than C. If coordinator ownership still creates unacceptable tails or requires unsustainable staffing, stop treating it as the default national design. |
| **4** | **M6 — Validate the building-risk rule against field-survey outcomes** | Establishes whether risk classification can support safe eligibility and identifies any strata where the rule is demonstrably unreliable. Poor validation would retire survey skipping and require stronger deployment eligibility. Good validation only in a narrow stratum would define the population for M1. |
| **5** | **M1 — Randomized survey skipping by building-risk stratum** | Provides the strongest causal evidence on whether skipping saves work or merely converts surveys into failed visits, second visits, tail delay, and complaints. A positive result could justify tightly bounded reintroduction; a negative result would retire it. Its current marginal value is below M5/M6 because skipping is already paused and is not necessary to preserve the simple-house pathway. It should follow M6 and use safeguards and stop rules. If management would never reintroduce skipping, M1 has little decision value and need not be run. |
| **6** | **M3 — Structured exception reason codes** | Distinguishes alternative failure mechanisms—such as customer readiness, address information, permits, access, or activation issues—and helps target fixes rather than treating all rework alike. It also improves experiment stratification and cost attribution. It is cheap enabling instrumentation and should start immediately despite its lower stand-alone ranking. |
| **7** | **M2 — Start timestamps for installation, activation, and manual activation** | Separates waiting time from work time and tests whether delays coincide with coordinator or specialist queues. This could redirect investment toward capacity or scheduling, but is less likely than cost, conformance, or ownership evidence to reverse the immediate deployment decision. |
| **8** | **M8 — Separate speed-to-appointment and working-service experience** | Reveals whether the small aggregate NPS decline conceals improved speed but substantially worse reliability perceptions. ## 6. Final recommendation

**Decision: implement E now, with D as its experimental core. Do not approve A, and do not invoke B solely on the current aggregate results.**

- **Immediate action:** Freeze further national deployment. Audit open and recently completed pilot cases for unapproved billing before activation, absent required permits, unresolved failed visits, and post-billing complaints; remediate affected customers. Restore required permit and activation controls and relieve overloaded coordinator/second-visit capacity.
- **Pause:** Predictive survey skipping outside an approved M1 experiment; post-billing activation as routine practice; any relaxation of mandatory permit checks; and sole-person ownership without shared context or backup.
- **Scale only in a controlled manner:** For verifiably simple, low-risk houses, expand a modified pathway consisting of merged validation/scheduling and accountable ownership with backup, normal survey/permit policy, and successful activation before billing. Use concurrent controls and staged expansion. Apartments, old buildings, business customers, and rural installs should remain in controlled comparisons or baseline-like guarded paths—not receive unrestricted R.
- **Measure next:** Start M7 and M4 immediately; conduct M5 and M6 as the next decision experiments; instrument M2/M3 in parallel and add M8. Run M1 only after M6 supports a safe eligible population and management is genuinely willing to act on a positive result.

**Broader-rollout gate.** Require evidence by segment, not aggregate averages:

1. **Credible comparison and complete follow-up:** Complete segment/risk attributes, concurrent randomized or well-matched controls, replication beyond the original pilot regions, and samples large enough to assess p95 and complaints with complete 60-day cost follow-up.
2. **A retained benefit:** For simple houses, retain at least a 3-day median improvement—half the pilot’s observed gain—or an explicitly approved equivalent economic benefit. Other segments must demonstrate their own material benefit.
3. **No hidden tail or quality deterioration:** Pre-register non-inferiority margins for p90, p95, first-time-right, complaints, second visits, manual activation, and SLA. A reasonable provisional set for management to ratify before results are examined is **+2 days at p90, +3 days at p95, 2 percentage points first-time-right, and +1 percentage point complaints**, with confidence bounds inside those margins. These are risk-appetite choices, not findings from the pilot.
4. **Sound total economics and customer outcome:** Sixty-day fully loaded cost per successfully activated case must be no higher than control, and working-service experience must not deteriorate.
5. **Mandatory controls and resilience:** No process-design-induced permit or activation-control violations, effective ongoing conformance monitoring, and successful M5 absence/surge testing with approved utilization headroom.

If only simple houses pass, keep deployment restricted to them. If ownership fails the resilience test, adopt shared-team ownership or stop scaling it. If severe mandatory violations cannot be controlled immediately, roll back the affected process while the evidence is reassessed.
