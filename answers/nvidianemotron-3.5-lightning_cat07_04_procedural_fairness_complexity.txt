The eventlog attributes, while rich, leave several critical dimensions unobserved or potentially mismeasured, limiting the strength of any fairness inference.

- **Documentation_completeness as a languageaccess proxy** – “Incomplete documentation” is recorded as a binary/event attribute, but its causality is unclear. Languagepreference applicants may submit complete forms that are flagged incomplete because staff cannot read/verify them, or may submit incomplete forms due to lack of languageappropriate guidance. - **Missing applicant needs and experiential outcomes** – case_outcome captures approval/rejection but does not record quality of explanation, dignity of treatment, clarity of reasons, or whether the applicant felt heard. These are central to procedural fairness but are absent from the data. An applicant may be approved yet receive a poorly explained decision, or be denied with adequate reasoning; the current metrics cannot differentiate.
- **Unobserved resource workload and capacity** – Resource assignment is observed only at the caselevel (org:resource), and waitingtime medians are aggregated. Dailygranularity workload, staff turnover, stress, training level, or intermittent overloads are not captured. - **Appeal quality and rework nature** – Appeal rates and rework rates are counted, but the log does not distinguish between meritorious appeals (system error) and applicantdriven appeals (misunderstanding). Similarly, “rework” may involve correcting applicantsubmitted errors versus staffside processing mistakes. - **Historical bias in prior_case_count** – Priorcase count may reflect how often an applicant has interacted with the system, which itself may be higher for groups that have faced repeated barriers (e.g., language minorities, persons with disabilities). - **Channel and region as coarse categorizers** – “Channel” (online/inperson) and “region” are binary/aggregate fields. Inperson applicants may differ in many unobserved ways (transportation access, digital literacy, disability status). Region may correlate with local policy implementation, staffing levels, or cultural factors not captured in the model. - **Temporal dynamics** – The adjusted model controls for “month,” but not for withinmonth seasonality, policy changes, or surge periods (e.g., endofquarter, benefitopen enrollment). If certain groups are overrepresented during highvolume periods, their cycle times may be affected by transient system load rather than enduring procedural features.

The following options aim to reduce procedural disparities while acknowledging tradeoffs. Each includes a likely side effect or unintended consequence.

**2. Servicelevel targets for support steps**

May increase caseworker workload if pods are fully burdened, requiring hiring or workload redistribution. **4. Decisionexplanation improvements**
- **Option**: Standardize decision letters and notification scripts to include plainlanguage reasons, appeal rights, and contact information for support steps. Offer multilingual decision explanations and visual pathway maps showing where the applicant’s case currently sits.
- **Side effects / tradeoffs**: Improves perceived fairness and may reduce unnecessary appeals (if applicants understand reasons), thereby lowering appealrework burden. Does not directly reduce cycle time but can decrease downstream reprocessing. Requires investment in content development, translation, and staff training. # 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence, even after controlling for a broad set of casemix covariates, reveals **procedural unfairness in several dimensions, but the overall picture is mixed and does not support a blanket conclusion of discrimination.**

- **Timeliness disparities persist** across adjusted models: Group B and Group C experience significantly longer cycle times (+2.6 and +4.1 days) even after risk, documentation, channel, and other controls. The 90thpercentile gaps are even wider, indicating that tail wait times are a genuine concern.
- **Procedural burden is unevenly distributed** languagesupport and accommodationreview steps, both rightspreserving, add measurable waits (3.8 and 4.7 days) with limited specialist capacity. Group B and Group C applicants are disproportionately exposed to these steps, and no servicelevel targets exist to guarantee timely delivery.
- **Routing consistency is compromised by resource concentration** and regional handover patterns. Group B’s extra handovers (general  bilingual  general) and Group C’s (general  accommodation specialist  supervisor) are not tied to applicant complexity but to administrative structure, creating unequal procedural experiences.
- **Adjusted outcome gaps remain** in approval probability (5.5 pp for Group B, 3.1 pp for Group D) and appeal/rework rates, suggesting that observed complexity does not fully explain differences. However, risk_score_band may itself encode historical bias, and documentation_completeness may be affected by language access, meaning some “controlled” differences may reflect measurement limitations rather than pure procedural fairness.
- **Legitimate routing explanations** account for a substantial share of the observed patterns: Group D’s riskdriven path, Group C’s accommodationreview path, and Group B’s languagesupport needs are all consistent with policy design and documented casemix differences.

**Conclusion:** The process is **procedurally unfair in specific, identifiable dimensions**—particularly timeliness for Groups B and C, burden from rightspreserving support steps without adequate capacity or SLAs, and inequitable handovers/regional practices. Much of the disparity is explainable by legitimate casemix and policy requirements, but the absence of targeted capacity, monitoring, and quality metrics for support steps means that applicants who need those steps inherently experience slower, more burdensome procedures. The evidence does not support concluding that the system is intentionally discriminatory, nor does it support dismissing the disparities as purely attributable to casemix differences. A calibrated assessment is that the process **currently exhibits procedural unfairness that is partially remediable through the mitigation options outlined** (capacity addition, SLA targets, routing redesign, monitoring), while requiring careful attention to the potential bias in risk scores and documentation flags.

**Final calibrated wording:** The available evidence indicates that the casemanagement process is **procedurally unfair along several measurable dimensions** (timeliness, supportstep burden, routing handovers) even after controlling for observed casemix, but the majority of the observed raw gaps are consistent with legitimate routing driven by risk, language needs, and accommodation requests. The process cannot be deemed fully fair, nor can it be confidently labeled discriminatory; it is best described as **indeterminate with respect to intentional discrimination but procedurally unfair in its current implementation for groups relying on language support and accommodation review**, and **amenable to targeted improvements** as detailed in the mitigation section.
