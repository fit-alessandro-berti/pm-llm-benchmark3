# 1. Stress scenarios

### Scenario A — Winter demand-mix surge: Urgent and Complex-panel heavy
- Total arrivals increase and the mix shifts toward Urgent and Complex-panel orders, comparable to or worse than the observed peak mix (Urgent 29%, Complex-panel 20% versus normal 18% and 13%).
- Routine volume may not fall proportionally, so total work increases while a larger share requires medical validation and specialist review.
- Likely accompanying effects: more priority exceptions, more fragmented preparation/assay schedules, and more manual review.

### Scenario B — Assay degradation or one-machine outage
- M2 failure rate rises further, or M1 is unavailable for planned/unplanned maintenance.
- This is especially harmful because assay machines already operate at 98% utilization in peak conditions, with a median queue of 18 hours.
- Repeat-after-failure orders rise, particularly after night shifts and M2 use; repeats consume assay capacity twice and create a reinforcing overload loop.

### Scenario C — Medical-validation or specialist-review absence
- One medical validator is absent during a peak, or the specialist is unavailable during a high-complexity period, including the recurring every-second-Friday specialist absence.
- Complex-panel work rises at the same time as medical validation demand: Complex-panel orders are 3.1 times more likely to require medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to require specialist review.
- The immediate risk is not only slower final validation: completed assay work accumulates upstream, raising work-in-process and increasing the probability of prioritization conflicts.

### Scenario D — Courier compression, delayed route, or both
- Couriers arrive late and then release a compressed wave of samples, especially around the current 10:00 and 17:00 arrival spikes.
- Intake, registration, and pre-analytical work are compressed into fewer hours. Courier intake utilization reaches 88% in peak periods, while pre-analytical check reaches 96%.
- A delay can turn otherwise manageable Urgent orders into near-breach orders before analytical work begins.

### Scenario E — Combined “bad winter day”
- Compressed courier arrivals coincide with high Urgent/Complex-panel mix, M2 instability, and reduced specialist availability.
- This is the most important design case because bottlenecks interact: intake delay consumes SLA time, pre-analytical defects generate recollection loops, machine instability generates repeat loops, and completed complex work accumulates before specialist/medical validation.
- The process should not promise that every target remains achievable in this case; it should protect contractual Urgent work, preserve traceability, and prevent uncontrolled growth of Routine and Complex-panel WIP.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

## Scenario A — Winter demand-mix surge

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Courier intake and pre-analytical check tighten first.**  
   Peak pre-analytical utilization is already 96%, with a 9.5-hour median queue. The performance DFG shows `Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check` rising from 2 to 9 hours and `Pre-analytical check -> Test preparation` from 4 to 13 hours. More arrivals, particularly from concentrated courier deliveries, quickly create an intake backlog.

2. **Accession correction becomes a hidden unblocker bottleneck.**  
   Peak correction queue is 14 hours at 91% utilization. Although only 420 peak cases enter manual accession correction, it prevents cases from reaching preparation and has a feedback path to recollection. Delayed corrections keep samples in limbo and consume clerk time through physician communication.

3. **Preparation and assay capacity then become the system constraint.**  
   Preparation reaches 89% utilization and its queue grows to 11 hours. Machine utilization reaches 98%, with an 18-hour median queue. The DFG shows more peak batch waiting: `Test preparation -> Batch wait` increases from 760/16,550 normal to 1,180/8,140 peak, a much larger share of prepared work. At the same time, Urgent bypass falls from 74% to 58%, evidence that priority handling itself degrades when the machine schedule is congested.

4. **Technical validation and then medical validation accumulate.**  
   Technical validators exceed capacity at 101% peak utilization and queue 16 hours. Medical validation reaches 93% utilization but has a 22-hour median queue, indicating demand clustering and downstream dependency rather than average utilization alone. The `Technical validation -> Medical validation` edge rises from 8 to 24 hours.

5. **Specialist review becomes the tail bottleneck for Complex-panel cases.**  
   Peak `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation` time is 48 hours, versus 18 hours normal; specialist review queue is 36 hours. Complex-panel work therefore drives the high tail, not merely the mean.

**How interventions migrate the bottleneck**

- **I1, strict Urgent-first dispatch**, can reduce Urgent machine waiting but pushes Routine and potentially non-Urgent Complex-panel work behind repeated priority arrivals. The result can be higher Routine WIP, longer batch waits, and eventually a larger technical/medical validation wave.
- **I2, larger Routine batches**, improves preparation setup efficiency but increases the time until a Routine sample enters the next assay slot. If Urgent exceptions are frequent, batches are repeatedly broken and neither efficiency nor Urgent responsiveness is achieved.
- **I8, registration automation**, may reduce registration queues but moves more work faster into pre-analytical check, already at 96% utilization. Without intake smoothing and defect triage, it accelerates congestion rather than release.
- **I5, evening medical-validation shift**, relieves the final-validation queue but can expose technical validation and specialist review as the next bottlenecks. It is useful only if the upstream cases are technically complete and specialist-reviewed.

## Scenario B — Assay degradation or one-machine outage

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Assay queue becomes immediate constraint.**  
   With 98% peak utilization, there is effectively no recovery capacity. Any loss of machine hours or lower effective yield drives `Batch wait -> Assay run` beyond the observed 22-hour median.

2. **Repeat assay loop amplifies the outage.**  
   Peak `Assay run -> Repeat assay` is 1,260 cases out of 8,500-ish assay entries, versus 1,140 in the much larger normal period. The repeat loop is proportionally much worse in peak. The edge `Repeat assay -> Assay run` rises from 10 to 26 hours. More M2 use under stress can raise failures, which creates more repeats, which consumes more machine time, further delaying first-pass work.

3. **Preparation becomes blocked by downstream machine availability.**  
   Prepared samples wait, benches fill, and staff may attempt to create larger batches to preserve setup efficiency. That may improve local utilization but worsens Urgent responsiveness.

4. **Technical validation may temporarily appear to improve, then become bursty.**  
   During the outage, validators can be starved of completed assays. When the machine backlog clears, technical validators—already at 101% utilization in peak—receive a burst and form the next queue.

5. **Medical validation and specialist review receive a delayed wave.**  
   The delay shifts rather than eliminates the backlog. Complex-panel cases may be technically ready together and then exceed medical/specialist review capacity.

**How interventions migrate the bottleneck**

- **I3, dedicate M1 to Urgent and Complex-panel work**, protects critical work if M1 is healthy, but routing Routine entirely to M2 is unsafe when M2 is the higher-failure machine. It can increase Repeat-after-failure work and make M2 a self-reinforcing bottleneck.
- **I1, strict Urgent-first**, is appropriate only as a bounded emergency policy. With machine degradation, it can starve Routine first-pass runs and non-Urgent Complex work long enough that their later validation demand becomes a second surge.
- **I2, larger batches**, is especially risky during an outage. It may reduce setups but increases the cost of a machine failure and the delay for a batch already waiting.
- **I5, extra medical validation**, has little immediate value while the machine is constrained; the correct first response is to preserve assay yield and allocate scarce assay slots by risk and due-time.

## Scenario C — Medical-validator or specialist-review absence

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Medical validation and/or specialist review immediately constrains completion.**  
   Medical validation queue is already 22 hours in peak. Specialist review queue is 36 hours, with 48 hours on `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation`. The absence creates a final-stage backlog even if intake and assay are stable.

2. **Technical validation becomes blocked or artificially overproduces.**  
   Technical validators already run at 101% peak utilization. If they continue validating all cases without release capacity downstream, they generate a large medically-unvalidated inventory. This can be operationally unsafe if handoffs and ownership become unclear.

3. **Assay and preparation may become constrained by storage, handling, and WIP limits.**  
   If downstream work accumulates, continuing to maximize assay throughput can produce excessive completed-but-unreleased work while newly arriving Urgent samples still need timely processing.

4. **Complex-panel cases dominate the tail.**  
   Their higher likelihood of both medical validation and specialist review means that generic queues can hide their actual urgency. A single specialist absence disproportionately harms Complex-panel cases.

**How interventions migrate the bottleneck**

- **I5, evening medical-validation shift**, directly helps a medical-validator absence but does not solve specialist-review shortage. It can also move the limiting step to technical validation unless validator handoff capacity is protected.
- **I10, outsource Complex-panel overflow**, can reduce specialist-review queue, but the 12-hour transport delay can be unacceptable for near-breach Urgent Complex-panel cases. It also adds traceability and handoff failure modes.
- **I4, cross-train technical validators for accession correction**, is counterproductive during a validation absence: pulling technical validators away from their existing 101% peak load increases downstream accumulation.
- **I9, hold Routine releases for batch reporting**, may reduce release-work interruptions but deliberately worsens Routine cycle time and does not relieve medical or specialist queues. It should not be a default response.

## Scenario D — Courier compression or delay

**Likely bottleneck sequence**

1. **Courier intake and registration experience arrival spikes.**  
   Courier intake median queue rises from 1 to 7.5 hours at peak; registration from 0.7 to 2.8 hours. The `Register order -> Receive sample` edge rises from 5 to 8 hours. A delayed courier creates apparent downstream urgency because SLA time is consumed before pre-analytical work starts.

2. **Pre-analytical check becomes the operational choke point.**  
   It is at 96% utilization in peak and drives costly failure loops. Recollection paths are substantial: peak `Pre-analytical check -> Request recollection` is 780 and returns from recollection take 60 hours median. Poorly handled intake defects therefore create long-cycle cases and consume clerk capacity.

3. **Accession correction blocks samples that could otherwise proceed.**  
   Peak correction queue of 14 hours is longer than the pre-analytical median queue. It is a small pool with only two specialists, so arrival compression can quickly overwhelm it.

4. **Preparation, assay, and validation receive a delayed batch wave.**  
   Even if the courier backlog clears, the compression creates synchronized demand at each subsequent station. This produces long queue tails and makes staffing by daily average misleading.

**How interventions migrate the bottleneck**

- **I7, smoother courier pickup slots**, is robust because it reduces the variance feeding several downstream stages. However, if not matched with receiving/pre-analytical staffing windows, it merely shifts the spike.
- **I8, registration automation**, can help during a courier wave but risks sending a larger wave into pre-analytical check. It should be coupled to controlled release into the pre-analytical queue, not simply faster registration.
- **I6, early defect triage**, prevents defective samples from occupying preparation and assay capacity, but excessive early rejection may increase recollections, physician calls, and dissatisfaction. It must distinguish clear defects from remediable administrative corrections.
- **I4, correction cross-training**, can unblock cases after intake, but if technical validation is already overloaded it transfers failure from early flow to late release.

## Scenario E — Combined bad winter day

The expected pattern is:

1. Courier compression creates a rapid pre-analytical and accession correction queue.
2. Priority handling increases Urgent bypass attempts; preparation and assay become fragmented.
3. M2 instability increases repeat loops and consumes assay slots.
4. Technical validation accumulates after delayed assay completion.
5. Medical validation and specialist review become the tail bottlenecks for Complex-panel work.
6. Routine work is at risk of indefinite starvation if the only response is strict Urgent-first dispatch.

The redesign must therefore control **flow variability, rework loops, and WIP by class**, rather than maximizing utilization at each station. At 96–101% utilization, local efficiency policies are likely to make the whole system less stable.

# 3. Robust redesign package

## A. Establish class-based flow lanes with explicit WIP caps, not unlimited Urgent-first priority

Create three operational lanes:

1. **Critical lane:** Urgent cases, including Urgent Complex-panel cases.
2. **Complex lane:** non-Urgent Complex-panel cases and specialist-review candidates.
3. **Planned Routine lane:** Routine and stable Repeat-after-failure work, with protected but limited capacity.

Use due-time and clinical risk within each lane, rather than a permanent “all Urgent before everything” rule. Reserve a bounded share of preparation and assay slots for each lane, with a small flex pool that can be reassigned based on triggers.

**Why robust**
- Protects the 18-hour Urgent contractual target.
- Prevents Routine starvation and uncontrolled WIP.
- Makes Complex-panel work visible before it reaches medical validation or specialist review.
- Avoids relying on average processing times when mix changes.

**Dependencies**
- Reliable order classification at registration.
- Visible due-time, assay requirements, and specialist-review likelihood.
- Real-time WIP and queue-age visibility by lane.

**Where it can fail**
- If Urgent arrivals exceed the reserved/flex capacity for many hours, Routine capacity will still have to be sacrificed.
- If classification is inaccurate, cases may enter the wrong lane and distort priorities.
- A lane system does not create machine capacity during an outage; it only allocates scarcity more safely.

## B. Smooth arrivals and control admission to the constrained pre-analytical stage

Implement **I7 courier-slot smoothing** as a default operating change, supported by appointment windows, route-level volume forecasts, and escalation for late couriers. Pair it with a controlled intake release rule: registration may be automated or accelerated, but the pre-analytical queue should have a visible WIP threshold.

Use **I8 registration automation** only for complete digital-metadata orders, with exception routing for incomplete orders rather than forcing uncertain orders through automatically.

**Why robust**
- Reduces the variance driving courier intake, registration, pre-analytical check, accession correction, preparation, and assay.
- Attacks a root cause of peak instability instead of merely adding downstream capacity.
- Automation frees clerks for exception handling and recollection communication, which becomes important during defect waves.

**Dependencies**
- Courier cooperation and route data.
- Rules that define “complete digital metadata.”
- Staff scheduling aligned with smoother arrivals.

**Where it can fail**
- If a major courier delay causes unavoidable compression, smoothing alone cannot eliminate the surge.
- Faster registration without pre-analytical capacity control will worsen the 96% utilization bottleneck.
- Automation can create silent data-quality errors if metadata validation is weak.

## C. Introduce early pre-analytical defect triage with a correction-versus-recollection split

Deploy **I6** as a two-hour triage target, but do not make it a blanket rejection system. At triage, classify samples into:
- **Clear reject/recollect:** irreparable specimen issue; notify quickly.
- **Correctable accession issue:** route immediately to correction specialists.
- **Proceed with documented exception:** where clinically and procedurally allowed.
- **Standard flow:** release to preparation.

Use a short, standardized physician-facing defect message to reduce clerk handling time for recollection loops.

**Why robust**
- Prevents defective or ambiguous samples from consuming scarce preparation and assay capacity.
- Shortens the time to recollection decision; the current recollection-to-receipt median is 42 hours normal and 60 hours peak, so late discovery is especially expensive.
- Separates correction work from true recollection work, protecting the small accession-correction pool.

**Dependencies**
- Clear specimen-acceptance rules and clinical governance.
- Auditing of false rejects and false accepts.
- Fast communication channels to collection sites/physicians.

**Where it can fail**
- Overly conservative triage increases recollection demand and may overload clerks and physicians.
- If correction specialists remain overloaded, “correctable” samples can still wait 14 hours in peak.
- Early triage improves quality but may initially reveal more defects, making performance appear worse before the system stabilizes.

## D. Stabilize assay operations around yield, protected slots, and repeat-loop control

Do not permanently dedicate M1 to one case class and M2 to Routine work. Instead, use:
- **Protected time/slot blocks on healthy machine capacity** for near-breach Urgent and time-sensitive Complex-panel work.
- **A controlled Routine batch cadence** rather than universal larger batches.
- **M2 quality surveillance by shift, assay, and operator**, because repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 use.
- **A repeat-assay review rule:** repeated failures should receive a rapid technical/root-cause check before consuming another standard queue slot.

Use larger Routine batches only when the Urgent queue and machine recovery buffer are healthy.

**Why robust**
- Reduces the reinforcing loop of machine failure  repeat assay  machine overload  longer waits.
- Preserves the benefits of batching without allowing it to trap Urgent samples.
- Avoids concentrating all Routine workload and its repeat risk on M2.

**Dependencies**
- Machine-level reliability data and routing logic.
- A defined recovery buffer; at 98% utilization, no schedule is resilient without slack.
- Quality authority to pause or restrict problematic M2 operating conditions.

**Where it can fail**
- In a full M1 outage, protected slots cannot compensate for insufficient total machine capacity.
- Excessive protected slots can reduce effective throughput through fragmentation.
- Repeat review consumes expert time and may delay a small number of cases; it must be targeted to repeat-prone patterns.

## E. Create a downstream completion cell for technical, medical, and specialist flow

Create a visible “completion board” for technically complete cases, grouped by:
- Urgent due-time,
- Complex-panel status,
- specialist-review requirement,
- medical-validation readiness,
- age in queue.

Maintain a small cross-functional daily huddle during peaks involving technical validation, medical validation, specialist review, and operations. Its purpose is to sequence ready work and prevent medically pending inventory from becoming invisible.

Use **I5 evening medical validation** as a pre-planned flex capability for defined peaks, not as a permanent default. Protect technical-validation handoff capacity before activating it.

**Why robust**
- Addresses the observed peak queues: 16 hours at technical validation, 22 hours at medical validation, and 36 hours at specialist review.
- Makes the Complex-panel tail visible before breach rather than after.
- Avoids treating final validation as a single generic queue.

**Dependencies**
- Appropriate credentialing and handoff governance.
- Forecasted peak staffing and fatigue controls.
- Specialist review availability and clear escalation rules.

**Where it can fail**
- If specialist review is absent, extra medical validation cannot clear specialist-dependent work.
- If technical validators are diverted to accession correction, the completion cell may be starved.
- Evening shifts can create fatigue, handoff errors, or next-day staffing gaps if used continuously.

## F. Use limited, traceable partner-lab overflow for selected Complex-panel work

Prepare **I10** as a contingency, not a universal peak solution. Send only eligible non-near-breach Complex-panel cases where:
- the partner’s lower specialist-review queue offsets the 12-hour transport delay,
- sample stability permits transfer,
- traceability and result integration are verified,
- the partner can meet the required assay and clinical governance standard.

Do not outsource Urgent Complex-panel cases that are already close to the 18-hour release target unless the expected local specialist delay clearly exceeds the transport-adjusted partner path.

**Why robust**
- Provides a relief valve for the specialist-review bottleneck, which has a 36-hour peak median queue and 48-hour median hold-to-medical edge.
- Preserves scarce in-house specialist attention for urgent and clinically sensitive work.

**Dependencies**
- Pre-negotiated capacity, transport chain-of-custody, interoperability, and quality agreements.
- Daily forecast of specialist-review backlog and partner turnaround.
- Clear consent, regulatory, and reporting rules.

**Where it can fail**
- Transport delay and handoff complexity can worsen turnaround or create traceability failures.
- Partner capacity may also be constrained during regional winter surges.
- Overuse can hollow out internal learning about recurring Complex-panel issues.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

## Policy A — Bounded Urgent protection during demand-mix surge
**Activate when**
- Urgent mix exceeds 25% of arrivals for two consecutive intake windows, or
- projected Urgent release risk shows more than 10% of Urgent WIP with less than 6 hours remaining to the 18-hour target, or
- Urgent preparation/assay queue age exceeds 4 hours.

**Action**
- Activate protected Urgent slots at preparation and assay.
- Use due-time ordering within Urgent cases.
- Preserve a minimum planned Routine/Complex capacity share; do not apply unlimited I1 strict Urgent-first dispatch.
- Reassess every 2–4 hours.

**Deactivate when**
- Urgent mix falls below 22% and near-breach Urgent WIP is below 5% for two consecutive review periods.

**Guardrail**
- If Routine queue age or WIP exceeds its defined cap, restore Routine slots even while Urgent protection remains active. This prevents the deferred Routine wave from overwhelming later stages.

## Policy B — Assay degradation / outage mode
**Activate when**
- A machine is unavailable for more than one scheduled run block, or
- assay queue median exceeds 12 hours and rising, or
- repeat-assay share exceeds 12% of assay attempts, or
- M2 repeat rate is materially above M1 or its own recent control limit.

**Action**
- Stop routing all Routine work to M2; route by reliability, assay compatibility, and due-time.
- Reserve healthy-machine capacity for near-breach Urgent cases and selected Complex panels.
- Reduce or suspend large Routine batches if they would occupy scarce recovery capacity.
- Trigger root-cause checks for repeat-prone M2/night-shift patterns.
- Delay low-risk Routine work only with explicit patient/provider communication and a recovery plan.

**Deactivate when**
- Machine availability is restored, repeat rate returns below the warning band for two run cycles, and assay queue age declines for two consecutive review periods.

**Guardrail**
- Do not activate extra medical-validation staffing as the primary response unless sufficient technically complete work exists; otherwise it is idle capacity while the true bottleneck remains assay.

## Policy C — Medical validator or specialist absence mode
**Activate when**
- Medical-validation queue median exceeds 16 hours, or
- specialist-review queue median exceeds 24 hours, or
- planned absence overlaps with Complex-panel mix above 16%, or
- more than a defined threshold of Urgent/Complex cases are waiting for medical or specialist action.

**Action**
- Activate I5 temporary evening medical-validation shift if credentialed coverage is available.
- Prioritize specialist-review slots by Urgent status, remaining due-time, and clinical consequence—not arrival order alone.
- Stop diverting technical validators to accession correction except for a clearly bounded emergency.
- Use I10 for eligible non-near-breach Complex-panel overflow where expected partner turnaround beats local expected delay.

**Deactivate when**
- Medical queue is below 10 hours and specialist queue below 16 hours for a full operating day, with no forecasted absence-driven surge.

**Guardrail**
- Do not outsource cases merely because they are Complex. Compare local predicted completion time versus partner transport-plus-completion time.

## Policy D — Courier disruption mode
**Activate when**
- A route is more than 90 minutes late, or
- forecast arrival volume in a two-hour window exceeds 130% of planned intake capacity, or
- courier intake queue exceeds 5 hours and pre-analytical queue exceeds 7 hours.

**Action**
- Re-slot inbound deliveries where possible and stage intake by due-time and sample stability.
- Deploy registration automation only for clean digital orders.
- Activate two-hour defect triage and fast-track clearly correctable accession cases.
- Temporarily assign clerks to standardized recollection communication, while protecting pre-analytical expertise.
- Delay non-urgent registration completion only if it prevents unsafe pre-analytical overload; never obscure receipt timestamps or due-time risk.

**Deactivate when**
- Arrival flow stays within planned windows for two consecutive windows and pre-analytical queue returns below 5 hours.

**Guardrail**
- Avoid treating faster registration as success if pre-analytical WIP is rising; the release rule into pre-analytical must remain active.

## Policy E — Combined bad-day escalation
**Activate when**
- At least two scenario triggers are active simultaneously, especially assay degradation plus high Urgent mix, or courier disruption plus specialist absence.

**Action**
- Convene an operations/quality escalation huddle every 2–4 hours.
- Freeze nonessential process changes and avoid ad hoc local prioritization.
- Enforce lane WIP caps, protect near-breach Urgent work, reserve a minimum completion capacity for Complex work, and use pre-approved partner overflow selectively.
- Communicate projected delays early to clinical customers for non-Urgent work.

**Deactivate when**
- Only one scenario trigger remains active and key queues are declining.

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **A severe assay outage remains structurally difficult.** With machines already at 98% utilization in peak, queue control and prioritization cannot replace lost analytical capacity. The redesign can protect the most time-critical work, but it cannot preserve all routine targets during a prolonged outage.

- **Specialist dependence remains a single-point vulnerability.** The specialist-review pool has one specialist unavailable every second Friday and a 36-hour peak queue. Partner overflow helps only for eligible work and is vulnerable to transport and partner capacity.

- **Recollection loops remain long and externally dependent.** Even with early triage, the recollection-to-receipt median is 60 hours in peak. The lab can shorten decision time but cannot fully control collection-site response, physician communication, or patient return.

- **Technical-validator cross-training has an opportunity cost.** Using technical validators for accession correction may solve a 14-hour correction queue while worsening technical validation, already at 101% peak utilization. This is a redesign-created vulnerability if cross-training is activated without a technical-load guardrail.

- **Protected slots can reduce effective machine throughput.** The lane design intentionally keeps some slack and reserves critical capacity. If applied too rigidly during a stable period, it can create underused slots or fragmented machine schedules. The policy must allow protected capacity to revert to shared capacity when risk is low.

- **Automation can shift errors rather than eliminate them.** Registration automation may reduce clerk queues but may propagate incomplete or incorrectly mapped metadata into pre-analytical and accession workflows unless exception detection is strong.

- **Partner-lab overflow adds traceability and integration risk.** I10 can reduce specialist delay but adds transport delay, sample-stability constraints, handoff defects, duplicate-result risk, and uncertainty about the partner’s own winter capacity.

- **Routine work may still experience intentionally managed delay.** This is an explicit trade-off, not a failure of measurement. The redesign should make the delay visible, bounded, and communicated rather than allowing Routine work to become an invisible backlog behind Urgent work.

## Lagging outcome metrics: review daily and weekly

- Urgent release performance: percentage released within 18 hours, plus the number and causes of breaches.
- Routine release performance: percentage within 72 hours, with queue-age distribution rather than only median.
- Median and p90 cycle time by case type: Routine, Urgent, Complex-panel, and Repeat-after-failure.
- Rework and repeat-assay rates by machine, shift, assay type, and case type.
- Percentage of cases experiencing a queue over 12 hours; the peak baseline is already 54%.
- Cancellation and recollection rates, including time from defect identification to recollection request.
- Outsourced Complex-panel turnaround, transport exceptions, traceability deviations, and partner-versus-local predicted turnaround accuracy.
- Backlog recovery time after a disruption, measured by how long it takes queues and WIP to return below warning bands.

The key operating rule is to act on the leading indicators before p90 cycle time and SLA breaches confirm the problem. The observed peak data show that by the time queues reach their historical peak medians—18 hours at assay, 22 hours at medical validation, and 36 hours at specialist review—the system is already in a recovery problem rather than a normal dispatch problem.
