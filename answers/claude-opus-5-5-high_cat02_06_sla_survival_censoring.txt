# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin and scale.** \(t = 0\) is the `Submit` event of each case. Time is elapsed (calendar) hours, because the SLA is an elapsed-time SLA. Business-hour clocks would define a different estimand and are not used here. The supplied durations and ages are integers with no ties, so all 32 event/censoring times are distinct.

**Horizon.** The primary horizon is \(\tau = 72\) h, the SLA. A secondary horizon of \(\tau_2 = 96\) h is used only for late resolution. Beyond 96 h the risk set has three or fewer cases (S26, S13 and briefly S32), so any statement about \(t > 96\) is anecdotal.

**Events.**
- `Resolve` is the event of interest (operational success).
- `Reject` and `Withdraw` are internal terminal outcomes. They are **competing events**: once one occurs, resolution cannot happen. They are not censoring, because nothing about the case remains unobserved.
- `Transfer` is ambiguous by design. From the internal organization's point of view it is a terminal disposition, meaning the case leaves the internal process. From the point of view of the customer's eventual resolution it is **loss to follow-up**, because the external outcome is unknown. How it is encoded depends on the estimand (Section 2).

**Censoring.** Only `open` rows are right-censored, administratively at the single extract cutoff, at their age. Transfer is censoring only under an explicit assumption (scenario T2 below).

**Observed breach versus censoring.** An open case whose age already exceeds 72 h (S09, S11, S25, S26) is **not censored with respect to its 72-hour status**. It is a known, already-observed breach for every "not resolved or not disposed by 72 h" estimand, and it is censored only for questions about what happens after its age. An open case younger than 72 h (S31, S15, S10, S29, S24) has an **unknown** 72-hour status. It must be censored, never dropped and never counted as compliant or breached.

**Estimands.** The subscript on each \(F\) is the cause (resolve, reject, withdraw or transfer). \(S_{any}\) is the probability of no terminal disposition of any kind yet.

| ID | Estimand | Definition | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1 (primary) | Real-world probability of **not being resolved within the SLA** | \(1 - F_{res}(72)\), where \(F_{res}\) is the cumulative incidence of `Resolve` with reject, withdraw and (in the primary scenario) transfer as competing events | "What share of submitted cases do not get the service outcome within 72 h?" (customer-facing SLA attainment) |
| E1h (hypothetical, contrast only) | Probability of not being resolved by 72 h *in a world without* rejection, withdrawal or transfer | \(S^{KM}_{res}(72)\), with the other outcomes censored | A counterfactual that no real process has. It is shown only to demonstrate the bias |
| E2 | **Cumulative incidence of resolution after the SLA** (late resolution) | \(F_{res}(96) - F_{res}(72)\). The landmark version is \([F_{res}(96) - F_{res}(72)] / S_{any}(72)\) | "How much breached work is still recovered, and how quickly?" |
| E3 | Probability of **still having no terminal disposition at 72 h** | \(S_{any}(72)\), all-cause Kaplan-Meier (KM) with resolve, reject, withdraw and transfer all counted as terminal | "How many cases will sit in the overdue backlog?" (capacity and backlog governance) |
| E4 | Cause-specific cumulative incidences at 72 h | \(F_{rej}(72)\), \(F_{wd}(72)\), \(F_{tr}(72)\) | "Is non-resolution driven by delay, by customer drop-out, by rejection, or by hand-off?" |
| E5 | Restricted mean time without disposition | \(\mathrm{RMST}(72) = \int_0^{72} S_{any}(t)\,dt\) | Average hours of the SLA window a case occupies (a workload measure without proportional-hazards assumptions) |

**Point estimates.** These use the Aalen-Johansen estimator, transfer as a competing event, and open cases censored (see Section 8). All are observations of the estimator on 32 cases, not population truths:
- \(F_{res}(72) = 0.304\), so **E1 = 0.696**.
- \(F_{rej}(72) = 0.101\), \(F_{wd}(72) = 0.140\), \(F_{tr}(72) = 0.075\).
- **E3 = \(S_{any}(72)\) = 0.380**, with 9 cases in the risk set at 72 h.
- **E2 = 0.143** (late resolutions at 74, 76 and 88 h).
- **E5 = 50.4 h**.
- The hypothetical E1h = 0.639. It understates real-world non-resolution because KM that censors competing events overstates attainable resolution (0.361 versus 0.304).
- A case-resampling bootstrap gives an approximate 95% interval for E1 of **[0.52, 0.86]**, which shows how little 32 cases can pin down.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**The naive estimate.** Looking only at resolved cases, 4 of 13 breached (S03, S13, S20, S28), giving **30.8%**: cohort A 2/6 = 33%, cohort B 2/7 = 29%. This discards 19 of 32 cases, and every discarded group is prognostically different.

1. **Survivor (selection) bias from dropping open cases.** The four oldest open cases, S09 (92 h), S11 (81 h), S25 (79 h) and S26 (104 h), are **already-observed breaches**, yet a resolved-only analysis never sees them. Adding just these known breaches to the resolved cases moves the breach share from 4/13 to 8/17 = 47%. Resolved-only analysis conditions on having reached `Resolve` by the cutoff, which selects faster cases. Long cases are exactly the ones that have not finished yet (length-biased sampling of completions).

2. **Open cases below versus above 72 h are different kinds of information.**
   - S09, S11, S25 and S26 have a known 72-hour status (breached). Censoring them "at 72" or dropping them wastes certain information.
   - S31 (23 h), S15 (35 h), S10 (47 h), S29 (51 h) and S24 (67 h) have an unknown 72-hour status. Counting them as compliant ("not breached yet") biases E1 downward, and counting them as breached ("still open") biases it upward. The Aalen-Johansen estimator gives them an expected combined 0.74 resolutions by 72 h. Individually, the estimated conditional probabilities of resolving by 72 h are S31 0.26, S15 0.21, S10 0.13, S29 0.13 and S24 **0.00**.
   - The S24 value is a sparse-data artefact, not a prediction. No resolution happened in (67, 72] among the observed cases, even though S24 had recent activity (`Review` at 63 h).
   - The count bounds make the stakes explicit. Nine cases are known resolved by 72 h, 18 are known not resolved and 5 are unknown, so E1 lies in **[18/32, 23/32] = [0.563, 0.719]** without any assumption about the censored cases.

3. **Competing risks are not censoring.** S05 and S23 were rejected within 11 h, S12 withdrew at 15 h and S06 at 27 h. Treating them as censored, which is KM on resolution, estimates the probability of resolution by 72 h as 0.361 instead of the Aalen-Johansen value of 0.304. That overstates attainability by about 6 percentage points. In the priority strata the distortion is larger. P3 has F_res(72) = 0.20, but its non-resolution comes mainly from rejections (S05, S23, S14) and withdrawals (S12, S30), not from slowness. A resolved-only or KM view blends "not served" with "served late".

4. **Informative censoring from transfers and long inactivity gaps.**
   - All three transfers (S08, S21, S32) are **P1 and escalated**. Transfer is a process decision triggered by case characteristics, not a random loss.
   - Under T2 (censoring), their future is imputed from the P1/escalated cases still at risk. Those are S11 (idle 55 h after `Escalate`), S25 (idle 48 h after `Escalate`) and S29, which are themselves stalled. The imputation pool is tiny and atypical.
   - S32 was transferred at 96 h, after already breaching, which suggests transfer can be a *consequence* of delay (an escape valve). That would make the transfer hazard depend on the unobserved resolution hazard, which is the definition of informative censoring.
   - Long event gaps have the same problem. S26 has had no event for 72 h (last event `Review` at 32 h), S11 for 55 h, S25 for 48 h and S09 for 31 h. If these cases were abandoned, informally closed or waiting on the customer, "open" is misrecorded and their censoring is not independent of outcome.
   - Among cases younger than 72 h, S10 (escalated at 28 h, idle since) is the one whose censoring is most suspect.

5. **Cohort imbalance and non-randomized, incomplete rollout.**
   - Cohort B has more P1 cases (5/17 = 29% versus 3/15 = 20%).
   - The prefixes show that **every B case with priority P1 or P2 starts `Submit>AutoCheck`, while none of the five B P3 cases do** (S17, S20, S23, S26, S30). Rollout non-compliance is therefore perfectly aligned with priority.
   - Cohort A also shows AutoCheck after Screen in S01 and S15, so the "control" period had some exposure.
   - B cases are more recent, so they have shorter potential follow-up. B has 5 open cases, versus 4 in A.
   - The naive 33% versus 29% suggests B is better. Aalen-Johansen E1 is 0.708 (A) versus 0.684 (B), and priority-standardized it is 0.688 versus 0.675. The bootstrap 95% interval for B − A is **[−0.38, +0.29]**, so there is no evidence of any difference.
   - On E3 the ordering flips: B has *more* undisposed cases at 72 h (0.416 versus 0.339), largely because A had more early rejections.

6. **Duration summaries of completed cases.** The mean duration of resolved cases (49.8 h) excludes the long censored and transferred tail. It systematically understates cycle time and must not be used as the SLA metric.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

The binding constraints of these data are: n = 32; only 13 resolutions; 9 cases at risk at 72 h; 3 at risk per priority stratum at 72 h; **no intermediate event timestamps**; a common cutoff with unknown entry dates; and three competing outcomes plus an ambiguous transfer.

| Method | Estimand it targets | Fit to these data | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kaplan-Meier** | All-cause \(S_{any}(t)\) (E3). With competing events censored, it gives the hypothetical E1h | Correct and simple for E3, because every terminal outcome is an event and only open cases are censored. **Wrong for E1**, where it gives 0.361 versus 0.304 | Use for E3 only |
| **Aalen-Johansen / cumulative incidence** | E1, E2 and E4 directly, as real-world probabilities in the presence of competing events | Nonparametric, no proportional-hazards assumption, handles the 9 censored cases, and works with 13 events. Stratification by cohort or priority is descriptive only | **Primary** |
| Cause-specific hazards (Cox or piecewise-exponential per cause) | Etiology: "does this covariate change the instantaneous rate of resolution among cases still in process?" | With 13 resolution events, at most about one covariate (priority, with cohort only as a stratum). Hazard ratios do not map one-to-one onto E1, because a covariate raising the rejection hazard lowers \(F_{res}\) even with an unchanged resolution hazard | Secondary, for mechanism, reported next to the cumulative incidences |
| Subdistribution hazards (Fine-Gray) | Covariate effects directly on \(F_{res}\) | The risk set retains rejected, withdrawn and transferred cases artificially, which makes the hazard ratio hard to interpret. It requires censoring weights, which are unstable here because censoring depends on calendar entry. A proportional subdistribution hazard is implausible across stages | Sensitivity only, if a regression on E1 is demanded |
| Restricted mean time (RMST and restricted mean time lost by cause) | E5: mean hours without disposition in [0, 72], decomposed into time lost to each cause | Assumption-light and stable in small samples. Interpretable for workload: 50.4 h overall, 49.6 h (A), 51.1 h (B) | **Secondary** (workload and governance) |
| Landmarking | Dynamic risk: \(P(\text{resolved by } 72 \mid \text{active at } s)\) | Feasible using only information valid at \(s\): age plus baseline covariates. Estimated at 0.259 for s = 24 h (25 cases active) and **0.133 for s = 48 h** (16 active). Stage at \(s\) is unknown for terminal cases because intermediate timestamps are missing, so process covariates can enter only once timestamps are available | **Secondary** (alerting), baseline-covariate version now |
| Multi-state model (Aalen-Johansen state occupation, transition-specific hazards) | Stage occupation over time, stage-specific hazards, where the tail comes from | The right representation (Section 5), but it **needs entry times into each state**, which are not supplied. The only state-time information is age − gap for open cases (the last event time). Estimating it now would require fabricated timestamps | Target design once event logs are obtained. For now, use the multi-state representation only for descriptive state-at-cutoff tables |

**Recommendation.**
- **Primary:** Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence of resolution at 72 h (E1), with reject, withdraw and transfer as competing events (T1). Report the cause-specific cumulative incidences (E4) and the all-cause KM (E3) from the same fit, so readers see *why* cases are not resolved. Stratify descriptively by priority, then by cohort, with priority-standardized cohort contrasts and bootstrap intervals. The reasons: it defines the estimand first, it is valid under competing events, it assumes no model, and the sample cannot support more.
- **Secondary:** RMST(72) with restricted time lost by cause, plus a baseline-covariate landmark analysis at 24 h and 48 h. These give workload and dynamic-alert quantities without proportional hazards. Cause-specific hazards stratified by priority are reported for mechanism only. Once event timestamps are available, the secondary analysis becomes a **multi-state model with time-varying stage and dormancy covariates**.
- **What not to do:** no regression with escalation, rework or full prefix as a baseline covariate (Section 5); no cohort "effect" claims; no tail estimates beyond 96 h.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

**Observed control flow** (PM4Py variants and the directly-follows relation from prefixes; counts are observations):
- There are 27 distinct prefixes among 32 cases, so full-variant features are hopelessly sparse and must be grouped into path families.
- Entry is `Submit>Screen` (20 cases) or `Submit>AutoCheck` (12, all in cohort B).
- Of 12 `Escalate` occurrences, 4 were followed by `Resolve`, 3 by `Transfer`, 2 by `Review`, and **3 are the last observed event of an open case** (S10, S11, S25).
- `Review` is followed by `Resolve` 6 times, `Escalate` 5, `Rework` 3, `Withdraw` 3 and `Reject` once.
- Rework loops appear in S07, S13 and S27. S27 goes `Rework>Resolve` without a second `Review`, which is a possible conformance deviation.

These are branching frequencies without times, **not hazards**.

**Features and their timing class.**

| Feature | Derivation | Timing class | Leakage / bias risk | Permitted use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Priority | Given | Baseline | Low. It is a legitimate policy variable, but it drives transfer and escalation | Stratifier and adjustment |
| Cohort (A/B) | Given | Baseline (calendar period, intention-to-treat policy exposure) | Confounded with period, workload and follow-up length | Descriptive contrast, standardized by priority |
| Entry path / `autocheck_first` | First two activities, via `get_prefixes_from_log(log, 2)` | Early post-baseline. It is a **post-treatment** marker of actual policy uptake | Perfectly aligned with priority within B (no B P3 case starts with AutoCheck). A naive E1 of 0.630 versus 0.736 is mostly priority composition | Per-protocol sensitivity only. It is a mediator of cohort, so it must not be adjusted for when estimating the cohort contrast |
| Current stage (last activity) | Last element of the prefix | **Time-varying** | For terminal rows the last activity *is the outcome*, so it is pure leakage. For open rows it is the state at cutoff, entered at the known time age − gap | Time-varying covariate in a landmark or multi-state model, using stage-at-\(s\) only |
| Repeated review (`n_review` ≥ 2: S03, S07, S13, S29) | Count of `Review` | Time-varying, post-baseline | Counted over the full prefix, so it includes events after 72 h (for S03 the second Review may be after 72 h) | Only as a count up to landmark \(s\) (requires timestamps) |
| Rework (`rework_count`) | Given, and visible in the prefix | Time-varying, post-baseline | Same as above. Rework also requires survival long enough to happen | Time-varying only |
| Escalated | Given | Time-varying, post-baseline. Its timing is unknown except for S10 (≤ 28 h), S11 (26 h) and S25 (31 h), where `Escalate` is the last event of an open case | **Immortal time and reverse causation.** Six of 12 escalated cases have follow-up ≥ 72 h, versus 3 of 20 non-escalated. Among resolved cases, 3/5 escalated resolved late versus 1/8 non-escalated. A case must remain unresolved long enough to be escalated, and escalation is plausibly *triggered by* SLA proximity. Treating it as baseline credits escalated cases with guaranteed unresolved time and makes escalation look harmful. The counter-examples are S04 (29 h) and S18 (12 h), early P1 escalations that resolved fast | Only as a time-dependent state (a multi-state transition) or a landmark covariate "escalated by \(s\)" |
| Escalation timing | Time of `Escalate` | Time-varying | Unavailable for terminal cases | Needs timestamps |
| Inactivity gap (`last_event_gap_h`) | Given | Time-varying ("time since last event") | **Definitional leakage.** It is 0 for every terminal row and positive for every open row, so it perfectly separates censored from terminal rows and must never enter a model fitted on mixed rows | Online dormancy feature for *currently open* cases: gap at time \(s\) |
| Dormant flag (gap ≥ 12 h, assumption A2) | Derived | Time-varying | Threshold is arbitrary. Vary it over 12, 24 and 48 h | Alert trigger and dormancy state |
| Path family (fast-track without Review; review; rework loop; escalation; transfer) | Grouped variants | Must be truncated at \(s\) | The full variant contains the terminal activity, which is outcome leakage | Stage-at-\(s\) grouping only |
| Remaining SLA slack (72 − age) | Derived | Time-varying | None, since it is known at prediction time | Core dynamic covariate |
| Workload or queue length at \(s\) | Not supplied | Time-varying, exogenous | Not available | Needs data (Section 9) |

A PM4Py subtlety: `get_prefixes_from_log(log, k)` produces **length-k** prefixes, not **time-s** prefixes. A 3-event prefix may be reached at 5 h in one case and 60 h in another. Length-based prefixes are valid for sequence structure (for example, the entry path), but not as landmark covariates without timestamps.

**State at 72 h, where it is observable without fabrication.** For open cases older than 72 h, the last event occurred at age − gap:
- S09: `Review` at 61 h.
- S26: `Review` at 32 h.
- S11: `Escalate` at 26 h.
- S25: `Escalate` at 31 h.

All four were therefore in a known stage at 72 h, and **all four were dormant**: no event in the preceding 11–46 h at the 72-hour mark. Hypothesis H1: the post-SLA tail consists mainly of *stalled* cases (waiting after Review or Escalate), not of cases being actively worked slowly. This cannot be tested for S03, S13, S20, S28 and S32, whose intermediate event times are unknown.

**Proposed multi-state representation** (transient states; the absorbing states are Resolved, Rejected, Withdrawn and Transferred; Transferred exits internal observation):

| From state | To state | Observed transitions (count, no times) | Stage hazard to estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submitted | Intake (Screen and/or AutoCheck, either order) | 32 | \(\lambda_{S \to I}(t)\) (near-instant, likely) |
| Intake | Review | 17 via Screen, 2 via AutoCheck | \(\lambda_{I \to Rv}(t \mid \text{priority, policy})\) |
| Intake | Escalated | 5 via Screen, 2 via AutoCheck | \(\lambda_{I \to E}\) (fast-track P1) |
| Intake | Resolved / Rejected / Withdrawn | 2 / 2 / 1 | Direct-disposal hazards |
| Review | Rework | 3 | \(\lambda_{Rv \to Rw}\) |
| Rework | Review / Resolved | 2 / 1 | Loop-return hazard |
| Review | Escalated | 5 | \(\lambda_{Rv \to E}(t)\), which likely increases as SLA slack shrinks |
| Review | Resolved / Rejected / Withdrawn | 6 / 1 / 3 | Cause-specific exits |
| Escalated | Resolved / Transferred / Review | 4 / 3 / 2 | Post-escalation exits. Three cases (S10, S11, S25) are still sitting here |
| Any transient state | Dormant (gap > threshold) | S09, S10, S11, S25, S26 at cutoff | Dormancy entry, with an exit back to active on the next event |
| Any state | "Post-SLA" flag at \(t = 72\) | Deterministic clock overlay, not a stochastic transition | Clock-reset semantics: time since Submit (SLA) versus time in state (sojourn) |

In text form the diagram is:

`Submitted → Intake → {Review ⇄ Rework} → Escalated → {Resolved | Transferred | Review}`

with every transient state also able to move to Rejected or Withdrawn, and a Dormant sub-state attached to Review and Escalated. The SLA outcome is then a functional of the state-occupation probabilities: E3 = P(in any transient state at 72), and E1 = 1 − P(in Resolved at 72). Stage-specific tail risk comes from \(P(\text{in Escalated or Review-dormant at } 72)\).

| SA9 | Cross-bounds (best for one cohort, worst for the other) | A ∈ [0.600, 0.733], B ∈ [0.529, 0.706] | | **B − A ∈ [−0.204, +0.106]: the sign is not identified** |
| SA10 | Cohort policy, ITT versus per-protocol, priority-standardized | ITT standardized: A 0.688, B 0.675. Per-protocol (AutoCheck-first versus not): 0.630 versus 0.736 | AutoCheck-first 0.444 versus 0.357 | Within priority: P1 B worse (0.800 versus 0.667, n = 5/3), P2 B better (0.514 versus 0.619, n = 7/7), P3 equal (0.800). The per-protocol "benefit" is confounded, because AutoCheck-first has no P3 cases |
| SA11 (proposed) | T5 delta-adjusted transfers and IPCW for dormancy | Tipping point: the smallest δ at which the P1 ranking or the B − A sign flips | | Requires modelling. Report δ at the flip |
| SA12 (proposed) | Dormant open cases ≥ 72 h (S11, S25, S26, S09) reclassified as unlogged closure | E1 unchanged | E3 falls if they are treated as disposed | E2 and the tail are driven by whether they are "alive" |

**Qualitative conclusions that could reverse.**
1. **Which priority is worst.** P3 is worst on E1 (0.800), driven by rejections and withdrawals. P1 is worst on E3 (0.469), driven by stalled escalations. Under T3, P1 is best on E1 (0.469). Under SA4, P3 is best. Any "P-level X has an SLA problem" statement depends on the estimand and the transfer rule.
2. **Whether cohort B improved.** The direction depends on the estimand (E1: B slightly better; E3: B worse), on the censoring assumption (SA9 allows either sign), and on the stratum (within P1 B looks worse, within P2 better). The robust conclusion is **no demonstrable policy effect**, not "B is better".
3. **The magnitude of "breach"** ranges from 0.38 (SA4, any disposition counts) to 0.72 (SA7). The spread comes from definitions far more than from sampling noise, although sampling noise is also large (bootstrap interval for E1 [0.52, 0.86]).
4. **The escalation story.** "Escalation causes breaches" in a naive analysis could reverse to "early escalation speeds resolution" (S04, S18) under a time-aware design.

**Conclusions that appear robust** across SA0–SA10:
- A majority of submitted cases are not resolved within 72 h (E1 ≥ 0.56 even at the best-case bound).
- Once a case is 48 h old without a disposition, its chance of resolution within the SLA is small (0.133 at landmark s = 48 h).
- The observed post-72 tail contains dormant cases.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**Match the estimand to the decision.**

| Decision | Estimand | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| External SLA reporting ("% of customers served within 72 h") | E1, with transfer rule T1 (or T3 only if the contract says so), always shown with E4 | It is the customer-relevant probability and does not hide rejections and withdrawals |
| Backlog and capacity planning | E3 and E5 (RMST), plus expected overdue counts | Capacity is consumed by any undisposed case, whatever its eventual outcome |
| Case-level alerting and triage | Landmark \(P(\text{resolved by } 72 \mid \text{active at } s, \text{priority, stage}, \text{gap})\) | Uses only information available at \(s\) |
| Recovery of breached work | E2 (late-resolution incidence, landmark version) | Tells whether post-breach effort pays off |
| Customer-experience and drop-out work | \(F_{wd}\), and whether withdrawal clusters near 72 h (S30 at 70 h) | Withdrawal may be a delay symptom |
| External-partner governance | \(F_{tr}\) plus linked external outcomes | Transfers are concentrated in P1 escalations |

**Alerts.**
- A two-signal rule. (i) Dynamic risk from the landmark model: under the pooled baseline estimate, any case still active at 48 h has only about a 13% chance of being resolved within the SLA, so a 48-hour checkpoint is a natural alert point. (ii) A **dormancy trigger**: no event for more than the threshold (12/24 h, tuned by backtesting) in `Review` or `Escalated`.
- Hypothesis H1 (Section 5) suggests dormancy is the actionable signal. S10 (idle since `Escalate` at 28 h) would be flagged now; S26 would have been flagged around 44–56 h instead of reaching 104 h unnoticed.
- Alerts are *prognostic*. They say "this case is likely to breach", not "intervening will prevent the breach".

**Workload actions.**
- The expected number of currently open, not-yet-breached cases that will fail E1 is \(\sum_i [1 - P(\text{res by } 72 \mid \text{active at age}_i)]\) ≈ 5 − 0.74 ≈ **4.3 of 5** (S31, S15, S10, S29, S24). The S24 estimate should be smoothed, because its conditional probability of 0 is an artefact.
- Staff or re-prioritize by expected overdue load (E3 and RMST by stage), not by the case count.
- Priority should shape the *response*, not the risk definition. P3 non-resolution is mostly rejection and withdrawal, so extra reviewer capacity may not help there, while P1 non-resolution is stalled escalation and transfer.

**SLA governance.**
- Write the estimand into the SLA definition: time origin, horizon, clock rules for reject, withdraw and transfer, and the treatment of open cases.
- Stop publishing resolved-only breach rates.
- Publish E1 with its bound interval (the definition-driven range) and its sampling interval.
- Evaluate policy B with a design that supports causal claims (Section 9), not from these risk scores.

**Do not present risk scores as causal effects.** A high predicted risk for escalated or AutoCheck-first cases reflects selection (who gets escalated, who got the policy) and timing. Stating "escalation increases breach risk by X" or "AutoCheck reduces breach by 10 points" from these data would be wrong. Causal questions need target-trial emulation: define time zero at the eligibility or decision point, clone and censor or use sequential trials for escalation timing, and apply IPCW for transfer.

**Calibration checks.**
- Use censoring-aware calibration at 72 h: IPCW-weighted observed versus predicted \(F_{res}(72)\) within risk deciles (here, realistically, tertiles) and **within each priority × cohort cell**.
- Use the time-dependent Brier score and a time-dependent AUC at 72 h with the competing-risks definition, where cases with competing events count as controls.
- Correct for optimism with a bootstrap or leave-one-out, because n = 32.
- Recalibrate after any policy change (cohort B is itself a drift event).

**Fairness checks by priority and cohort.**
- Priority is a legitimate differentiator of *urgency*, but the model should be equally calibrated within each priority. Check alert rates, false-alert rates (alerted cases resolved within 72 h) and missed breaches (unalerted cases that breached) per priority and cohort.
- A specific risk: if alerts use E1, **P3 cases will be flagged as high risk because they are often rejected or withdrawn**, diverting attention from P1 stalls. Use cause-specific risk (delay risk = \(P(\text{no disposition at } 72)\) or \(F_{res}\) conditional on not being rejected) for delay alerts.
- A second risk: dormant long-running P3 cases (S09, S26) could be systematically deprioritized if routing is by priority alone. Monitor the age distribution of open P3 cases.
- Cohort: rollout non-compliance was aligned with priority (P3 in B did not receive AutoCheck-first), so check that the new policy is not silently excluding P3.

def encode(data, transfer_code=4, open_rule="censor", stale_gap=STALE_GAP):
    """Return (time, cause) without dropping any case.
    open_rule: 'censor' (non-informative), 'worst' (open<72 never resolves by 72),
               'best' (open<72 resolves just after cutoff), 'stale_worst' (only dormant open<72 -> worst)."""
    d = data.copy()

    d.loc[d.status == "transferred", "cause"] = transfer_code
    d["time"] = d["t"]

        d.loc[m, "time"] = TAU + 1e-6       # known still unresolved at 72 h, censored just after
    elif open_rule == "best":
        d.loc[op, "time"] = d.loc[op, "t"] + 1e-6
        d.loc[op, "cause"] = 1
    return d

def aalen_johansen(time, cause, causes=(1, 2, 3, 4)):
    """Nonparametric CIF per cause + all-cause KM survival (distinct times; ties handled by grouping)."""
    tab = pd.DataFrame({"time": time, "cause": cause})
    out, S, cif = [], 1.0, {c: 0.0 for c in causes}
    n = len(tab)
    for t, g in tab.groupby("time", sort=True):
        d_all = int((g.cause > 0).sum())
        for c in causes:
            cif[c] += S * (g.cause == c).sum() / n if n > 0 else 0.0
        S *= (1 - d_all / n) if n > 0 else 1.0
        out.append({"time": t, "n_risk": n, "S_any": S, **{f"F{c}": cif[c] for c in causes}})
        n -= len(g)
    return pd.DataFrame(out)

def at(curve, h, col):
    sub = curve[curve.time <= h]
    return (1.0 if col == "S_any" else 0.0) if sub.empty else float(sub[col].iloc[-1])

def km_single_cause(time, cause, c=1):
    """1 - KM treating every other outcome as censoring: a HYPOTHETICAL estimand (no competing events)."""
    tab = pd.DataFrame({"time": time, "cause": cause}).sort_values("time")
    S, n, rows = 1.0, len(tab), []
    for t, g in tab.groupby("time", sort=True):
        S *= 1 - (g.cause == c).sum() / n
        rows.append({"time": t, "S": S}); n -= len(g)
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

def rmst(curve, h):
    """Restricted mean time without any terminal disposition up to h (area under S_any)."""
    ts = [0.0] + [t for t in curve.time if t < h] + [h]
    ss = [1.0] + [s for t, s in zip(curve.time, curve.S_any) if t < h]
    return float(sum(s * (b - a) for s, a, b in zip(ss, ts[:-1], ts[1:])))

        "n_risk_at_72": int((d.time > TAU).sum()),
        "RMST_72_h": rmst(cv, TAU),

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Limitations of the present analysis.**
- n = 32, with 13 resolutions and 9 cases at risk at 72 h. Every stratified estimate rests on 3–17 cases. The bootstrap interval for E1 is [0.52, 0.86], and the cohort difference interval is [−0.38, +0.29].
- The case-resampling bootstrap ignores the uncertainty in the transfer and censoring assumptions, which (Section 6) is at least as large as the sampling uncertainty.
- Stepwise Aalen-Johansen estimates produce artefacts: for example, a conditional resolution probability of 0 for S24, and tail estimates driven by single cases beyond 96 h.
- Every process-aware covariate except priority, cohort and entry path is time-varying with unknown timing. No stage-specific hazard can be estimated from this extract without fabricating timestamps.
- The "open" label for long-idle cases may itself be a data-quality problem.

**Additional data, in priority order.**
1. **Exact timestamps for every event.** These are needed for time-varying covariates (stage, escalation time, rework, dormancy), for the multi-state model, for valid landmarking with process state, and for testing hypothesis H1 (the tail is stalled cases). This is the single largest gain in inferential power.
2. **External outcomes after transfer** (resolution and time), or at least a sample linked with the external organization. This would replace the T1–T5 assumptions with observation. The P1 conclusions currently swing from 0.47 to 0.75 depending on the transfer rule. It would also allow a model of *why* transfers happen (the transfer hazard), for IPCW.
3. **Submission dates and the cutoff date.** These are needed to handle staggered entry, period effects and calendar-time confounding of cohort, and to check that administrative censoring is non-informative (for example, whether B has systematically shorter potential follow-up).
4. **The contractual SLA clock definition.** Whether the clock stops at reject, withdraw or transfer, whether pauses count (for example, waiting on the customer), and whether the SLA is elapsed or business time. This decides which of E1 and E3 and which transfer rule is the governing estimand. The spread is 0.38 to 0.72.
5. **Time-varying workload and capacity:** queue length and staffing per stage over time, and reviewer and escalation-desk availability. These are confounders for both the policy effect and the dormancy effect, and features for the alert model.
6. **Assignment mechanism and rollout compliance:** which cases were eligible for automated screening, why P3 cases in B did not get AutoCheck-first, and the escalation-policy rules and triggers (for example, whether they are SLA-proximity-triggered). These are needed for an ITT versus per-protocol analysis, for target-trial emulation, and to separate the policy from the period.
7. **Explicit status and reason codes for dormant cases:** waiting on the customer, on hold, informally closed. These separate genuine censoring from unlogged terminal events (S26, S11, S25, S09).
8. **Case attributes available at submission** (type, complexity, channel, customer segment), which are legitimate baseline covariates for risk adjustment and fairness audits beyond priority.
9. **A larger sample or longer window**, so that stratified cumulative incidences, cause-specific models with more than one covariate, and calibration or fairness checks per priority × cohort cell become estimable rather than illustrative.
