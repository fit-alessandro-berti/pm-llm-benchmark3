# 1. Fairness dimensions

- **Outcome parity:** Approval rates range from 58% to 78% (D and A), but raw differences combine case mix, access needs, routing, and possible unequal treatment. Parity is a useful signal, not a fairness verdict.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** The preliminary model still finds lower approval probabilities for B (-5.5 percentage points), C (-2.2 points), and D (-3.1 points) versus A. These are conditional associations; without uncertainty intervals, overlap checks, model diagnostics, and a defensible covariate set, they do not establish discrimination or its absence.
- **Procedural burden and consistency:** Count required steps, requests, handovers, and rework for comparable cases. Legitimate risk screening, language support, and accommodation review can explain different paths. The question is whether similarly situated applicants receive the prescribed steps consistently, and whether a support need creates avoidable extra steps or queue exposure.
- **Timeliness:** Compare end-to-end time and each transition's waiting time, including upper-tail delays. B and C have much longer raw median and 90th-percentile cycle times. Delays at language support, accommodation review, and supervisor review identify plausible procedural burdens even if some added steps are justified.
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** Presence of a Language Support Contact or Accommodation Review does not show that support was timely, effective, understandable, or respectful. Measure whether the support met the applicant's stated need and whether applicants could complete the process with it.
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Appeal rates are higher for B and D; reopen/rework is higher for B and C. These may signal errors, unclear explanations, or a more difficult process, but can also reflect case complexity, awareness of appeal rights, or access to help. Appeal quality and outcomes matter as well as counts.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** B and C depend on small specialist pools, and B has additional handovers in two regions. Limited capacity can create systematic queue exposure. D's greater use of fraud review is plausibly related to measured risk, but differences in queue delay and reviewer treatment still need examination.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

1. **Define comparisons and estimands.** Estimate group differences in approval, cycle time, appeals, rework, number of steps, requests, and handovers. Report both (a) standardized overall group differences and (b) comparisons among cases with similar documented legitimate needs. Treat these as disparity estimates, not causal effects unless stronger identification assumptions are justified.
2. **Match or stratify on pre-process case characteristics.** Start with region, month, household-size band, channel, prior-case count, and documented need for language or accommodation support, alongside risk and documentation measures. Compare within common-support strata and show the unmatched results too. Channel, documentation completeness, and risk score may reflect unequal access or past system decisions, so do not treat adjustment for them as automatically removing unfairness. Avoid adjusting for downstream events such as supervisor review, support contact, or appeal when estimating the total process disparity; those may be mechanisms through which disparity occurs.
3. **Compare paths conditionally.** Estimate variant frequencies within relevant eligible strata: for example, compare applicants requiring language support with similar channel, documentation, and complexity profiles; compare accommodation-request cases with comparable need profiles; compare high-risk cases routed to fraud review. Compare step counts, omissions, loops, and rework as well as named variants. Path-conditioned outcome comparisons are useful diagnostics, but conditioning on a path can hide disparity if the path itself is affected by group membership or access.
4. **Compare resource assignment.** Within region, month, activity, and comparable case strata, compare assignment rates, number of distinct workers, specialist concentration, handovers, queue time, and outcomes by group. Account for repeated cases per worker and regional clustering. The daily-granularity workload data cannot fully separate capacity constraints from assignment choices.
5. **Decompose elapsed time.** Break cycle time into transition intervals and, if available, activity service time versus queue time. Report medians and upper quantiles by group and stratum, then standardize the mix of paths and needs. A decomposition can locate delays, but a group's longer total time should not be attributed to any one step without case-level timing and uncertainty estimates.
6. **Test sensitivity to biased covariates.** Fit nested specifications: minimal demographic/group and time/region adjustment; adjustment for operational complexity; then models adding risk and documentation measures. Compare estimates with and without these variables, test alternative risk-band codings, and inspect overlap and calibration by group. Because risk scores may encode historical disparities and documentation completeness may reflect language barriers, present models including them as conditional diagnostics, not as the sole “fair” estimate.
7. **Check subgroups.** Repeat key analyses by region and channel, and test group-by-region and group-by-channel interactions. Examine intersections such as language need plus in-person channel and accommodation need plus region where sample sizes allow. Use uncertainty intervals and avoid overinterpreting small cells.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The top paths suggest substantial routing differences. V1, the short path without a document request or specialist step, accounts for 42% of A but only 18–22% of B, C, and D. V2, which includes a request for documents, is more common for B (34%) than A (24%), and also common for C (29%). That residual merits review of request thresholds, document accessibility, and whether applicants receive adequate help.

V4 includes language support and a document request. Its share is 24% for B and 1% for A, consistent with the stated support policy and B's language needs. It is not inherently unfair that B follows this path. However, the 3.8-day median wait from Language Support Contact to Eligibility Assessment, dependence on only seven bilingual caseworkers, and added handovers in two regions indicate a potentially avoidable burden. B's adjusted supervisor-review probability is also 8 points higher, and its adjusted cycle time is 2.6 days longer. The provided data do not establish whether those extra reviews are justified in comparable cases.

V5, the accommodation path, is 31% of C and 2% of A, as expected given C's 62% accommodation-request share. The 4.7-day wait from Accommodation Review to Eligibility Assessment, concentration on five specialists, and additional generalist-to-specialist-to-supervisor handovers for C are concerns about access and timeliness, not evidence that the review itself is improper. C's adjusted cycle time remains 4.1 days longer, while its adjusted approval difference is smaller (-2.2 points). This points to a procedural delay signal even where the adjusted outcome estimate is modest.

V3 is much more common for D (28%) than A (9%), consistent with D's higher risk-score share and the stated risk-based fraud-review rule. That is a plausible legitimate routing difference. Still, D's fraud-review wait is 2.8 days versus 2.2 for A, and the score itself requires audit. V6 is more common for D (13% versus 4% for A), consistent with D's higher appeal rate; it does not tell us whether appeals are resolved fairly. The adjusted appeal difference for D is +5.7 points, the largest of the three adjusted appeal differences.

Across groups, the transition waits also point to broad bottlenecks: B waits longer for identity verification (1.4 versus 0.8 days), document follow-up (1.6 versus 0.4), and supervisor-to-draft transition (1.9 versus 1.0). C has a 1.6-day supervisor-to-draft wait. The groups' total median cycle times (13.7 days for B, 15.9 for C, 12.1 for D, 8.1 for A) and 90th percentiles (34.2, 38.8, 29.5, and 21.4 days) reinforce the timeliness concern. These are group summaries, not within-path adjusted effects; the top variants may not cover all cases, and their median durations are not group-specific. The summary alone cannot show how much of the total difference is caused by each route or queue.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

- **Legitimate case complexity:** Higher risk, incomplete records, or unusual eligibility facts can appropriately lead to fraud review, document requests, or supervisor review and can lengthen cases.
- **Rights-preserving steps with insufficient capacity:** Language and accommodation reviews are policy-required supports, but small specialist teams and no dedicated service targets can turn a necessary support into a longer queue.
- **Access barriers that appear as case complexity:** Language barriers or inaccessible forms may make documentation seem incomplete, triggering more requests even when applicants are making comparable efforts.
- **Uneven routing or review thresholds:** Regional officers or supervisors may apply different referral standards, especially if guidance is unclear or workload is high.
- **Resource concentration and handovers:** Specialist dependence can create queueing, repeated explanations, lost context, and inconsistent ownership of a case.
- **Historical bias in risk scoring:** If prior enforcement or outcome patterns shaped the score, D's higher routing and adverse outcomes may partly reproduce historical disparities rather than only measure present case risk.
- **Differences in appeal access or understanding:** Higher appeal rates can mean more adverse decisions, but can also reflect better awareness, advocacy, or support. Rework may likewise reflect process errors or legitimate new information.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The case-level attributes do not capture all relevant need or complexity. Risk bands may be coarse, inconsistently applied, or historically biased. Documentation completeness can be an effect of language access, disability access, or administrative burden; adjusting for it may conceal part of the process disparity. Channel may also reflect access constraints rather than a neutral choice. Household size, prior cases, and region may be measured too broadly to capture the facts that justify an individual route.

The log does not record the content, quality, or timeliness of explanations, whether translation or accommodation was effective, whether applicants had to repeat information, or whether they could use an alternative channel. An appeal flag does not reveal whether an appeal was filed, accessible, upheld, or resolved promptly, nor whether an applicant knew they could appeal. Reopen/rework does not distinguish agency error from applicant updates or legitimate new evidence.

Resource workload is observed only daily, which may miss within-day queues, leave, skill mix, case difficulty, and assignment discretion. Event timestamps alone may not distinguish active service time from waiting time unless activity start/end or lifecycle data are present. Finally, the preliminary adjusted estimates provide no confidence intervals, diagnostics, or evidence of common support. They may be sensitive to model specification and cannot establish intent or causal discrimination.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

- **Add language-support and accommodation-review capacity**, including cross-training, backup coverage, and accessible remote support where appropriate. This can reduce specialist queues and handovers. Trade-offs include training and budget costs, uneven quality during rapid expansion, and potential privacy or accessibility problems with remote options.
- **Set service-level targets for support steps**, with escalation when a target is missed and reporting of both median and tail waits by need and group. Targets make delays visible. Poorly designed targets can encourage rushed or superficial support, clock-stopping, or prioritization of easy cases; pair them with quality checks and guard against those incentives.
- **Redesign routing to reduce handovers**, for example by assigning a continuing case owner or using a supported “warm handoff” to a specialist. This can reduce repeated explanations and lost context. It may concentrate work on fewer staff, reduce independent review, or fail where expertise cannot be safely generalized; retain specialist judgment and audit quality.
- **Improve decision explanations** with plain-language reasons, accessible formats, translated materials, and clear appeal steps. Better explanations can support understanding and informed appeals. They require review for accuracy and may initially increase appeals by making rights more visible; that increase should not automatically be treated as worsening fairness.
- **Monitor adjusted disparities and process burdens** on a regular schedule: outcomes, cycle-time quantiles, support-step waits, requests, variants, handovers, appeals, and rework, broken down by group, need, region, and channel. This can detect drift. Small subgroup counts can create noisy or privacy-sensitive results, so use uncertainty ranges and appropriate safeguards.
- **Audit risk_score_band and documentation_completeness** for group-specific calibration, error rates, missingness, threshold effects, and downstream routing and outcomes. Test alternative specifications and human review. Audits can reveal proxy effects, but changing a score without validating replacement criteria can reduce useful risk detection or shift discretion into less visible decisions.
- **Review referral and document-request criteria across regions**, using comparable case samples and documented reasons. Standardization can reduce arbitrary variation. Over-standardization can ignore legitimate case-specific facts; allow reasoned exceptions and audit them.
- **Track appeal and rework quality**, including reasons, resolution time, reversal rates, and whether the applicant received assistance. This distinguishes access from underlying decision quality. More detailed review adds staff burden and must protect sensitive information.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence is **indeterminate overall, with material procedural-fairness concerns**. Some routing differences—especially risk review for D and support steps for B and C—are consistent with stated policy and different recorded needs. At the same time, B's and C's specialist queues, extra handovers, longer waits, higher rework, and residual adjusted delays warrant investigation. Adjusted outcome disparities also remain, particularly for B, but the preliminary model cannot establish their cause. A fair conclusion requires case-level comparisons within legitimate-need strata, uncertainty estimates, audits of the risk and documentation measures, and direct measures of support and decision quality.
