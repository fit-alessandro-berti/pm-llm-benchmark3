The DFG reports adjacent-event frequencies, not complete case paths or proof that controls were effective. For example, 1,600 emergency-triage transitions to skipping the system authorization check and 1,800 manual-pack transitions to unscanned dispatch are warning signals, not necessarily counts of distinct unauthorized or unsafe orders; some dispatches also follow temporary release. Phase lengths, case mix, incomplete cases and the incidence denominators may differ, so raw case counts and rates are not directly comparable without normalization and cohort definitions. Move-on-log events describe observed work outside the normative model; move-on-model events indicate missing modeled evidence. Neither alone proves an actual violation: validate the underlying records and applicable obligations.

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

Resilience is the ability to protect essential service while maintaining the controls needed for safe, authorized and traceable supply, then return to a demonstrably controlled state. A deviation from the baseline workflow can support that goal: B substitutes a trained manual authorization check; A protects urgent hospitals if eligibility and release are verified; I and J can ration scarce capacity transparently; K surfaces and corrects inventory discrepancies. These are conditional judgments, not proof that each individual case complied.

C is a risky shortcut because familiarity substitutes for authorization. G as practiced, with no equivalent contemporaneous handoff record, sacrifices traceability for speed. E and H become concealed noncompliance if reconstructed entries are presented as original scans or adequate proof without supporting evidence. F's sampling and controlled-product review are useful, but they do not compensate for missing required cold-chain records. Missing documentation is a control or evidence gap; it does not by itself establish that temperature was out of range or authorization was absent.

A, D, E, F, G, H and I are ambiguous in their observed forms: urgency, capacity and the availability of a verified substitute matter. Risk appetite can govern approved service delays, the sampling of supplementary assurance, or which eligible order is served first; it cannot waive a mandatory authorization, custody, temperature or release requirement. D's adequacy decays as the snapshot ages. K and L are necessary recovery work but are not equivalent to real-time compliance. In particular, T4's hospital median recovery and improved conformance do not establish that the backlog or audit tail has been resolved. The low T2 fitting-trace rate signals a major process change, some of it legitimate; the specific missing controls and outcomes determine whether it is acceptable.

# 4. Recovery process model

The following is a proposed controlled-degradation process. Thresholds are initial operating triggers to calibrate through exercises, legal and quality review; required product controls remain mandatory regardless of thresholds.

1. **Detect, contain and activate.** On loss or suspected compromise of the warehouse system, unreliable inventory synchronization or scanning, or inability to verify a required control, incident command isolates affected systems, records the activation time and scope, appoints operations, quality/compliance, security and inventory leads, and selects the last verified snapshot. Activate manual operations only after the quality lead confirms that the approved offline controls, staff, forms and evidence storage are available. If they are not, hold affected releases and escalate urgent clinical needs through approved alternative supply channels. Reassess at least each shift and whenever system integrity or control capacity changes.
2. **Receive and triage.** Assign a unique order and incident identifier, record order time, customer, product, quantity, clinical urgency and required delivery time. Classify product risks and identify recall or quarantine status. Prioritize urgent hospital and other clinically urgent orders, then time-sensitive orders, then standard and bulk work. A pharmacist or designated clinician resolves uncertain urgency; document overrides. Triage does not itself authorize a shipment.
3. **Authorize and allocate.** A trained authorized person verifies customer and product eligibility against a controlled, dated offline source, corroborating by an authenticated call when necessary; record source, time, checker and decision. If verification fails or the source is unreliable, hold and escalate; do not infer authorization from history. Reserve stock against a serialized shared manual ledger tied to the snapshot, including lot, quantity, location and each subsequent movement. Use a single owner or controlled locking and shift reconciliation; if stock or lot identity cannot be established, quarantine or hold until verified.
4. **Pick, pack and release with minimum controls.** Capture order, product, quantity, lot/serial and expiry as applicable, location, timestamps and operator at the point of work; independently verify the pick and shipment against the order. For every order, verify authorization, correct product and quantity, release status and documented handoff. For cold-chain goods, record required storage and transit conditions and review the available temperature evidence and any excursion before release; hold where required evidence or condition cannot be verified. For controlled substances, apply required dual control, secure storage and transport, and record both identities and custody transfers. For recall-sensitive goods, verify current recall/quarantine status and lot identity before release; block affected or unidentifiable lots. Hospital urgency affects priority, not these controls. Quality/pharmacist release must be documented before dispatch; sample reviews are supplemental.
5. **Dispatch and delivery.** When scanning is unavailable, use an approved sequential manual handoff record capturing order, package and relevant lot/serial, quantity, dispatch time, sender, courier identity and custody acknowledgment. Use secure transport and temperature monitoring where required. Obtain a time-stamped recipient acknowledgment and discrepancies; controlled goods require the applicable stronger custody evidence, and cold-chain deliveries require condition evidence. Authenticated phone confirmation can supplement, but not replace, required evidence. Escalate missing or conflicting proof promptly.
6. **Manage the backlog.** Place deferred orders in one visible queue with reason, risk, promised time, age and owner. Re-rank each shift by clinical urgency, product risk, deadline and age, with aging escalation so standard and bulk orders do not starve. Notify customers of delays, alternatives or cancellation; document reasons and review group-level impacts. Orders still pass authorization, allocation and release controls when resumed.
7. **Reconcile and remediate.** On restoration, verify system integrity and synchronize in a controlled sequence. Match each manual order, reservation, lot movement, release, handoff and delivery to system records and physical stock; use unique IDs to prevent duplicates. Route mismatches to an owned exception queue, investigate inventory and patient/customer impact, quarantine affected stock where warranted, and involve quality, compliance and incident response. Preserve original evidence and distinguish contemporaneous records from later entries. If a required event cannot be substantiated, record it as unresolved, assess applicable reporting, recall and corrective-action duties, and escalate; do not mark it compliant merely because a field was filled later. Allow operational or financial close only under an approved policy that preserves a separately visible, owned compliance hold and does not imply audit closure.
8. **Exit and learn.** Return each workflow to normal only after system integrity, inventory synchronization, scanning and control performance are verified with representative cases and quality signs off. Keep incident command and remediation ownership until aged exceptions, backlog and corrective actions meet agreed closure criteria; review unresolved high-risk cases individually. Capture lessons, update offline lists and procedures, and exercise the process.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Define the incident start, phase, product and customer segments, numerator, denominator, event time, data source and owner for every measure. Report both rates and counts, including cases still open and evidence entered late. Compare with a comparable baseline and service targets; account for phase length, demand, case mix and censoring of unfinished cases. Targets below are examples for management to approve, except that required controls must be met in every applicable case.

| Indicator | Leading or lagging | Definition and use |
|---|---|---|
| Safe throughput and capacity | Leading and lagging | Orders received, safely released and dispatched per hour versus arrivals, by segment and product; include staffing and control-check queue capacity. Flag sustained arrivals above safe completion capacity. Count an order as safely released only after its required controls are evidenced. |
| Essential service protection | Leading and lagging | Proportion of clinically urgent orders awaiting triage or approaching their required delivery time; hospital and other urgent fill rate, on-time delivery and median/p90 cycle time by group. Compare hospital's T2 91% fill and 10/21-hour median/p90 with agreed targets and baseline 8/15 hours. |
| Traceability and custody | Leading and lagging | Percentage of shipments with complete contemporaneous order, product, lot/serial where required, quantity and custody/handoff records; aged unmatched movements and dispatch-to-proof delay distribution. Track traceability exception rate and unresolved cases against baseline 0.8%. |
| Control completeness | Leading and lagging | Before release, percentage of applicable orders with documented authorization, release, temperature evidence and controlled-substance dual control; number held for missing evidence. Separately report missing-temperature and missing-dual-control rates against baseline 0.4% and 0.2%, using applicable-product denominators and separately displaying all-order rates if those were used in the supplied summaries. |
| Backlog and remediation age | Leading and lagging | Count and age distribution, including p90 and oldest case, by urgency, customer and product, for both unfulfilled orders and post-close audit exceptions. Track arrivals, completions and overdue items per shift; flag breaches of agreed time limits. |
| Rework and inventory quality | Leading and lagging | Open reconciliation discrepancies, unverified stock and duplicate allocations; correction case rate, time to resolution and physical-to-ledger variance. Compare rework with baseline 3.5% and report subsequent corrections by incident cohort. |
| Fairness and access | Leading and lagging | Fill, delay, cancellation, backlog age and override rates by hospital, pharmacy and bulk, adjusted or stratified by urgency, product availability and order mix. Investigate persistent disparities and monitor complaints; compare pharmacy's 61% T2 fill with hospital's 91% without assuming the groups have identical needs. |
| Recovery and evidence integrity | Leading and lagging | Percentage of systems and representative transactions independently verified; open, overdue and unsubstantiated compliance exceptions, time from operational close to remediation, and confirmed incidents or reportable breaches. Report operational recovery and compliance closure separately. |

Review leading indicators at least each shift during disruption and lagging indicators through remediation closure. Set named owners and escalation thresholds in advance, with immediate escalation for an unverified mandatory control, suspected unsafe product or loss of custody. A falling exception rate alone is insufficient if incomplete or late records are excluded.

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept documented delays, reduced throughput and transparent rationing when needed to protect clinically urgent supply and maintain required controls. It should not accept assumed authorization, unrecorded custody, missing mandatory temperature or dual-control evidence, or the presentation of later reconstruction as proof that a control occurred. Stronger evidence that an approved offline substitute is effective, current and auditable could justify broader degraded-mode throughput; evidence of unsafe product, unreliable inventory, unauthorized shipments or persistent unequal delays would justify tighter holds, alternative supply and a revised allocation policy. Operational recovery should be declared separately from closure of the backlog and compliance tail.
