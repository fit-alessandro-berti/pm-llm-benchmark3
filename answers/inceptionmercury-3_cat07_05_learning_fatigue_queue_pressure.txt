# 1. Competing mechanism table

The table below treats each mechanism as one candidate that may overlap with others. No single row explains the full pattern.

# 2. Integrated explanation

The pattern is a compound effect, not a single story. At the start of most shifts, especially Early and Night, the open backlog is made up of carryover cases that are disproportionately complex and urgent. Senior resources often take these cases. The first 1–5 events therefore have long service times, high complexity, and a high share of high-priority work. Their error rates are not the highest because they are handled by experienced people with low queue pressure and because complex work is done more carefully when there is time.

As the shift proceeds, the mixture shifts from complex carryover cases to more ordinary cases. Resources also settle into their work patterns and begin to batch similar document-review or classification tasks. This produces a genuine-looking decline in average service time across sequence bins 6–50. It is partly learning/warm-up and partly case-mix change and partly batching.

Later in the shift, the picture changes because demand builds or inherited queues remain high. This is clearest on the Day and Late shifts. As WIP rises above roughly 70, workers respond by spending less time per case. They skip extra checks, rely more on automated classifications, and postpone quality inspections. Service time falls, but errors and rework rise. The WIP/complexity table shows this speed-quality tradeoff: high WIP reduces service time for both complexity bands but increases error rates. The effect is much worse for high-complexity cases, showing that pressure does not affect all cases equally.

This explains why service time can fall while errors rise only under certain conditions. Service time is sensitive to complexity, batching, and queue pressure: easier cases and stronger pressure both make the average time lower. Error rate is sensitive to the interaction of pressure with complexity, experience, and time-on-shift. Under low WIP, workers can maintain the same quality later in the shift. Under high WIP, they cut corners, and the corners they cut are often the ones that prevent errors.

Fatigue is often added to the explanation because errors rise in the final hours. But the data do not support “fatigue only.” The Early shift has relatively low error rates in its last two hours. Night staff are slower but not the most error-prone, despite working at an unfavorable circadian time. The rise in errors appears when queue pressure is also high. Fatigue may amplify the effect of pressure, but it is not the primary cause of late-shift problems.

Circadian and time-of-day effects also matter. Night work involves fewer cases but more urgent exceptions, so slower service reflects case mix and possibly lower alertness. Day-shift late peaks and Late-shift inherited backlogs create high-pressure conditions at very different clock times. Any analysis must separate elapsed-shift time from clock hour.

Finally, batching and workarounds create artificial patterns. When Document Review is batched, service time is very short for many consecutive events, making the resource look fast. When Quality Check is deferred to the last 45 minutes, the end of the shift has a burst of corrections. This can make errors look like late-shift worker failures when the root cause is earlier work plus delayed inspection.

The integrated explanation is therefore: early slow work reflects complex carryover cases handled by experienced staff; middle-shift speed-up reflects easier cases, warm-up, and batching; late-shift speed-up reflects queue pressure and workarounds; late-shift errors reflect the combination of pressure, complexity, experience, and, to a lesser degree, time-on-shift and circadian timing.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The goal is to separate the mechanisms with event-log data, not to rely on aggregated tables.

### Data preparation and feature enrichment

Use PM4Py to standardize the event log and add service time, waiting time, and temporal features.

```python
import pm4py
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.formula.api import mixedlm
from statsmodels.genmod.generalized_estimating_equations import GEE
from statsmodels.genmod.families import Binomial

# Assume event_log is loaded as a pandas DataFrame.
df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    event_log,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

df = pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp"
)

df = pm4py.extract_temporal_features_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

# Define analysis bands.
df["seq_bin"] = pd.cut(
    df["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, np.inf],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"]
)

df["wip_band"] = pd.cut(
    df["wip_at_start"],
    bins=[-np.inf, 30, 70, np.inf],
    labels=["low", "medium", "high"]
)

df["complexity_band"] = np.where(df["case_complexity_score"] >= 0.6, "high", "low")
df["clock_hour"] = df["time:timestamp"].dt.hour
```

### Within-resource comparisons

Because resources differ in experience and in the cases they receive, always compare resources on similar cases.

```python
# Descriptive table: resource x sequence bin x WIP band x complexity band.
resource_table = (
    df.groupby(
        ["org:resource", "seq_bin", "wip_band", "complexity_band"],
        observed=True
    )
    .agg(
        cases=("case:concept:name", "count"),
        service_time=("service_time", "mean"),
        error_rate=("error_flag", "mean"),
        rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean"),
        mean_queue=("queue_length_at_start", "mean")
    )
)
```

Fit multilevel models with random intercepts for resources.

```python
# Service-time model.
model_time = mixedlm(
    "service_time ~ C(seq_bin) + C(wip_band) + C(complexity_band) + "
    "C(priority) + C(shift_id) + resource_experience_months + C(activity_family)",
    df,
    groups=df["org:resource"]
).fit()

# Error model using GEE with resource clusters.
model_error = GEE.from_formula(
    "error_flag ~ C(seq_bin) + C(wip_band) + C(complexity_band) + "
    "C(priority) + C(shift_id) + resource_experience_months + C(activity_family)",
    groups=df["org:resource"],
    data=df,
    family=Binomial()
).fit()
```

The key coefficient is `C(seq_bin)` after controlling for WIP, complexity, priority, shift, activity, and experience. If it remains significant, there is a within-shift position effect; if it disappears, the apparent position effect is explained by the other variables.

### Within-shift position effects and time-of-day controls

Compare shifts that overlap at the same clock hour, e.g., Early and Day both work 09:00–14:00.

```python
overlap = df[df["clock_hour"].between(9, 13)]
overlap_summary = pd.crosstab(
    overlap["shift_id"],
    overlap["seq_bin"],
    values=overlap["error_flag"],
    aggfunc="mean"
)
```

Model both `minutes_since_shift_start` and `clock_hour` together. If only elapsed time matters, the clock-hour term will be less important; if only clock time matters, the elapsed-time term will shrink.

### WIP/queue stratification and complexity adjustment

Compute service time and error rate in every `wip_band x complexity_band` cell, separately by resource group. Also use the continuous complexity score.

```python
for wip in ["low", "medium", "high"]:
    subset = df[df["wip_band"] == wip]
    print(subset.groupby(["complexity_band", "org:resource"], observed=True)
          .agg(service_time=("service_time", "mean"),
               error_rate=("error_flag", "mean"),
               rework_rate=("rework_flag", "mean")))
```

For a regression discontinuity around the WIP threshold:

```python
# Select events near the WIP=70 threshold.
near_threshold = df[(df["wip_at_start"] >= 60) & (df["wip_at_start"] <= 80)]
near_threshold["high_wip"] = (near_threshold["wip_at_start"] >= 70).astype(int)
```

This can help test whether crossing WIP=70 causes a jump in error rate beyond what a smooth trend would predict.

### Batching detection

Use PM4Py’s batch discovery and also create a simple inter-event gap flag.

```python
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df)

# PM4Py batch discovery.
batches = pm4py.discover_batches(
    log,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    resource_key="org:resource"
)

# Manual gap-based batching flag.
df = df.sort_values(["org:resource", "time:timestamp"])
df["prev_end_same_resource"] = df.groupby("org:resource")["time:timestamp"].shift(1)
df["gap_same_resource_min"] = (
    df["time:timestamp"] - df["prev_end_same_resource"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60
df["same_activity_as_prev"] = (
    df.groupby("org:resource")["concept:name"].shift(1) == df["concept:name"]
)
df["batch_candidate"] = (df["gap_same_resource_min"] <= 1.0) & df["same_activity_as_prev"]
```

Then compare batched and unbatched events after matching activity, complexity, WIP, and resource experience.

### Automation interaction

For Bot_Risk events, merge downstream human corrections on the same case.

```python
risk_events = df[df["concept:name"] == "Risk Classification"]
corrections = df[df["concept:name"] == "Rework Correction"]

merged = pd.merge(
    corrections,
    risk_events[["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp", "queue_length_at_start"]],
    on="case:concept:name",
    suffixes=("_corr", "_risk")
)
merged = merged[merged["time:timestamp_corr"] > merged["time:timestamp_risk"]]
```

Then calculate correction rates by `queue_length_at_start` at the time of the automated classification.

### Rework and downstream error analysis

Use `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` to see which activities generate rework, and trace the time lag from original event to correction.

```python
rework_per_activity = pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity(log)
```

Also compare paths with and without early Quality Check:

```python
pm4py.filter_paths_performance(
    log,
    paths=[
        ["Initial Triage", "Document Review", "Risk Classification"],
        ["Initial Triage", "Document Review", "Risk Classification", "Quality Check"]
    ]
)
```

### Process-mining overview

Visualize the event distribution and dotted chart, then discover performance DFGs for low-WIP and high-WIP logs.

```python
pm4py.view_events_distribution_graph(log)
pm4py.view_dotted_chart(log)

low_wip_log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(df[df["wip_at_start"] < 30])

low_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(low_wip_log)
```

`pm4py.cluster_log` can be used to group resources or cases with similar behavioral profiles, but it should be exploratory, not the basis for punishment.

# 4. Intervention options

| Intervention | Expected benefit | Risk | How to monitor with event-log indicators |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Strictly cap shift length** | Limits exposure to late-shift fatigue. If time-on-task contributes to late errors, this should reduce error/rework near shift end. | Handovers increase, work may be left in the middle of high-WIP periods, and queue pressure may simply be transferred to the next shift. It does not address pressure or case mix. | Interrupted time-series of error and rework rates by clock hour and elapsed shift time. Track open-case age, handover waiting, and queue length at shift boundaries. |
| **2. Move complex cases away from first hour** | Avoids loading the start of the shift with the hardest cases, potentially allowing warm-up before complex work. | Complex carryover cases are often urgent and high-priority. Delaying them may harm customers and increase WIP later. Seniors currently appear to absorb these cases early; removing them could expose juniors to complex work at a different time. | Track delay-to-start for high-complexity/high-priority cases. Compare service time, error rate, and rework by complexity band and clock hour. Watch case survival and completion time. |
| **3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70** | Flags a known risk zone. It can trigger extra support, temporary routing changes, or a deliberate slowdown before errors rise. | If the alert is just a notification, it may pressure workers to work faster, making quality worse. Alert fatigue is possible if the threshold is too sensitive. | Use the WIP threshold as an intervention point. Compare error/rework/service time before and after the alert. Monitor the number of alerts and whether an action protocol is attached. |
| **4. Force random assignment of cases** | Eliminates selection bias and makes resource comparisons fair. It would provide clean data on case-mix effects. | Randomization ignores resource expertise, case urgency, and customer needs. Complex cases could be sent to juniors or night staff, causing harm. It would also break purposeful batching and increase waiting time. | If implemented as a pilot, compare error rates, service times, and workload fairness across randomized arms. Monitor adverse outcomes for high-priority cases. |
| **5. Introduce mandatory micro-breaks in final two hours** | Restores attention if fatigue contributes to late errors. May improve well-being and reduce end-of-shift lapses. | Removing staff from the queue during peak demand increases WIP and waiting time. If late errors are mainly caused by queue pressure, breaks may make things worse. | Run a controlled pilot. Compare final-two-hour error/rework rates, service time, waiting time, and queue length between break and no-break shifts. |
| **6. Defer low-priority quality checks to the next shift** | Balances end-of-shift QC bursts, allows fresh staff to check work, and may improve detection of errors. | Delayed QC means errors are discovered later. Low-priority cases can still have serious defects. This may shift the timing of rework without reducing it. Compliance or regulatory QC should not be deferred. | Monitor QC backlog, time from event to QC, time from QC to correction, rework rate, and case cycle time. Compare priority groups separately. |
| **7. Add an extra senior reviewer during day-shift demand peaks** | Adds capacity and expertise when WIP and queue length rise. This directly targets the high-pressure, high-error window. | Cost and scheduling complexity. A senior reviewer may become a bottleneck if they are not integrated into the flow. It may also change case selection. | Measure WIP/queue distribution, service time, error rate, rework, and time-to-close during the peak. Use a DFG to see whether Quality Check or Reviewer paths become bottlenecks. |
| **8. Penalize workers with higher late-shift error rates** | No expected benefit. Raw late-shift error rates are not a fair performance measure. | Punitive action would blame workers for queue pressure, case mix, automation interactions, and circadian effects they do not control. It would encourage underreporting, batching to hide errors, gaming, fear, and worse decisions. It is not a process-improvement tool. | Do not implement. If leadership insists on an accountability metric, use a risk-adjusted residual after controlling for WIP, complexity, activity, shift, and resource experience. Even then, use it for coaching and support, not punishment. |

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions from this event log can easily be unfair or misleading.

- **Selection bias.** Resources are not assigned cases randomly. Seniors take complex cases early, juniors may receive simpler cases, and night staff receive urgent exceptions. Comparing raw error rates across resources compares different work, not different skill. Any worker-level analysis must control for complexity, priority, activity, shift, WIP, and case timing.

- **Measurement error.** `error_flag` reflects detected errors, not all errors. If Quality Check is delayed to the end of the shift, errors in earlier work are detected later and may be attributed to the wrong time period or the wrong worker. Service time recorded in the event log may include waiting, system latency, batching, or multi-tasking. The `start_timestamp` may reflect when the system says work started, not when cognitive work began.

- **Queue pressure is created by management and demand, not only by worker behavior.** High WIP and long queues are the result of arrival rates, staffing levels, shift design, and backlog carryover. Workers respond rationally by working faster. If that leads to errors, the cause is partly systemic. Blaming individuals for a speed-quality tradeoff created by management ignores the real lever.

- **Automation quality can be misattributed to humans.** Bot_Risk performs Risk Classification instantly. If the bot makes a classification that a human later corrects, the correction may be logged as a human rework event. The error may be a bot error, not a human error. Under high queue pressure, humans may trust or override the bot differently, changing the apparent error rate.

- **Punitive use of error rates is harmful.** Error rates are rare events with high variance. Small samples, different case mixes, and shift assignments make rankings unstable. Punishing workers for late-shift errors creates a climate of fear, underreporting, defensive behavior, and gaming. It also disproportionately harms juniors, night staff, and workers assigned to hard cases. The goal should be to improve the system, not to assign blame.

- **Fairness in shift schedules.** Night and late shifts are staffed by particular people. Circadian effects, fewer cases, and more urgent exceptions are outside individual control. Operating policies should account for these factors rather than penalizing the people who work at difficult times.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

The dashboard should combine event-log KPIs with process-mining views. It should be filterable by shift, resource, activity, complexity band, WIP band, and time window.

| Indicator | Definition / calculation | Suggested view | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Service time distribution** | `service_time` from `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`. Show median, P85, and mean by activity, resource, complexity band, WIP band, and sequence bin. | Boxplots, heatmap, performance DFG | Shows speed-up, batching, and pressure effects. |
| **Waiting time** | Waiting time from case arrival or previous event to event start, plus total case waiting time. Use `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_arrival_finish_rate`. | Distribution by shift and activity, bottleneck DFG | Captures queue pressure, backlog, and handoff delay. |
| **WIP and queue length** | `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start`. Percentage of events with WIP > 70. Mean and percentiles by hour and shift. | Time-series line chart, control chart | The main pressure indicator. |
| **Error rate** | Proportion of `error_flag = 1`. Also risk-adjusted error rate after controlling for complexity, WIP, activity, and shift. | Control chart by shift/hour, table by resource and WIP band | Primary quality outcome. |
| **Rework rate** | Proportion of `rework_flag = 1`. Use `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` to identify activities that lead to rework. | Stacked bar by activity, time-to-rework histogram | Downstream quality and error costs. |
| **Complexity mix** | Share of high-complexity cases and mean `case_complexity_score` by shift, hour, sequence position, and resource. | Stacked area chart, line chart | Essential for interpreting service time and error changes. |
| **Batching indicators** | Batch size, number of batches, and fraction of events in batches. Use `pm4py.discover_batches` and gap-based batch flags. | Table by resource/activity, timeline/dotted chart | Reveals workarounds and artificial speed. |
| **Handover / queue ownership changes** | Number of distinct resources per case, number of ownership changes, and waiting time at handover. Dotted chart colored by resource. | DFG with resource-change frequency, handover histogram | Shows fragmentation, coordination cost, and shift boundary effects. |
| **Worker-load fairness** | Events per resource, total service minutes per resource, complexity-weighted load, and Gini coefficient across workers per shift. | Bar chart, table | Detects overloaded workers and unfair assignment patterns. |
| **Backlog at shift boundaries** | Number of open cases at shift end, mean age of open cases, and complexity share of carryover cases. | Shift-boundary table, queue-at-shift-start chart | Explains why the next shift starts with complex, slow cases. |
| **Shift vs clock-hour comparison** | Error rate and service time by `clock_hour` and `minutes_since_shift_start`, including overlap analysis for Early vs Day shifts. | Scatterplot or heatmap | Separates time-on-shift from circadian/time-of-day effects. |

The dashboard should always be viewed as a system monitoring tool, not a worker ranking tool. It should be used to identify bottlenecks, unfair assignments, automation failures, and pressure points before drawing conclusions about individual performance.
