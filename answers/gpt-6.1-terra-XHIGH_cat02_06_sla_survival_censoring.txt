# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

Set time zero to `Submit` and measure elapsed hours. A resolution at or before 72 hours meets the deadline; at exactly 72 it is on time. Follow each case until its first internal terminal event or the extract cutoff. The table gives terminal times and open-case ages, but not intermediate event times. Treat the supplied cases as the analysis cohort; their selection into the extract is not specified.

Two useful 72-hour estimands are:

1. **Internal SLA failure**, `1 − F_R(72)`, where `F_R(72)` is the cumulative incidence of internal `Resolve` by 72 before `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`. Thus any of those three exits without a preceding internal resolution counts as a failure of this particular internal-success definition. This is useful for governing the internally observed resolution process. Open cases younger than 72 are right-censored; cases open at 72 are already known failures of the deadline, even though their eventual outcomes are censored. An open case at exactly 72 would be a known failure if its observation establishes that no resolution occurred at the deadline.
2. **Internal workload at the deadline**, `P(T_terminal > 72)`, where the first terminal disposition is any of `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`. This is useful for staffing the internal backlog at the deadline. It is not the same as SLA failure: cases withdrawn or transferred before the deadline have left the internal workload but have not resolved internally.

A different end-to-end SLA could credit external resolution after transfer. Its numerator and denominator must be agreed explicitly: transfer at 68 hours is neither a known end-to-end success nor a known end-to-end breach without external follow-up. If the desired quantity is resolution *after* 72, define a finite follow-up horizon `tau > 72` and estimate `F_R(tau) − F_R(72)`; it is distinct from all deadline failures because it excludes cases that never resolve and depends on follow-up beyond 72. No defensible long-run probability can be identified here without further assumptions and follow-up.

| Example | Internal SLA failure by 72 | No internal terminal disposition at 72 |
|---|---|---|
| S03, resolved at 76 | Known failure: resolution occurred after the deadline. It is still in the risk set for resolution at 72. | Known yes: its terminal event occurs after 72. |
| S09, open at age 92 | Known failure by 72; eventual resolution time is right-censored at 92. | Known yes at 72; right-censored at 92 for later follow-up. |
| S06, withdrawn at 27 | Known failure under the internal-success definition; withdrawal is a competing event at 27. | Known no: terminal at 27. |
| S08, transferred at 68 | Known failure under the internal-success definition; transfer is a competing event at 68. External success by 72 is unknown. | Known no: internal terminal disposition at 68. |

For the observed sample, internal on-time resolution is known for 4/15 in A and 5/17 in B. Without assumptions about the eventual outcomes of the open cases younger than 72, its sample fractions are bounded by 4/15–6/15 in A and 5/17–8/17 in B. These are partial-identification bounds for these cases, not confidence intervals or population estimates. For the internal workload estimand the corresponding bounds are 4/15–6/15 and 5/17–8/17. Different estimands happen to have the same bounds here; the cases contributing to them differ. The survival estimates below make assumptions to handle incomplete follow-up.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

For cumulative incidence, use `0` for censoring, `1` for internal resolution, `2` for withdrawal, `3` for rejection, and `4` for transfer. The event indicators below are at the observed end of follow-up, not baseline features. An open case observed beyond 72 contributes its known state at 72; administrative censoring at its later age must not erase that information.

| Observed status | Primary indicators and competing-risk code | Primary observation rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---|---|---|
| resolved | `R=1`, other terminal indicators 0; code 1 | Resolution event at recorded duration, including if after 72. | For a terminal-disposition analysis, count as a terminal event. |
| withdrawn | `W=1`, `R=0`; code 2 | Competing event at duration. | Censor at withdrawal for a model of a hypothetical resolution process without withdrawal, requiring strong assumptions; or group withdrawal with rejection as a single competing cause. |
| rejected | `J=1`, `R=0`; code 3 | Competing event at duration. | Censor for an analogous hypothetical net-risk analysis, or group with withdrawal. |
| transferred | `X=1`, `R=0`; code 4 | Competing internal exit at duration. | (i) Censor at transfer to estimate a hypothetical net resolution risk under an independent-censoring assumption; (ii) credit immediate resolution at transfer as an explicitly optimistic proxy; (iii) link external outcomes and retain transfer as a state, censoring only when end-to-end observation actually ends. Transfers after 72, such as S32, were already unresolved at 72 regardless of later external success. |
| open | All terminal indicators 0; code 0 | Right-censor at age at cutoff. Younger than 72 means deadline outcome unknown; age above 72 establishes no internal terminal event by 72. | Investigate whether continued observation or linkage changes follow-up, and use censoring adjustments or scenarios if cutoff censoring is informative. |

For the workload estimand, all four observed terminal statuses are events and only open cases are censored. For end-to-end success, treating transfer as ordinary independent censoring is a substantive, generally unverified assumption: external outcomes and selection for transfer may be related to case difficulty. Crediting every transfer immediately is a deliberately optimistic scenario, not an observation or an estimate of actual external resolution.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Among resolved cases alone, 4/6 in A and 5/7 in B resolved by 72. These fractions condition on a future outcome and omit both exits without resolution and open cases. They are not estimates of the submitted-case SLA failure probability. S03 and S13 resolved late; S09 and S11 were already unresolved after 72 but disappear from a resolved-only calculation. Including only completed cases also favors early exits because long-running cases are less likely to have completed by the freeze, particularly when cases start at different calendar times.

S10 and S15 are open at 47 and 35 hours: their deadline outcomes are unknown, so calling them breaches is premature. S09 and S11 are open at 92 and 81 hours, so they are already known breaches even though their eventual times and outcomes remain unknown. The same distinction applies to S24 at 67 versus S25 at 79. Survival methods retain all of them in the appropriate risk sets.

Withdrawal and rejection, such as S06 and S05, preclude the observed internal `Resolve`; censoring them as if their subsequent internal resolution experience were representative changes the question and can overstate the observed probability of resolution. S08 and S21 may have very different external outcomes from cases remaining inside; censoring them can be informative. Long inactivity gaps, such as S11's 55 hours and S26's 72 hours, might indicate stalled work, missing events, or delayed recording; none of those explanations is established by the table. Cutoff censoring may also depend on calendar start time, cohort, and case mix.

Cohorts differ in size and mix: A has 15 cases, including 3 P1, while B has 17, including 5 P1. B followed a policy change, but assignment was not random and compliance was incomplete. Age distributions, unmeasured difficulty, calendar conditions, and recording practices can also differ. An apparent cohort contrast is descriptive, not proof of a policy effect.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

Use **Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence** as the primary analysis for internal resolution by 72, with withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as competing events and open cases censored at cutoff. Report `F_R(72)` and `1 − F_R(72)`, the competing-cause incidences, numbers at risk, and uncertainty. The complement includes exits without resolution and cases still active. Administrative censoring must be independent of outcome conditional on the variables or strata used; inspect start dates and follow-up patterns when available. The empirical bounds above provide an assumption-light companion. The sample is too small for reliable priority-by-cohort estimates or a complex adjusted model; show counts and cautious intervals, and pool only when scientifically defensible.

For the secondary workload question, use **Kaplan–Meier** with any internal terminal disposition as the event and open cases censored. Its survival at 72 estimates the probability of remaining internally active, subject to independent censoring and sufficient follow-up. Do not use `1 − KM` for resolution while censoring competing events as if it were the real-world probability of resolution; it estimates a different, hypothetical net quantity under stronger assumptions.

Additional tools answer different questions:

- **Cause-specific hazards** model the instantaneous rate of a particular event among cases still free of every terminal event. They help investigate stage or priority associations, but their coefficients alone do not give absolute resolution probabilities. Combine all cause-specific hazards to obtain cumulative incidences.
- **Fine–Gray subdistribution hazards** relate covariates to a cumulative incidence using a different risk-set construction. Their coefficients are not ordinary event rates or causal effects. Sparse events and competing outcomes make either regression approach unstable here; use descriptive curves first.
- **Restricted mean time** is the area under a survival curve through a prespecified horizon, such as 72 hours. With terminal survival it is expected internal active hours up to 72; with resolution-free survival it is expected hours without resolution, including time after competing exits if that state is defined to persist. State the curve and whether it is an observed-world or hypothetical net quantity. It summarizes burden but does not replace a deadline probability.
- **Landmarking** predicts from a fixed elapsed age, such as 24 or 48 hours, using only cases still active and observable then and only information recorded by then. Estimate subsequent absolute event probabilities up to the original 72-hour deadline, with censoring and competing exits handled explicitly. The current table lacks intermediate timestamps, so it cannot actually reconstruct those landmark features.
- A **multi-state model** can represent changing process stages and terminal causes. It can estimate stage occupancy and transitions if timestamped events and adequate data become available. Non-Markov behavior, repeat visits, and event recording practices may require history or duration-in-state terms.

Avoid unwarranted precision: report the observed counts, risk sets, estimator and censoring assumptions. Use appropriately constructed confidence intervals or case-level bootstrap intervals where support permits, but flag the extreme instability of tiny strata. Do not extrapolate a curve beyond observed support.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

`cohort` and `priority` are candidate baseline features only if known at Submit; verify when priority is assigned. Cohort is a proxy for calendar and exposure conditions, not a randomized treatment. If priority can change, record its history. With timestamped event histories, build current stage, time in stage, repeated-review count, rework count so far, escalation status and time since escalation, path so far, and time since the last event as time-varying covariates. For example, S07 has a repeated review and rework; S13 also escalates. S16 and S17 exhibit different ordering of AutoCheck and Screen. These are observed full-prefix descriptions, not valid Submit-time predictors.

`escalated`, `rework_count`, final path, and the terminal status are determined during or after the case. Treating them as baseline predictors uses future information. In particular, cases classified as escalated must remain under observation long enough to reach escalation, producing immortal-time bias; escalation may also be triggered by emerging difficulty and be a mediator of policy effects. For prediction use properly timed covariates or landmarks. For causal questions define a time-specific intervention and handle time-varying confounding and competing outcomes; neither an escalation coefficient nor a cohort coefficient is a causal effect by itself.

`last_event_gap_h` is defined at terminal event or cutoff. For an open case it is only an as-of-cutoff inactivity measurement; for terminal cases its supplied value is zero. Using that end-of-observation field as a baseline or earlier-landmark feature leaks the observation endpoint. Recalculate inactivity at each prediction time from timestamped events, distinguish logging delay from true inactivity, and use only cases still observable at that time.

One possible event-driven state representation, with repeat visits permitted, is:

`Submitted → Screening ↔ Review ↔ Rework → Review; Submitted/Screening/Review/Rework → Escalated → Review`

`Any active state → Resolve | Withdraw | Reject | Transfer (external follow-up unknown)`

AutoCheck can occur before or after Screen, so represent it as an additional state with transitions supported by observed ordering, or as a time-varying flag rather than forcing a single sequence. The diagram is a proposed model, not a claim that every arrow occurred or that the states are mutually exclusive in the source system. More detailed states may be required if escalation and review overlap. Open is observation at cutoff, not a process state or terminal event. Stage-specific hazards and landmark covariates cannot be estimated from these prefixes alone because intermediate timestamps are absent.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

1. **Primary internal estimand:** transfer, withdrawal, and rejection are competing internal exits. Estimate resolution cumulative incidence and internal terminal survival at 72. Report partial bounds as well as model-based estimates.
2. **Transfer scenarios:** censor transfer when following a hypothetical resolution process, explicitly assuming conditional independent censoring and adequate support; compare with the primary competing-event analysis. Separately credit each transfer as an immediate resolution as an optimistic proxy. This changes the event definition, so it is not an unbiased end-to-end estimate. When linked external outcomes are available, estimate the actual end-to-end endpoint instead. Transfer assumptions can reverse cohort comparisons, especially because B has two transfers and A one, including B's early S21.
3. **Withdrawal/rejection and other censoring:** compare the primary observed-world cumulative incidence with a hypothetical net analysis censoring withdrawal and rejection, explaining that the difference mixes estimand changes with assumptions. Assess administrative censoring by cohort, priority, calendar start, workload, and as-of-time inactivity when available. Use inverse-probability-of-censoring weights only with measured predictors, positivity, and a defensible censoring model; inspect weights and support. Vary unobserved risk among censored cases in a tipping-point or multiple-imputation analysis, and display the sample bounds. Long gaps may reflect outcome-dependent observation, so neither weighting nor imputation removes uncertainty automatically.
4. **Policy comparison:** present unadjusted and priority-stratified cohort results, then, with a larger dataset, standardize to a common baseline case mix and examine calendar-time trends, actual rollout and compliance, and alternative exposure definitions. Consider an interrupted-time or other quasi-experimental design only if its assumptions and pre-policy trends are credible. The direction of an adjusted cohort contrast, and any proposed policy effect, could reverse with case mix, calendar trends, noncompliance, or unmeasured confounding.

Differences between scenarios do not establish which assumption is correct. In particular, treating competing exits as censoring changes the target rather than simply correcting the original estimate.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Use the internal cumulative incidence for SLA reporting and the terminal-survival curve for staffing. For a case active at a landmark, use an appropriately validated model to estimate its probability of failing to resolve by the original deadline, conditional on its state and information available at that landmark. Combine this with time remaining, predicted workload, and action capacity to set alert thresholds; a high-risk alert can trigger review of stalled cases or allocation of staff. Track whether alerts are acted on and whether intervention changes subsequent outcomes. Do not interpret a predicted risk, or an association with escalation, as the effect of intervening.

Publish the endpoint, treatment of transfer and other exits, cutoff, denominator, known breaches, unresolved young cases, and uncertainty alongside any SLA figure. Monitor transfers separately so that moving work outside the system cannot silently improve a reported internal backlog measure. Link external outcomes if the governing SLA is end-to-end.

Validate predictions on later cases using censoring-aware calibration at the relevant horizon, including observed versus predicted risks and, where sample size permits, calibration curves and time-dependent Brier scores. Check calibration, alert rates, false-alert and missed-breach rates by priority and cohort, with uncertainty and adequate sample sizes. Distinguish genuine group differences in case mix from inconsistent performance, review access to interventions, and audit missingness and transfer patterns. The 32 cases here are insufficient for a credible fairness audit or deployment-grade model.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

The following executable Python uses pandas and PM4Py. It implements Aalen–Johansen and terminal-event Kaplan–Meier directly, so no survival library is required. Install `pandas` and `pm4py` in the execution environment. The early-transfer-as-resolution run is a proxy scenario, not an observed resolution estimate. Risk-set counts at 72 are just before 72; censoring at exactly 72 is treated after events at the same time.

```python
from io import StringIO
import pandas as pd

raw = """case|cohort|priority|duration_or_age_h|observed_status|prefix|escalated|rework_count|last_event_gap_h
S01|A|P2|18|resolved|Submit>Screen>AutoCheck>Resolve|no|0|0

S03|A|P2|76|resolved|Submit>Screen>Review>Escalate>Review>Resolve|yes|0|0
S04|A|P1|29|resolved|Submit>Screen>Escalate>Resolve|yes|0|0
S05|A|P3|9|rejected|Submit>Screen>Reject|no|0|0
S06|A|P2|27|withdrawn|Submit>Screen>Review>Withdraw|no|0|0
S07|A|P2|54|resolved|Submit>Screen>Review>Rework>Review>Resolve|no|1|0
S08|A|P1|68|transferred|Submit>Screen>Review>Escalate>Transfer|yes|0|0

S23|B|P3|11|rejected|Submit>Screen>Reject|no|0|0
S24|B|P2|67|open|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Review|no|0|4
S25|B|P1|79|open|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Escalate|yes|0|48

S30|B|P3|70|withdrawn|Submit>Screen>Review>Withdraw|no|0|0
S31|B|P2|23|open|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen|no|0|3
S32|B|P1|96|transferred|Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Escalate>Transfer|yes|0|0
"""
df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(raw), sep="|")
horizon = 72

# 0 = censoring; 1 = resolution; 2, 3, 4 = competing causes.
def encode(data, transfer="compete", censor_other_exits=False):
    codes = {"open": 0, "resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2,
             "rejected": 3, "transferred": 4}
    if transfer == "censor":
        codes["transferred"] = 0
    elif transfer == "credit_immediately":
        codes["transferred"] = 1
    elif transfer != "compete":
        raise ValueError("Unknown transfer scenario")
    if censor_other_exits:
        codes["withdrawn"] = codes["rejected"] = 0
    out = data.copy()
    out["time"] = out["duration_or_age_h"]
    out["code"] = out["observed_status"].map(codes)
    return out

# AJ increments: S(t-) * d_k(t) / Y(t). Censorings at t are
# included in Y(t), then removed along with events after that time.
def aj_at(data, tau):
    survival = 1.0
    cif = {k: 0.0 for k in (1, 2, 3, 4)}
    for t in sorted(data.loc[data.time <= tau, "time"].unique()):
        at_risk = int((data.time >= t).sum())
        at_t = data[data.time == t]
        counts = {k: int((at_t.code == k).sum()) for k in cif}
        for k in cif:
            cif[k] += survival * counts[k] / at_risk
        survival *= 1.0 - sum(counts.values()) / at_risk
    return survival, cif

# KM for remaining free of any internal terminal disposition.
def terminal_km_at(data, tau):
    survival = 1.0
    for t in sorted(data.loc[data.time <= tau, "time"].unique()):
        at_risk = int((data.time >= t).sum())
        at_t = data[data.time == t]
        terminal = int((at_t.observed_status != "open").sum())
        survival *= 1.0 - terminal / at_risk
    return survival

primary = encode(df)
print("Primary estimates: internal resolution CIF and active-at-72 KM")
for cohort, group in primary.groupby("cohort"):
    active, cif = aj_at(group, horizon)
    print(f"Cohort {cohort}: n={len(group)}, "
          f"at risk just before 72={int((group.time >= horizon).sum())}, "
          f"resolution CIF={cif[1]:.3f}, "
          f"internal SLA failure=1-CIF={1-cif[1]:.3f}, "
          f"active at 72={terminal_km_at(group, horizon):.3f}, "
          f"other cause CIFs W/J/X={cif[2]:.3f}/{cif[3]:.3f}/{cif[4]:.3f}")

print("\nDescriptive cohort/priority strata; very small samples")
for (cohort, priority), group in primary.groupby(["cohort", "priority"]):
    _, cif = aj_at(group, horizon)
    known_on_time = ((group.observed_status == "resolved") &
                     (group.time <= horizon)).sum()
    print(f"{cohort}/{priority}: n={len(group)}, "
          f"on-time resolutions observed={known_on_time}, "
          f"at risk just before 72={int((group.time >= horizon).sum())}, "
          f"resolution CIF={cif[1]:.3f}, "
          f"active KM={terminal_km_at(group, horizon):.3f}")

print("\nSensitivity: event definitions and assumptions differ")
scenarios = [
    ("transfer competing", "compete", False),
    ("transfer censored: hypothetical net process", "censor", False),
    ("transfer credited immediately: optimistic proxy", "credit_immediately", False),
    ("transfer, withdrawal, rejection censored: hypothetical net process", "censor", True),
]
for label, transfer, censor_other in scenarios:
    encoded = encode(df, transfer, censor_other)
    estimates = []
    for cohort, group in encoded.groupby("cohort"):
        _, cif = aj_at(group, horizon)
        estimates.append(f"{cohort}={cif[1]:.3f}")
    print(f"{label}: 72h resolution/proxy CIF " + ", ".join(estimates))

# PM4Py EventLog preserves sequence without inventing timestamps.
from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace, Event
import pm4py
log = EventLog()
for row in df.itertuples(index=False):
    trace = Trace(attributes={"concept:name": row.case})
    for activity in row.prefix.split(">"):
        trace.append(Event({"concept:name": activity}))
    log.append(trace)
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
print(f"\nPM4Py: {len(log)} traces, {len(variants)} observed variants")
df["repeated_review_observed"] = df.prefix.str.split(">").map(
    lambda activities: activities.count("Review") > 1)
print("Repeated Review in full observed prefixes (descriptive only):")
print(df.groupby("cohort")["repeated_review_observed"].agg(["sum", "count"]).to_string())
```

The code retains every open case, including those already beyond 72, in the appropriate risk set. The stratified estimates are illustrative, not reliable subgroup comparisons. The tied-time convention assumes that a case censored exactly at an event time is observed through that time; the supplied table has no such ambiguous 72-hour record. If PM4Py is unavailable, install it to run the log construction; simple prefix splitting can reproduce the displayed repeated-review count but does not replace the requested PM4Py variant analysis.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

1. Obtain exact `Submit`, intermediate-event, terminal-event, and cutoff timestamps, including timezone and clock rules. These are essential for landmark features, stage durations, inactivity, event ordering, and validating the elapsed-time SLA. Establish whether there are missing or delayed records and how cases enter the extract.
2. Link transfers to external receipt, resolution, withdrawal, and rejection outcomes and their timestamps, including cases lost after handoff. Agree whether the SLA is internal or end-to-end and how external outcomes count.
3. Obtain case start dates, the extraction window and cohort-selection rules, and any left truncation or exclusions. These determine censoring patterns, calendar effects, and whether the sample represents the target submissions.
4. Record time-varying workload, staffing, queues, routing, severity, priority changes, and event-recording delays. These support realistic risk prediction, censoring assessment, and stage-hazard modeling.
5. Record assignment mechanisms, policy rollout dates, actual exposure and compliance, and relevant pre-change trends and case-mix variables. These are needed to investigate policy effects; cohort labels alone cannot identify them.
6. Collect substantially more cases and longer follow-up, especially across priority and outcome groups. Small risk sets, unknown external outcomes, potentially informative observation, and unmeasured confounding limit both numerical precision and causal interpretation here.
