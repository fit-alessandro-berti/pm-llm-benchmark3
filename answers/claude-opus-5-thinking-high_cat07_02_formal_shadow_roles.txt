# 1. Proposed role structure

The structure below is deliberately **not** a partition. Nine roles are proposed, but three resources hold *two* roles (Giorgio, Chen, Mateo), one "resource" is an automation boundary rather than an organizational role, and two resources sit in a restricted sub-pool that a clustering algorithm will merge into the front office. Membership is derived from three signals combined — activity mix, complexity distribution, and *decision authority* (who is allowed to be the last word on A6/A7/A9) — never from raw event counts alone.

**Two structural observations that no single-resource row captures:**

- **Authority is distributed, not concentrated.** 80 of 218 A9 events (37%) are performed by non-supervisors: Mateo 44, Giorgio 20, Fatima 16. Either three people hold undocumented delegated authority, or A9 is being recorded for two different acts (preparing an approval vs granting one). This is a data-semantics question with direct governance consequences.
- **The late shift is a different organization.** Only Chen (technical), Ivan (quality) and Lina (temp) work late. There is no supervisor, no security specialist, no commercial specialist and no coordinator out of hours. That single fact explains Chen's 24 A6 events, Ivan's 4, and plausibly the two critical cases that waited more than two days in weeks 7–8.

# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

The test applied for "core" is not frequency alone. An activity is core for a role when it satisfies at least two of: (a) a dominant share of that resource's own events, (b) a dominant share of that *activity's* total volume across the organization, (c) alignment with certification and complexity, and (d) authority to terminate a decision. Peripheral activities are then split into three distinct kinds — **spillover** (capacity-driven), **shadow** (competence exercised without mandate), and **artifact** (recording or scoping noise) — because they carry very different management implications.

**R0. Automated intake boundary (Nora).** Core: A1, A2 precheck. Peripheral: none — and that is the defining property. Nora never appears in A3–A14, so every case she cannot dispose of becomes a handover. The 760/610 asymmetry is the boundary's failure rate: ~150 cases needed human entitlement work after automated registration. Complexity: she only ever touches the bottom of the distribution (0 high, 0 critical), which is expected but also means her low unit cost cannot be extrapolated to the rest of the log. Substitution: none possible in either direction — no human substitutes for her at 24/7 cadence, and she cannot substitute for a human on anything above trivial complexity. Authority: zero. Her A2 events must be labelled *precheck*, not *validation*, or entitlement control appears far better staffed than it is.

**R1. Front-office routine resolution (Ben, Amira).** Core: A2, A4, A12, A1 — the fragment-1 loop, executed at low complexity and high volume (Ben ~1,366 events, Amira ~1,375). Peripheral, spillover: A3 Diagnose (60/75) and small A5/A10 volumes, i.e. cases they could have escalated but absorbed. Peripheral, shadow: **Amira's** 18 A7 and 42 A8 — small absolutely, but a certified pricing mandate is absent, and her 0.34 median with 9 criticals shows she is not merely handling trivial cases. Ben's 5 A7 and 25 A8 are better read as artifact/incidental. Peripheral, artifact: A11 (6/3) and A14 (4/2) — trace counts, almost certainly co-recorded activity rather than genuine quality gating. Substitution: they are the *receivers* of substitution pressure (fragment 6: temp closes → reopen → Amira/Ben/Mateo rework), a cost that never appears in the temps' own metrics. Authority: entitlement certification gives them real authority over A2 — the reason their A2 is a control and the temps' A2 is not.

**R2. Surge intake pool (Kai, Lina).** Core: A2, A4, A12, A1 — the same four activities as R1, which is exactly why the roles must be separated on non-activity grounds. Peripheral, artifact: A3 (8/12) and A8 (3/4) — 20 events out of ~1,690 across the pair; these are almost certainly scope leakage or shared-session recording, not capability. Complexity: 0.18/0.19, zero critical, 6 high between them — they are not doing the same *work* as Ben even though they perform the same *activities*. Substitution: they are the substitution mechanism itself (seasonal load), and weeks 8–10 quantify its cost — 17 reopens traced to incomplete A2 notes, i.e. the defect is located precisely in the certified step they are not certified for. Authority: none; their A12 Close events are the most consequential unsupervised act in the log, because closing terminates a case without any A11 gate in fragment 1.

**R3. Technical resolution (Dina standard, Chen complex).** Core for both: A3, A5, A10 — a tight, mutually reinforcing triple (~710 events each) that is genuinely a role. Peripheral, spillover: A2/A4/A12 front-office touches (Chen 18/30/28, Dina 24/36/25) — technicians finishing their own cases end-to-end rather than handing back. Peripheral, shadow (Chen only): **24 A6 Security Review events**, four times Dina's 6, on the late shift when Erik (day) is unavailable. Peripheral, artifact-or-early-signal: A11 (10–12) and A14 (11–14) — small, but higher than the front office, suggesting genuine post-implementation verification. Complexity is where the tiers separate decisively: Chen 0.71 with 42 criticals, Dina 0.48 with 9. Substitution: Chen substitutes *upward* into security; nothing in the data shows Dina substituting for Chen on critical work. Authority: both hold configuration certification; only Chen behaves as if he holds a security screening mandate, and no evidence says he formally does.

**R4. Commercial / pricing authority (Fatima).** Core: A7 (52% of all pricing exceptions) and A8 (90). Peripheral, spillover: A2 (22), A12 (18), A3 (8), A10 (6) — commercially-flagged cases she carries herself. Peripheral, authority-relevant: **A9 (16)** — this is not noise; a specialist performing supervisor approvals is either delegated authority within a pricing threshold or a recording ambiguity, and the difference matters for audit. Complexity: 0.67, 130 high+critical out of 258 cases — she is a genuinely high-complexity actor at modest volume. Substitution: her weeks 3–4 absence is the cleanest natural experiment in the dataset. Cover existed (Giorgio) and throughput was maintained (31 A7 + 18 A8), but the median pricing exception duration nearly doubled, 38 → 74 minutes. Cover ≠ equivalence. Authority: pricing certification makes her the only unambiguous owner of A7 outcomes.

**R5. Commercial shadow / hybrid (Giorgio).** Core, formal: A2 (190), A4 (150), A12 (130), A1 (90) — he is a working senior front-office agent, and this is the majority of his volume. Core, informal: A7 (72) and A8 (88). The decisive arithmetic is that only 31 of his 72 A7 events fall inside Fatima's absence, so **41 pricing reviews (57%) are routine, not emergency cover** — this is a standing second channel into pricing, not a contingency. Peripheral: A9 (20) — a front-office agent recording supervisor approvals is the sharpest authority anomaly in the log; A3 (40) and A13 (10) round out a genuine escalation-capable generalist. Complexity: 0.58 with the widest span (210 low, 120 high, 36 critical), consistent with someone who both runs routine volume and takes complex commercial cases. Substitution: he is the organization's actual commercial resilience — and its measured degradation. Authority: dual certification (entitlement **and** pricing) is the structural reason the shadow exists; the shadow is a mandate gap, not a skill gap.

**R6. Risk / security control gate (Erik).** Core: A6, 96 events, two-thirds of the organization's entire security review volume. Peripheral: A10 (18), A3 (16), A5 (10), A14 (9), A11 (8), A13 (6), A8 (4) — he stays attached to cases after review rather than reviewing and exiting, which is why his profile shows technical contamination. Complexity: 0.86 median, 55 criticals from ~166 cases, only 5 low — the most concentrated high-risk exposure in the organization. Substitution: weeks 7–8 show the subtle version — under *high* Enterprise Security load his A6 count *fell*, because Mateo pre-filtered low-risk referrals. False-escalation complaints fell (real benefit) but two critical cases waited more than two days (real cost). His event count is therefore not a demand signal at all; it is a signal about the filter upstream of him. Authority: he is a gate — his A6 outcome can stop a case — and gates must be measured by the tail they prevent, not by throughput.

**R7. Escalation brokerage (Mateo).** Core, and it is not any single activity: his core is the *routing relation* itself. The operational anchor is A13 Reopen (42, 30% of all reopens) plus presence in all 14 activities at 2–13% shares, appearing in fragments 3, 5 and 6 as the hinge between functions. Peripheral: everything else individually — A6 (16), A11 (20), A14 (18), A7 (10) — no one of which would justify membership in the corresponding specialist role. Complexity: 0.77 with 148 high and 76 critical, the second-highest criticality in the log despite only ~415 events. Substitution: he is the substitution *engine*, and both episodes bound his authority — week 5 gave 21 preliminary A9 events of which **13 (62%) were rechecked by Hana**, and weeks 7–8 gave the security filter with the two-day tail. Authority: this is the crux. He exercises broad *discretion* (what gets escalated, to whom, how fast) with essentially no *decision authority*. Discretion without authority and without documented criteria is the highest-leverage and least-governed position in this organization.

**R8. Supervisory decision authority (Hana).** Core: A9 (138, 63% of all approvals) and A13 adjudication (24). Peripheral: A8 (22), A11 (12), A2 (12), A3 (10), A7 (8), A12 (8) — supervisory sampling and spot involvement, spread thin by design. Complexity: 0.79, 130 high + 62 critical — she sees the hard end of the log almost exclusively. Substitution: she is the one role for which the data shows substitution *failed* to be equivalent, and it failed in the direction that matters (13 of 21 rechecked). Authority: definitionally hers; the observable marker is asymmetric recheck — she rechecks others, nobody rechecks her. That asymmetry, not frequency, is what makes cosine(Hana, Mateo) = 0.54 an *understatement* of the distance between them.

**R9. Quality assurance and knowledge stewardship (Ivan, Julia).** Core: A11 (89% of all quality checks between them) and A14 (77% of all KB updates). The A14 concentration deserves separate weight — they are the de facto owners of organizational memory, which is a distinct function from inspection and is what the temps' incomplete-notes problem should have been routed into. Peripheral, and governance-relevant: A10 (38/35), A5 (26/22), A3 (20/16) — roughly 121 events of hands-on configuration and implementation. Repairing a defect is not inspecting it; if these events land on cases the same analyst later quality-checks, the gate is self-reviewing. Peripheral, artifact: Ivan's 4 A6 — late-shift stopgap, same gap that produces Chen's 24. Complexity: 0.52/0.48, tracking the mid-range of what they inspect, which is itself a finding — the gate is *not* concentrated on the high-complexity cases where escapes are most expensive. Substitution: none observed; the pair is mutually redundant across shifts, making this the most resilient role in the organization. Authority: procedural veto over closure in fragment 2, but note that fragment 1 (the highest-volume path, and the one the temps run) bypasses A11 entirely.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

## 3.1 R07_Giorgio — standing commercial shadow (required)

**What the evidence shows.** Giorgio's formal title is Senior Service Agent in Front Office, and his largest volumes are unambiguously front-office (A2 190, A4 150, A12 130, A1 90). Yet he performed 72 A7 Pricing Exception Reviews — 31% of the organization's total, against Fatima's 52% — and 88 A8 Customer Negotiations, slightly *more* than Fatima's 90. The decisive figure is the split: **31 of the 72 A7 events occurred during Fatima's weeks 3–4 absence, leaving 41 in normal operation.** A substitute has a duty cycle; Giorgio has a standing second channel. His dual certification (entitlement **and** pricing) is the enabling condition, and cosine(Amira, Giorgio) = 0.84 versus cosine(Fatima, Giorgio) = 0.71 confirms the hybrid: closer to the front office in *activity shape*, but carrying commercial *content* neither pure cluster explains.

**Benefits.** He is the reason a six-day absence of the sole pricing specialist did not stall commercial cases. He resolves pricing questions inside the front office without a handover, which shortens the path for cases where the pricing question is discovered at validation time. He is also the only realistic internal successor to R4.

**Risks.** Three, in order of severity. (1) *Mandate gap*: 41 steady-state pricing decisions per ten weeks are being made outside the documented pricing function, so pricing consistency and exception thresholds are unauditable on that channel. (2) *Measured capability gap*: during his cover, median pricing exception duration rose from 38 to 74 minutes — he can do the work, but not at the specialist's tempo, and duration is a proxy for confidence and possibly for rework. (3) *Hidden authority*: his 20 A9 events mean a front-office agent appears in the approval record; if any of those approvals relate to his own pricing reviews, the case has no independent check at all.

**Recommendation.** Formalize rather than suppress: name a "Commercial Liaison" or Tier-2 commercial competency with explicit scope (exception value ceiling, segments and product lines in scope), require Fatima or Hana counter-sign above the ceiling, prohibit self-approval, and backfill the front-office capacity the formalization consumes.

## 3.2 R13_Mateo — cross-functional escalation broker (required)

**What the evidence shows.** Mateo is present in all 14 activities with no share above roughly 12.5%, which is precisely the profile that frequency clustering cannot represent — every distance metric will place him near a generalist or a temp. The complexity table refutes that immediately: median 0.77, 148 high-complexity and 76 critical cases, second only to Erik on criticality. He owns 42 of 140 reopens (30%), and he is the hinge in three of the six frequent fragments — fragment 3 (security → coordination → implement), fragment 5 (reopen → coordination → specialist → approval/close) and fragment 6 (temp-driven rework). The similarity caveats are explicit that he is a router, not a peer: cosine(Hana, Mateo) = 0.54 "different decision authority", cosine(Erik, Mateo) = 0.39 "rare specialist vs cross-functional router".

**Benefits.** He absorbs coordination cost that would otherwise appear as queueing between four separate functions. His weeks 7–8 security triage measurably reduced false escalation complaints, protecting the scarcest specialist in the organization. He converts reopens — the most expensive event class — into routed, owned work.

**Risks.** (1) *Unaccountable filtering.* The weeks 7–8 episode is the canonical warning: a broker who filters demand also silently sets the risk appetite. Two critical cases waited more than two days, an outcome invisible in Erik's own metrics because it manifested as *fewer* A6 events. (2) *Authority creep.* His 44 A9 events sit next to a 62% recheck rate (13 of 21) during week 5. Preliminary approvals that mostly require rework are a queue-shortening device, not delegated authority, and the log records both identically. (3) *Single point of failure.* No other resource has a comparable cross-functional footprint; his absence has never been observed and its cost is therefore unknown.

**Recommendation.** Keep the role, govern the discretion. Publish written referral criteria for security triage, log every filtered referral with the reason (so suppressed escalations are countable), separate "prepared for approval" from "approved" in the event vocabulary, add a tail-latency SLA on critical cases that bypasses the filter, and cross-train a deputy broker.

## 3.3 R05_Erik — rare but critical specialist (required)

**What the evidence shows.** Erik generates roughly 170 events in ten weeks, about an eighth of Ben's output, and would be pruned as noise by any minimum-support clustering threshold. Yet he performs 96 of 146 A6 events (66%), and his complexity profile is the most extreme in the organization: median 0.86, 82 high and 55 critical against only 5 low-complexity cases. Fragment 3 shows his structural position — he is a gate that sits between diagnosis and implementation, and cases pass through him precisely when they are dangerous.

**Benefits.** A single certified specialist provides consistent risk judgement, and consistency is worth more than throughput for a control activity. His non-A6 tail (A10 18, A3 16, A5 10) shows he remains engaged after review rather than issuing verdicts and disengaging, which improves remediation quality.

**Risks.** (1) *Volume-based invisibility.* Any capacity model driven by event counts will conclude Erik is underused. The correct measure is the tail he prevents, which is unobservable in a log of events that did *not* go wrong. (2) *Demand is mediated.* Weeks 7–8 prove his workload is set by Mateo's filter, not by actual risk, so his event count cannot be used for staffing arithmetic. (3) *Coverage concentration.* He is day shift only. The late shift has no security specialist, which is why Chen recorded 24 A6 and Ivan 4 — an undocumented deputy arrangement covering roughly a third of security reviews with an uncertified or differently-certified actor.

**Recommendation.** Protect and formalize rather than optimize. Establish a documented on-call and deputy arrangement (Chen is the evident candidate, with defined scope limits), track critical-case time-to-security-review as a tail metric rather than a mean, and never size this role from A6 counts.

## 3.4 R14_Nora — automation boundary role (additional)

Nora is a system, not a colleague, and treating her as either a resource or a role produces errors in both directions. She performs 760 A1 and 610 A2 events at median complexity 0.11 with zero high or critical cases. Two facts matter. First, **the 150-event gap between her registrations and her prechecks is the automation's escape rate** — those cases arrive at humans partially processed, and it is unknown whether the human A2 that follows is a full validation or a top-up. Second, her A2 events inflate the apparent staffing of entitlement control: 610 of 2,251 validations (27%) are machine prechecks, not certified validations, so the certified share of the entitlement control is much smaller than the column total suggests. *Benefit:* she removes the entire low-complexity intake burden from humans, which is what makes the current front-office headcount viable. *Risk:* her presence in role-mining output distorts every social network — she will appear as the dominant handover source in the handover-of-work network purely because she starts most cases, creating a false impression of centrality and of a tightly coupled intake team. Recommendation: exclude her from role clustering and social-network mining, model her as a boundary with an explicit fallout rate and a defined degraded-mode plan, and re-label her A2 as `Validate Entitlement (precheck)`.

## 3.5 R11_Kai / R12_Lina — temporary workforce boundary (additional)

Kai and Lina execute exactly the four front-office activities at near-identical profiles (cosine 0.98) and substantial volume (~871 and ~821 events), which makes them look like full front-office members to any activity-based method. Three attributes contradict that: temporary contract, 0.3–0.4y tenure, and **no certifications** — including no entitlement certification, which is the qualification that makes A2 a control step. Their complexity distributions confirm the difference in kind rather than degree (0.18/0.19; 6 high-complexity cases between them; zero critical). The weeks 8–10 evidence closes the argument: 17 reopened cases traced to incomplete A2 notes, i.e. the defect appears exactly at the uncertified control. Fragment 6 shows where the cost lands — reopened cases return to Amira, Ben or Mateo, whose complexity and cost per event are far higher. *Benefit:* genuine, large-scale absorption of seasonal low-complexity load at low cost. *Risk:* a quality liability that is displaced onto other roles' metrics and onto the reopen rate rather than onto the temps' own numbers, plus the fact that their fragment-1 path reaches A12 Close with no A11 quality gate. Recommendation: treat as a scope-restricted pool — mandatory structured A2 note templates, an A11 sampling gate on temp closes, no A12 authority on medium-or-higher complexity, and either certify them or explicitly cap their scope.

## 3.6 R09_Ivan / R10_Julia — quality gatekeeping with an embedded rework shadow (additional)

The 0.97 similarity makes this the cleanest cluster in the log, and that cleanliness conceals two shadow functions. First, **knowledge stewardship**: 200 of 261 A14 events (77%) are theirs, making them the owners of organizational memory — a materially different function from inspection, and precisely the mechanism that should have captured the temps' entitlement-note defect. Second, **embedded rework**: they perform roughly 121 A5 Configure and A10 Implement events plus 36 A3 Diagnose. Fixing a defect is not the same act as gating it, and if any analyst quality-checks a case they themselves configured or implemented, the independence of the gate is compromised on that case. Their complexity profile (0.52/0.48) adds a coverage concern: the gate sits in the mid-range, not on the high-complexity cases where escapes cost most. *Benefit:* fast defect closure without a handover round-trip, near-continuous coverage via the day/late shift split, and the only genuinely resilient two-person role in the organization. *Risk:* self-review, and a KB function that is invisible in the role label and therefore unresourced and unmeasured. Recommendation: split A11 into `Quality Check (independent)` and `Rework/Fix`, enforce that no analyst quality-checks their own repair, resource A14 explicitly, and extend A11 coverage over high-complexity and temp-closed cases.

## 3.7 R03_Chen — de facto late-shift duty officer (additional)

Chen's 0.94 similarity to Dina hides a second role. He works the **late shift**, when no security specialist, no supervisor, no commercial specialist and no coordinator is on duty. He recorded 24 A6 events (4× Dina's 6), 42 critical cases (vs Dina's 9), median complexity 0.71 (vs 0.48), and 15 reopens (vs 7). The similarity table itself concedes this: "Chen works more high/critical cases and more security-adjacent cases". *Benefit:* out-of-hours continuity — without him the late shift would have no capability above standard technical work. *Risk:* an uncertified or informally-certified actor is exercising security judgement with no supervisory authority available; his security certification is not listed, only configuration. Recommendation: decide explicitly whether this is a duty-officer role with defined scope and escalation triggers, or a gap to be closed by extending Erik's coverage or formal on-call.

# 4. Ambiguous assignments and alternative interpretations

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

```python
"""
Role discovery for the order-to-resolution process.

Design principle: role-mining output is an INPUT to interpretation, never a
conclusion. Every clustering result is re-joined with case_complexity,
temporary_substitution and authority-bearing activity shares before any
statement about roles or authority is made.
"""

import itertools
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 0. Vocabulary and constants
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

CASE_KEY, ACT_KEY, TS_KEY, RES_KEY = (
    "case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp", "org:resource",
)

# Activities that terminate or gate a decision. Authority claims may only be
# supported by these, and only in combination with recheck/complexity evidence.
AUTHORITY_ACTIVITIES = {
    "A6 Security Review",
    "A7 Pricing Exception Review",
    "A9 Supervisor Approval",
}

# Automation is a process boundary, not an organizational role. Nora starts most
# cases, so leaving her in inflates her centrality in every SNA metric.
AUTOMATION_RESOURCES = {"R14_Nora"}

SUBSTITUTION_WINDOWS = {                      # from the documented episodes
    "R07_Giorgio": [("2024-W03", "2024-W04")],  # covering R06_Fatima
    "R13_Mateo":   [("2024-W05", "2024-W05")],  # covering R08_Hana
}

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load and format
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

df = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution.csv")
df[TS_KEY] = pd.to_datetime(df[TS_KEY], utc=True)

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id=CASE_KEY,
    activity_key=ACT_KEY,
    timestamp_key=TS_KEY,
)
# format_dataframe standardizes the three mandatory columns; org:resource and the
# domain attributes (case_complexity, customer_segment, product_line,
# temporary_substitution, escalation_level) survive as ordinary columns and are
# what make the mined clusters interpretable.

log = log.sort_values([CASE_KEY, TS_KEY]).reset_index(drop=True)

# Two views: the full log for reality, and a human-only log for role mining.
log_human = log[~log[RES_KEY].isin(AUTOMATION_RESOURCES)].copy()

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Baseline role discovery (deliberately treated as a hypothesis)
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

roles_all = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log,
    activity_key=ACT_KEY,
    resource_key=RES_KEY,
    timestamp_key=TS_KEY,
    case_id_key=CASE_KEY,
)
roles_human = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log_human,
    activity_key=ACT_KEY,
    resource_key=RES_KEY,
    timestamp_key=TS_KEY,
    case_id_key=CASE_KEY,
)

def roles_to_frame(roles):
    """discover_organizational_roles -> [ (activity_list, {resource: count}) ].

    Flatten to a tidy frame so cluster membership can be joined with case
    attributes. Note the 'originator_importance' column is a FREQUENCY weight,
    not a seniority or authority weight.
    """
    rows = []
    for idx, (activities, originators) in enumerate(roles):
        total = sum(originators.values()) or 1
        for res, cnt in originators.items():
            rows.append({
                "mined_role_id": f"MR{idx:02d}",
                "mined_role_activities": tuple(sorted(activities)),
                RES_KEY: res,
                "events_in_role": cnt,
                "originator_importance": cnt / total,
            })
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

roles_df_all = roles_to_frame(roles_all)
roles_df = roles_to_frame(roles_human)

# Sensitivity check: resources whose membership changes when automation is
# removed were being clustered by intake volume, not by function.
membership_shift = (
    roles_df_all.groupby(RES_KEY)["mined_role_id"].nunique()
    .compare(roles_df.groupby(RES_KEY)["mined_role_id"].nunique(),
             keep_equal=False)
)
print("membership sensitive to automation removal:\n", membership_shift)

# A resource in >1 mined role is a hybrid CANDIDATE (expect Giorgio, Mateo).
multi_role = roles_df.groupby(RES_KEY)["mined_role_id"].nunique()
hybrid_candidates = sorted(multi_role[multi_role > 1].index)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Activity-based resource similarity (and why it is not enough)
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    log_human, activity_key=ACT_KEY, resource_key=RES_KEY,
)
sim_pairs = getattr(sim, "connections", sim)          # dict {(r1, r2): value}

sim_df = pd.DataFrame(
    [{"res_a": a, "res_b": b, "activity_similarity": v}
     for (a, b), v in sim_pairs.items()]
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Handover-of-work, working-together, subcontracting
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    log_human, beta=0, resource_key=RES_KEY,
    timestamp_key=TS_KEY, case_id_key=CASE_KEY,
)
together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    log_human, resource_key=RES_KEY,
    timestamp_key=TS_KEY, case_id_key=CASE_KEY,
)
subcontract = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
    log_human, n=2, resource_key=RES_KEY,
    timestamp_key=TS_KEY, case_id_key=CASE_KEY,
)
# pm4py.view_sna(handover) / pm4py.save_vis_sna(handover, "handover.png")

def sna_to_frame(net, value_name):
    conns = getattr(net, "connections", net)
    return pd.DataFrame(
        [{"source": s, "target": t, value_name: v} for (s, t), v in conns.items()]
    )

ho_df = sna_to_frame(handover, "handover_strength")
wt_df = sna_to_frame(together, "working_together")
sc_df = sna_to_frame(subcontract, "subcontracting")

# Brokerage: high out-degree spread ACROSS distinct home teams, not high volume.
team_of = log_human.drop_duplicates(RES_KEY).set_index(RES_KEY).get(
    "home_team", pd.Series(dtype=object)
)  # if home_team is not in the log, join it from an HR reference table.

ho_df["cross_team"] = ho_df.apply(
    lambda r: team_of.get(r["source"]) != team_of.get(r["target"]), axis=1
)
brokerage = (
    ho_df[ho_df["cross_team"]]
    .groupby("source")
    .agg(distinct_partners=("target", "nunique"),
         cross_team_handovers=("handover_strength", "sum"))
    .sort_values("distinct_partners", ascending=False)
)
# Expectation: Mateo tops distinct_partners while ranking low on total volume.
# That combination — broad and thin — is the broker signature that cosine
# similarity on activity vectors structurally cannot express.

# Subcontracting A -> B -> A identifies specialist consultation: a case that
# leaves a technician for Erik's A6 and returns. High subcontracting IN-degree
# with low total volume is the rare-critical-specialist signature.

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Join role output with complexity, substitution and authority
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 5a. Normalize complexity. case_complexity may be categorical or numeric.
COMPLEXITY_ORDER = {"low": 0.15, "medium": 0.45, "high": 0.75, "critical": 0.95}
if log_human["case_complexity"].dtype == object:
    log_human["complexity_score"] = (
        log_human["case_complexity"].str.lower().map(COMPLEXITY_ORDER)
    )
    log_human["complexity_band"] = log_human["case_complexity"].str.lower()
else:
    log_human["complexity_score"] = log_human["case_complexity"].astype(float)
    log_human["complexity_band"] = pd.cut(
        log_human["complexity_score"], [-0.01, 0.3, 0.6, 0.85, 1.01],
        labels=["low", "medium", "high", "critical"],
    )

# 5b. Substitution flag, from the attribute and from the documented windows.
log_human["iso_week"] = (
    log_human[TS_KEY].dt.isocalendar().year.astype(str) + "-W"
    + log_human[TS_KEY].dt.isocalendar().week.astype(str).str.zfill(2)
)
log_human["is_substitution"] = log_human["temporary_substitution"].fillna(False).astype(bool)
for res, windows in SUBSTITUTION_WINDOWS.items():
    for w_start, w_end in windows:
        mask = ((log_human[RES_KEY] == res)
                & log_human["iso_week"].between(w_start, w_end))
        log_human.loc[mask, "is_substitution"] = True

# 5c. Resource profile: this table, not the cluster labels, is the deliverable.
res_profile = (
    log_human.groupby(RES_KEY)
    .agg(events=(ACT_KEY, "size"),
         distinct_activities=(ACT_KEY, "nunique"),
         cases=(CASE_KEY, "nunique"),
         median_complexity=("complexity_score", "median"),
         p90_complexity=("complexity_score", lambda s: s.quantile(0.90)),
         critical_events=("complexity_band",
                         lambda s: (s == "critical").sum()),
         substitution_events=("is_substitution", "sum"))
)
res_profile["substitution_share"] = (
    res_profile["substitution_events"] / res_profile["events"]
)

# 5d. Steady-state vs substitution profile, per resource and activity.
steady_vs_sub = (
    log_human.pivot_table(index=[RES_KEY, ACT_KEY], columns="is_substitution",
                          values=CASE_KEY, aggfunc="count", fill_value=0)
    .rename(columns={False: "steady_state", True: "during_substitution"})
    .reset_index()
)
steady_vs_sub["sub_share"] = steady_vs_sub["during_substitution"] / (
    steady_vs_sub["steady_state"] + steady_vs_sub["during_substitution"]
)
# The single most important query in this analysis:
#   sub_share near 1.0  -> genuine temporary cover, do NOT infer a role
#   sub_share well < 1.0 -> a STANDING shadow role (Giorgio's A7: ~31/72)
shadow_signal = steady_vs_sub[
    (steady_vs_sub[ACT_KEY].isin(AUTHORITY_ACTIVITIES))
    & (steady_vs_sub["steady_state"] > 0)
].sort_values("steady_state", ascending=False)

# 5e. Authority share, computed only over decision-bearing activities.
auth = log_human[log_human[ACT_KEY].isin(AUTHORITY_ACTIVITIES)]
authority_share = (
    auth.groupby([ACT_KEY, RES_KEY]).size().rename("n").reset_index()
)
authority_share["share_of_activity"] = authority_share.groupby(ACT_KEY)["n"].transform(
    lambda s: s / s.sum()
)
# Non-supervisors with material share of A9 are authority anomalies to
# investigate (expected: Mateo, Giorgio, Fatima ~37% of A9 combined).

# 5f. Recheck detection: the empirical test of authority.
#     Substitution is NOT proof of competence; a recheck rate is the measurement.
auth_sorted = auth.sort_values([CASE_KEY, TS_KEY])
auth_sorted["next_res"] = auth_sorted.groupby([CASE_KEY, ACT_KEY])[RES_KEY].shift(-1)
auth_sorted["was_rechecked"] = auth_sorted["next_res"].notna() & (
    auth_sorted["next_res"] != auth_sorted[RES_KEY]
)
recheck = (
    auth_sorted.groupby([RES_KEY, ACT_KEY])
    .agg(decisions=("was_rechecked", "size"),
         rechecked=("was_rechecked", "sum"))
)
recheck["recheck_rate"] = recheck["rechecked"] / recheck["decisions"]
# Expectation: Mateo's A9 recheck rate ~0.6 in the substitution week, Hana's ~0.
# Asymmetric recheck (X reviews Y, nobody reviews X) is the authority marker.

# 5g. Final resource card: cluster label PLUS everything that qualifies it.
resource_card = (
    roles_df.merge(res_profile, on=RES_KEY, how="outer")
            .merge(authority_share.pivot(index=RES_KEY, columns=ACT_KEY,
                                         values="share_of_activity")
                                  .add_prefix("auth_share_"),
                   on=RES_KEY, how="left")
            .merge(recheck.reset_index()
                          .pivot(index=RES_KEY, columns=ACT_KEY,
                                 values="recheck_rate")
                          .add_prefix("recheck_"),
                   on=RES_KEY, how="left")
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Guards against over-interpreting frequency clusters as authority
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------------

# GUARD 1 -- similarity is compared against complexity divergence. A high-cosine
# pair with divergent complexity is a seniority split, not one role.
sim_df = (
    sim_df.merge(res_profile[["median_complexity", "p90_complexity",
                              "critical_events"]],
                 left_on="res_a", right_index=True)
          .merge(res_profile[["median_complexity", "p90_complexity",
                              "critical_events"]],
                 left_on="res_b", right_index=True, suffixes=("_a", "_b"))
)
sim_df["complexity_gap"] = (
    sim_df["median_complexity_a"] - sim_df["median_complexity_b"]
).abs()
sim_df["critical_ratio"] = (
    (sim_df["critical_events_a"] + 1) / (sim_df["critical_events_b"] + 1)
)
sim_df["verdict"] = np.select(
    [
        (sim_df.activity_similarity >= 0.90) & (sim_df.complexity_gap >= 0.15),
        (sim_df.activity_similarity >= 0.90) & (sim_df.complexity_gap < 0.15),
        (sim_df.activity_similarity.between(0.60, 0.90)),
    ],
    ["SAME SKILL FAMILY, DIFFERENT SENIORITY/RISK",   # Chen vs Dina (0.94)
     "GENUINE PEER PAIR",                            # Ivan vs Julia (0.97)
     "BRIDGING / HYBRID -- inspect manually"],        # Fatima vs Giorgio (0.71)
    default="DISTINCT ROLES",
)

# GUARD 2 -- rare-but-critical detection: low volume must never mean low value.
res_profile["events_pct"] = (
    res_profile["events"].rank(pct=True)
)
res_profile["criticality_pct"] = (
    res_profile["median_complexity"].rank(pct=True)
)
res_profile["rare_critical_flag"] = (
    (res_profile["events_pct"] <= 0.35) & (res_profile["criticality_pct"] >= 0.70)
)  # expected: Erik. NEVER prune such resources by a min-support threshold.

# GUARD 3 -- flat-profile broker detection. Cosine cannot see this, so measure
# profile entropy: high entropy + high complexity + high cross-team degree.
prof = (
    log_human.pivot_table(index=RES_KEY, columns=ACT_KEY, values=CASE_KEY,
                          aggfunc="count", fill_value=0)
)
p = prof.div(prof.sum(axis=1), axis=0)
res_profile["activity_entropy"] = -(p * np.log(p.where(p > 0))).sum(axis=1)
res_profile["max_activity_share"] = p.max(axis=1)
res_profile["broker_flag"] = (
    (res_profile["activity_entropy"] >= res_profile["activity_entropy"].quantile(0.75))
    & (res_profile["median_complexity"] >= 0.65)
)  # expected: Mateo

# GUARD 4 -- certification and contract are joined from HR, never inferred.
hr = pd.DataFrame([
    # resource, formal_title, tenure_years, contract, home_team, certs, shift
    ("R11_Kai",  "Temp Agent", 0.4, "temporary", "Front Office", frozenset(), "day"),
    # ... full reference table ...
], columns=["org:resource", "formal_title", "tenure_years", "contract",
            "home_team", "certifications", "shift"])

resource_card = resource_card.merge(hr, on=RES_KEY, how="left")
resource_card["uncertified_control_work"] = resource_card.apply(
    lambda r: bool(
        r["events"] and isinstance(r["certifications"], frozenset)
        and "entitlement" not in r["certifications"]
    ),
    axis=1,
)
# Cross-check against A2 volume: Kai/Lina perform ~520 A2 events with no
# entitlement certification. Activity-based clustering merges them with Ben;
# the certification join is the only thing that keeps them separate.

# GUARD 5 -- reopen attribution: quality liability follows the closer.
reopen_cases = set(
    log_human.loc[log_human[ACT_KEY] == "A13 Reopen Request", CASE_KEY]
)
closers = (
    log_human[log_human[ACT_KEY] == "A12 Close Request"]
    .sort_values(TS_KEY)
    .groupby(CASE_KEY)[RES_KEY].first().rename("closed_by").reset_index()

closers["was_reopened"] = closers[CASE_KEY].isin(reopen_cases)
closers = closers.merge(
    log_human.groupby(CASE_KEY)["complexity_band"].first().reset_index(),
    on=CASE_KEY, how="left",
)
# Stratify by complexity: temps handle easier cases, so an unstratified reopen
# rate would flatter them.
reopen_rate = (
    closers.groupby(["closed_by", "complexity_band"])["was_reopened"]
    .agg(["mean", "size"]).rename(columns={"mean": "reopen_rate", "size": "closes"})
)

# GUARD 6 -- automation boundary: quantify the precheck escape rate.
nora = log[log[RES_KEY].isin(AUTOMATION_RESOURCES)]

prechecked = set(nora.loc[nora[ACT_KEY] == "A2 Validate Entitlement", CASE_KEY])
fallout = registered - prechecked                     # expected ~150 cases

    .groupby(["customer_segment", "product_line"])[CASE_KEY].nunique()

# 7. Targeted hypothesis tests (the questions Section 4 needs answered)

# 7a. Giorgio's pricing work: standing shadow or emergency cover?

# Conclusion: capability present, tempo not equivalent -> cover, not competence.

chen_sec = log_human[(log_human[RES_KEY] == "R03_Chen")
                     & (log_human[ACT_KEY] == "A6 Security Review")]

# 7d. Quality-gate independence: do Ivan/Julia check what they fixed?
```

# 6. Operational interpretation

## 6.1 Staffing

Read the structure as **three capacity layers with very different substitutability**, and never as fourteen interchangeable FTEs.

The *volume layer* — Nora, Kai, Lina, Ben, and most of Amira — is elastic and can be scaled with demand, subject to a hard constraint: the certified entitlement step (A2) must not scale with it. Roughly 520 A2 events currently come from uncertified temps and 610 from automated precheck, so of 2,251 validations only about half are performed by certified staff. Any further growth in the volume layer requires either certification or a compensating control.

The *specialist layer* — Erik, Fatima, Chen, and the quality pair — must be sized on peak and on tail risk, not on average utilization. Erik's ~170 events would look like a fraction of an FTE to a utilization model; that model would be wrong, because his 55 critical cases are the ones that generate the largest downside.

The *coordination layer* — Mateo, Hana, and Giorgio's commercial half — is the least visible and the most fragile. It has no redundancy at all: single-person coverage for brokerage, for supervisory approval, and for commercial pricing.

Two concrete staffing gaps follow directly. First, single points of failure at Fatima (pricing), Erik (security), Hana (approval) and Mateo (coordination) — four roles, four individuals, and in three cases the cover arrangement is informal. Second, the **late shift is structurally under-covered**: only Chen, Ivan and Lina work late, meaning no approval authority, no security specialist, no commercial specialist and no coordinator is available out of hours. That is the most plausible structural explanation for the two critical cases that waited more than two days in weeks 7–8, and it is not fixable by adding more front-office headcount.

## 6.2 Training and development

Four targeted interventions, each traceable to specific evidence.

**Temps (Kai, Lina) — highest return.** The 17 reopens traced to incomplete A2 notes are a training-and-tooling defect, not a discipline problem. Structured note templates with mandatory fields, a short entitlement module, and a quality-sampling gate on temp closes address the defect at source. Until then, cap their scope: no A12 Close on medium-or-higher complexity without a second check.

**Dina — succession for the complex technical tier.** She is at 0.48 median complexity with 9 criticals against Chen's 0.71 and 42. With 1.2y tenure that is expected, but the gap is currently unmanaged. A deliberate ladder — paired critical cases with Chen, then supervised solo — turns a similarity artifact into real depth.

**Amira — deliberate commercial development.** She is already doing 18 A7 and 42 A8 with a 0.84 similarity to Giorgio. Rather than letting a second undocumented shadow form, formalize her as the planned successor for commercial coverage with defined scope. This is the cheapest available fix for the pricing single point of failure.

**Chen — formalize the security deputy.** His 24 A6 events are a real capability with no documented mandate. Certify him for a defined subset of security reviews, with explicit escalation triggers to Erik, and the late-shift gap narrows without new headcount.

**Giorgio — close the tempo gap.** The 38 → 74 minute duration jump shows what training actually needs to address: not whether he can review pricing exceptions, but how quickly and confidently he does so on unfamiliar exception types.

## 6.3 Risk control

Five control weaknesses are visible, and all five are invisible to activity-frequency clustering.

**Undocumented approval authority.** 80 of 218 A9 events (37%) are performed by non-supervisors. Either three people hold delegated authority that is nowhere documented, or A9 conflates "prepared for approval" with "approved". Split the event vocabulary, then define and publish approval thresholds.

**Unaudited risk filtering.** Mateo's weeks 7–8 security triage set the organization's effective risk appetite with no written criteria and no record of suppressed referrals. Require every filtered referral to be logged with a reason so the false-negative rate becomes measurable, and add a critical-case latency SLA that bypasses the filter.

**Quality-gate independence.** Ivan and Julia perform roughly 121 A5/A10 events. Any case where the same analyst fixes and then quality-checks is self-reviewed. Query 7d in the code answers this in one pass; if the count is material, separate the fix and check roles on the same case.

**Uncertified control execution.** Temps perform the entitlement control without entitlement certification, and the resulting defect is already measured. This is the clearest control finding in the dataset.

**Unmonitored automation boundary.** About 150 cases exit automated precheck into human hands, and no one owns that fallout rate. It needs a monitored threshold, a composition report by segment and product line, and a documented degraded-mode plan for Nora being unavailable — she currently handles 46% of registrations at 24/7 cadence with no human fallback plan on record.

**Self-approval in the commercial channel.** Giorgio records both A7 (72) and A9 (20). If any of those approvals sit on his own pricing reviews, the case has no independent check whatsoever. Check first, then prohibit.

## 6.4 Resilience

Resilience in this organization is currently supplied by the shadow roles, and this is the central strategic point: **Giorgio, Chen and Mateo are the reason the process absorbed two absences and a load spike within ten weeks.** The correct response is neither to eliminate the shadows (which would remove the resilience) nor to leave them undocumented (which leaves the risk unmanaged), but to convert each into a named, scoped, trained and audited arrangement.

Practically, that means: a documented deputy for each single point of failure (Amira and Giorgio for pricing, Chen for security, a named deputy broker for Mateo, and a defined approval-escalation path when Hana is absent); extending specialist or approval coverage into the late shift; and — critically — **measuring resilience by degradation, not by throughput**. Both substitution episodes maintained volume while degrading a quality dimension: pricing duration nearly doubled, and 62% of substitute approvals required rework. A dashboard that tracked only throughput would have reported both weeks as normal.

## 6.5 Cautions about changing staffing based only on mined roles

**Caution 1 — Low event volume does not mean low value; for control roles the relationship can invert.** Erik generates about an eighth of Ben's events and would appear underutilized in any frequency-based capacity model, yet he handles 55 critical cases and two-thirds of all security reviews. Worse, his volume is not even a demand signal: in weeks 7–8, under *higher* Enterprise Security load, his A6 count *fell* because of upstream filtering. Reducing security capacity on that evidence would be exactly backwards — and the cost would appear not as fewer events but as longer tails on critical cases, which is what the two >2-day waits already demonstrate.

**Caution 2 — Substitution demonstrates coverage, never equivalence, and never permanent competence.** Both episodes maintained throughput and degraded quality: Giorgio sustained 31 pricing reviews while median duration went from 38 to 74 minutes; Mateo produced 21 preliminary approvals of which 13 (62%) required Hana's recheck. A staffing decision reading only the event counts would conclude that pricing and supervisory approval are each covered by two people. They are not. Before formalizing any substitution into a role, measure duration, rework, recheck and downstream reopen rates — and remember that Giorgio's cover was itself only possible because he had spare capacity in his front-office work, capacity that formalization would consume.

**Caution 3 — High activity-profile similarity does not imply equal authority, risk exposure, or accountability.** Chen and Dina sit at 0.94 similarity across a 0.23 median-complexity gap and a 4.7× difference in critical cases. Kai and Lina would show high similarity to Ben while lacking the certification that makes their shared A2 activity a control. Any headcount move that treats a high-similarity pair as interchangeable — reallocating Chen's critical work to Dina, or replacing a permanent agent with a temp — moves activity volume successfully and moves capability not at all.

**Caution 4 — The observation window is short, contaminated, and unrepresentative.** Ten weeks contain two documented absences, a product-line incident spike, and a seasonal surge that is the entire reason the temps are present. Profiles mined from this window encode those anomalies as if they were normal operation: Giorgio's pricing share, Mateo's approval count and Erik's A6 volume are each distorted by a specific episode. Any structural staffing change should be re-derived from a window without active substitutions, or at minimum computed on the steady-state split shown in the code (section 5d).

**Caution 5 — Mined roles describe behaviour, not accountability, and cannot see what did not happen.** The log records who performed an activity; it does not record who was responsible, who was consulted, or which risks were averted. Nothing in the event data distinguishes Hana's authoritative A9 from Mateo's preliminary one, or Erik's decisive A6 from Chen's stopgap. Nor does the log contain the escalations Mateo filtered out, the defects the quality gate caught, or the incidents that never occurred because a security review happened. Role mining should therefore be used to *generate* questions for the process owner and to locate control gaps — and the ambiguous assignments in section 4 should be resolved by the named discriminating queries, and by conversation with the people involved, before a single reporting line is changed.
