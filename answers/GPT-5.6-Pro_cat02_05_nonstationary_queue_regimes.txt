# 1. Data audit and estimands

The observation window for each row is the 12-hour opening interval ([07{:}00,19{:}00)). For a set (R) containing (D) days, define:

* Arrival intensity: (\hat{\lambda}*A=\sum*{d\in R}A_d/(12D)), in cases per open hour.
* Completion intensity: (\hat{\lambda}*C=\sum*{d\in R}C_d/(12D)), also in cases per open hour.
* Net inflow: (A_d-C_d). This is a pressure indicator; it is an inventory change only if arrivals, completions, and WIP cover exactly the same population and there are no other exits, transfers, cancellations, or counting differences.
* Mean WIP (L): the opening-hours time average represented by the 15-minute snapshots, in cases. Because every day has the same 12-hour opening duration, daily mean WIP values can be equally averaged across a contiguous set of full days.
* Available capacity: (12\times\text{staff_fte}) FTE-hours per day. Completions divided by FTE-hours is a descriptive output rate, not a structural per-server service rate.
* Service behavior: `median_service_min` and `p90_service_min` are active handling-time quantiles. They exclude waiting. Neither is a mean service time, and neither can be substituted for mean flow time or used directly to calculate utilization (\rho=\lambda E[S]/c).

A median of the daily median-service values is used below as a robust summary. It is not the median of all case-level service times. The same warning applies to the median of daily p90 values.

There are two important internal reconciliation problems.

First, the representative hourly arrival vectors sum to their daily arrival totals, but they do not reproduce the stated `peak_08_10_pct`, even though the bins are said to be 07–08, 08–09, and so forth.

| Date       | Percentage from the 08–09 and 09–10 vector bins | Stated `peak_08_10_pct` |     Gap |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------: | ----------------------: | ------: |
| 2026-04-07 |                                           16.8% |                     31% | 14.2 pp |
| 2026-04-15 |                                           18.1% |                     32% | 13.9 pp |
| 2026-04-21 |                                            9.6% |                     47% | 37.4 pp |
| 2026-04-25 |                                           11.8% |                     22% | 10.2 pp |

The hourly vectors can still be analyzed for burstiness, but they do not validate the daily peak field. A clock shift, a different interval definition, or a data-pipeline discrepancy would have to be resolved before staffing an alleged 08:00–10:00 peak.

Second, over the complete four weeks there are 2,519 arrivals and 2,409 completions, a difference of +110. Under a closed, consistently scoped queue this would imply that ending inventory exceeds starting inventory by 110. That is incompatible with the final daily mean WIP values of 22 and 19 under a simple stock-flow interpretation. For example, ending inventory of at least 110 would imply at least 112 cases at the start of May 3 because May 3 has 45 arrivals and 47 completions; even after all 47 completions, WIP could not fall below 65. Therefore, at least one closed-queue assumption is false: there may be other exits, inconsistent scopes, re-entry counts, or a WIP definition that excludes some carried cases. Consequently, cumulative arrivals minus completions is used below only as a relative imbalance indicator.

Major threats to naive inference are:

1. **Weekday/weekend composition:** weekdays have substantially different arrivals, staffing, WIP, and temporal profiles from weekends.
2. **Outage contamination:** April 15 is a structural service interruption, not a draw from an ordinary service-time distribution.
3. **State-dependent recovery:** April 16–17 completions are supplied by accumulated inventory and extra capacity; they are not a normal capacity benchmark.
4. **Case-mix or coding change:** the complex percentage rises from roughly 19–27% to 39–47%, but the data cannot distinguish a real mix shift from a classification change.
5. **Unknown intervention exposure:** staffing began sometime during April 29, making that day neither cleanly pre- nor post-intervention.
6. **Endogenous staffing:** additional staff appear when WIP or backlog pressure is already high. A raw association between staffing and throughput is therefore confounded by operational response.
7. **Unknown boundaries and overnight carry-over:** 07:00 and 19:00 stock levels are unavailable, while cases persist during the closed period.
8. **Non-additive service quantiles:** daily medians and p90s do not identify the case-level mean, variance, or pooled quantiles.
9. **Different arrival and completion cohorts:** cases completed on a day may have arrived earlier and can have a different complexity distribution from that day’s arrivals.
10. **Intraday aggregation:** daily totals conceal bursts, outage intervals, queue smoothing, staff breaks, and hour-specific capacity.
11. **Serial dependence and change points:** adjacent daily rows share backlog state, operating policy, and case-mix conditions; they are not independent replications.
12. **FTE aggregation:** mean FTE does not reveal skill mix, concurrency by activity, breaks, reassignment, or whether complex cases consume multiple resources.

Several mechanisms are separated by this segmentation:

* The April 15 throughput collapse is caused by unavailable service capacity, not merely longer cases.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different alternative is a **phase-first inventory-state segmentation**. It treats weekday/weekend as a covariate rather than creating separate regimes, and it interprets the sequence as four latent states: stable operation, disruption and dissipation, sustained accumulation, and intervention-assisted drain.

| ----- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -----------: | --------------: | -------------: | -------------------: | -------: | -------: | -----------------------------: |
| A     | Apr 6–14     | Low-mix, approximately balanced pre-shock system                     |         86.8 |            86.2 |             +5 |          25 / 48 min |     18.7 |     5.17 |                          21.0% |

| C     | Apr 20–28    | High-mix accumulation under approximately unchanged nominal staffing |         99.1 |            87.3 |           +106 |          35 / 83 min |     39.2 |     5.17 |                          44.3% |
| D     | Apr 29–May 3 | Intervention transition and apparent inventory drain                 |         88.6 |            92.2 |            −18 |          33 / 76 min |     30.4 |     5.30 |                          43.2% |

This alternative tells a useful hysteresis story: the queue does not instantly return to baseline when the outage ends, and the high-mix period begins from a potentially altered inventory state. It also aligns with a transparent change-point scan: after excluding weekends and the outage, the strongest standardized split is between April 17 and April 20; within the high-mix period, the strongest response split is between April 28 and April 29.

Its principal weakness is calendar composition. Phase A has two weekend days out of nine, while Phase D has two weekend days out of five. Consequently, Phase D’s mean arrivals of 88.6 per day are not evidence that the intervention reduced demand. The weekday arrival means are approximately unchanged: 114.0 before intervention, 116 on the transition day, and 114.5 on the two clean post weekdays.

The primary segmentation is better for operational staffing because it preserves weekday/weekend differences. The alternative is better for describing backlog-state transitions and intervention timing. Neither uniquely identifies the mechanism:

* A **case-mix explanation** attributes the April 20 change mainly to more complex variants.
* A **coding-change explanation** treats part of the complexity jump as measurement drift.
* A **burst-and-congestion explanation** attributes the service tail partly to short intervals in which arrivals temporarily exceed service capacity.
* A **carry-over explanation** treats elevated WIP and completion rates as consequences of inherited inventory.
* A combined explanation is most consistent with the supplied evidence, but its components cannot be separately estimated from the aggregates.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

For the hourly vectors, define the index of dispersion or Fano factor as the sample variance of the 12 hourly counts divided by their mean. The coefficient of variation is the sample standard deviation divided by the mean. These are descriptive across unequal clock hours: a homogeneous Poisson benchmark would have a Fano factor near one, but deterministic time-of-day variation can itself produce a value above one. Therefore, the figures do not constitute formal Poisson overdispersion tests.

On April 21, the two largest arrival hours contain 58 of 115 arrivals, while the two largest completion hours contain only 25 of 101 completions. The queue therefore converts a highly irregular input stream into a relatively regular output stream. A daily model using only 115 arrivals loses the operationally important fact that 34 cases arrive in one hour and 24 in another separated hour. It would also wrongly associate the burst with 08:00–10:00 if it relied on the inconsistent `peak_08_10_pct` field.

At the daily level:

* The pooled arrival Fano factor is 10.03 and the completion Fano factor is 10.67.
* After separating weekdays, the arrival Fano factor falls to 0.66; for weekends it is 0.73.
* Within low-mix and high-mix weekdays, arrival Fano factors are only 0.26 and 0.29, respectively.
* These reductions show that most pooled daily dispersion is generated by regime mixture, calendar composition, and changes in the mean—not necessarily by a stationary overdispersed arrival process. Values below one in seven-day subsets should not be interpreted as firm evidence of underdispersion.

The very high lag-7 arrival correlation is based on only 21 paired observations and combines day-of-week matching with a secular increase. It is not an estimate of a stable seven-day autoregressive coefficient. Similarly, the high lag-1 correlations for complexity and service largely reflect a step change around April 20.

A transparent standardized split heuristic, excluding weekends and the outage, selects April 17 versus April 20 as the strongest change-point candidate. A nearly competitive split occurs around April 14–16 because outage recovery also changes WIP, staffing, and service. Within the post-April-20 weekdays, the strongest split is April 28 versus April 29, followed by April 29 versus April 30. This supports treating April 29 as a transition rather than assigning it confidently to either side.

No global stationary model is credible. At most:

* Low-mix weekdays have approximately stable daily totals, but their hourly rates still follow a pronounced time-of-day profile.
* High-mix weekdays have relatively stable daily arrival totals but an accumulating inventory state and at least one highly bursty hourly profile.
* The outage and recovery period is structurally nonstationary.
* The post-intervention period is too short and is explicitly draining inherited inventory.

Four deliberately selected representative days cannot identify an arrival distribution, a negative-binomial parameter, a full hourly seasonal profile, or robust autocorrelation. They demonstrate plausible mechanisms rather than estimate their population frequencies.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little’s Law is distribution-free: it does not require FIFO, Poisson arrivals, or exponential service. It does require a consistent customer population, compatible time averages and throughput rates, and either a long-run rate-stable system or sufficient finite-window boundary information.

Here, (L) is observed only during opening hours. Combining it with throughput in cases per open hour produces **opening-hours-equivalent flow time**. It does not produce wall-clock elapsed time because cases can remain in the system overnight while no service occurs. Calendar flow time would require full 24-hour WIP exposure or case-level timestamps.

Completions are used as the primary throughput proxy because they are actual system outputs. Arrivals are a reasonable sensitivity proxy only in near-balanced windows. Active handling medians and p90s do not enter the Little’s-Law calculation.

# 7. Operational implications

**Staff by calendar type and workload state, not by the pooled average.** Approximately six FTE are associated with balance in the low-mix weekday regime. With almost the same staffing in the high-mix weekday regime, arrivals rise from 1.41 to 1.60 cases per FTE-hour, the complex share more than doubles, and net imbalance reaches about +13 cases per day. This supports conditional surge staffing when the high-mix state is present, but not a claim that seven FTE is universally optimal.

The post-intervention weekday results are operationally encouraging: with 6.75 FTE, arrivals per FTE-hour fall back to approximately 1.41, completions exceed arrivals, and WIP declines. Nevertheless, service median and p90 remain far above the low-mix baseline. The intervention appears to compensate for harder work rather than eliminate it.

**Do not cut weekend staffing solely because absolute WIP is lower.** Weekend WIP per FTE exceeds weekday WIP per FTE, and the high-mix weekend regime accumulates about six more arrivals than completions per day. The move from 2.5 to 3 FTE on May 2–3 coincides with balanced arrivals and completions, but two observations are insufficient to establish the effect.

**Treat outage recovery as a separate operating mode.** The center should have a predefined recovery playbook that records the outage-end inventory, protects temporary recovery capacity, prioritizes cases by age and complexity, and defines when the queue has returned to a stable state. The 120.5 completions per recovery day should not be used as a normal productivity target; it depends on abundant waiting work and additional staff.

**Smooth and staff intraday peaks only after repairing the time-field discrepancy.** The April 21 vector supports flexible staffing, staggered appointments, controlled release of work, callback windows, or cross-trained reserve capacity around observed bursts. It does not validate an 08:00–10:00 peak. Operational decisions should use a reconciled 15-minute arrival series with a documented timezone and interval convention.

**Evaluate the intervention with same-day-type and backlog-adjusted comparisons.** Future evaluations should record the exact intervention start and end times, staff added by skill, cases exposed before and after the change, opening and closing inventory, and any simultaneous coding changes. A longer interrupted-time-series comparison, a staggered rollout, or a matched control queue would distinguish additional capacity from genuine productivity improvement more credibly than the two clean post weekdays.

**Collect the data needed to close the stock-flow identity.** At minimum, record:

* WIP at 07:00 and 19:00 and preferably every 15 minutes, including overnight inventory scope.
* All queue entries, completions, cancellations, withdrawals, transfers, and re-entries with case identifiers.
* Event-level arrival, service start, suspension, resumption, and completion timestamps.
* Time-varying staffing by skill and actual availability rather than only daily mean FTE.
* Complexity definition/version and both arrival-cohort and completion-cohort case mix.
* Exact outage and intervention intervals.
* Waiting time and active service time separately.

A policy that looks beneficial under pooled averages but is risky under segmentation would be to schedule approximately 5.1 FTE every day because that is the four-week pooled mean. It would materially under-resource weekdays that operated with 6–7 FTE, while ignoring that weekend pressure per FTE is already relatively high. Another risky conclusion would be to scale the temporary intervention on the claim that it increased productivity by 20%: raw completions rose by about 20%, but output per FTE-hour rose by only about 6%, over two post weekdays, during an apparent inventory-drain period.

def dispersion(x):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    if len(x) < 2 or x.mean() <= 0:

        "mean": x.mean(),

        "CV": x.std(ddof=1) / x.mean(),

# 4) Transparent change-point heuristic, not ground truth.
#
# Candidate splits maximize standardized between-segment separation.
# Results depend on feature selection and minimum segment size.
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
def best_split(frame, features, min_segment):
    x = frame.sort_values("date").reset_index(drop=True)

    if len(x) < 2 * min_segment:
        raise ValueError("Not enough observations for the requested split.")

    standard_deviation = x[features].std(ddof=0)
    if (standard_deviation == 0).any():
        raise ValueError("A change-point feature is constant.")

    z = (
        (x[features] - x[features].mean())
        / standard_deviation
    )
    total_sse = ((z - z.mean()) ** 2).to_numpy().sum()
    candidates = []

    for k in range(min_segment, len(x) - min_segment + 1):
        left = z.iloc[:k]
        right = z.iloc[k:]

        within_sse = (
            ((left - left.mean()) ** 2).to_numpy().sum()
            + ((right - right.mean()) ** 2).to_numpy().sum()
        )
        candidates.append(
            {
                "left_end": x.loc[k - 1, "date"].date(),
                "right_start": x.loc[k, "date"].date(),
                "between_segment_score": total_sse - within_sse,
            }
        )

    return (
        pd.DataFrame(candidates)
        .sort_values("between_segment_score", ascending=False)
        .reset_index(drop=True)
    )
