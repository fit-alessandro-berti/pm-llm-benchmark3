# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

I distinguish four uses:

1. **Exploration/discovery:** summarize observed and plausible behavior, tolerate incomplete logging, and expose uncertainty. Higher fitness and generalization may justify lower precision, but mandatory policy must not be inferred from such a model.
2. **Conformance enforcement:** decide whether cases violate policy. False acceptance of skipped checks is unacceptable; false rejection from missing context must be reported as *indeterminate*, not automatically noncompliant.
3. **Simulation/prediction:** generate realistic future behavior. Both underfitting and enumerative overfitting are harmful; probabilities and data conditions would also be needed.
4. **Stakeholder communication/change analysis:** favor compact, compositional structures with explicit constraints and ownership.

I interpret process-tree `+` as parallel composition, `X` as exclusive choice, `->` as sequence, and `tau` as silent behavior. Acceptance means exact language membership under standard process-tree semantics, not whether an alignment can obtain nonzero fitness.

The policy is incomplete because the log lacks:

- the external/internal attribute governing Supplier Review;
- the affected-check attribute governing post-correction rechecks;
- an explicit statement about whether Supplier Review must precede correction or approval in every external case.

I therefore distinguish **valid**, **forbidden**, and **indeterminate pending data/context**. Missing events should not be solved by making mandatory policy activities optional.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

## Strongest case for Model S

Model S is compact, compositional, fast to align, understandable, and naturally supports unseen combinations. It accommodates either mandatory-check order, either approval, and arbitrary interleavings of the four review activities. For exploratory analysis of incomplete logs, that broad language can prevent rare or newly combined behavior from being mislabeled merely because it was absent from training. Its high holdout fitness and generalization are consistent with F4’s unseen combinations.

## Strongest case against Model S

Its apparent robustness is achieved by making **every review optional**. This is not merely noise tolerance: it changes policy semantics. It also places all selected reviews in parallel, so Expedite Review can occur before either mandatory check. It cannot represent correction at all and therefore cannot express P6 or distinguish P7.

Concrete undesirable or ambiguous traces admitted by S include:

1. `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Skips both mandatory checks: B3.

2. `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Skips Technical Review.

3. `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`  
   Skips Credit Check.

4. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Expedite occurs too early: B4.

5. `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`  
   Supplier review does not compensate for missing mandatory checks.

6. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`  
   Performs only the optional urgent review before approval.

7. `Register Dossier, Release Order` is **not** accepted because one approval is structurally mandatory, but the near-minimal trace with either approval is accepted. Thus S correctly represents P5 while violating P2.

8. `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`  
   Structurally accepted even for an internal order, because the conditioning attribute is absent from the model. Its policy status depends on case data.

Model S also rejects all traces containing `Correct Dossier` and repeated checks, including policy-valid correction behavior. It accepts only one occurrence of each review because the tree contains no loop.

### Intentional tolerance versus accidental semantic flaws

- **Intentional tolerance:** optional Supplier Review may approximate unknown sourcing or missing events; allowing both mandatory-check orders is correct; allowing combinations is useful for exploration.
- **Accidental semantic flaws:** optionalizing Credit Check and Technical Review; parallelizing Expedite Review with its prerequisites; failing to model correction; and conflating “not observed” with “optional by policy.”

Calling these merely “noise tolerance” would hide material compliance errors.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

## Strongest case for Model C

Model C encodes several frequent variants explicitly. Every listed branch contains both mandatory checks before approval, exactly one approval, and fixed first/last activities. It therefore rejects B3, B4, and B8. Its high training precision is consistent with a narrow language, making it potentially useful as a catalogue of common observed variants or a regression fixture.

## Strongest case against Model C

Model C is a finite enumeration rather than a stable policy abstraction.

- **Branch enumeration:** each new combination requires another full sequence. Supplier Review × Expedite Review × approval type × check order × correction outcome grows combinatorially.
- **Duplicate labels:** the same business activity maps to many structural transitions. This complicates ownership, annotations, impact analysis, and diagnostic interpretation.
- **Unseen combinations:** valid combinations are rejected merely because no branch was written. B5 is one example.
- **Approval asymmetry:** Auto Approval appears only in the final branch and is unavailable in supplier, expedite, correction, or alternate combinations.
- **Order asymmetry:** several features are supported only under `Credit Check` then `Technical Review`.
- **Expedite asymmetry:** no branch permits `Technical Review, Credit Check, Expedite Review`; none permits expedite before supplier, despite P4 allowing either relative order after both mandatory checks.
- **Correction handling:** the single correction branch always repeats both checks. It rejects the process owner’s check-specific rework cases and cannot repeat corrections.
- **Holdout degradation:** 0.973 to 0.744 strongly suggests sensitivity to variant composition. F4 offers a concrete explanation.
- **Maintainability:** policy changes must be copied across branches, creating omissions and inconsistent edits.
- **Memorization artifact:** training precision of 0.941 can result from reproducing frequent positive sequences while excluding legitimate but unseen combinations. Without negative tests, it is not “audit precision.”

Model C’s strictness is not consistently policy-based. It is strict about unenumerated combinations but incomplete about known policy dimensions. Treating every unseen combination as noncompliant is especially unsafe where the omitted combinations are predictable products of already approved choices.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Ranked by likely decision impact:

1. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior.**  
   S can align missing checks cheaply because those checks are silent alternatives. Its high fitness therefore partly measures policy relaxation, not quality.

2. **Positive-log-only precision.**  
   Precision compares enabled behavior with observed positive traces; it does not test B3, B4, B8, double approval, early release, or other forbidden behavior. C’s 0.941 is not an audit-valid specificity estimate.

3. **Split sensitivity and possible leakage.**  
   No seeds, case IDs, temporal boundary, deduplication policy, or preprocessing are supplied. A random case split may place near-identical traces in both sets, while rare combinations may cluster by chance. There is no proof of leakage, but comparability is not established.

4. **Missing-event bias.**  
   Missing Supplier Review events can make valid external cases look internal and reward models that skip supplier review. Missing mandatory events similarly reward S. Enforcement must separate logging defects from policy exceptions.

5. **Generalization rewarded by excessive permissiveness.**  
   S’s 0.907 may reflect broad option and concurrency constructs. Generalization is not semantic validity.

6. **Alignment-cost sensitivity.**  
   Fitness, diagnostics, and sometimes practical acceptance depend on log/model move costs, silent-transition costs, duplicate-label choice, and final-marking treatment. None were published.

7. **Duplicated-label effects.**  
   C’s repeated labels create many candidate synchronous moves and may distort alignment search, diagnostics, token visitation, runtime, and any undocumented duplicate count. Duplicate labels are not automatically unsound, but here they arise from variant copying.

8. **Silent-transition effects.**  
   S’s four `tau` choices encode skips as normal model behavior. Petri-net conversion adds invisible routing whose cost and structural impact can influence simplicity and alignments.

9. **Runtime confounding by model size and benchmark design.**  
   C is likely slower because of many exclusive branches and duplicate labels, but median runtime hides tail behavior. Trace length, hardware, caching, concurrency, and timeouts are unknown.

10. **Stakeholder-score methodology.**  
    The large difference is plausible, but an informal mean without role counts, tasks, uncertainty intervals, or comprehension checks is vulnerable to presentation and familiarity effects.

These pathologies do not imply the reported values are false. They mean the values do not support the teams’ stronger governance claims.

# 7. Third Alternative

I recommend a **small attribute-conditioned procedural family with explicit three-valued policy checks**:

- a compact procedural backbone, `model_third`, captures control-flow common to all cases;
- sourcing and correction-impact rules are evaluated externally;
- missing required attributes produce **indeterminate/data-quality exception**, not automatic compliance or violation.

## 7.1 Compact formal description

Let:

- `R` = Register Dossier;
- `C` = Credit Check;
- `T` = Technical Review;
- `S` = Supplier Review;
- `E` = Expedite Review;
- `D` = Correct Dossier;
- `M`, `A` = Manager/Auto Approval;
- `O` = Release Order.

Backbone language:

\[
R \cdot (C \parallel T) \cdot S? \cdot
\left(D \cdot R_{\text{affected}}\right)^*
\cdot E? \cdot (M \oplus A) \cdot O
\]

where each correction iteration requires a nonempty affected-set recheck:

\[
R_{\text{affected}} \in \{C,\ T,\ C\parallel T\}.
\]

To keep the process tree compact and avoid enumerating whole variants, only the local recheck fragment is enumerated. Supplier Review is placed after the initial checks and before correction/approval in this operational baseline. P4 is satisfied because Expedite occurs after both mandatory checks and can be before or after Supplier Review; the implementation allows both by making Supplier and Expedite unordered after the checks. A separate constraint requires Expedite to precede correction/approval if it occurs.

For transparent enforcement, the external checker additionally requires:

- Supplier Review exactly once iff `source_type == "external"`;
- Supplier Review absent iff `source_type == "internal"`;
- after each correction, the checks specified by `affected_checks` repeat before approval or another correction;
- without a preceding unresolved correction, mandatory-check repetition is forbidden.

Because process-tree loops are awkward for check-specific rework, the external trace validator is authoritative for P6–P7.

## 7.3 Declarative policy and external checks

```python
POLICY = {
    "first": "Register Dossier",
    "last": "Release Order",
    "mandatory_initial_checks": {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"},
    "supplier_condition_attribute": "source_type",
    "supplier_condition_value": "external",
    "affected_checks_attribute": "affected_checks",
    "approvals": {"Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"},
    "expedite": "Expedite Review",
    "correction": "Correct Dossier",
}

def assess_trace(events, case_attrs):
    """
    Returns (status, reasons), where status is:
      compliant | noncompliant | indeterminate
    events: ordered activity-name list
    case_attrs:
      source_type: "external" | "internal" | missing
      affected_checks:
        list of sets, one per Correct Dossier, e.g.
        [{"Technical Review"}, {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}]
    """
    reasons = []

    if not events or events[0] != POLICY["first"]:
        return "noncompliant", ["Register Dossier must be first"]
    if events[-1] != POLICY["last"]:
        return "noncompliant", ["Release Order must be last"]

    approvals = [
        i for i, x in enumerate(events)
        if x in POLICY["approvals"]
    ]
    if len(approvals) != 1:
        return "noncompliant", ["Exactly one approval is required"]
    approval_i = approvals[0]
    release_i = len(events) - 1
    if approval_i >= release_i:
        return "noncompliant", ["Approval must precede release"]

    # Initial mandatory checks: first occurrences must precede approval.
    first_check = {}
    for check in POLICY["mandatory_initial_checks"]:
        idx = next((i for i, x in enumerate(events) if x == check), None)
        if idx is None or idx >= approval_i:
            return "noncompliant", [f"Missing initial {check} before approval"]
        first_check[check] = idx

    # Expedite must be after both initial checks and before approval.
    for i, x in enumerate(events):
        if x == POLICY["expedite"]:
            if i <= max(first_check.values()) or i >= approval_i:
                return "noncompliant", [
                    "Expedite Review must follow both initial checks "
                    "and precede approval"
                ]

    # Supplier condition.
    source_type = case_attrs.get(POLICY["supplier_condition_attribute"])
    supplier_positions = [
        i for i, x in enumerate(events) if x == "Supplier Review"
    ]
    if source_type == "external":
        if len(supplier_positions) != 1 or supplier_positions[0] >= approval_i:
            return "noncompliant", [
                "External order requires exactly one Supplier Review "
                "before approval"
            ]
    elif source_type == "internal":
        if supplier_positions:
            return "noncompliant", [
                "Internal order should not contain Supplier Review"
            ]
    elif source_type is None:
        reasons.append(
            "Cannot decide Supplier Review obligation: source_type missing"
        )
    else:
        return "indeterminate", [f"Unknown source_type value: {source_type!r}"]

    corrections = [
        i for i, x in enumerate(events) if x == POLICY["correction"]
    ]
    affected = case_attrs.get(POLICY["affected_checks_attribute"])

    if corrections and affected is None:
        reasons.append(
            "Cannot decide correction-specific rechecks: affected_checks missing"
        )
    elif affected is not None and len(affected) != len(corrections):
        reasons.append(
            "affected_checks does not match the number of corrections"
        )
    elif affected is not None:
        for k, corr_i in enumerate(corrections):
            next_boundary = (
                corrections[k + 1]
                if k + 1 < len(corrections)
                else approval_i
            )
            actual = {
                x for x in events[corr_i + 1:next_boundary]
                if x in POLICY["mandatory_initial_checks"]
            }
            required = set(affected[k])
            if not required:
                return "noncompliant", [
                    "Every correction must affect at least one mandatory check"
                ]
            if actual != required:
                return "noncompliant", [
                    f"After correction {k + 1}, expected {sorted(required)}, "
                    f"observed {sorted(actual)}"
                ]

    # Every repeated mandatory check must fall inside a post-correction segment.
    correction_segments = []
    for k, corr_i in enumerate(corrections):
        end = corrections[k + 1] if k + 1 < len(corrections) else approval_i
        correction_segments.append((corr_i, end))

    for check in POLICY["mandatory_initial_checks"]:
        positions = [i for i, x in enumerate(events) if x == check]
        for repeated_i in positions[1:]:
            if not any(start < repeated_i < end
                       for start, end in correction_segments):
                return "noncompliant", [
                    f"Repeated {check} without preceding correction"
                ]

    if reasons:
        return "indeterminate", reasons
    return "compliant", []
```

## 7.4 Explicit semantics

- **Mandatory checks:** both occur once initially, in either order, before Expedite and approval.
- **Supplier Review:** optional in the generic structural model only because sourcing data are missing; in enforcement it is required for external and absent for internal orders.
- **Expedite Review:** optional, after both initial checks and before approval. It may be before or after Supplier Review because those nodes are parallel.
- **Approval:** XOR gives exactly one Manager or Auto Approval.
- **Correction/rechecks:** each correction is followed by Credit only, Technical only, or both before the next correction/approval. The checker compares this with `affected_checks`.
- **P7:** repeated mandatory checks outside a post-correction segment are rejected.

## 7.5 Limitations

The procedural model alone cannot know whether an omitted Supplier Review is valid or missing. It also cannot know whether the correct subset was rechecked. Cases lacking `source_type` or `affected_checks` must therefore be routed to review as **indeterminate**. The tree’s supplier optionality is an uncertainty envelope, not a claim that supplier review is discretionary. The tree also fixes correction after the optional supplier/expedite stage; if stakeholders require supplier or expedite after correction, the backbone must be revised rather than hidden in alignments.

# 9. Validation Experiments

## 9.1 Reproducible data protocol

1. Version the event log, PM4Py, Python, parser strings, and code.
2. Publish:
   - case-ID derivation;
   - activity mapping;
   - lifecycle filtering;
   - timestamp ordering and tie-break rules;
   - duplicate handling;
   - incomplete-case filtering;
   - attribute-imputation policy.
3. Run repeated grouped case splits with fixed seeds, for example `11, 29, 47, 83, 101`.
4. Add a **temporal holdout** to detect drift and avoid near-duplicate leakage.
5. Store hashes of train/test case-ID lists for every run.
6. Deduplicate only for a separately reported sensitivity analysis, never silently.

## 9.2 Attribute-stratified holdouts

Stratify where attributes are available by:

- external/internal/unknown;
- approval type;
- check order;
- Supplier present/absent;
- Expedite present/absent;
- correction count;
- correction affected subset;
- missing-event indicators.

Use group-aware splitting by case. Include “leave-one-combination-out” tests, such as withholding external + Auto + alternate order.

## 9.3 Curated negative suite

At minimum include:

- skipped Credit;
- skipped Technical;
- both skipped;
- early Expedite;
- Expedite after approval;
- no approval;
- both approvals;
- release before approval;
- repeated checks without correction;
- correction without any affected recheck;
- wrong subset after correction;
- missing Supplier for known external;
- Supplier for known internal;
- second Register or events after Release.

Report exact accept/reject/indeterminate outcomes separately from alignment fitness.

## 9.4 Perturbation tests

For each clean trace:

- delete Supplier Review;
- delete each mandatory check;
- duplicate each check;
- duplicate approval;
- swap adjacent events;
- insert early Expedite;
- delete correction while retaining rechecks;
- delete affected-check attributes;
- alter source type.

This measures whether a model degrades safely under logging errors rather than merely obtaining a high fitness score.

## 9.5 Alignment-cost sensitivity

Evaluate a published grid of log/model move costs, especially:

- mandatory checks;
- Supplier Review;
- Expedite Review;
- correction;
- approvals;
- silent transitions.

Do not use low-cost skipping as the compliance decision. Report rank and classification stability across settings.

## 9.6 Structural checks

For every converted model:

- workflow-net soundness;
- dead transitions;
- reachable final marking;
- proper completion;
- boundedness where available;
- play-out sanity checks;
- probe membership tests;
- duplicate-label inventory under a documented definition.

## 9.7 Metrics

Report separately, per split and stratum:

- alignment fitness;
- alignment precision;
- token-based generalization;
- arc-degree simplicity;
- model size, silent transitions, duplicate visible labels;
- median, p95, and timeout alignment runtime;
- negative-suite false-accept rate;
- positive-suite false-reject rate;
- indeterminate rate;
- policy-check diagnostic accuracy.

Use confidence intervals across seeds/cases. Do not collapse these into one score.

## 9.8 Stakeholder comprehension protocol

Recruit representatives from compliance, operations, IT, audit, process ownership, and analytics. Randomize model presentation order. Ask participants to:

1. classify a fixed trace set;
2. identify where a policy change must be made;
3. explain Supplier and correction semantics;
4. estimate confidence;
5. complete tasks under time measurement.

Report accuracy, time, confidence calibration, role, sample size, and qualitative themes—not only a 1–5 preference score.

SEEDS = [11, 29, 47, 83, 101]

def split_case_ids(event_df, seed, test_size=0.20):
    case_col = "case:concept:name"
    case_table = event_df[[case_col]].drop_duplicates().reset_index(drop=True)
    groups = case_table[case_col].to_numpy()

    splitter = GroupShuffleSplit(
        n_splits=1, test_size=test_size, random_state=seed
    )
    train_idx, test_idx = next(
        splitter.split(case_table, groups=groups)
    )
    return (
        set(case_table.iloc[train_idx][case_col]),
        set(case_table.iloc[test_idx][case_col]),
    )

def to_event_log(df):
    # Pin PM4Py version and formatting assumptions.
    formatted = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id="case:concept:name",
        activity_key="concept:name",
        timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    )
    return pm4py.convert_to_event_log(formatted)

def evaluate_model(log_train, log_test, model):
    net, im, fm = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model)
    sound = pm4py.check_soundness(
        net, im, fm, print_diagnostics=True
    )

    result = {
        "sound": bool(sound),
        "fitness_train": pm4py.fitness_alignments(
            log_train, net, im, fm
        ),
        "fitness_test": pm4py.fitness_alignments(
            log_test, net, im, fm
        ),
        "precision_train": pm4py.precision_alignments(
            log_train, net, im, fm
        ),
        "precision_test": pm4py.precision_alignments(
            log_test, net, im, fm
        ),
        "generalization_train": pm4py.generalization_tbr(
            log_train, net, im, fm
        ),
        "generalization_test": pm4py.generalization_tbr(
            log_test, net, im, fm
        ),
        "simplicity": pm4py.simplicity_petri_net(
            net, im, fm, variant="arc_degree"
        ),
        "diagnostics_test": pm4py.conformance_diagnostics_alignments(
            log_test, net, im, fm
        ),
    }
    return result

def benchmark_alignment(log, model):
    net, im, fm = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model)
    timings = []
    for trace in log:
        single_log = pm4py.objects.log.obj.EventLog([trace])
        start = time.perf_counter()
        pm4py.conformance_diagnostics_alignments(
            single_log, net, im, fm
        )
        timings.append(time.perf_counter() - start)
    return {
        "median_s": float(np.median(timings)),
        "p95_s": float(np.percentile(timings, 95)),
    }

def run_repeated_splits(event_df, models):
    all_results = []
    for seed in SEEDS:
        train_ids, test_ids = split_case_ids(event_df, seed)
        train_df = event_df[
            event_df["case:concept:name"].isin(train_ids)
        ].copy()
        test_df = event_df[
            event_df["case:concept:name"].isin(test_ids)
        ].copy()

        log_train = to_event_log(train_df)
        log_test = to_event_log(test_df)

        for name, model in models.items():
            metrics = evaluate_model(log_train, log_test, model)
            all_results.append({
                "seed": seed,
                "model": name,
                "train_case_ids_hash_input": sorted(train_ids),
                "test_case_ids_hash_input": sorted(test_ids),
                "metrics": metrics,
            })
    return all_results

## 9.10 Rejection rule

Reject a compliance model regardless of aggregate metrics if it:

- accepts any critical negative such as skipped mandatory checks, early Expedite, no/double approval, or release before approval;
- classifies missing governing attributes as compliant rather than indeterminate;
- cannot represent an approved correction subset;
- is unsound or has dead policy-relevant transitions;
- produces materially unstable compliance decisions under reasonable alignment costs;
- exceeds an approved false-accept threshold on the curated negative suite.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

- **Model S:** retain only as a clearly labeled exploratory envelope or visualization of activity combinations. It must not generate compliance decisions, audit claims, or policy simulations. Its title should state that mandatory checks are not represented faithfully.
- **Model C:** retire as the primary process model. It may be archived as a historical frequent-variant catalogue or used as a regression suite, but not described as “audit-ready.”
- **Compliance:** neither original is suitable. Use the third alternative only after:
  1. parser and loop semantics are version-pinned and probe-tested;
  2. soundness and reachability pass;
  3. critical negative traces are all rejected;
  4. B1–B8 and approved adversarial positives behave as specified;
  5. source and affected-check attributes are captured or missing values produce indeterminate review;
  6. repeated seeded, temporal, and attribute-stratified validation is completed;
  7. alignment costs and preprocessing are published;
  8. compliance, operations, IT, audit, and process ownership approve the explicit semantics;
  9. a monitoring process tracks indeterminate rates, drift, and logging quality.

The board should also prioritize adding `source_type` and correction-level `affected_checks` to the operational data model. Until then, no model can reliably adjudicate P3 and P6 from event sequences alone.

# 11. Dissenting View

The recommendation may overengineer a problem that Model C already handles conservatively. Compliance systems often should reject unseen behavior until governance approves it; broad compositional generalization can normalize creative workarounds. Model C’s low holdout fitness may therefore be a feature, not a defect, especially when the holdout contains missing Supplier Review events or combinations never formally authorized. Its explicit branches are inspectable testable procedures, whereas the proposed hybrid splits semantics across a process tree, conditioned variants, Python checks, and attributes that are presently absent. That distribution creates its own audit risk: a trace can fit the backbone yet fail an external rule, and users may report whichever result is more favorable. The loop also introduces substantially more behavior than the finite catalogue and depends on parser semantics that require careful verification.

Alternatively, Model S may be entirely appropriate if the actual deliverable is discovery rather than enforcement. Its excellent holdout fitness, runtime, simplicity, and stakeholder comprehension are operational advantages. Forcing policy into the discovery model could obscure logging defects and make analysts mistake absent events for violations. A cleaner governance approach might retain S for discovery and use a separate hand-authored rules engine, without creating another procedural model whose data conditions cannot yet be evaluated.

I would change my recommendation if a reproducible evaluation showed that C rejects only genuinely unauthorized behavior, covers all approved combinations, and remains maintainable under realistic changes. I would favor S more strongly if the scope were contractually limited to exploration and users demonstrably did not treat it as policy. Conversely, high false-accept rates for the third alternative, ambiguous loop behavior, poor stakeholder task accuracy, or persistently unavailable governing attributes would justify replacing it with a pure rules engine or stricter conditioned models.
