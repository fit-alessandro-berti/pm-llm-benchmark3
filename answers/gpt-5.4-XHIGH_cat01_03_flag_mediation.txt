## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands
| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E1 | P1 vs P0: suppress visible flag display, keep `risk_score` and other human information unchanged | arriving cases in the post-flag system | `triage_done - arrival` | total policy effect | Yes | hidden risk and dedicated staffing may still correlate with who is effectively recognized as high risk |
| E2 | P1 vs P0 | arriving cases in the post-flag system | `cycle_time = close - arrival` | total policy effect | Yes | staff may substitute from visible flag to `risk_score` or other cues, so P1 may not remove downstream routing |
| E3 | split treatment: triage-priority component on, review-routing component off, versus both off | arriving cases with support near the operational rule | `triage_done - arrival` | separable direct effect of the priority-lane component | Partly | the current system does not independently randomize priority-lane and review-routing components |
| E4 | P2 vs P0: keep priority triage, remove automatic mandatory manual review | arriving cases eligible for current flagged handling | `cycle_time` | total policy effect | Yes | once review becomes discretionary, post-triage human judgment creates treatment-confounder feedback |
| E5 | under P0, replace the distribution of `manual_review` occurrence with the distribution induced by P2 | shift/day clusters of arriving cases | `cycle_time` | interventional indirect effect via manual-review occurrence | Partly | `manual_review` changes shared queues and is confounded by post-triage information seen by humans |
| E6 | under P0 routing, replace `manual_queue_wait` with the distribution induced by P3 protected capacity | shift/day clusters of reviewed cases | `cycle_time` | interventional indirect effect via manual-review waiting | Partly | shared-queue interference and incomplete measurement of staffing and backlog |
| E7 | under P0 routing, replace escalation rule or senior capacity with P4 | reviewed cases or shift/day clusters | `cycle_time` | interventional indirect effect via escalation occurrence/queue | Partly | manual-review findings are post-treatment confounders for escalation and later closure |
| E8 | P2 vs P0, or P3 vs P0 | arriving cases | observed `decision_outcome` distribution, e.g. `Pr(Reject)`; audit accuracy if later measured | total policy effect on final outcome / quality proxy | Yes for observed decision; No for latent correctness | the data have no ground-truth quality label, so approve/reject mix is not correctness |

I would treat total policy effects, separable effects, and interventional indirect effects as primary. I would not use classical natural direct or natural indirect effects as the main target, because shared review and escalation queues create interference, and manual-review findings create exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounding that makes cross-world contrasts hard to defend.

## 2. Mechanism graph
```text
U_underlying_risk -> risk_score, flag, manual_review, escalation, cycle_time, decision_outcome
complexity -> risk_score, flag, triage_service, manual_review, escalation, cycle_time, decision_outcome
risk_score -> flag, manual_review, escalation, decision_outcome
arrival_load -> triage_wait, manual_queue_wait, escalation_queue_wait, cycle_time
flag -> priority_lane, manual_review
priority_lane -> triage_resource, triage_wait
triage_resource -> triage_service
triage_wait -> cycle_time
triage_service -> manual_review, cycle_time
manual_review -> review_resource, manual_queue_wait, manual_service, escalation
review_resource -> manual_queue_wait, manual_service, escalation

manual_service -> escalation, decision_outcome, cycle_time
escalation -> escalation_queue_wait, senior_service, decision_outcome

senior_service -> decision_outcome, cycle_time

```

```text
t0 arrival/intake:
U_underlying_risk ---->
                      risk_score ---->
complexity ---------->             flag ----> priority_lane ----> triage_wait ----> triage_service ----> triage done
arrival_load -------->                  \
                                         +---------------------> manual_review? ----> review_resource ----> manual_queue_wait ----> manual_service
                                                                                                                     |
t1 post-review:                                                                                                      +----> escalation? ----> escalation_queue_wait ----> senior_service
                                                                                                                                                           |
t2 close: decision_outcome <-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------+---------------------------+

cycle_time accumulates:
triage_wait + triage_service + manual_queue_wait + manual_service + escalation_queue_wait + senior_service + residual_to_close

shared-queue spillovers:
other cases' manual_review and escalation choices ----> manual_queue_wait, escalation_queue_wait
```

Interference arises because review and senior queues are shared, so one case's flag-induced routing changes other cases' waiting times. Exposure-induced confounding arises because the flag changes who gets manual review and which reviewer handles the case; those post-treatment variables then affect both escalation and cycle time. Treatment-confounder feedback appears when manual-review occurrence, review duration, or review findings both depend on the flag and then confound the later escalation-to-cycle-time relation. This is why individual-level natural direct and indirect effects are weak here. Cluster- or shift-level interventional analogues are more defensible because they let queue state move with the policy instead of pretending each case's mediator can be changed in isolation.

The observed pattern is consistent with front-end acceleration and downstream delay. Flagged cases reach triage start in 0.24 hours on average versus 2.48 for unflagged, and arrival-to-triage-done is 0.84 versus 3.43 hours. The reversal begins after triage: all flagged cases enter manual review, their mean wait from triage completion to manual start is 6.50 hours, and 60% are escalated, producing a mean total cycle of 16.51 hours versus 8.17 for unflagged. Among flagged cases, escalation is associated with 21.42 hours total cycle versus 9.15 without escalation. These contrasts are not automatically causal. Flagged cases are concentrated in medium and high complexity, `risk_score` is imperfect, different resources handle flagged and unflagged triage, and queue delays are jointly produced by other cases and staffing patterns, especially overnight.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis
| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `flag -> priority_lane -> triage timing` | visible flag buys access to a faster triage lane and resource pool | flagged `arrival-to-triage-start` is 0.24h vs 2.48h; `arrival-to-triage-done` is 0.84h vs 3.43h; flagged triage uses T1/T2 only | the fast lane is caused by flag visibility rather than some other hidden assignment rule | flagged cases may arrive better documented or be routed to a better staffed team for reasons correlated with risk | separable priority component or P1 | if capacity is fixed, speeding flagged triage can slow unflagged triage |
| `flag -> manual_review` occurrence | the policy sends flagged cases to review almost automatically | 100% of flagged cases receive manual review vs 40% of unflagged cases | review logging is complete and flag status affects routing beyond human discretion alone | hidden risk or complexity, not the flag, may be why those cases would have been reviewed anyway | P2 | shorter cycles, but possible quality or compliance loss if true high-risk cases skip review |
| `flag -> manual_queue_wait` | flagged routing creates long waits before review starts | `triage-done-to-manual-start` is 6.50h for flagged vs 1.88h among reviewed unflagged; flagged high-load cases wait 7.42h vs 5.12h under normal load | manual start timestamps reflect actual work start, not delayed data entry | off-shift staffing or reviewer assignment, not queue pressure from flagging, explains the wait | P3 | gains for flagged cases may come at the expense of unflagged cases unless capacity is additive or capped |
| `flag -> escalation` | flagged cases are escalated more often after review | escalation rate is 60% for flagged vs 20% for unflagged; flagged escalated cases average 21.42h total cycle vs 9.15h for flagged non-escalated | reviewers partly react to the policy bundle, not only to underlying risk | truly riskier cases are both more likely to be flagged and to need escalation | P4 | tightening escalation rules can reduce delay but risks under-escalation |
| `complexity/risk confounding` | longer flagged cycles partly reflect harder cases, not flag handling | flagged cases are all medium/high complexity; high-complexity cases average 19.77h total cycle; `risk_score` is stated to be imperfect | `complexity` and `risk_score` do not fully absorb `U_underlying_risk` | the apparent flag effect shrinks sharply once comparing locally similar cases | local-threshold or within-score/complexity analysis rather than an operational policy | narrower support and reduced generalizability |
| shared-queue interference | one case's review/escalation delays another case | manual and senior queues are shared; M2 cases have mean manual wait 6.29h and escalation wait 7.29h | resource IDs correspond to real shared capacity pools | reviewer-specific case mix, not spillovers, drives the difference | P3 with true added capacity, or queue caps at the cluster level | redistribution of delays across flagged and unflagged cases |
| reviewer/resource assignment | flagged cases may be routed to different workers with different speed or stringency | flagged triage is handled only by T1/T2, unflagged only by T3/T4; M2 disproportionately handles flagged high-complexity review | resources differ in speed, strictness, or availability | assignment is purely by difficulty, so resource differences are just case-mix differences | rebalance assignment or estimate resource fixed effects | changing assignment may reduce specialization and consistency |
| decision-quality tradeoff | extra review and escalation may improve correctness even while delaying closure | overall reject rate is 20% in both flag groups, but that does not measure correctness; flagged high-complexity reject rate is 40% vs 67% for unflagged high-complexity | observed approve/reject and later audits proxy quality | different base risks and reviewer thresholds drive outcome differences | P3, or P2 with audit sampling and rollback rules | faster throughput may worsen false approvals or false rejections |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification
| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Is there a local causal effect of being flagged at all? | use the documented flag rule, or infer the operational frontier, and compare cases just above and below it within narrow time windows and complexity strata | local function of `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, hour/shift | continuity at the threshold, no precise manipulation, and stable local staffing | covariate smoothness around the threshold and a jump in flag probability, triage wait, or review rate | no discontinuity in review or cycle time, or a discontinuity only in hidden-risk proxies |
| Are longer flagged cycles mainly queue-mediated? | estimate a queue-state-aware hazard model for manual-review start and escalation start using backlog at each handoff | `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, handoff time, current manual backlog, current senior backlog, reviewer/resource IDs | measured backlog captures the relevant queue state and priority rules | backlog should strongly predict waits; the flagged coefficient should attenuate after backlog adjustment if queue mediation is central | flagged waits remain nearly unchanged after queue-state control |
| What is the policy effect of relaxing mandatory review or adding protected capacity? | run a randomized encouragement or cluster-randomized pilot: marginal flagged cases or shifts assigned to P2-like discretionary review, or P3-like protected capacity | randomization blocks by score band, complexity, load, and shift | compliance is measured, assignment is as-if random within blocks, and spillovers are mostly within clusters | first-stage change in review routing or protected-slot access, plus balance across blocks | no first-stage, no cycle-time gain, or quality metrics deteriorate materially |
| How much of the effect runs through occurrence versus waiting versus escalation? | use cluster-level g-computation or TMLE with stochastic interventions on `manual_review`, `manual_queue_wait`, and `escalation` | `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, triage timing, reviewer pool, observed queue sizes, review duration | no unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding conditional on observed history, partial interference model, and positivity | decomposition should be stable across model forms and only use supported regions | mediated shares are unstable, flip sign, or rely on unsupported counterfactual regions |
| Is residual time-trend confounding still present? | use a negative-control exposure: future flagged arrivals in the next 2 hours predicting current case pre-triage outcomes | current `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, clock time | future arrivals cannot cause already-completed triage timing | the negative-control association should be null after adjustment | future flagged volume predicts current triage wait, implying unremoved time confounding |
| How sensitive are conclusions to hidden risk? | simulate or bound a latent `U_underlying_risk` that affects both flagging/routing and downstream duration, then report the tipping point that erases the estimated effect | observed `risk_score`, `complexity`, `arrival_load`, timing variables | the sensitivity model spans realistic hidden-risk strength | plot effect estimates under increasing hidden confounding | even modest hidden confounding would erase the manual-review or queue-mediated effect |
| Are spillovers large enough to change the policy choice? | use an interference-aware design: randomize protected capacity by shift/day/queue cell and estimate own-case and spillover effects on unflagged cases | shift fixed effects, `arrival_load`, queue occupancy, case mix | partial interference within queue cells and stable cluster implementation | compare unflagged waits and cycle times across treated and control clusters | unflagged harm offsets flagged gains, or spillovers dominate own-case gains |

## 6. Policy comparison
| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0: current system | flagged triage stays very fast; unflagged triage stays slower | heavy flagged demand on manual review and senior queues continues | flagged cases remain long-cycle, especially high-complexity and escalated cases | baseline unknown; preserves current safeguards | current spillovers likely already present | strong descriptive evidence | use only as baseline comparator |
| P1: hide flag display, keep score/info visible | likely slows currently flagged triage because the explicit priority cue is removed | manual review and escalation may fall only partially if staff infer risk from score and other cues | ambiguous: some flagged cases may speed up overall if review declines, but triage benefit may be lost | moderate: standardized handling may degrade while discretionary behavior rises | could reduce concentration in flagged queues, but effects are hard to predict | weak to moderate | not the first pilot |
| P2: keep priority triage, remove automatic mandatory manual review | preserves fast triage for flagged cases | should substantially reduce manual-review demand and some escalations | likely the largest cycle-time reduction for medium-risk flagged cases | highest risk of missed issues, false approvals, or compliance failures | likely positive spillovers for all queues if safe | moderate | pilot only on marginal cases with strong quality guardrails |
| P3: keep routing, add protected manual-review capacity for flagged cases | triage unchanged | should reduce flagged manual-review waiting; queue harm depends on whether capacity is truly additive or tightly capped | moderate cycle-time reduction, especially under high load | lower risk than P2 because review is preserved | neutral to negative if capacity is additive; harmful if it just displaces others | moderate to strong | preferred first pilot |
| P4: keep manual review, change escalation criteria or add senior capacity | little triage change | reduces senior bottleneck if capacity is added, or lowers escalation volume if criteria tighten | targeted benefit mainly for high-complexity or escalated cases | moderate to high if criteria tighten; lower if adding senior capacity | can help all reviewed cases if true senior capacity is added | moderate | useful second pilot after learning whether manual waiting is the main bottleneck |

Preferred limited pilot: P3.

- Eligibility: flagged medium- and high-complexity cases arriving in pre-specified high-load or high-backlog windows.
- Randomization or assignment rule: randomize 4-hour shift blocks 1:1 to protected flagged-review capacity versus usual queue, blocked by time of day and expected load; use cluster assignment because queues create spillovers.
- Queue-capacity guardrail: cap protected capacity at one reviewer-equivalent or 20% of manual-review hours, and suspend treatment in any block where unflagged manual backlog exceeds a preset threshold.
- Primary and secondary outcomes: primary outcomes are flagged `manual_queue_wait` and flagged total `cycle_time`; secondary outcomes are unflagged wait/cycle spillovers, escalation rate, decision mix, reopen/appeal rate, and audit failure rate if available.
- Minimum process data needed: start and end timestamps for triage, review, escalation, and close; queue counts at each handoff; `risk_score`; `complexity`; `flag`; resource IDs; final decision; later audit or appeal outcomes.
- Stopping conditions: stop if unflagged waits rise materially, if audit or appeal problems worsen for flagged cases, or if the protected reviewer sits idle often enough to show that the capacity rule is poorly targeted.

def mean_or_nan(series: pd.Series):
    series = series.dropna()
    return np.nan if series.empty else round(series.mean(), 2)


def summarize(df_cases: pd.DataFrame, name: str, mask: pd.Series, stage_cols):
    sub = df_cases.loc[mask].copy()
    if len(sub) < 5:
        warnings.warn(f"Small subgroup: {name} has only {len(sub)} cases.")
    row = {"subgroup": name, "n": len(sub)}
    for col in stage_cols:
        support = int(sub[col].notna().sum())
        row[col] = mean_or_nan(sub[col])
        row[f"{col}_support"] = support
        if 0 < support < 5:
            warnings.warn(f"Low stage support: {name}, {col}, support={support}.")
    return row

## 8. Reversal conditions
1. If latent `U_underlying_risk` strongly predicts both mandatory downstream handling and long completion times even within observed `risk_score` and `complexity`, then the apparent flag-mediated delay is mostly confounding and my policy ranking would weaken or reverse.
2. If queue interference dominates so strongly that protected flagged capacity materially lengthens unflagged waits, then P3 would stop being the preferred pilot even if flagged cases speed up.
3. If reducing review or escalation causes materially worse audit, appeal, or error outcomes, then shorter cycle time should not be preferred and P2 or aggressive P4 changes would become unattractive.
4. If timestamps mainly reflect batched data entry, overnight backfilling, or completion logging rather than true stage start and end times, then the inferred manual and escalation waits are measurement artifacts rather than real queues.
5. If there is poor overlap near the operational flag rule, so flagged and unflagged cases occupy disjoint risk/complexity regions, then local causal comparisons are too weak and only descriptive conclusions remain credible.
6. If reviewers adapt behavior under the pilot by reconstructing hidden risk, escalating defensively, or changing documentation effort, then P1, P2, or P3 pilot effects may not transport to steady-state operation.

## 9. Final synthesis
Directly observed: flagged cases reach triage much faster, with mean arrival-to-triage-start of 0.24 hours versus 2.48 for unflagged, and mean arrival-to-triage-done of 0.84 versus 3.43. But flagged cases then almost always receive manual review, wait longer to start it, and are escalated more often, yielding a mean total cycle of 16.51 hours versus 8.17. High-complexity cases are slow regardless, so these are descriptive contrasts, not adjusted effects.

Plausible mechanism: the flag behaves like a policy bundle. It appears to buy fast access to a dedicated triage lane and then trigger heavy use of shared manual and senior queues. Manual-review occurrence and manual-review waiting are separate mediators, and escalation adds another major delay layer.

What remains unidentified is how much of the downstream delay is caused by the visible flag rather than latent risk omitted by `risk_score`, whether extra review and escalation improve correctness or compliance, and how much flagged routing delays unflagged cases through shared queues. That makes classical natural direct and indirect effects weak here.

The most useful next evidence comes from a guarded P3 pilot with shift-level randomized protected manual-review capacity for eligible flagged cases and explicit spillover monitoring on unflagged cases. That pilot preserves review while directly testing whether queue waiting, rather than review occurrence or escalation criteria, is the main reducible bottleneck.
