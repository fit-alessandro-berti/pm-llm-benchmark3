## 1. What is observed

Alpha and Beta gained branch-wide availability in April. Management chose them for high volume and historically slow service. Gamma went live in July after a readiness pilot. Delta and Epsilon had no formal rollout through August. Availability records an offer to the branch. The automated fast-path share counts cases completing one particular route and omits automated cases that escalated. It rose from 4% to 48% at Alpha, from 6% to 50% at Beta and from 4% to 32% at Gamma. Even months without formal availability show 4–6% on that path. The origin of those records needs verification.

The shared fraud desk opened in March. Routing campaigns sent about 10% more incoming cases to Alpha and Beta and about 5% more to Gamma following their respective launches. Productive staffing changed at four branches, and registered complexity rose at several branches around the May product change. Local practices could also change. Alpha’s median fell from 49.4 hours in March to 43.3 in August, while its p90 went from 99.4 to 101.2 hours. Both series are monthly branch quantiles. The panel lacks case durations, open-case ages, variant-specific waits and fraud-desk queue times. CSAT averages cover survey respondents, with roughly 20–45% responding and no branch-month response counts.

For A1, A2, A4 and A6, hold other branches’ offer schedules fixed and specify the same external routing, staffing and product policies under both alternatives. Let the branch’s own uptake, referral decisions and consequent queues respond to its offer. If extra routing or local redesign is inseparable from launch, the historical intervention is that whole package. Its effect and the effect of the software alone are different targets. Training, eligibility rules and software version are part of the intervention definition. For A3, formal availability is a poor instrument for use because offer timing was selected and it could affect even manually handled cases through queues.

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary · cohort-specific differences in changes | April and July launches separately. Estimate calendar-matched differences for each relative month against month −1. A calendar-matched change addresses two features that a simple before/after misses. Untreated branches also slowed around the May product change and sped up in July. Moreover, Gamma had only two months of formal exposure by August, when the April adopters had five. Comparing Gamma with an experienced adopter would subtract part of the experienced branch’s own response. A single two-way fixed-effects adoption coefficient can mix such comparisons and obscure heterogeneous effects. Separate cohorts preserve both the comparison date and the exposure age.

Equal branch weights answer a branch-policy question and can be audited with five branches. A longer history could support matching on workload and untreated trends. Here, a fitted synthetic comparison could match three early observations by chance. I would keep contemporaneous fast-path share, referrals and rework out of the total-offer adjustment set because they can transmit the effect. For arrivals, staffing and registered risk, establish from dated decisions which changes would have occurred without launch. Standardizing to fixed loads or case mix answers a separate operating question if rollout itself changed them. Few pre-periods, a pilot of uncertain timing and a shared desk leave the counterfactual uncertain even when both donors give similar answers.

## 5. Quantitative evidence

**Raw changes.** For each adopter, compare the mean of its first two enabled months with the mean of the immediately preceding two months. The entries are post minus pre. Rates use percentage points, CSAT uses scale points, and negative time changes mean faster service. These are equal-month changes in reported quantiles, with no pooling or arrival weighting of cases.

|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alpha | Feb–Mar → Apr–May | +24.5 | +0.75 | +7.90 | −0.60 | +2.25 | +0.010 |
| Beta | Feb–Mar → Apr–May | +24.5 | +2.00 | +7.75 | −1.25 | +2.05 | −0.015 |
| Gamma | May–Jun → Jul–Aug | +24.5 | −8.45 | −15.60 | −2.00 | +1.00 | +0.175 |

**Calendar comparisons.** Average Alpha and Beta equally for the April cohort. Subtract the mean change of Delta and Epsilon over the very same months. For example, the early cohort’s p90 rose 7.825 h against a 13.900 h rise for the two controls, a difference of −6.075 h. The July cohort saw an absolute fall against controls that also improved.

**Intensity.** Across the 40 branch-months, a linear regression with branch and calendar-month indicators associates a 10 pp higher completed automated fast-path share with −1.30 h in median, −1.84 h in p90, −0.53 pp in rework, +0.25 pp in escalation and +0.026 respondent CSAT points. The dose rises with exposure age, and cases on that path are selected by triage and successful completion. Within Alpha, for example, the share rose from 25% in April to 39% in June while p90 rose from 97.3 to 119.9 h. The slope cannot price the benefit of sending another ten percent of cases through automation.

**Pre-trends and reference dates.** Against the two controls, the April cohort’s median gap changed by +0.35 h from January to February and −0.65 h from February to March. The cohort average hides opposite January-to-February changes at Alpha (−1.65 h relative) and Beta (+2.35 h). Gamma’s relative median rose 1.45 h from April to May and fell 0.90 h from May to June. Selection on historical slowness makes some spontaneous recovery plausible. Three early pre-months and Gamma’s possible pilot exposure give little power to distinguish recovery from treatment. Using only the last pre-month and launch month gives median/p90 contrasts of −2.25/−4.10 h for April and −3.65/−6.70 h for July.

**Counterfactual sensitivity.** Using Delta or Epsilon alone gives April median contrasts of −4.73 to −3.23 h and p90 contrasts of −7.48 to −4.68 h. For July the ranges are −5.10 to −3.25 h and −9.40 to −5.90 h. Both donors share the fraud desk. If the untreated adopter change would have differed from their average by an additional amount *d*, the offer contrast is the reported difference minus *d*. An unmeasured relative improvement of 3.975 median hours or 6.075 p90 hours would erase the respective April contrasts. As an explicit stress test, allow drift of ±5 median hours and ±10 p90 hours, magnitudes comparable to observed control movements across these windows. Across cohorts this gives approximately −9.2 to +1.1 h and −17.7 to +4.0 h, respectively, rounded outward. These are assumption-driven scenarios, not confidence limits or empirical bounds on confounding.

For CSAT, suppose illustratively that exactly 30% respond in every cell. If the nonrespondent change relative to controls were −0.05 points in each cohort, the all-customer contrasts would be 0.30 × 0.0825 + 0.70 × (−0.05) = −0.010 in April and −0.004 in July, despite positive respondent contrasts. Actual response fractions and nonrespondent ratings are unknown.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | High-volume, historically slow branches went first | Regression from unusually bad performance can exaggerate time savings | Recover longer prehistory, assignment scores and dates of management decisions | Restrict claims to studied cohorts and randomize the next offers |
| Nonparallel trends | Untreated branches differ in baseline speed and response to the product change | A faster underlying adopter recovery favors rollout. The reverse masks gains | Compare calendar-matched leads, placebo dates and individual donors | Use drift scenarios and withdraw causal magnitude claims |
| Time-varying confounding | Campaigns, product changes and local redesign coincide with rollout | Rising burden can hide gains, concurrent process improvements can inflate them | Obtain campaign rules, dated work-practice logs, routing origins and untreated product strata | Evaluate the combined package where changes are inseparable |
| Endogenous intensity | A completed automated fast path requires eligibility, use and success | Selection of easy cases can exaggerate benefits. A backlog can depress share and lengthen times together | Log eligibility, offers, overrides, escalated automation and reasons at registration and triage | Use assignment for policy inference. Avoid per-case savings from the share slope |
| Shared-desk spillovers | Treated referrals use capacity serving controls | Longer control waits can exaggerate relative gains. Common queue harm can disappear from the contrast | Link referral source, queue arrivals, wait, service and backlog across all branches | Add network harm to the decision and vary coverage before full expansion |
| Staffing | Alpha lost two FTEs, Beta gained one, Gamma and Delta also changed | Alpha’s loss can mask gains, Beta’s gain can mimic them, a faster Delta can attenuate gains | Compare actual versus planned hours by role, shift and referral pool | Make any transfer claim conditional on capacity |

## 7. Effect assessment

A single percentage reduction is indefensible. The data support comparisons of changes in specific branch quantiles at stated exposure ages. A network percentage would also need a target case population, its counterfactual distribution and a specified denominator.

Scores judge the strength of evidence for attributing the indicated direction to availability in adopters’ first two months, on a subjective 0–100 scale. They are not probabilities from a fitted model. The time ranges use the differential-drift scenarios in §5. The rate ranges show the spread across cohort and single-donor specifications with zero additional drift. They share potential confounding. For example, an independent adopter-specific reduction of 2.05 pp in recorded loops or increase of 0.90 pp in referrals would erase the corresponding April contrast.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

expand with an embedded evaluation

Start with eligible teams in both Delta and Epsilon. This includes two different risk mixes and preserves a contemporaneous comparison within each branch. Use at least 12 weeks of baseline measurements to pair operationally separate teams within branch on shift, registration risk, volume and prior delay. Randomly assign one team per pair to receive access and training first. Keep its partner on usual triage for at least 12 concurrent weeks. Fix eligibility and the incoming routing rule before assignment, retain manual rescue, and record cross-team help and crossovers. Analyze all arrivals by assigned team. Follow entry cohorts through closure or account for censoring and report the ages of cases still open.

Log registration, eligibility, actual triage method, overrides, specialist and fraud referrals, queue entry, service start and finish, information requests, resolution, closure and reopens under a linked case ID. Record arrivals, closures, productive hours by role, queue length and age by shift. Estimate median and p90 separately and check resolution accuracy on a blinded sample. Track desk waits, referral sources and backlog across all five branches, comparing network trends with prespecified baseline levels by shift and risk. Collect survey invitations and responses and sample nonrespondents. The randomized comparison measures an offer at the trial’s coverage level. Watch for network deterioration even if early teams outperform deferred teams.

Agree thresholds before assignment. For example, release the deferred wave only if the estimated median gain is at least two hours and its assignment-based interval excludes zero. Require one-sided 95% harm limits below +2 hours for case p90 and desk p90 wait, below +1 pp for rework, and above −0.10 points for customer ratings after nonresponse sensitivity. Review queues and misroutes weekly. Suspend automated handling of the affected case class immediately on confirmation of a high-severity error. If too few independent teams or unresolved spillovers leave these margins uncertain, maintain limited coverage and add randomized waves as capacity permits. Recheck the desk guardrail after each increase in coverage before enabling the remaining volume.

assert d.set_index(keys).index.sort_values().equals(panel_keys.sort_values())
assert d.notna().all().all()
d["m"] = pd.to_datetime(d.month).dt.month
d["g"] = d.branch.map(offers)
assert d.automation.eq((d.m >= d.g).astype(int)).all()
assert (d[["arrivals", "fte", "median_cycle_h"]] > 0).all().all()
assert d.p90_cycle_h.ge(d.median_cycle_h).all() and d.csat_1to5.between(1, 5).all()
assert d.filter(regex="_pct$").apply(lambda s: s.between(0, 100)).all().all()
print("Fast-path shares recorded before/without formal availability:",
      sorted(d.loc[d.automation.eq(0), "automated_fast_path_pct"].unique()))
for y in ys:
    print(y, d.pivot(index="month", columns="branch", values=y), sep="\n")

# Relative to first formal availability. Never-adopters have missing event time
# and zero indicators. The omitted reference for adopters is event month -1.
d["event"] = (d.m - d.g).astype("Int64")
for k in sorted(d.event.dropna().unique()):
    if k != -1:
        name = f"lead_{-k}" if k < 0 else f"lag_{k}"
        d[name] = d.event.eq(k).fillna(False).astype(int)

fast = "automated_fast_path_pct"
def mean_before_after(branch_names, g):
    a = d.loc[d.branch.isin(branch_names)]
    cols = [fast] + ys
    pre = a.loc[a.m.isin([g - 2, g - 1]), cols].mean()
    post = a.loc[a.m.isin([g, g + 1]), cols].mean()
    return pd.DataFrame({"pre": pre, "post": post, "change": post - pre})

for b, g in offers.items():
    print(b, "unweighted averages of monthly summaries", sep=" | ")
    print(mean_before_after([b], g).round(4))

print("WARNING: At most five branch clusters (only three or four in cohort fits). "
      "Clustered standard errors are unstable. Do not use ordinary significance cutoffs.")
print("WARNING: An average or regression of monthly medians or p90s is not a "
      "pooled case quantile or an individual time saving.")

def association(frame, outcome, term):
    fit = smf.ols(f"{outcome} ~ {term} + C(branch) + C(month)", data=frame).fit(
        cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": frame.branch, "use_correction": True})
    # Only the target coefficient's diagonal is needed from this low-rank covariance.
    variance = fit.cov_params().loc[term, term]
    se = variance ** 0.5 if variance >= 0 else float("nan")
    return round(fit.params[term], 4), round(se, 4)

# Cohort comparisons require parallel untreated changes in the measured outcome,
# no pilot effects, stable measurement and no differential spillovers/cointerventions
# for a causal reading. The data do not establish these conditions. Calendar
# matching absorbs common shocks only. Use each never-adopter with equal weight.
for g in sorted(set(offers.values())):
    cohort = [b for b in offers if offers[b] == g]
    treated = mean_before_after(cohort, g)["change"]
    donors = mean_before_after(controls, g)["change"]
    gap = treated - donors
    print("Cohort", g, pd.DataFrame({"adopter change": treated,
                                    "control change": donors, "difference": gap}), sep="\n")
    for c in controls:
        print("Single donor", c, (treated - mean_before_after([c], g)["change"])[ys].round(4))

    # Calendar contrasts by relative month, including placebo leads. Reference g-1.
    by_month = d[d.branch.isin(cohort)].groupby("m")[ys].mean()
    by_control = d[d.branch.isin(controls)].groupby("m")[ys].mean()
    series_gap = by_month - by_control
    event_diff = series_gap - series_gap.loc[g - 1]
    event_diff.index = (event_diff.index - g).rename("relative month")
    print("Descriptive event series", g, event_diff.round(4), sep="\n")

    # Descriptive binary-availability panel fits, separately by cohort, 2+2 months.
    local = d[d.branch.isin(cohort + controls) & d.m.between(g - 2, g + 1)]
    for y in ys:
        print("Local offer association (coefficient, clustered SE)", g, y,
              association(local, y, "automation"))
    for y, drift in [("median_cycle_h", 5), ("p90_cycle_h", 10)]:
        print("Untreated differential-drift scenario", g, y,
              (round(gap[y] - drift, 4), round(gap[y] + drift, 4)))

# Descriptive intensity model only. A causal slope would require exogenous
# increments in share, comparable cases and versions, no time-varying selection
# or queue interference, and a common linear response. A completed path is
# selected after registration. Month and branch effects do not solve selection.
d["share_per_10pp"] = d[fast] / 10
for y in ys:
    print("Share association per +10 pp (coefficient, clustered SE)", y,
          association(d, y, "share_per_10pp"))

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Every adopter recorded a 21 pp jump in completed automated fast paths at launch. Alpha and Beta’s p90s rose in their first two rollout months relative to baseline, while Gamma’s fell.
- Suggestive: Against the same calendar months at Delta and Epsilon, both cohorts had more favorable median, p90 and recorded rework changes, alongside a larger increase in specialist referrals.
- Conditionally causal: If the controls represent each cohort’s untreated changes, including equivalent exposure to external shocks, with stable measurement, no anticipation and negligible differential spillovers, the offer reduced the average branch-month median by about four hours in the first two months at the adopting branches.
- Not supported: Automation alone reduces cycle time by a single X% across branches, creates net service capacity, or guarantees faster resolution and higher satisfaction for customers after full expansion.
