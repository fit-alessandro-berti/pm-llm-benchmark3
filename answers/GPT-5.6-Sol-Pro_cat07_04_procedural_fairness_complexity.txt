# 1. Fairness dimensions

Procedural fairness asks whether applicants receive **relevant, proportionate, consistent, understandable, and timely treatment**, not whether every group reaches the same outcome. Different routes can be justified when needs or risks differ, but necessary support should not become a procedural penalty.

| Dimension                                | Evidence                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Fairness interpretation                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Outcome parity**                       | Approval is 78% for Group A, 63% for B, 69% for C, and 58% for D.                                                                                                                                                   | The raw gaps of 15, 9, and 20 percentage points are disparities, not proof of discrimination. Group B has more incomplete documentation and language needs; Group C more accommodation requests; Group D higher measured risk and prior-case counts.                                                                              |

The concern is whether applicants face avoidable repetition, fragmented ownership, duplicate requests, or scrutiny beyond what comparable cases receive.                                                                                                                     |

The evidence therefore points to different conclusions for different dimensions: raw outcome parity is substantially affected by case mix, while timeliness, handovers, resource concentration, and residual routing differences remain independently concerning.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

## Define separate estimands

One group coefficient cannot answer all fairness questions. The analysis should estimate at least five distinct quantities:

1. **Total experienced disparity:** the actual difference in end-to-end burden between groups, including the consequences of language, accommodation, channel, and regional access. This should not control away the support routes whose burden is under investigation.

2. **Like-for-like decision disparity:** approval and review differences among cases with comparable recorded eligibility-relevant characteristics.

3. **Need-conditional routing consistency:** whether applicants with the same language, accommodation, documentation, or risk need are routed similarly.

4. **Within-route performance disparity:** whether comparable cases entering the same route receive similar waits, handovers, rework, and review intensity.

5. **Resource-mediated disparity:** how much of the group gap is associated with assignment to particular workers, specialist pools, regions, or handover chains.

“Disparity” should mean a measured difference. “Legitimate routing” should mean a documented rule based on a relevant need, applied consistently and proportionately. “Procedural unfairness” can arise when a justified route has avoidable burdens. A finding of intentional or direct discrimination would require stronger evidence about decision rules, discretion, policy violations, and possibly qualitative records.

## Matching and stratification

For the principal like-for-like comparison, use exact or coarsened-exact matching where feasible on:

* month and region;
* channel;
* household-size band;
* prior-case-count band;
* risk-score band;
* documentation completeness as recorded at a clearly defined baseline time.

Within support-specific analyses, also stratify on the actual need:

* language preference or a validated `needs_language_support` indicator;
* `disability_accommodation_requested`;
* high-risk status when assessing fraud routing.

Use overlap weighting, entropy balancing, or propensity-score matching only after checking common support and covariate balance. Group A should not automatically be forced to serve as the comparator for rare support routes: only 2% of A needs language support and 4% requests accommodation. For those analyses, a pooled comparison among all applicants with the same need may be more defensible.

Cases outside common support should be reported as a separate population rather than extrapolated through a model.

## Treat variables according to their causal position

The analysis should not indiscriminately “control for everything.”

* **Baseline context:** region, month, household size, and prior cases are plausible adjustment variables.
* **Potentially constrained choices:** channel may capture operational differences but also unequal digital access. Estimate both total disparities without controlling for channel and within-channel disparities.
* **Potentially biased or endogenous controls:** `risk_score_band` may encode prior disparities, while `documentation_completeness` may be partly caused by language accessibility, form design, or prior support. Estimates should be shown both with and without these variables.
* **Process mediators:** document requests, supervisor review, resource assignment, variants, handovers, waits, appeal, and rework should not be placed in the primary approval model as ordinary confounders. They are mechanisms to be studied separately.

## Path and variant comparison

Within matched or weighted strata:

* compare normalized variant shares, not raw counts;
* estimate conditional probabilities for important transitions, such as completeness check to document request, risk screening to fraud review, and eligibility assessment to supervisor review;
* compare outcome, appeal, and rework rates within the same variant;
* compare duration within the same variant and direct path;
* examine whether groups reach the same activity through different preceding paths or handover chains.

The cycle-time outcome should be split into at least:

* submission to first identity verification;
* submission to first decision draft;
* submission to first applicant notification;
* notification to benefit activation or closure;
* post-decision appeal duration.

Otherwise, the higher frequency of V6 can mechanically increase total cycle time for B and D even when the pre-decision process is the main object of interest.

## Resource-assignment comparison

Model assignment to a worker or organizational role conditional on:

* region and calendar date;
* channel;
* documented case mix;
* required language or accommodation capability;
* risk route;
* available workload measures.

Then distinguish:

1. **selection into resources:** whether comparable cases are disproportionately assigned to overloaded or lower-capacity resources;
2. **performance within resources:** whether group differences remain among cases handled by the same worker or team;
3. **resource composition:** whether group gaps disappear after standardizing all groups to the same resource-capacity distribution.

Use multilevel models with resource and region random effects where sample sizes permit. Compare workload, handover degree, case concentration, tail delay, rework, and approval variation. Standard errors should account for clustering by worker and region.

## Waiting-time decomposition

For each group, decompose total pre-decision duration into:

* differences in how frequently each route is used;
* differences in elapsed time within the same route;
* differences attributable to handovers;
* residual time outside the major measured paths.

A useful standardized decomposition is:

[
\Delta T_g =
\sum_p(\pi_{gp}-\pi_{ref,p})\mu_{ref,p}
+
\sum_p\pi_{gp}(\mu_{gp}-\mu_{ref,p}),
]

where (\pi_{gp}) is group (g)'s probability of route or path (p), and (\mu_{gp}) is its matched mean or median duration on that path. The first term represents **route-mix composition**; the second represents **within-route performance**.

The supplied log contains only a completion timestamp. Therefore, direct-succession intervals combine queueing, active work, applicant response time, weekends, and possible unlogged activity. True service-versus-waiting decomposition requires activity start timestamps or lifecycle events, work calendars, and ideally queue-entry timestamps.

## Sensitivity analysis

Report a sequence of estimates:

* minimal adjustment: month and region;
* plus channel and household size;
* plus prior-case count;
* plus risk and documentation completeness;
* excluding risk;
* excluding documentation completeness;
* treating documentation completeness as a mediator rather than a confounder;
* replacing the risk band with audited underlying risk inputs, where available.

Also test alternative definitions of completeness, different risk-band cutoffs, business-calendar versus calendar time, first-decision versus final-closure duration, and inclusion of open or censored cases. Use bootstrap confidence intervals clustered at resource level, report practical-effect thresholds, and correct or hierarchically model the many path and subgroup comparisons.

## Region and channel checks

At minimum, estimate group differences separately for:

* each region, with particular attention to the two regions with extra handovers;
* online versus in-person cases;
* region-by-channel combinations;
* applicants requiring versus not requiring each support type.

Partial pooling should be used for small cells. A large overall group coefficient driven by two regions would call for a regional operational remedy; a stable difference across regions and channels would support a broader procedural explanation.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

## Group B

Group B has the clearest combination of route-mix differences and residual operational burden.

* Its fast-path V1 share is 19%, compared with 42% for A.
* V2, involving missing-document requests, is 34% versus 24%.
* V4, involving language support, is 24% versus 1%.
* V6, involving appeal, is 8% versus 4%.
* Its adjusted probabilities are 9.5 points higher for document requests and 8.0 points higher for supervisor review.
* Its adjusted cycle time remains 2.6 days longer, and adjusted approval is 5.5 points lower.

Much of the higher V2 and V4 incidence is plausibly associated with the 46% incomplete-document share, 57% language-support need, and 38% in-person share. That explains why different routing occurs; it does not explain why the route must be slow.

The important within-process delays are:

* 1.4 versus 0.8 days from submission to verification;
* 1.6 versus 0.4 days from completeness check to sending a missing-document request;
* 3.8 days from language support to eligibility assessment;
* 1.9 versus 1.0 days from supervisor review to decision draft.

The concentration of B cases among seven bilingual workers and overloaded regional officers, plus general-to-bilingual-to-general handovers, supports a specialist-capacity and coordination explanation. These are procedural concerns even when the language contact itself is required.

The adjusted supervisor-review increase is also concerning because it remains after the preliminary case-mix controls. It could represent unmeasured complexity, inconsistent escalation thresholds, or caution by overloaded workers; the current evidence cannot distinguish those explanations.

## Group C

The incidence of V5 is largely explicable by need: 62% of C requests accommodation, and 31% follows the accommodation path, compared with 2% of A. Accommodation review is therefore not itself evidence of unfair treatment.

The procedural concern is the operation of that route:

* V5 has a median duration of 21.6 days, compared with 5.8 days for V1.
* The accommodation-review-to-eligibility interval is 4.7 days.
* Adjusted cycle time remains 4.1 days longer, the largest adjusted time disparity.
* Supervisor review remains 5.5 points more likely.
* C has the highest raw 90th-percentile duration, 38.8 days, and the highest rework rate, 24%.

Five specialists may be an inherently narrow capacity pool, and two also perform supervisor reviews. This creates possible self-congestion and serial dependencies. The evidence supports a structural concern that exercising an accommodation right is associated with a longer and more fragmented procedure. It does not establish that individual workers treat C applicants adversely.

The residual approval difference of 2.2 points is smaller than the B difference and cannot be interpreted without uncertainty intervals. Timeliness and rework are currently the stronger concerns for C.

## Group D

Group D's high V3 share—28% versus 9% for A—is broadly consistent with its 51% high-risk share and 49% high-prior-case-count share. Subject to the validity of the risk score, additional fraud review is the most clearly justified group-specific routing pattern.

Correspondingly:

* the raw approval difference of 20 points falls to an adjusted difference of 3.1 points;
* adjusted cycle time is only 0.9 days longer;
* adjusted document-request and supervisor-review differences are relatively small;
* the risk-screening-to-fraud-review wait is 2.8 days versus 2.2 for A.

The main unresolved signal is appeal burden. D has a 16% raw appeal rate, a 13% V6 share, and an adjusted appeal probability 5.7 points above A. This could arise from legitimately more adverse decisions, disagreement over risk evidence, poor explanations, score errors, or inconsistent initial decisions. Appeal outcomes and reversal reasons are necessary before deciding among these mechanisms.

Because the legitimacy of D's routing depends heavily on `risk_score_band`, historical-bias and calibration audits are central rather than optional.

## Cross-cutting disparities

The most important system-level pattern is that the two rights-preserving routes are also among the slowest:

* V4 language support: 19.2-day median;
* V5 accommodation review: 21.6-day median;
* V1 fast route: 5.8-day median.

Some difference is unavoidable because V4 and V5 contain additional work. The evidence does not identify how much is necessary service time. However, explicit waits of 3.8 and 4.7 days, specialist scarcity, and extra handovers show that the entire difference cannot simply be labeled applicant complexity.

The lack of support-specific service-level targets reinforces the concern: the organization has recognized the right to support without ensuring comparable timeliness when that right is exercised.

The variant summaries are abbreviated, so V2 or V4 should not be interpreted as a denial merely because activation is not shown. Similarly, V6 is partly a post-decision route. Outcome-specific path analysis and separate first-decision timing are required.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

**Measurement bias.** `documentation_completeness` may be subjective, inconsistently recorded, or measured after an applicant has already encountered inaccessible forms. A later completeness value must not be used as though it were a baseline characteristic. Channel may also be constrained by disability, language, internet access, or local office availability rather than freely chosen.

**Historical bias in risk scores.** `risk_score_band` is a model output, not an objective ground truth. Its training labels, features, missing-data treatment, thresholds, and override practices are unknown. A model can be calibrated overall while producing systematically different false-positive or referral rates for particular groups.

**Missing applicant needs.** The log lacks the type and intensity of accommodation, language proficiency, literacy, urgency of need, availability of an advocate, digital access, transportation constraints, and whether documents are realistically obtainable. Broad group labels cannot represent these needs, and applicants may have intersecting language, disability, regional, and channel disadvantages.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Daily workload cannot resolve whether a case entered a queue during an intraday surge, whether a specialist was absent, how many cases were actively being worked, or how complex the worker's other cases were. Worker skill, tenure, schedule, language proficiency, decision quality, and case-allocation rules are absent.

**Insufficient temporal semantics.** Only `time:timestamp` is listed. Without activity starts, queue-entry timestamps, lifecycle transitions, and work calendars, elapsed direct-following time cannot be cleanly divided into active service, internal waiting, applicant response, or nonworking time.

**Appeal quality is unmeasured.** `appeal_flag` does not show the appeal ground, whether assistance was available, whether the decision was reversed, whether new evidence was introduced, how long review took, or whether the initial explanation was understandable. A high appeal rate can indicate both accessible redress and poor first-instance quality.

**Outcome quality is too coarse.** `case_outcome` does not capture partial awards, benefit amount, timeliness of activation, accuracy of the legal rationale, consistency with similar decisions, explanation quality, respectful treatment, or applicant understanding. Equal approval rates would therefore not establish procedural fairness.

**Possible selection and censoring.** It is unclear whether open cases, withdrawn applications, incomplete appeals, and cases not yet mature at extraction are included. Excluding unfinished cases can understate the longest delays and affect groups differently.

**Model uncertainty is missing.** The preliminary adjusted estimates have no standard errors, confidence intervals, balance diagnostics, overlap assessment, functional-form checks, or information about interactions. Small residual differences cannot be treated as established effects without these diagnostics.

**Normative rules are incomplete.** The log shows what occurred, not the legal basis for fraud referral, supervisor escalation, repeated documentation requests, or acceptable accommodation timing. A frequent pattern is not necessarily a fair rule, and an infrequent pattern is not necessarily a violation.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Support steps should be preserved and improved, not removed to reduce measured cycle time.

| Mitigation                                    | Proposed design                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Side effects and safeguards                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Add language-support capacity**             | Hire or contract additional qualified bilingual workers, cross-train selected generalists, provide scheduled surge coverage, and maintain backup coverage by region.                                                | Cross-training can dilute expertise; contractors may create confidentiality or continuity risks; unused specialist capacity can be costly. Monitor translation quality and worker workload, not only throughput.      |
| **Add accommodation-review capacity**         | Increase specialist staffing, separate specialist and supervisor duties where they create serial queues, and create backup capacity for common accommodation types.                                                 | Fragmenting roles may create new handovers, while generalizing reviews may reduce specialist quality. Use competency standards and outcome audits.                                                                    |
| **Create support-step service-level targets** | Set explicit targets for language-contact-to-eligibility and accommodation-review-to-eligibility time, alongside an end-to-end target that continues to count support time.                                         | A narrow target may encourage superficial or rushed support, queue jumping, or displacement of other applicants. Include quality, tail-time, and rework measures and do not “pause the clock” to hide support delays. |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers**      | Retain a persistent case owner; use warm handovers with shared notes; embed or virtually consult specialists; parallelize support with other eligible work where legally safe.                                      | Persistent ownership can overload generalists, parallel work can require later correction, and embedded specialists may have lower utilization. Pilot by region and monitor both delay and error.                     |
| **Improve document assistance**               | Provide multilingual and accessible document checklists, early completeness assistance, clear examples, and a single consolidated request rather than multiple fragmented requests.                                 | More intensive intake may lengthen the first interaction and require additional staff. Overly standardized lists may fail unusual cases; retain a route for individualized assistance.                                |
| **Improve decision explanations**             | Produce translated and accessible notices, state the evidence and rule used, distinguish missing evidence from ineligibility, and offer clear appeal instructions.                                                  | Better explanations may initially increase drafting time and translation cost. Templates can become formulaic or inaccurate, so use quality review and applicant comprehension testing.                               |
| **Monitor adjusted procedural disparities**   | Maintain dashboards for transition probabilities, route-specific median and tail waits, handovers, resource concentration, rework, appeals, and approval differences after multiple adjustment sets.                | Small cells can be unstable or identifying; dashboards can invite metric gaming. Apply privacy thresholds, confidence intervals, audit trails, and substantive rather than purely statistical alert thresholds.       |
| **Audit `risk_score_band`**                   | Test calibration, false-positive referral rates, overrides, score distributions, and fraud-review yield by group, region, and channel. Review historical labels and features and compare results without the score. | Removing predictive inputs without alternatives can increase missed fraud or workload. Remediation should seek equally valid, less biased evidence rather than simply suppressing group differences.                  |
| **Audit `documentation_completeness`**        | Review assessor agreement, missingness, timing of measurement, language/channel effects, and whether equivalent documents receive equivalent classifications.                                                       | More structured assessment can reduce discretion but become inflexible. Preserve documented exceptions and monitor whether standardization creates new burdens.                                                       |
| **Standardize escalation thresholds**         | Require coded reasons for supervisor and fraud referrals, periodically review matched cases, and provide feedback where resources apply thresholds inconsistently.                                                  | Excessive standardization can suppress justified professional judgment. Permit overrides with documented reasons rather than prohibiting discretion.                                                                  |
| **Use appeal and rework feedback loops**      | Track reversals and repeated evidence requests back to the original route, notice, worker, and rule. Prioritize recurrent system causes rather than blaming individual applicants.                                  | Reversal-based performance measures can discourage difficult cases or legitimate adverse decisions. Adjust for case mix and use review findings for learning rather than automatic discipline.                        |

# 7. PM4Py analysis plan

The current PM4Py public API provides top-level interfaces for DataFrame formatting, variants, DFG and performance-DFG discovery, case and path filtering, organizational networks, duration statistics, service/waiting insertion, and declarative discovery. The code below uses those public-style interfaces; exact aliases should be pinned and tested against the installed PM4Py version. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][1])

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from collections import defaultdict
from typing import Any

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py


CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACT = "concept:name"
TS = "time:timestamp"
RES = "org:resource"
GROUP = "applicant_group"

CASE_ATTRIBUTES = [
    GROUP,
    "channel",
    "language_preference",
    "risk_score_band",
    "documentation_completeness",
    "household_size_band",
    "disability_accommodation_requested",
    "prior_case_count",
    "region",
    "case_outcome",
    "appeal_flag",
]


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Read, validate, and format
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

df = pd.read_csv("benefits_event_log.csv")
df[TS] = pd.to_datetime(df[TS], utc=True, errors="raise")
df = df.sort_values([CASE, TS, ACT], kind="mergesort").reset_index(drop=True)

required = {CASE, ACT, TS, RES, *CASE_ATTRIBUTES}
missing = required.difference(df.columns)
if missing:
    raise ValueError(f"Missing required columns: {sorted(missing)}")

# Check which purported case attributes vary inside cases.
# Do not silently propagate a later risk/completeness value backward.
variation = df.groupby(CASE)[CASE_ATTRIBUTES].nunique(dropna=False)
varying = variation.columns[(variation > 1).any(axis=0)].tolist()
if varying:
    print("Attributes varying within cases; inspect measurement timing:", varying)

# Stable identity/context fields should normally be invariant.
stable_fields = [
    GROUP, "channel", "language_preference",
    "household_size_band",
    "disability_accommodation_requested",
    "region",
]
stable_variation = df.groupby(CASE)[stable_fields].nunique(dropna=False)
if (stable_variation > 1).any().any():
    raise ValueError("Unexpected within-case changes in stable case attributes")

log = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACT,
    timestamp_key=TS,
)


def filter_cases_by_attribute(
    event_log: pd.DataFrame,
    attribute: str,
    values: list[Any],
) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """
    level='case' retains complete cases with at least one matching event.
    It avoids trimming individual events out of the selected cases.
    """
    return pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
        event_log,
        attribute,
        values,
        level="case",
        retain=True,
        case_id_key=CASE,
    )


groups = sorted(log[GROUP].dropna().unique())
group_logs = {
    g: filter_cases_by_attribute(log, GROUP, [g])
    for g in groups
}


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Variant distributions and variant-specific slices
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

def count_variant_value(value: Any) -> int:
    # PM4Py may return integer counts for DataFrames or trace lists
    # for EventLog representations.
    return int(value) if isinstance(value, (int, np.integer)) else len(value)


variant_results = {}

for group, glog in group_logs.items():
    variants = pm4py.get_variants(
        glog,
        activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TS,
        case_id_key=CASE,
    )
    n_cases = glog[CASE].nunique()

    shares = {
        variant: count_variant_value(count) / n_cases
        for variant, count in variants.items()
    }

    variant_results[group] = {
        "counts": variants,
        "shares": dict(
            sorted(shares.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
        ),
    }

# Example: inspect the two most common discovered variants for Group B.
b_variants = variant_results["Group B"]["counts"]
top_b = sorted(
    b_variants,
    key=lambda v: count_variant_value(b_variants[v]),
    reverse=True,
)[:2]

top_b_log = pm4py.filter_variants(
    group_logs["Group B"],
    top_b,
    retain=True,
    activity_key=ACT,
    timestamp_key=TS,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

# Compare outcome, appeal, handover count, and duration within each
# discovered variant rather than comparing variant frequency alone.


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Frequency DFGs and performance DFGs
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

dfg_results = {}

for group, glog in group_logs.items():
    frequency_dfg, starts, ends = pm4py.discover_dfg(
        glog,
        activity_key=ACT,
        timestamp_key=TS,
        case_id_key=CASE,
    )

    performance_dfg, perf_starts, perf_ends = (
        pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(
            glog,
            activity_key=ACT,
            timestamp_key=TS,
            case_id_key=CASE,
            perf_aggregation_key="all",
            # Supply an actual work calendar before setting
            # business_hours=True.
            business_hours=False,
        )
    )

    dfg_results[group] = {
        "frequency": frequency_dfg,
        "performance": performance_dfg,
        "starts": starts,
        "ends": ends,
    }

# Normalize frequency edges by the number of eligible source events
# or cases. Raw DFG edge counts are inappropriate because group sizes differ.


# ------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Direct path elapsed-time decomposition
# ------------------------------------------------------------------

edges = log[[CASE, ACT, TS, GROUP, "region", "channel", RES]].copy()
edges["next_activity"] = edges.groupby(CASE)[ACT].shift(-1)
edges["next_timestamp"] = edges.groupby(CASE)[TS].shift(-1)
edges["next_resource"] = edges.groupby(CASE)[RES].shift(-1)

edges["elapsed_days"] = (
    edges["next_timestamp"] - edges[TS]
).dt.total_seconds() / 86400.0

edges["path"] = list(zip(edges[ACT], edges["next_activity"]))
edges["handover"] = (
    edges["next_resource"].notna()
    & edges[RES].notna()
    & (edges[RES] != edges["next_resource"])
)

edge_summary = (
    edges.dropna(subset=["next_activity", "elapsed_days"])
    .groupby([GROUP, "region", "channel", "path"], observed=True)
    .agg(
        path_occurrences=(CASE, "size"),
```

PM4Py's performance DFG measures elapsed time between directly following events; handover and working-together functions provide organizational-network views; log skeletons and DECLARE discovery identify descriptive behavioral constraints. None of these alone establishes a legal or fairness norm. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][2])

The PM4Py outputs should feed a case-level statistical layer containing matched or weighted approval models, survival or quantile models for time, multinomial models for route selection, and multilevel resource effects. Process discovery identifies *where* disparities arise; causal attribution requires the additional design described above.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The process does **not** provide enough evidence to conclude that the raw approval differences are discriminatory. Case mix plausibly explains a substantial portion of the differences, particularly Group D's fraud routing and much of Groups B and C's support routing.

Nevertheless, the process appears **procedurally unfair in specific operational dimensions**, especially for applicants dependent on language or accommodation support. Groups B and C experience material residual delays, specialist-capacity constraints, extra handovers, elevated review or document burdens, and substantially worse tail times. These burdens are not made fair merely because the underlying support steps are legitimate.

The most calibrated conclusion is therefore: **credible evidence of structural unfairness in timeliness and resource allocation; unresolved conditional disparities in decisions and escalation; indeterminate evidence regarding intentional or direct discrimination.**
