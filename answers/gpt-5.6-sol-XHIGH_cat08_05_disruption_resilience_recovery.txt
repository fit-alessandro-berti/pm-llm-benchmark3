# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

An adaptation is resilient when it:

1. Preserves an essential service outcome.
2. Retains mandatory controls or uses an approved equivalent control.
3. Generates contemporaneous, attributable evidence.
4. Is time-bounded and recoverable.
5. Does not transfer unmeasured harm to another customer group or to the post-incident audit queue.

A process deviation is therefore not automatically bad. Emergency triage, a hospital fast lane, deferring bulk orders, and adding an invoice hold are legitimate variants even though they reduce conformance to the baseline model. Conversely, throughput is not resilient if orders move only because authorization, release, custody, or temperature controls disappeared.

**Strongest evidence of true resilience**

- **B** was a genuine alternate authorization control, subject to list freshness and evidence quality.
- The controlled portion of **A**—hospital priority coupled with B and the required product controls—protected the mission without necessarily lowering standards.
- The all-controlled-substance component of **F** was directionally resilient, although the missing dual-control rate shows that execution or evidence was incomplete.
- **I and J** are valid capacity-allocation strategies when rules are explicit, aging-sensitive, and fair.
- **K** is a necessary recovery control because manual reservations cannot safely remain detached from system inventory.
- **L** is valuable as an exception-detection mechanism, but not as proof that the original shipment was compliant.

**Predominantly risky shortcuts**

- **C** explicitly substitutes assumption for authorization. It is not made acceptable by the product being ambient or the customer being familiar.
- **G** eliminates the normal dispatch event and creates a chain-of-custody gap. An offline equivalent is needed before physical handoff.
- The direct **manual pack  dispatch without scan** branch is particularly concerning because it suggests both missing release and missing dispatch evidence.
- Applying **H** as the sole POD for controlled or cold-chain products is inadequate.

**Conditional or ambiguous adaptations**

- **A** is resilient as a priority lane but noncompliant when priority is used to bypass authorization or product controls.
- **D** is defensible immediately after T1 but unsafe once the snapshot is stale or the data-integrity impact is uncertain.
- **E** is a valid continuity mechanism only if unique IDs, lot capture, duplicate prevention, and accountable sign-offs are built in.
- **F** is useful as a risk-focused gate, but sampling cannot replace mandatory release or cold-chain controls.
- **H** may be proportionate for low-risk ambient deliveries when corroborated, but not for products requiring strong custody evidence.
- **I and J** involve legitimate risk appetite over service allocation, but not over regulatory controls.
- **L** can either reveal control debt or conceal it: it is resilient when unresolved cases remain visible and are correctly classified, but misleading when later data entry is used to make an unperformed control appear to have occurred.

The log alone does not prove that every missing event equals an unperformed physical action. Some scans or checks may have occurred but not been recorded. However, an explicit “skip authorization” event is strong evidence of an actual omission, while missing temperature history or dual-control evidence is often impossible to repair retrospectively. In regulated operations, inability to demonstrate a mandatory control remains a material exception even when staff believe it occurred.

The conformance results support this distinction. The drop to **44% fitting traces** during T2 partly reflects legitimate emergency variants not represented in the normative model. It does not, by itself, prove widespread noncompliance. The specific move-on-log and move-on-model findings—skipped authorization, dispatch without scan, missing release, and missing temperature records—are much more probative. Likewise, the rise to **76% fitting traces** in stabilization does not mean recovery was complete while remediation work remained open.

# 4. Recovery process model

The redesigned process should be an approved emergency model, not an improvised collection of exceptions.

**Notation:** `` means sequence; `[decision]` is a gateway; `||` means controls performed in parallel where appropriate.

### Trigger and operating modes

Proposed thresholds should be validated through site exercises, but the trigger must be explicit:

- **Activate controlled continuity mode** when any of the following occurs:
  - WMS or reservation functionality is unavailable for more than a defined short period, such as 15 minutes during active operations.
  - Mandatory scan events cannot be reliably recorded.
  - Inventory synchronization breaches an approved error threshold.
  - A cyber incident creates uncertainty about data integrity, even if some applications remain available.
- **Mode 1 — degraded digital:** Core inventory remains trusted; peripheral scans are buffered locally.
- **Mode 2 — trusted manual:** WMS is unavailable, but a signed, known-good snapshot and offline authorization lists are available.
- **Mode 3 — integrity uncertain:** Inventory, authorization, or recall data cannot be trusted. Normal release stops. Only legally permitted life-critical orders may proceed after independent physical and compliance verification.
- Loss of trained release staff, calibrated temperature evidence, controlled-product dual control, current recall status, or chain-of-custody forms is a **product-specific stop condition**, not a reason to lower the control.

Partial restoration of order intake does not end continuity mode. Each function must have one declared source of truth until inventory synchronization and scanning pass recovery tests.

### Proposed flow

`Detect incident  Contain cyber threat  Declare mode and incident ID  Register order  Triage  Validate authorization  Reserve inventory  Pick/pack  Product-specific controls and release  Dispatch evidence  POD  Operational completion  Reconciliation  Compliance close`

At each control gateway:

`[Required control complete?]`

- **Yes:** proceed.
- **No:** hold, defer, or cancel with reason.
- A shipment may use only a predefined, legally reviewed emergency pathway; there should be no generic “management override” for mandatory controls.

### Triage rules

Customer priority and product controls must be treated as separate dimensions.

1. **Validated hospital-critical clinical need**, where all required controls can be performed.
2. **Time-sensitive pharmacy or cold-chain need**, considering patient impact and expiry of the delivery window.
3. **Pharmacy-standard**, protected by a minimum service-capacity floor.
4. **Wholesale-bulk**, normally deferrable unless a documented downstream clinical shortage exists.

Within each tier:

- Apply FIFO by original promised date.
- Add priority as backlog age approaches the maximum allowed deferral.
- Consider cancellation and shortage impact, not revenue alone.
- Record who changed a priority and why.
- Publish capacity floors and maximum deferral times so pharmacies cannot be indefinitely starved by the hospital lane.

Product risk determines control effort and whether the order can safely proceed; it does not automatically make an order more commercially important.

### Minimum controls

All overlapping product rules apply cumulatively.

| Risk class | Minimum controls before dispatch |
|---|---|
| **All orders, including ambient** | Unique incident/order ID; duplicate check; customer/product authorization from a dated source; SKU, quantity, lot/batch and expiry where applicable; reservation in the designated ledger; documented pick/pack; quality/release decision; courier handoff evidence; POD plan. Customer history alone is insufficient. |
| **Cold-chain** | All common controls plus validated packaging, calibrated logger or equivalent temperature record, pack-out and handoff times, custody accountability, and documented excursion decision. No dispatch if temperature history cannot be generated. |
| **Controlled-substance** | All common controls plus current customer/product authority, dual control for pick and release, quantity reconciliation, secure storage and courier handoff, seal/custody record, and authenticated recipient signature. Missing dual control cannot be backfilled later. |
| **Recall-sensitive** | All common controls plus current recall/quarantine status, exact lot/batch linkage from inventory to customer, and rapid reverse-trace capability. Hold if the recall source or lot identity is uncertain. |
| **Hospital-critical customer** | Expedited queueing and compliance capacity, but no reduction in product controls. A lawful emergency authorization route may be used only if predefined and documented. |
| **Pharmacy-standard customer** | Standard controls plus a minimum service allocation and maximum backlog age. |
| **Wholesale-bulk customer** | Standard controls; order may be split or deferred to preserve critical capacity, with notice and a recorded reason. |

### Manual evidence capture

Use a controlled offline application or pre-numbered paper packets, not ungoverned spreadsheets. Each order record should contain:

- Incident ID, order ID, source, receipt time, and deduplication result.
- Customer and product authorization source, source version/date, verifier, time, and callback evidence.
- SKU, quantity, warehouse location, lot/batch, expiry, and recall status.
- Snapshot version and every offline reservation/decrement.
- Picker, checker, packer, and release identities and timestamps.
- Temperature device ID, readings, pack-out time, and excursion decision.
- Controlled-product dual signatures and quantity reconciliation.
- Courier identity, vehicle or manifest, seal, handoff time, and signatures.
- POD evidence and any reason for delayed entry.
- Every override, correction, and reconstruction, preserving the original value.

Buffered digital records should be stored on segmented, approved devices. Paper records should be scanned and indexed, but the original should remain retained according to policy.

### Backlog prioritization

`Deferred order  Revalidate need and authorization  Score clinical impact + age + promised date + cancellation risk  Assign tier  Reserve capacity  Process with full controls`

Controls should include:

- A pharmacy-capacity floor.
- A maximum deferral age with mandatory escalation.
- FIFO within each priority tier.
- Review of cancellations and split shipments.
- Daily reporting of fill and delay disparities by customer group.
- A documented rationale for every manual priority override.

### Reconciliation and audit remediation

`System technically restored  Security/data-integrity approval  End-to-end tests by product class  Controlled cutover  Import offline events  Three-way reconciliation  Exception classification  Compliance close`

Three-way reconciliation should compare:

1. The order and offline authorization/reservation record.
2. The physical pick, shipment, courier, and POD evidence.
3. System inventory, lot records, invoice, and customer account.

Exceptions should be classified as:

- **Reconstructable evidence defect:** A contemporaneous paper or courier record exists; enter it with a “reconstructed” flag, source, actor, and timestamp.
- **Inventory or transaction discrepancy:** Investigate duplicates, shortages, wrong lots, or incorrect invoices; correct without overwriting history.
- **Substantive or unprovable control failure:** Missing temperature history, unprovable authorization-at-shipment, missing controlled dual control, or uncertain custody. Escalate to quality/compliance for quarantine, customer notification, regulatory reporting, or recall assessment as applicable.

Use separate case statuses:

- **Physically delivered**
- **Commercially complete**
- **Reconciled**
- **Compliance closed**

The incident should not be declared fully recovered merely because normal medians return. Closure should also require that the remediation queue is below an approved size and age, with no unresolved high-risk cases. Restored systems should pass end-to-end tests for ambient, cold-chain, controlled, and recall-sensitive orders before manual mode is exited.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Indicators should be stratified by incident phase, customer segment, product type, and process variant. Aggregate throughput can otherwise hide poor pharmacy service or regulated-product control failures.

### Leading indicators

| Indicator | Definition and use |
|---|---|
| **Compliant throughput** | Orders dispatched per hour **with all applicable pre-dispatch controls complete**. Report beside gross throughput so dispatches created by missing controls do not appear resilient. |
| **Critical-service protection** | Validated urgent hospital demand filled on time and in full, plus unfilled clinical units and oldest urgent order. The T2 hospital fill rate of **91%** is relevant, but should not stand alone. |
| **Authorization completeness** | Percentage of orders with dated authorization evidence before reservation/release, by electronic, approved offline, and emergency route. “Assumed authorization” should be reported separately and targeted at zero. |
| **Product-control completeness** | Pre-dispatch percentage with release evidence; cold-chain logger and pack-out evidence; controlled dual control; and recall/lot verification. Mandatory missing controls should trigger a stop rather than merely become a KPI exception. |
| **Trace-link coverage** | Percentage of order lines linked to reservation, lot/batch, pick, release, courier handoff, and planned POD; count of unlinked or duplicate offline IDs. |
| **Evidence latency** | Time between physical action and evidence capture. Long or highly variable dispatch-to-POD delays should alert before the final traceability exception rate rises. |
| **Inventory trust** | Snapshot age, unmatched offline decrements, physical-to-ledger variance, duplicate reservations, and synchronization error. Expiry of snapshot validity should force independent verification. |
| **Backlog flow and age** | Backlog inflow, processing rate, net growth, p50/p90/oldest age, and cases approaching maximum deferral by segment. |
| **First-pass manual quality** | Percentage of manual packets accepted without correction; missing fields per 100 cases; label, lot, or quantity discrepancies detected before dispatch. This predicts later rework. |
| **Compliance capacity** | Available trained authorizers, pharmacists, dual-control staff, temperature devices, and secure couriers versus forecast demand. |
| **Fairness guardrails** | Fill, delay, and deferral rates by customer group; pharmacy service-floor utilization; priority overrides; and the ratio of each group’s service level to the overall service level. |

### Lagging indicators

| Indicator | Definition and use |
|---|---|
| **Service outcomes** | Median and p90 cycle time, fill rate, cancellation rate, partial-fill rate, and missed promised dates by segment. T4 p90s should be compared with baseline, not only with T2. |
| **Final traceability exception rate** | Cases that cannot be fully linked from authorization and lot through handoff and POD after reconciliation. Baseline **0.8%** is an anchor, not an acceptable target for mandatory evidence. |
| **Final control exceptions** | Missing cold-chain documentation, confirmed excursions, missing controlled dual control, authorization-at-shipment failures, and release failures. |
| **Rework and correction rate** | Corrected reservations, labels, lots, invoices, PODs, and customer records. Compare with the **3.5%** baseline and include labor hours, not just case count. |
| **Inventory reconciliation outcome** | Unexplained shrinkage, duplicate allocations, wrong-lot shipments, and time to restore trusted inventory. |
| **Backlog clearance** | Time to clear operational backlog, cancellations caused by aging, and service-level recovery by customer group. |
| **Audit-remediation tail** | Open cases, oldest case, high-risk cases, labor hours, and time from physical delivery or invoice close to compliance close. This must remain visible after normal operations resume. |
| **Customer-group fairness outcome** | Differences in fill, cancellation, and delay between hospitals, pharmacies, and wholesale customers, adjusted for documented urgency. |
| **Conformance by variant** | Fitness and fitting-trace percentage for the approved emergency model as well as the normal model. Interpret specific deviations, not fitness alone. |
| **External consequences** | Customer complaints, regulatory notifications, recalls, product loss, controlled-substance investigations, and confirmed patient-supply impacts. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower service, transparent deferral of low-impact bulk orders, and bounded manual variants when they preserve hospital supply with approved equivalent controls and contemporaneous evidence. It should not accept assumed authorization, uncontrolled use of stale inventory, dispatch without chain-of-custody evidence, missing cold-chain records, missing controlled-substance dual control, or a declaration of recovery while high-risk remediation remains open. This recommendation would change only with documented legal emergency authority, independently validated evidence that an alternate control is as effective as the normal control, or materially different patient-supply evidence that justifies reallocating capacity—not merely because a shortcut improves throughput.
