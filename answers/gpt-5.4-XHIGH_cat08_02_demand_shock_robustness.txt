# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### S1. Winter mix shift toward Urgent and Complex-panel

The peak log already shows that the process does not fail proportionally. Weekly starts rise by about 43%, but pre-analytical exceptions nearly double, assay repeats more than double, and the share of technically validated cases that still need Medical validation or specialist review rises from about 26% to about 50%. That means the dominant variant is no longer the straight-through routine path.

The likely bottleneck sequence is: courier and pre-analytical intake first, then preparation and batch wait, then assay, then technical validation, then medical validation and specialist review. The evidence is consistent across the event log and queue data: Pre-analytical check hits 96% utilization with 9.5h median queue, preparation queue rises to 11h, Batch wait to Assay run rises to 22h, assay queue to 18h, technical validation to 16h, medical validation to 22h, and specialist review to 36h.

Relieving one point can easily overload the next. If registration is automated without changing downstream controls, load reaches Pre-analytical check faster and the bottleneck simply moves there. If strict Urgent-first dispatch is used at preparation and assay, Urgent medians improve for some cases, but Routine cases accumulate WIP and Complex-panel cases age into the already stressed medical and specialist path.

### S2. Assay-machine degradation or outage

Here the first bottleneck is assay capacity and the repeat loop around it. In peak, Test preparation to Batch wait is 14h, Batch wait to Assay run is 22h, Assay run to Technical validation is 15h, and Repeat assay back to Assay run is 26h. The loop is especially dangerous because each repeat consumes another assay slot while also delaying downstream validation.

The bottleneck sequence becomes: preparation release and batch wait, then assay machines, then repeat loop, then a delayed surge into technical and medical validation when machines recover. This is why a local rule like dedicating M1 to Urgent and Complex-panel work is only partially helpful. It protects some high-priority work, but if Routine work is dumped onto the higher-failure M2, repeat demand rises and total assay load grows.

Relief can therefore backfire twice. First, Urgent bypassing reduces queue for that class but fragments machine schedules. Second, once the outage or degradation is corrected, technical validation and medical validation can be flooded by the release wave from assay, moving the bottleneck downstream.

### S3. Medical validator or specialist-review absence

Under this scenario the bottleneck starts at Medical validation and specialist review, not at registration or release. Peak utilization is already 93% for Medical validators and 87% for specialist review, with median queues of 22h and 36h respectively. The performance edges are also severe: Technical validation to Medical validation takes 24h in peak, and Hold for specialist review to Medical validation takes 48h.

The migration path is: Medical validation queue grows, then specialist review grows, then technically validated work piles up waiting for downstream clinical sign-off. Routine cases may still flow if they can release directly after Technical validation, but Urgent and Complex-panel cases are heavily exposed because they depend on medical validation. Complex-panel cases are even more exposed because they are much more likely to enter specialist review.

This is the scenario where intervention I4 is most dangerous as a default. Pulling technical validators into accession correction might help a 14h correction queue, but technical validation is already at 101% utilization in peak. That would move the bottleneck upstream and reduce the feed to medical validation at exactly the wrong moment.

### S4. Courier compression or delay

This scenario starts upstream but does not stay upstream. Arrival compression first overloads courier intake and receiving. The peak evidence is clear: courier queue is 7.5h, Register order to Receive sample is 8h, and Receive sample to Pre-analytical check is 9h. Once samples arrive in a burst, pre-analytical check and accession correction absorb the shock.

The migration path is: courier intake, then pre-analytical check, then accession correction, then recollection loop, then preparation and assay if poor samples are not screened early. The recollection path is especially harmful because Request recollection back to Receive sample is 60h in peak, and clerks lose time explaining errors to physicians, which feeds back into intake capacity.

This scenario also shows why automation alone is not robust. If registration is sped up but courier smoothing and early defect triage are not in place, pre-analytical check simply becomes the faster-growing bottleneck. If poor samples are rejected earlier, downstream waste falls, but the communication workload moves to clerks and physicians.

# 3. Robust redesign package

1. Replace blanket priority with service-class dispatch and WIP caps.
Use three lanes rather than a single global rule: Urgent cases get protected micro-slots at preparation and assay every shift; Complex-panel cases get age-based promotion once all required assays are ready or once they cross a queue-age threshold; Routine cases are batched, but with a maximum wait cap rather than a fixed large batch. This is more robust than I1 because it protects Urgent work without permanently starving Routine and non-urgent Complex work. It depends on reliable case-type visibility and queue-age visibility. It fails if Urgent share stays extremely high for many days or if one machine is down long enough that the reserved slots consume most capacity.

2. Add early pre-analytical defect triage and a fast exception lane for accession correction.
Adopt I6, but pair it with explicit prioritization of Manual accession correction because those cases unblock quickly and avoid long recollection loops. The peak log shows pre-analytical exceptions rising from about 7% of received samples to about 12%, and Request recollection returns take 60h, so finding bad samples late is very expensive. This is robust across demand-mix and courier scenarios because it removes waste before preparation and assay consume capacity. It depends on having standard reject criteria and enough clerk capacity for physician communication. It can fail by creating a communication bottleneck if the rejection decision is fast but the recollection request process is slow.

3. Smooth arrivals and use registration automation to reallocate clerk time, not just to push more work downstream.
Adopt I7 and I8 together. Courier smoothing reduces the 10:00 and 17:00 arrival spikes, while registration automation should be used mainly to free clerks for exception handling, recollection communication, and intake smoothing. Used this way, I8 is robust; used alone, it is not, because registration is only a moderate peak bottleneck compared with pre-analytics, assay, and validation. This package depends on couriers accepting staggered slots and on a meaningful share of orders having complete digital metadata. It fails if automation simply accelerates work into a 96% utilized Pre-analytical check station.

4. Use flexible assay routing with quality guardrails, not full machine dedication.
Do not adopt I3 as a hard default. Instead, reserve some reliable analyzer capacity for Urgent and Complex-panel work, but keep routing flexibility so Routine work can still use M1 when M2 repeat behavior worsens. Also create a supervised repeat lane for Repeat-after-failure cases, preferably on day shift rather than after night shift, because the data links repeats to night shifts and M2 usage. This is robust in both volume and machine-degradation scenarios because it limits self-inflicted repeat demand. It depends on machine-specific repeat monitoring by shift. It fails if a true machine outage removes too much absolute capacity.

5. Protect the clinical sign-off path during peaks.
Adopt I5 as a peak-mode default and add fixed daily specialist-review blocks. The point is not to accelerate all cases equally; it is to keep Urgent and Complex-panel cases from waiting behind a growing clinical-sign-off queue. This is robust because the peak path clearly becomes validation-heavy: Medical validation load grows much faster than starts, and specialist-review delay is already 48h from hold to Medical validation. It depends on temporary staffing being available without excessive fatigue. It fails if technical validation is weakened upstream or if specialist absence is severe enough that medical validators only move the queue from one clinical stage to the next.

6. Use partner-lab overflow only for selected Complex-panel cases.
I10 should not be a baseline design, but it is robust as a relief valve when predicted internal specialist delay is clearly worse than the 12h transport penalty. The right use case is non-urgent or long-aging Complex-panel overflow during specialist shortages or severe winter mix shifts. It depends on stable traceability and a clean handoff process. It fails for time-critical cases when transport delay dominates, and it creates its own chain-of-custody risk.

Do not adopt I9 as a default. The evidence does not show release activity as the primary bottleneck, so holding Routine releases adds pure waiting and worsens Routine age without protecting the real constraints.

Do not use I4 as a default peak action. Cross-training is valuable, but redeploying technical validators into accession correction while technical validation is at 101% utilization is a classic bottleneck migration error.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

| Scenario | Policy to activate | Switch on | Switch off |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1. High Urgent/Complex mix | Reduce Routine batch cap, reserve preparation and assay micro-slots for Urgent plus aged Complex-panel work, start evening Medical validation shift. | Urgent share exceeds 25% or Complex-panel exceeds 17% for 2 consecutive days, or Medical validation median queue exceeds 8h. | Both shares stay below 22% and 15% for 3 days and Medical validation queue falls below 4h. |
| S2. Machine degradation or outage | Suspend any large-batch Routine policy, stop concentrating Routine work on M2, route repeats to supervised daytime handling, escalate maintenance immediately, and consider Complex overflow outsourcing if internal specialist delay is also rising. | Any analyzer outage over 2h, overall Assay to Repeat rate over 10% for a shift, or M2 repeat rate above 1.5x M1. | Repeat rate falls below 8% for 2 consecutive shifts and assay queue falls below 8h. |
| S3. Medical validator or specialist shortage | Freeze redeployment of technical validators, activate evening Medical validation shift, create protected specialist-review blocks, and outsource selected Complex-panel overflow. | Planned absence overlaps with forecasted high Complex volume, specialist-review queue exceeds 12h, or oldest Urgent case waiting for Medical validation exceeds 4h. | Oldest Urgent case waiting for Medical validation stays below 2h and specialist-review queue stays below 8h for 2 days. |
| S4. Courier compression or delay | Activate split intake handling for Urgent and oldest samples, run early reject triage within 2h, shift clerk time to recollection communication, and temporarily meter release into pre-analytics if that queue blows out. | More than 35% of daily arrivals land within any 2h window, courier queue exceeds 4h, or Receive sample to Pre-analytical check exceeds 5h. | Arrival profile is stable for 2 days and Pre-analytical queue falls below 4h. |

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- A simultaneous machine problem and specialist shortage can still overwhelm the system; the redesign reduces instability but does not create enough slack to absorb every combined shock.
- Recollection loops remain partly external. Even with better early triage, recollection depends on physicians and patients, so the 60h return path cannot be compressed purely inside the lab.
- The redesign intentionally protects Urgent and aged Complex-panel work, so prolonged peaks will still push some Routine work toward its tail limits. That is a redesign-induced vulnerability, but it is preferable to invisible system-wide WIP explosion.
- Registration automation and early rejection can create a smaller but more complex exception queue. If staffing is not explicitly reassigned, the hidden bottleneck becomes clerk communication and case clarification.
- Conditional partner-lab overflow introduces traceability, transport, and handoff failure risk that does not exist in the fully internal path.
- Evening clinical shifts may create fatigue and handoff risk; if quality drops, the lab can trade queue time for validation mistakes.
