# 2. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is best explained as a sequence of different mechanisms becoming dominant at different parts of the work period.

At the beginning of shifts, cases are slower and somewhat more error-prone, but this should not be interpreted as simple worker underperformance. The first few events are disproportionately complex, high-priority, and inherited from previous shifts. The first 1–5 events have the highest mean complexity score, the highest high-priority share among the sequence bins, and notes indicating backlog carryover. Senior workers also appear to take complex cases early, especially after night or weekend backlog. So the early-shift pattern mixes warm-up with deliberate selection of difficult work.

From roughly positions 6–50, service time falls and error rates stay low or only slightly rise. This is the part most compatible with genuine warm-up, stabilization, reduced context switching, and lower case complexity. Workers may become faster because they settle into the activity, queues are more manageable, and the case mix becomes easier. Here, faster service is not automatically bad: errors move from 2.8% to 2.5% and then 2.7%, while complexity and high-priority share fall.

The late-shift pattern is different. Positions 51–75 and especially 76+ show faster service but worse quality. Mean service time falls to 11.4 and then 10.8 minutes, while error rates rise to 4.6% and 7.9%, and rework within 7 days rises to 8.2% and 13.7%. Since complexity is actually lower in these bins, the late deterioration is unlikely to be explained by harder cases alone. The stronger explanation is a speed-quality tradeoff under queue pressure, amplified by fatigue, batching, delayed quality checks, and resource experience.

The WIP table is central. Under high WIP, both low- and high-complexity cases are handled faster but with much higher error rates. Low-complexity cases drop from 12.7 minutes under low WIP to 9.4 minutes under high WIP, but error rises from 2.1% to 5.8%. High-complexity cases drop from 22.4 minutes to 16.8 minutes, but error rises from 4.2% to 10.9%. This is not ordinary learning. It looks like workers are accelerating work when the queue is overloaded, possibly by shortening review depth, batching similar tasks, postponing checks, or accepting higher uncertainty.

Experience changes the mechanism. Seniors also speed up, but their quality is relatively stable under low WIP and degrades less under high WIP. Mid-level workers and juniors show much larger high-WIP error increases. Juniors have two vulnerabilities: early events are more difficult for them, and later high-pressure work produces very high error rates. Night staff have a distinct pattern: fewer cases but more urgent exceptions, so their performance cannot be compared directly with day workers using raw rates.

Circadian and shift effects are present, but they are not separable from operations without further modeling. Day shift has late demand peaks and ends with high queue pressure. Late shift inherits day backlog. Early shift clears night backlog. Night shift handles fewer but more exceptional cases. Therefore, “last two hours” means different things across shifts: for Day, it may mean demand peak; for Late, inherited backlog; for Night, urgent exceptions and circadian burden.

Batching and delayed Quality Check can make late work look efficient in the short term while shifting defects downstream. If Junior_H and Junior_I perform 9–18 consecutive Document Review events with very short service times, the measured service time may fall because setup time and transition time are compressed. But if those batched reviews have higher correction or rework rates, the apparent productivity gain is partly illusory. Similarly, delaying Quality Check to the end of the shift can create correction bursts and hide earlier quality problems.

So the core interpretation is:

Service time falls early because of warm-up, stabilization, and easier case mix. Service time falls late mainly because of queue pressure, batching, and possibly rushed work. Errors rise late only in some conditions because the harmful speed-up occurs especially when high WIP, lower experience, complex cases, bot corrections, or end-of-shift quality bursts coincide. Fatigue may contribute, but the log does not support fatigue as the only or even necessarily the primary explanation.

# 3. Proposed analyses

The analysis should be designed to separate elapsed-shift effects from queue pressure, clock time, worker experience, case mix, activity mix, automation, and batching. Raw sequence-position averages are useful diagnostics, but they are not causal estimates.

**Statistical design**

Use event-level models with at least three outcome families:

| Outcome              | Suggested model                               | Key controls                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Service time         | log-normal, Gamma, or quantile regression     | activity, resource, shift, clock time, sequence position, WIP, queue length, complexity, priority, batch indicator, bot involvement |
| Error flag           | logistic or mixed-effects logistic regression | same controls plus activity-specific and resource-experience interactions                                                           |
| Rework within 7 days | logistic or survival/time-to-rework model     | same controls plus downstream path and quality-check timing                                                                         |

A useful event-level specification would be conceptually like:

```text
log(service_time) ~
    spline(sequence_position_within_shift)
  + spline(minutes_since_shift_start)
  + wip_band
  + queue_length_at_start
  + case_complexity_score
  + priority
  + activity_family
  + shift_id / shift_type
  + clock_hour
  + resource_experience_months
  + batching_features
  + bot_involved
  + wip_band × case_complexity_score
  + wip_band × resource_experience_group
  + sequence_position × wip_band
  + activity_family × wip_band
  + resource fixed effects
  + date/week fixed effects
```

For error and rework:

```text
logit(error_flag or rework_7d) ~
    same predictors
  + log(service_time)
  + high_wip × short_service_time
  + bot_involved × high_wip
  + batch_indicator × activity_family
```

The coefficient on `sequence_position_within_shift` should not be interpreted as fatigue or learning unless WIP, complexity, shift, activity, and resource selection have been adjusted.

**Within-resource comparisons**

Compare each worker to themselves. This helps avoid unfairly comparing Junior_H’s late batched Document Review against Senior_A’s early complex Eligibility Decision.

Questions:

| Comparison                                                                          | Purpose                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Same resource, same activity, same complexity band, low WIP: early versus mid-shift | Tests warm-up without strong queue pressure.              |
| Same resource, same activity, same complexity band, high WIP versus low WIP         | Tests queue-pressure speed-quality tradeoff.              |
| Same resource, same position range, different shifts or clock hours                 | Tests circadian/time-of-day effects where workers rotate. |
| Same resource, batched versus non-batched work                                      | Tests whether batching is efficient or risky.             |

**Within-shift position effects**

Do not treat sequence position as a pure fatigue measure. It is a composite of elapsed work time, accumulated queue pressure, case selection, and shift-specific demand. Use flexible bins or splines, and estimate separate position effects under low, medium, and high WIP.

Example interpretation target:

```text
Among Document Review events with similar complexity and priority, handled by the same worker group, under low WIP, does service time fall from positions 1–20 to 21–50 without higher error?
```

If yes, that supports warm-up. If errors rise only under high WIP, that supports pressure rather than pure fatigue.

**WIP and queue stratification**

Use the existing bands:

```text
Low WIP:    < 30
Medium WIP: 30–70
High WIP:   > 70
```

Then examine:

| Stratum                   | Interpretation                      |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Low WIP, low complexity   | Baseline process capability.        |
| Low WIP, high complexity  | Complexity burden without pressure. |
| High WIP, low complexity  | Pressure effect on routine work.    |
| High WIP, high complexity | Highest-risk operating zone.        |

The high-WIP/high-complexity cell is especially important because service time is shorter than under low WIP, but error rate is much higher. That suggests not only complexity but compression of work under pressure.

**Complexity adjustment**

Complexity should be modeled continuously and also checked in bands. Early-shift comparisons should be adjusted for `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, and activity mix. Otherwise, the analysis will punish workers for taking difficult carryover cases.

Also test whether complexity is itself selected by resource group:

```text
case_complexity_score ~ resource_group + shift + first_hour + queue_length + weekday/weekend
```

This reveals whether senior workers are receiving or choosing harder cases.

**Shift and time-of-day controls**

Separate:

| Variable                         | Meaning                                            |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| `sequence_position_within_shift` | How far into that worker’s shift the event occurs. |
| `minutes_since_shift_start`      | Elapsed shift time.                                |
| clock hour                       | Circadian and external demand pattern.             |
| shift type                       | Early, Day, Late, Night operating regime.          |
| queue inherited at shift start   | Carryover burden from previous shift.              |

A late Day-shift event at 16:30 is not equivalent to a late Night-shift event at 05:30, even if both are in the last hour of the shift.

**Batching detection**

Detect sequential batching by resource, activity, and short gaps. Useful features include:

| Feature                                          | Meaning                                 |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------- |
| consecutive same-activity run length             | Long runs may indicate batching.        |
| same resource + same activity + same queue batch | Possible batch processing.              |
| unusually short service time within batch        | Potential speed compression.            |
| end-of-shift Quality Check concentration         | Delayed control activity.               |
| batch-level downstream rework                    | Whether batching creates later defects. |

**Resource-experience interactions**

Do not just include experience as a control; interact it with pressure and position.

Important effects:

```text
high_wip × resource_experience_group
final_two_hours × resource_experience_group
complexity × resource_experience_group
batching × resource_experience_group
activity_family × resource_experience_group
```

This can distinguish stable senior speed-up from junior high-pressure degradation.

**Rework/error downstream analysis**

Immediate `error_flag` may miss latent defects. Use downstream indicators:

| Indicator                                    | Why it matters                                              |
| -------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| `rework_flag` within 7 days                  | Captures defects discovered after closure or quality check. |
| Rework Correction path frequency             | Shows whether fast work creates later work.                 |
| Quality Check delay                          | Tests whether errors are hidden until end of shift.         |
| Bot_Risk correction rate                     | Tests automation pressure interaction.                      |
| Specialist Referral after bot classification | Possible automation uncertainty spillover.                  |

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Basic formatting
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

df = raw_df.copy()

df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
df["start_timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["start_timestamp"], utc=True)
df["shift_start_time"] = pd.to_datetime(df["shift_start_time"], utc=True)

# Service time at event level.
df["service_time_min"] = (
    df["time:timestamp"] - df["start_timestamp"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60

df["clock_hour"] = df["time:timestamp"].dt.hour
df["weekday"] = df["time:timestamp"].dt.dayofweek

df["wip_band"] = pd.cut(
    df["wip_at_start"],

    labels=["low", "medium", "high"]
)

    df["case_complexity_score"],
    bins=[-np.inf, 0.50, 0.70, np.inf],
    labels=["low", "medium", "high"]
)

df["experience_group"] = pd.cut(
    df["resource_experience_months"],
    bins=[-np.inf, 12, 48, np.inf],
    labels=["junior", "mid", "senior"]
)

df["position_bin"] = pd.cut(
    df["sequence_position_within_shift"],
    bins=[0, 5, 15, 30, 50, 75, np.inf],
    labels=["1-5", "6-15", "16-30", "31-50", "51-75", "76+"]
)

# Format for PM4Py.
log_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,

    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Case-level service and waiting time
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

)

# Temporal features: hour, day, month, etc.

    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"

# Dotted chart: useful for seeing bursts, batched activities, and queue waves.
pm4py.save_vis_dotted_chart(
    log_df,
    "dotted_chart.png",
    show_legend=True

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Batching detection
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

batches = pm4py.discover_batches(
    log_df,
    activity_key="concept:name",

    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"

# Example custom run-length batching feature.
df = df.sort_values(["org:resource", "shift_id", "start_timestamp", "time:timestamp"])

same_resource_shift = (
    (df["org:resource"] == df["org:resource"].shift())
    & (df["shift_id"] == df["shift_id"].shift())
)

same_activity_as_previous = (
    same_resource_shift
    & (df["concept:name"] == df["concept:name"].shift())
)

df["new_run"] = ~same_activity_as_previous
df["run_id"] = df.groupby(["org:resource", "shift_id"])["new_run"].cumsum()

run_stats = (
    df.groupby(["org:resource", "shift_id", "run_id"])
      .agg(
          run_activity=("concept:name", "first"),
          run_length=("concept:name", "size"),
          run_start=("start_timestamp", "min"),
          run_end=("time:timestamp", "max"),
          mean_service_time=("service_time_min", "mean"),

mechanism_table = (
```

The important analytical principle is that each proposed mechanism should be tested under conditions where the rival explanations are restricted. For example, test warm-up under low WIP and matched complexity; test queue pressure at matched sequence position and activity; test circadian effects at matched WIP and complexity; test batching by comparing batched and non-batched runs of the same activity.

# 4. Intervention options

| Intervention                                                  | Expected benefit                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Risk                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | How to monitor impact with event-log indicators                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all workers                  | Could reduce extreme late-shift exposure and may help if fatigue is genuinely contributing to late errors. It also creates a clear operational boundary.                                                                                                                         | It addresses only one possible mechanism. If late errors are mostly caused by queue pressure, inherited backlog, batching, or delayed Quality Check, a strict cap may simply move the problem to the next shift. It may increase handovers and queue carryover. | Track last-two-hour error rate, rework within 7 days, handover frequency, queue at shift end, queue at next shift start, case age at handover, and number of cases left open because of the cap.                                                                                               |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour of each shift  | Could reduce early-shift overload and allow workers to warm up before handling difficult cases. It may help juniors especially.                                                                                                                                                  | It may delay urgent or high-risk cases. It may also remove deliberate senior triage of complex carryover work, which may currently be beneficial. | 3. Add a queue-pressure alert when WIP > 70                   | Strongly supported by the data. The most defensible interventions are not single-point fixes. A better package would combine a WIP > 70 alert, extra senior support during Day peaks, protected micro-breaks with capacity coverage, constrained assignment balancing, and monitoring of delayed Quality Check bursts. The intervention should target the system conditions that create rushed work, not just the people exposed to those conditions.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions can be unfair because the log does not represent a randomized experiment. Workers are exposed to different case types, different queue states, different activities, and different shifts. A junior worker handling late Day-shift batched Document Review under WIP > 70 is not comparable to a senior handling low-WIP routine cases, even if both are measured with the same error flag.

Selection bias is central. Seniors appear to take complex cases early, and night staff handle more urgent exceptions. If raw error rates are used, the people doing the hardest work may look worse, while workers who handle simpler or deferred work may look better. Random assignment can help measurement, but only within operationally safe skill and priority constraints.

Measurement error is also likely. Service time may be distorted by batching, delayed recording, instant Bot_Risk events, or events that are started but not cognitively worked continuously. Very short service times late in the shift may reflect genuine efficiency, but they may also reflect batch entry, superficial review, or postponed quality work. Error flags may capture only detected errors, while rework within 7 days captures a different and delayed quality signal.

Queue pressure is partly management-created. If staffing, demand peaks, bot quality, or shift handover policies create WIP > 70, it is misleading to blame individual workers for quality degradation under those conditions. The event log shows a system-level speed-quality tradeoff: high WIP is associated with faster service and higher error across both low- and high-complexity cases.

Automation quality should not be treated as neutral. Bot_Risk is instant, but human correction rises under high queue length. That could mean the bot struggles with high-pressure case mix, or that humans have less time to review bot outputs carefully. Either way, automation may shift risk downstream to human reviewers and Quality Check rather than simply reducing workload.

Punitive use of raw late-shift error rates should be avoided. It would reward case avoidance and under-reporting, punish workers exposed to queues and complex cases, and obscure the operational causes of errors. Worker-level analytics should be used for coaching, staffing, process redesign, and workload fairness, not for discipline based on unadjusted metrics.

Quality checks should not be removed to improve throughput. Delaying or weakening Quality Check may make service time look better while increasing rework, customer harm, regulatory exposure, or downstream correction burden. The correct question is how to schedule, staff, and prioritize quality controls so they prevent errors without creating end-of-shift bursts.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

A useful dashboard should combine process-mining, workload, quality, and fairness indicators. It should allow slicing by shift, resource group, activity family, WIP band, complexity band, priority, bot involvement, and time since shift start.

| ------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

The dashboard should avoid ranking workers by raw error rate. A fair view would show each worker’s exposure-adjusted context: complexity handled, WIP exposure, priority mix, activity mix, bot-correction burden, shift timing, and handover load. The best monitoring question is not “who is slow or error-prone?” but “under which process conditions does speed become unsafe, and how can staffing, routing, quality control, and automation be redesigned to reduce that risk?”
