## 1. Disruption timeline

The phases show improving control completeness, but not a clean return to normal. Cycle-time pairs below are **median / p90**, in the order **Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, Wholesale-bulk**. DFG counts are observed transitions, not necessarily complete case lineages; a missing return branch can reflect observation-window boundaries rather than a failed case.

- **T0 — Baseline, Day -30 to Day -1:** The dominant process was the expected sequence from receipt through authorization, reservation, picking, packing, quality/release, dispatch, proof of delivery, and invoice close. The DFG shows 42,000 starts and 36,800 invoice closures/END transitions. Cycle times were **8/15h, 23/49h, and 36/72h**. Traceability exceptions were **0.8%**, cold-chain documentation missing **0.4%**, controlled-substance dual control missing **0.2%**, rework **3.5%**, and cancellations **2.1%**. This was a relatively controlled baseline, not a perfect one.

- **T1 — Day 0 morning, system failure:** The WMS became unavailable following the cyber incident. There is no separate T1 performance summary, so T2 workaround results should not be attributed quantitatively to T1. Operationally, the key change was loss of trusted inventory, scanning, and possibly authorization-system access; the immediate risk posture became uncertainty about current stock and the integrity of electronic records.

- **T2 — Day 0 afternoon to Day 2, manual workaround:** Of 5,600 cases, 5,100 entered emergency triage: 2,500 followed manual authorization, **1,600 skipped system authorization—about 31% of triaged cases—and 1,000 entered the hospital fast lane**. Manual picking continued 3,600 cases into packing. However, 1,800 cases went directly from packing to dispatch without a temporary release control, and another 1,450 followed temporary release into scanless dispatch. Cycle times deteriorated to **10/21h, 54/118h, and 96/180h**. Hospital-critical fill remained reported at **91%**, while Pharmacy-standard fill fell to **61%**; no baseline fill rates are supplied, so this demonstrates relative service protection but not an absolute improvement over baseline. Traceability exceptions rose to **9.5%**, cold-chain documentation missing to **4.8%**, dual-control missing to **2.9%**, rework to **12.5%**, and cancellations to **7.8%**. Physical continuity was valuable, but the evidence indicates that it was purchased partly by weakening or deferring controls.

- **T3 — Day 3 to Day 6, partial recovery:** Order intake returned, but inventory synchronization and scanning remained unreliable. Cycle times improved to **9/18h, 38/90h, and 84/160h**, yet remained worse than baseline, especially at p90. Traceability exceptions fell to **5.4%**, cold-chain documentation missing to **2.3%**, dual-control missing to **1.4%**, rework to **9.0%**, and cancellations to **5.2%**. The direction is consistent with recovery, but the figures do not establish that system restoration alone caused the improvement: backlog clearance, case mix, and reconciliation work also changed.

- **T4 — Day 7 to Day 21, stabilization:** Most systems functioned, and hospital median cycle time returned to **8h**, but the tail remained degraded: **8/16h, 28/74h, and 58/120h** across the three segments. Compared with baseline, stabilization still had higher traceability exceptions (**2.2% versus 0.8%**), cold-chain documentation gaps (**0.9% versus 0.4%**), dual-control gaps (**0.5% versus 0.2%**), rework (**5.8% versus 3.5%**), and cancellations (**3.0% versus 2.1%**). The stabilization DFG still contains **1,500 backlog-prioritization branches, 1,400 manual-reservation reconciliations, 1,200 proof-of-delivery remediation branches, and 2,300 invoice-close-to-audit-remediation branches**. Thus, operational recovery preceded compliance and record reconciliation.

Conformance deteriorated from **0.96 fitness / 91% fitting traces** at baseline to **0.71 / 44%** during manual workaround, then recovered to **0.82 / 59%** and **0.90 / 76%**. T2 move-on-log deviations included authorization skipping, scanless dispatch, phone POD, and spreadsheet invoice holding; move-on-model deviations included missing quality/release, scan, POD, and temperature-control records. These findings support both actual control gaps and weak evidence capture. They do **not** establish that 56% of cases were noncompliant. Likewise, the absence of an explicit quality node in the T2 DFG does not prove every physically dispatched case skipped its check.

## 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**Resilience is the ability to preserve essential service while maintaining or faithfully duplicating mandatory controls under stress.** It is not simply completing more cases or introducing fewer process-model deviations. Conversely, “concealed noncompliance” here means that a real-time control gap is hidden behind continued throughput or apparent closure—not necessarily that anyone acted deliberately to conceal it.

- **Resilient capabilities demonstrated or reasonably supported:**  
  - **A**, when used only to prioritize preparation, protected urgent supply.  
  - **B** preserved an authorization control rather than abandoning it.  
  - **D** was reasonable at the initial outage, before its information became stale.  
  - **E** maintained physical warehouse flow.  
  - **F** strengthened attention to controlled substances.  
  - **I and J** protected scarce capacity and made recovery demand-oriented.  
  - **K and L** demonstrate valuable reconciliation capacity, although they are lagging controls rather than proof that the original shipment was fully compliant.

- **Risky shortcuts or potential concealed noncompliance:**  
  **C** relies on assumption rather than verified authorization. **A combined with C** turns mission priority into a control waiver. **D after the snapshot became stale** substitutes plausible information for current evidence. **E and G**, where the only evidence is reconstructed later, do not preserve real-time traceability. **F** does not provide cold-chain release assurance when only a sample is reviewed. **H** should not be the sole custody record for controlled products. The missing modeled quality step, scans, POD, and temperature records require case-level review; they should not be assumed either harmless or successfully performed merely because a dispatch occurred.

- **Context-dependent adaptations and risk appetite:**  
  **B, D, E, F, H, and J** can be sound controls when their data, authority, evidence, and expiry are explicit. Risk appetite may determine how much delay, redundancy, or manual expense to accept for standard ambient orders. It should **not** determine whether a cold-chain temperature record, controlled-substance authorization, or required release decision exists. A spreadsheet invoice hold can also be entirely legitimate; the concern is unsupported closure or an ownerless queue, not the spreadsheet itself.

The hospital fill rate and restored median cycle time are encouraging but do not erase the higher exceptions, p90 delays, and audit tail. Conversely, the 7.8% cancellation rate does not establish that all deferral decisions were wrong. Unequal service can be justified by clinical priority, but fairness requires documented, consistent criteria and comparison for comparable need and product risk.

Future conformance models should represent **approved manual controls**, so legitimate workarounds are not classified as deviations. Conversely, simply adding “skip authorization” and “dispatch without scan” to the normative model would lower move-on-log deviations while concealing the same risk. **Model fitness and compliance are different objectives.**

## 4. Recovery process model

The future process should preserve the normal control gates while introducing a time-bounded, evidence-producing fallback:

`Receive  Triage  Authorize  Reserve/verify  Pick  Pack  Quality/release  Dispatch event  POD  Reconcile  Invoice/case close`

**Operational dispatch is not the same as case closure.** Manual cases enter a controlled reconciliation branch before compliance closure.

1. **Activation and scope trigger**
   - Activate the continuity playbook when the WMS, authorization service, scanning service, or trusted inventory feed is unavailable; a restoration estimate exceeds one operating shift; manual evidence approaches its validity limit; a critical temperature/security control is unreliable; or backlog age breaches a predefined threshold.
   - Suspected cyber compromise also triggers credential revocation, access containment, and a distinction between **system unavailability** and **data integrity loss**. If records may have been altered, do not treat their return as trustworthy merely because the interface is visible.
   - Assign an incident commander, independent quality-release authority, compliance lead, security/IT lead, and customer/transport owners. Use named accounts and segregation of duties. Compliance staff need surge support; additional pickers cannot approve their own control exceptions.

2. **Receipt, data-trust decision, and triage**
   - Assign a unique case ID and capture customer, product, lot/serial, quantity, promised need, temperature/security requirements, and incident reference.
   - Assess whether the data source is **trusted, degraded but bounded, or untrusted**. A newly restored but unreconciled feed remains degraded. An old printed snapshot cannot become authoritative merely because it is still available.
   - Use explicit priority tiers:
     - **P1:** urgent clinical need, controlled-substance security, or cold-chain exposure requiring immediate action.
     - **P2:** other pharmacy-critical supply and time-sensitive orders.
     - **P3:** genuinely deferrable bulk demand.
   - Prioritize by clinical impact, product risk, stock expiry, and promised need—not customer name or convenience alone. Wholesale-bulk is not automatically low impact. Within a tier, use transparent promised-date/FIFO rules.
   - Record every deferral with reason, expected review date, customer contact, and alternative supply or escalation. Review rather than leave orders indefinitely in backlog.

3. **Authorization and inventory reservation**
   - For every order, verify customer authority, product authorization, controlled-substance designated receiving requirements where applicable, and recall/withdrawal restrictions.
   - Offline lists must have a version, effective period, owner, and expiry. Phone confirmation should use a verified, independent contact and relevant scope; a historical customer relationship alone is insufficient.
   - Before using a snapshot, freeze its version and time, physically verify higher-risk or fast-moving stock, and limit reservations to the verified quantity.
   - Where synchronization is uncertain, create a quarantined manual reservation rather than allowing the same stock to be committed again. Reconcile before further release. Apply appropriate expiry and recall controls, such as FEFO, without overriding recall restrictions.

4. **Minimum controls by product and customer risk**

   | Risk category | Non-negotiable recovery controls |
   |---|---|
   | **All customers and products** | Current customer/product authorization; documented quality/release decision; traceable case, stock movement, dispatch, and delivery evidence; segregation of duties. Hospital urgency does not waive these controls. |
   | **Cold-chain** | Validated packing and temperature monitoring, calibrated equipment, shipment-specific temperature evidence, documented excursion assessment, and authorized release before dispatch. Pharmacist sampling alone is insufficient. |
   | **Controlled substances** | Valid customer/site authorization, secured storage and transport, required dual control, lot/serial tracking, carrier identity, tamper/seal evidence, and custody reconciliation. Phone POD alone is insufficient. |
   | **Recall-sensitive items** | Current recall and withdrawal checks, lot/serial verification, and quarantine of uncertain or recalled stock. Generic authorization does not demonstrate recall clearance. |
   | **Standard ambient products** | Verified authorization, reliable stock evidence, release decision, and point-of-work traceability. This category may use simpler evidence mechanisms, but not no evidence. |

5. **Pick, pack, and manual evidence capture**
   - Use offline scanning where possible or an equivalent numbered paper/disaster record. Capture the event **when it happens**: case/product ID, lot/serial, quantity, actor, device, timestamp, source/destination, and exception.
   - Preserve an audit journal or signed event register so later synchronization is attributable and duplicate-resistant. Keep the original event time distinct from system-entry time.
   - For controlled goods, retain sequential document control and dual-person evidence. For cold-chain goods, start and associate the temperature logger at the appropriate packing stage.
   - If the incident is prolonged, refresh physical inventory before the snapshot validity period expires. An extension should require risk-based recounts and a named approval, not repeated reliance on Day 0 data.

6. **Release, dispatch, POD, and invoice**
   - Block dispatch when mandatory authorization, temperature, security, or release evidence is absent or unreliable. Quarantine the affected case and escalate; hospital urgency triggers executive/quality escalation and alternative supply, not retrospective approval.
   - At handoff, record carrier identity, case and seal identifiers, condition, actor, and timestamp—even if the ordinary electronic scan is unavailable.
   - Permit phone POD only under an approved low-risk exception with recipient identity and detailed confirmation. Use stronger signed/controlled proof for controlled and cold-chain deliveries.
   - Keep invoice control distinct from compliance close. A finance hold is acceptable if it has an owner, reason, evidence requirement, and review date. Do not label an order fully closed merely because an invoice workaround was used.

7. **Backlog management during continuing degradation**
   - Maintain separate queues by customer/product risk and age. Measure age from the promised need or approved deferral time, not merely from incident day.
   - Reconcile reservations before backlog picking, then apply the same authorization, temperature, security, and release gates.
   - Set review intervals—for example, high-risk exceptions daily and ordinary deferrals at least every 24 hours, with faster review when clinical need or capacity changes.
   - Publish allocation criteria and monitor who receives service, who is deferred, and why. This prevents operational triage from becoming an unmanaged commercial penalty.

8. **Reconciliation, audit remediation, and exit**
   - On recovery, preserve the incident records and import events into a quarantined reconciliation workstream. Compare physical inventory, reservations, order records, dispatch records, temperature evidence, custody records, POD, and invoices.
   - Detect duplicate imports and prevent uncertain cases from receiving another shipment. Do not backdate records to make them appear contemporaneous.
   - Prioritize missing authorization, temperature, controlled-substance, and recall evidence ahead of ordinary missing POD. Assess affected products and customers; initiate applicable quarantine, recall, notification, or regulatory-escalation procedures.
   - An audit task is complete only when evidence is located or validly reconstructed, the underlying case is assessed, and the required disposition is approved. If an authorization never existed, adding a later file does not prove that it existed at dispatch.
   - Declare recovery only after critical controls are demonstrably operating, inventory differences are resolved or contained, backlog is within age limits, and remaining exceptions have explicit quality/compliance acceptance. Use a stabilization observation period and exercise the fallback before retiring the incident team. The “invoice closed” count must not be used as the recovery definition.

## 5. Resilience indicators

Targets should be set before the incident by segment and product risk. The historical exception rates are useful regression alerts, not permanent acceptable limits; missing critical-control evidence should have a near-zero target. Both throughput and control quality must pass their thresholds.

| Dimension | Type | Indicator and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Throughput** | **Leading** | Productive cases per paid hour, inventory accuracy, worker/shelf capacity, and time blocked by missing data or equipment. Normalize for case complexity and product handling time. |
| | **Lagging** | Completed deliveries per available hour and first-pass completion, reported separately by segment/product. Raw case totals across unequal incident windows are not a productivity measure. |
| **Service protection** | **Leading** | Percentage of arriving orders triaged within the SLA, verified critical stock available, and capacity reserved for P1 demand. |
| | **Lagging** | Fill rate, median/p90 cycle time, and on-time delivery by customer and product risk. Report the tail even when the median recovers. A fill rate without a comparator or case mix can be misleading. |
| **Traceability** | **Leading** | Percentage of pick, pack, dispatch, and custody events captured at the point of occurrence; median scan-to-event latency; unexplained stock differences. |
| | **Lagging** | Traceability exceptions, late reconstructed events, unlinked lot/serial records, and percentage of cases reconstructable without unsupported assumptions. |
| **Control completeness** | **Leading** | Real-time percentage with current authorization, required temperature evidence, dual control, recall clearance, and documented release before dispatch. |
| | **Lagging** | Missing-document and dual-control rates, control failures by type, independent audit results, and quarantine/recall outcomes. Missing records must also be distinguished from confirmed actual temperature or security failures. |
| **Backlog age and recovery workload** | **Leading** | Current queue size, oldest and p90 case age, overdue review dates, unreconciled manual reservations, and audit tasks opened per day. |
| | **Lagging** | Net backlog burn-down, percentage older than SLA, aging audit cases, and percentage of operational cases still awaiting compliance closure. Track both count and age. |
| **Rework** | **Leading** | Pending corrections, first-pass yields by process variant, and recurring causes such as stale inventory or duplicate entry. |
| | **Lagging** | Rework/correction case rate, repeat correction rate, correction time, and cases requiring repack, recheck, or cancellation after release. |
| **Fairness** | **Leading** | Published allocation rules, documented exception approvals, comparable treatment within priority tiers, and review of deferral reasons by customer group. |
| | **Lagging** | Fill, cancellation, deferral, and cycle-time differences by group, adjusted for product risk and promised need. Investigate unexplained disparities rather than assuming equal treatment or intentional priority are equivalent to fairness. |

## 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

Effort is relative: **Low** means mainly process/configuration work; **Medium** means coordinated operational, quality, and training change; **High** means significant system, integration, or infrastructure work.

| Priority | Recommendation | Implementation effort | Expected benefit | Residual risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Approve a product/customer control matrix, delegated offline-authority procedure, exception expiry, and named incident roles before the next outage. Explicitly prohibit C and routine G. | **Low–Medium** | Quick reduction in improvised authorization and release decisions; lets teams protect hospitals without bypassing mandatory controls. | Compliance capacity can still be exhausted; requires trained backups and periodic rule review. |
| **2** | Build point-of-work evidence capture: offline scanning or signed paper fallback, unique case IDs, temperature loggers, controlled-order dual verification, and an importable event journal. | **High** | Converts E, F, and H from retrospective reconstruction into defensible contemporaneous evidence; improves traceability and recall capability. | Paper/device loss, synchronization duplicates, training errors, and compromised offline credentials remain possible. |
| **3** | Establish a reconciliation gateway and manual-inventory quarantine. Keep incident cases in **operational / reconciled / compliance-closed** states, with a daily aged work queue. | **Medium** | Reduces duplicate allocation, stock divergence, false closure, and the T4-style hidden workload tail. | Historical gaps cannot always be repaired; some cases will remain cancelled, quarantined, or formally accepted. |
| **4** | Implement risk-based backlog prioritization, prompt deferral notices, fixed review dates, and alternative-supply escalation. | **Low–Medium** | Protects urgent care while reducing silent cancellation and unmanaged customer harm. | Demand bursts and genuinely low-priority work may still accumulate; informal exceptions can reintroduce bias. |
| **5** | Implement a control-and-service dashboard using the leading/lagging measures above, segmented by customer and product—not a single average cycle time or fill rate. | **Medium** | Detects deterioration before invoices close and makes the long tail visible to management. | Metric gaming, false alarms, and poor data definitions can mislead if governance is weak. |
| **6** | Exercise manual failover regularly, including a stale-snapshot scenario, cold-chain excursion, controlled dispatch, cyber credential compromise, and system-return reconciliation. | **Medium** | Tests whether B and the fallback actually work under load; exposes hidden dependencies and evidence gaps before a real incident. | Skills and paper stock can decay; exercises may not reproduce peak demand or a sophisticated cyberattack. |
| **7** | Harden recovery architecture: segregated backups, tested restoration, access revocation, redundant scanning, and authorized alternate couriers with appropriate temperature and security controls. | **High** | Reduces dependence on improvised workarounds and makes rerouting safer, especially for controlled and cold-chain orders. | Complexity, integration failures, and new attack paths; redundancy must be tested rather than assumed. |

## 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept **higher manual effort, longer standard-order cycle times, and carefully prioritized capacity allocation** to protect hospitals and critical products. It should not accept assumed authorization, routine scanless dispatch, incomplete temperature/security evidence, or operational closure that hides unresolved compliance work. The preferred posture is risk-based triage with unchanged mandatory control floors—not a blanket suspension of controls for every order. Recommendations could be relaxed for standard ambient workflows if independently tested evidence shows current authorization, complete point-of-work traceability, physical-inventory agreement, controlled aging backlogs, and consistently applied allocation rules. Restoration of the interface or a recovered median cycle time alone is insufficient evidence that safe recovery has been achieved.
