## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation

The raw-to-mapped fitness gap is the critical diagnostic lens for March. Raw fitness collapses from 0.93 to 0.55, while mapped fitness recovers to 0.90. That ~0.35 difference is almost entirely attributable to the label migration (`Validate Eligibility`  `Eligibility Screen`) and duplicated `Open Case` events, plus missing resource identifiers that corrupt alignment traces. The remaining ~0.02–0.03 gap below baseline (0.92) is small enough to be noise or early behavioral drift, but the recovery demonstrates that the normative control-flow structure survived the migration largely intact. Therefore, the March drop should not be interpreted as a process break, but as a provenance/semantic event.

However, a label mapping recovers conformance without proving the business process was unchanged. Remapping is a post-hoc analytic fix: it removes the semantic mismatch between the old normative model and the new event vocabulary, but it does not establish whether assessors actually changed how they evaluate cases, whether document collection patterns shifted, or whether resource assignment rules were altered. If a true behavioral change happened precisely when the system changed—e.g., a new validation screen causing different review behavior—the mapping would hide it by forcing new labels into an old model. Thus mapped fitness is necessary but insufficient for process stability claims.

Duplicated technical events and missing resources affect different analyses asymmetrically. Duplicate `Open Case` events corrupt control-flow graphs (introducing spurious `Open Case  Register` edges) and inflate alignment costs because the normative model expects a single start. Removing them restores DFG topology and fitness, but this is a data-cleaning operation, not a process insight. Missing resource identifiers, concentrated in migrated activities and not uniformly distributed by team or case, primarily bias organizational analyses—resource profiles, handoff patterns, and workload balancing—without necessarily altering the sequence of activities. If analysts only look at mapped conformance, they miss the organizational drift that may explain later queueing.

The post-May DFG and performance patterns cannot be dismissed as logging drift alone. The normalized DFG rates (after mapping) show a sustained rise in `Assess  Request Info` from 12 to 29 per 100 cases, and `Assess  Senior Assessment` from 4 to 22, while `CollectDocs  Assess` and the decision-rate fragment decline. These are structural behavioral changes: a loop is being traversed more frequently, and a previously inconsistently recorded high-complexity path is becoming regular. Meanwhile median cycle times grow from 31.5 to 49 hours, p90 from 69 to 121, and reconstructed waiting before `Assess` increases much more than service time (which only moves ~0.6 h). Logging drift cannot explain increased loop frequency, growing senior-assessment usage, or right-tail cycle-time expansion. The combination of mapped-DGF structural change plus performance degradation confirms real behavioral and performance drift layered on top of the March semantic disruption.

## 9. Provisional attribution

The evidence supports a **multiple-change-point** account rather than a single global drift event, with distinct confidence levels by mechanism.

**Strongest conclusions (high confidence):** The March 2026 fitness collapse is primarily a **logging/semantic and technical-provenance drift** (confidence ~95%). The raw-to-mapped recovery (0.550.90) is too large and too precisely timed to the migration to be coincidental; the simultaneous appearance of `Eligibility Screen`, duplicated `Open Case`, and missing resources confirms an IT migration effect. The **supplier-driven rework surge** beginning May 5 is also strongly supported (confidence ~85%). Supplier Z’s share jumps from 18% to 43% exactly on the disruption date, and the `AssessRequest Info` rate doubles in parallel. The temporal alignment and supplier-specific non-randomness make a pure population or seasonal explanation unlikely.

**Plausible but not definitive (moderate confidence, 60–75%):** **Staffing-driven queueing** from June 15 is highly plausible given the assessor exits, stable service times (~4.85.4 h), and reconstructed queue growth, but it is mediated by the July 1 priority rule, which redistributes rather than simply removes capacity. I am ~75% confident that staffing loss increased aggregate delay, but only ~60% confident about the precise split between queue expansion and priority-induced redistribution. The **seasonal population drift** in October is clearly visible (high-complexity 29%41%, volume +8%, senior-assessment rate 922), and historically renewal season explains this; however, because this is the first year recorded under migration and staffing deficit, I cannot fully separate seasonal population effects from behavioral responses to that load (confidence ~80% for seasonal presence, ~60% for pure population vs. interaction). **Persistent behavioral adaptation** is plausible because, even after supplier recovery (Sep 10) and seasonal load reduction (Dec), mapped fitness (0.90) remains below baseline (0.92–0.93), rework stays at 21% vs. 12%, and senior-assessment frequency remains elevated. I estimate ~65% confidence that the process has adapted rather than merely lagging behind recovery.

**Where mediation and interaction prevent clean attribution:** The May–July window is the most entangled. Supplier disruption increases document incompleteness, which raises loop frequency; staffing cuts simultaneously reduce assessment capacity, which amplifies the delay per loop. The priority rule (July 1) interacts by accelerating high-complexity cases through the assessment queue—potentially reducing their specific wait—while standard cases bear the delay, making aggregate median and p90 misleading without segmentation. Furthermore, the migration’s missing-resource problem could bias resource-level queue analysis, making it harder to confirm whether observed delays are due to fewer assessors or to unassigned events. I treat these as **interacting mechanisms** rather than additive.

**What would revise the account:** If event-level data show that Supplier Z cases have identical case-mmix and document-completeness distributions to others, the supplier hypothesis weakens and a pure process-change or measurement explanation strengthens. If queue-wait analysis by priority class shows no redistribution (i.e., both high and standard cases wait longer uniformly), the priority-r redistribution claim weakens. If multi-year data reveal October is always high-complexity with the same cycle-time profile, seasonal drift is confirmed; if 2026 October exceeds historical norms after controlling for mix, staffing interaction dominates. If the missing-resource problem is resolved and mapped fitness fully returns to 0.93 with no structural DFG change, I would revise to conclude that 2026 drift was almost entirely logging + supplier + temporary staffing, with no persistent behavioral adaptation.

In summary: March = logging; May = supplier; June–July = staffing + priority interaction; October = seasonal + interaction; December = intervention + partial recovery; persistent gap = plausible adaptation, requiring longer observation.
