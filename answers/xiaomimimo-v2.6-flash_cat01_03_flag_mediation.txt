**Where interference, exposure-induced confounding, and treatment-confounder feedback arise (180 words):**

Interference arises because manual-review and escalation queues are shared: one case's flag-driven routing (flag  manual_review) lengthens manual_queue_wait for every other case already in that queue. Exposure-induced confounding occurs because the flag determines manual_review occurrence, and manual_review confounds escalation  cycle_time while itself being a consequence of the exposure — violating the requirement that mediator–outcome confounders be pre-treatment for natural effects. Treatment-confounder feedback appears when earlier flagged cases alter shared queue state, which then affects subsequent cases' waiting times and reviewers' escalation decisions, creating a dynamic loop where the "confounder" (queue state) is a function of prior exposures. Naive mediation decompositions that condition on manual_review would therefore bias estimates in ways standard regression adjustment cannot fix.

| P4: retain manual review, change escalation criteria or capacity | No effect (triage unaffected) | Reduces escalation_queue_wait and/or escalation occurrence; minor effect on manual queue (cases not escalated close faster) | Modest: addresses the 420 min escalation wait but not the larger 405 min manual wait | Depends on criteria: tighter criteria may miss genuinely complex cases; looser criteria over-consume senior time | Restricting escalation may improve overall flow; adding senior capacity is expensive | Low–moderate: escalation is one of several delays, not the largest | Complementary to P2 or P3; insufficient alone to close the 987 vs 345 min gap |

**Selected pilot: P2 restricted to flagged, medium-complexity cases.**

- **Eligibility:** flag = 1 AND complexity = Medium (moderate risk group; excludes High complexity as safety reserve).
- **Randomization:** Among eligible cases, randomize 50/50 to "mandatory manual review" (control) vs "review only if triggered by reviewer judgment" (treatment). Priority triage retained for both arms.
- **Queue-capacity guardrail:** If control-arm manual_queue_wait median exceeds 6 hours, or treatment-arm queue wait rises by >30% due to redistribution, pause and reassess.
- **Primary outcome:** Total cycle time (arrival  close).
- **Secondary outcomes:** Manual-review occurrence rate, escalation rate, triage wait, decision-outcome distribution, reviewer workload (cases/reviewer/shift), 7-day appeal or override rate.
- **Minimum process data needed:** Start and completion timestamps for all stages; queue-entry timestamps (when case enters manual/escalation queue); queue depth at entry; reviewer ID; flag value, risk_score, complexity, arrival_load; decision outcome; any subsequent appeal or correction.
- **Stopping conditions:** (1) Treatment-arm reject rate differs from control by >5 pp; (2) 7-day appeal rate exceeds historical baseline by >3 pp; (3) queue-capacity guardrail triggered; (4) after 80 eligible cases per arm or fixed calendar period, whichever comes first.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk.** If unobserved case characteristics (beyond `risk_score` and `complexity`) are substantially more prevalent in flagged cases and independently drive longer service times at every stage — because genuinely harder cases require more investigation — then the observed cycle-time gap reflects case need rather than flag-driven routing. My recommendation to reduce mandatory review (P2) would reverse: the thorough process may be warranted, and the bottleneck diagnosis would shift to service efficiency rather than queueing.

2. **Queue interference.** If removing mandatory manual review for flagged cases merely displaces the delay elsewhere — e.g., cases that would have been caught in review are later escalated through even longer queues, or if shared-queue congestion would remain high from unflagged cases alone — then P2 would not improve cycle times, and the mechanism ("mandatory review congests the queue") would be wrong. The preferred intervention would shift toward P3 (add capacity) instead of P2 (remove stages).

3. **Decision quality.** If a randomized P2 pilot shows that flagged cases without mandatory review have a significantly higher rate of wrong approvals, subsequent appeals, or overrides (exceeding the stopping threshold), the quality cost outweighs the time savings. The recommendation would shift toward P3 (add protected capacity while retaining all reviews), accepting longer cycle times in exchange for preserved decision integrity.

4. **Timestamp semantics.** If `triage_start` for unflagged cases actually records queue entry (when the case "arrives at triage") while for flagged cases it records actual work start — or vice versa — the apparent 12 vs 150-minute triage advantage could be an artifact of inconsistent logging. This would shift the bottleneck diagnosis: perhaps the real delay for unflagged cases occurs earlier (arrival  true triage start), and the priority lane's benefit is smaller than observed. Which stage to optimize would change.

5. **Positivity / lack of overlap.** If within every stratum of `risk_score`, `complexity`, and `arrival_load`, only one flag value exists (e.g., no unflagged cases with risk_score > 0.58 or no flagged cases with risk_score < 0.61), then no within-stratum comparison is possible. The causal effect of the flag (independent of case mix) would be non-identifiable from observational data, and any targeted policy recommendation would be unsupported — requiring a randomized design before acting.

6. **Reviewer behaviour change.** If reviewers systematically slow their service time or escalate more readily when they see the flag (knowing the case is "high-risk"), rather than based on actual case need, then the flag causes behavioral delay. Suppressing the flag (P1) while retaining automated routing could reduce service and escalation times — but if reviewers then under-investigate genuinely risky cases, quality drops. Evidence of flag-dependent reviewer pacing (flagged vs unflagged service-time differences within the same reviewer) would reverse the preferred policy from P2 toward P1.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed:** Flagged cases reach triage in a median 12 minutes versus 150 for unflagged, with shorter triage service (36 vs 57 minutes). All flagged cases undergo manual review (10/10) versus 4/10 unflagged, and six flagged cases escalate versus two unflagged. Downstream delays dominate: flagged cases wait a median 405 minutes to start manual review and 420 minutes to start escalation. Median total cycle time is 987 minutes for flagged versus 345 for unflagged. Decision outcomes are identical (8 approve, 2 reject per group). Triage resources split perfectly by flag (T1/T2 vs T3/T4).

**Plausible mechanism:** The flag triggers a routing cascade — priority lane, mandatory manual review, increased escalation — that adds stages and shared-queue waits far exceeding triage savings. Congested manual-review queues are the largest observable delay.

**Unidentified:** Whether routing or case mix drives the gap; whether display or automated routing triggers the cascade; natural direct/indirect effects under cross-world assumptions; whether identical reject rates reflect appropriate decisions or insufficient power.

**Most useful next evidence:** A randomized P2 pilot (remove mandatory review for medium-complexity flagged cases, retain priority triage) cleanly isolates the review-mandate contribution, providing causal estimates of both performance and quality effects to inform sustainable redesign.
