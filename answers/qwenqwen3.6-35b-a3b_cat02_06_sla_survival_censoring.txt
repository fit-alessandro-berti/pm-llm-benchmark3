# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time Origin:** `Submit` event.
**Time Horizon:** 72 hours (SLA limit).
**Time Variable:** $T$ denotes time to event. For open cases, we observe $C = \min(T, \text{age})$ with censoring indicator $\delta$.

**Estimands:**
The phrase "probability of breaching the SLA" is ambiguous. We define three estimands to address different operational needs.

1.  **Estimand E1: SLA Breach Risk (Probability of No Resolution by 72h).**
    *   *Definition:* $P(T_{\text{resolve}} > 72)$.
    *   *Scope:* Includes cases that resolve after 72h, cases that withdraw/reject/transfer at any time, and open cases still in progress.
    *   *Operational Decision:* **SLA Compliance & Customer Satisfaction.** This measures the fraction of cases that do not achieve the desired outcome (`Resolve`) within the contract window. It captures all sources of "failure" to resolve, including legitimate withdrawals and external transfers.

2.  **Estimand E2: Cumulative Incidence of Late Resolution (Competing-Risk Adjusted).**
    *   *Definition:* $P(T_{\text{resolve}} > 72 \cap T_{\text{resolve}} < \infty)$.
    *   *Scope:* Includes only cases that eventually resolve but do so after 72h. Excludes cases that withdraw, reject, or transfer (assuming these are absorbing states where resolution probability is zero or undefined).
    *   *Operational Decision:* **Process Efficiency & Bottleneck Identification.** This isolates the risk that a case will eventually succeed but fail the SLA due to internal delays. It helps distinguish between cases that are "stuck" versus cases that are "done" via other outcomes.

3.  **Estimand E3: Stagnation Risk (Probability of No Terminal Disposition by 72h).**
    *   *Definition:* $P(T_{\text{terminal}} > 72)$, where $T_{\text{terminal}}$ is time to any terminal outcome (`Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`).
    *   *Scope:* Cases still in `open` status at 72h.
    *   *Operational Decision:* **Workload Management & Resource Allocation.** This identifies cases that have not reached any conclusion, indicating potential bottlenecks or cases requiring intervention to move toward a terminal state.

**Contribution of Specific Cases:**
*   **S03 (76h, resolved):**
    *   E1: Breach = Yes (76 > 72). Contributes to breach probability.
    *   E2: Late Resolution = Yes. Contributes to late resolution risk.
    *   E3: Stagnation = No (Terminal at 76h).
*   **S09 (92h, open):**
    *   E1: Breach = Yes (Not resolved by 72h; censored at 92h implies $T_{\text{resolve}} > 72$).
    *   E2: Late Resolution = Uncertain/Censored. Contributes to risk set but event status is censored.
    *   E3: Stagnation = Yes (Open at 92h > 72h).
*   **S06 (27h, withdrawn):**
    *   E1: Breach = Yes (Not resolved). Contributes to breach probability.
    *   E2: Late Resolution = N/A (Competing event at 27h). Does not contribute to resolution risk.
    *   E3: Stagnation = No (Terminal at 27h).
*   **S08 (68h, transferred):**
    *   E1: Breach = Yes (Not resolved). Contributes to breach probability.
    *   E2: Late Resolution = Unknown/Competing. Depends on assumption: if transfer implies no internal resolution, it is a competing event; if transfer implies external resolution, it may be informative censoring.
    *   E3: Stagnation = No (Terminal at 68h).

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

Analyzing only `resolved` cases (S01, S02, S03, S04, S07, S13, S16, S17, S18, S19, S20, S27, S28) yields biased conclusions:

1.  **Survivor Bias:** Resolved cases are a selected subset. S09 (open, 92h) and S26 (open, 104h) are likely to have longer resolution times than resolved cases. Excluding them underestimates the true distribution of resolution times and SLA breach risk.
2.  **Informative Censoring:** Open cases are not missing at random. S09 has `last_event_gap_h`=31, S26 has gap=72. Long gaps suggest difficulty in progression. If open cases have systematically longer gaps, censoring them as "resolved at cutoff" or dropping them biases the hazard estimate.
3.  **Competing Risks:** Withdrawals and Rejections prevent resolution. Cohort B has withdrawals (S22, S30) and rejections (S23). If Cohort B has a higher withdrawal rate, a naive analysis of resolution times might show "faster" resolution for B, but this could be driven by cases leaving the system earlier, not by improved efficiency.
4.  **Cohort Imbalance & Policy Change:** Cohort B (S16-S32) follows a policy change involving automated screening. S16 shows `Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Resolve`, whereas S01 shows `Submit>Screen>AutoCheck>Resolve`. The order of `AutoCheck` and `Screen` changed. Naive analysis pooling cohorts ignores this structural shift. S18 (B, P1) resolved in 12h, while S04 (A, P1) took 29h. Pooling might mask cohort-specific improvements or regressions.
5.  **Active Open Cases:** S15 is open at 35h. S09 is open at 92h. S15 is below SLA, S09 is above. Dropping S15 biases the "open" distribution toward longer durations, inflating perceived stagnation risk.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Comparison of Methods:**
*   **Kaplan-Meier (KM):** Estimates $P(T > t)$. Invalid for E2 because it treats competing events (Withdraw/Reject) as censored, overestimating resolution probability. Appropriate for E1 if transfers are censored, but ignores competing risks.
*   **Aalen-Johansen / Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF):** Estimates cause-specific probabilities in the presence of competing risks. Directly estimates E2. Handles Withdraw/Reject/Transfer as competing events. Recommended for E2.
*   **Cause-Specific Hazards:** Models hazard of resolution conditional on not having had a prior event. Useful for understanding process dynamics but does not directly give probabilities.
*   **Subdistribution Hazards (Fine-Gray):** Models effect of covariates on CIF. Useful for regression but complex to implement without specialized libraries.
*   **Restricted Mean Time (RMT):** Summarizes survival curve up to 72h. Good for comparing cohorts but less intuitive for SLA breach probability.
*   **Landmarking:** Analyzes risk conditional on being open at time $t$ (e.g., 48h). Useful for dynamic alerts but requires sufficient data at landmark.
*   **Multi-State Model:** Models transitions between process states (e.g., Screen $\to$ Review $\to$ Resolve). Captures process structure from prefixes.

**Recommendations:**
*   **Primary Approach: Aalen-Johansen / Cumulative Incidence Function (CIF).**
    *   *Justification:* The data contains multiple terminal outcomes (Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer). E2 is the most actionable metric for process improvement (reducing late resolutions). CIF correctly accounts for the fact that a withdrawal removes a case from the risk set of resolution. It provides the probability of resolution by time $t$, allowing direct calculation of breach risk as $1 - \text{CIF}(72)$.
*   **Secondary Approach: Multi-State Model with Process-Aware Covariates.**
    *   *Justification:* Prefixes reveal a process with loops (`Review>Rework>Review`) and parallel paths (`AutoCheck` vs `Screen`). A multi-state model can estimate transition hazards (e.g., hazard of moving from `Review` to `Resolve`). This allows integration of process features (e.g., time in `Review`, presence of rework) and can handle the policy change (Cohort B) by modeling state-specific effects. It addresses the "process-aware" requirement.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

**Derived Features from Prefixes:**
1.  **Current Stage:** Last activity in `prefix`. E.g., S09 is in `Review`.
2.  **Path Pattern:** Sequence of activities. E.g., `AutoCheck` before `Screen` vs. `Screen` before `AutoCheck`.
3.  **Rework:** Binary flag `rework_count > 0` or pattern `Review>Rework>Review` in prefix.
4.  **Escalation:** `escalated` flag or `Escalate` in prefix.
5.  **Inactivity Gap:** `last_event_gap_h`.
6.  **Time in Current Stage:** `duration_or_age_h` minus time of last activity (requires timestamps; unavailable, so use `last_event_gap_h` as proxy for inactivity).

**Leakage and Immortal-Time Risks:**
*   **Immortal Time Bias:** `Escalated` and `Rework` occur after case start. Treating them as baseline covariates (e.g., "Is escalated?" at t=0) is invalid. A case cannot be escalated before it exists. If used as baseline, cases that escalate later are misclassified as "not escalated" for the time before escalation, biasing the hazard of escalation toward null or creating spurious associations.
    *   *Mitigation:* Use `Escalated` as a time-varying covariate. Or, define baseline features only from pre-escalation activities (e.g., first activity after Submit).
*   **Leakage:** Using `rework_count` as a predictor for resolution time is safe only if rework has occurred before the prediction time. For survival analysis, rework is a process event, not a predictor. Using it as a feature in a predictive model for breach risk at t=0 would leak future information.
*   **Baseline vs. Time-Varying:**
    *   *Baseline:* `priority`, `cohort`, `prefix` pattern (if pattern is fixed at start, e.g., S16 always starts with AutoCheck).
    *   *Time-Varying:* `current_stage`, `escalated`, `rework`, `last_event_gap_h`.

*   **Calibration:** Check if predicted breach probabilities match observed frequencies in bins (e.g., cases predicted 20% breach risk should have ~20% breach rate).
*   **Fairness:** Check if breach risk is independent of sensitive attributes (if available). Check if `escalated` flag is used fairly.

**Causal vs. Predictive:**
*   Risk scores are predictive. A high risk score for a case in `Review` does not mean moving it to `AutoCheck` will reduce risk (leakage/confounding). Policy changes should be evaluated using causal methods (e.g., difference-in-differences) if rollout data permits.

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**Missing Data for Stronger Inference:**
1.  **Exact Event Timestamps:** Only `duration_or_age_h` and `last_event_gap_h` are available. Without timestamps for each activity, we cannot compute exact transition durations, hazard rates, or time-varying covariates accurately. We must assume uniform distribution of time within gaps or use gaps as proxies.
2.  **External Outcomes:** For `transferred` cases, we do not know if they resolved externally. This makes censoring informative. External resolution speed affects E2 sensitivity.
3.  **Cutoff/Start Dates:** The observation cutoff is implied by `duration_or_age_h` for open cases, but exact dates are unknown. This prevents assessing time-varying workload or seasonality.
4.  **Time-Varying Workload:** No data on staff availability or case arrival rate. Hazards may vary with workload.
5.  **Assignment Mechanism:** Assignment was not randomized. Cohort B policy change may correlate with case complexity or priority distribution. P1 transfers (S08, S21, S32) suggest P1 cases are more likely to transfer, but sample is small.
6.  **Policy Rollout Compliance:** "Rollout compliance was incomplete." We do not know when the policy applied to each case. Cohort B may contain mixed policy adherence.
7.  **Rework Details:** `rework_count` is available, but not the reason for rework. Rework may indicate complexity or error, affecting resolution time.

**Impact:**
*   Lack of timestamps limits process mining to control-flow analysis only; performance analysis is approximate.
*   Informative censoring for transfers and open cases requires strong assumptions.
*   Cohort comparisons are confounded by non-random assignment and policy rollout timing.
*   Recommendations should focus on relative comparisons and risk stratification rather than absolute causal effects.
