# 1. Disruption timeline

| period | operational state and dominant variants | performance and risk posture |
|---|---|---|
| **T0 — Day -30 to -1: baseline** | Normal sequence from authorization through reservation, scanning, release, dispatch, proof of delivery, and invoice close. Compliance holds, backorders, and rechecks were visible exceptions rather than substitutes for controls. | 42,000 cases; fitness **0.96**, with **91%** fitting traces. Traceability exceptions were **0.8%**, missing cold-chain records **0.4%**, and missing controlled-substance dual control **0.2%**. This establishes that normal operations were controlled, though not perfect. |
| **T1 — Day 0 morning: outage onset** | WMS became unavailable. Electronic inventory reservation, scans, and portions of authorization and order-to-delivery visibility failed. Operations shifted from system-enforced control to human triage. | Immediate risks were stale inventory, duplicate or unauthorized release, loss of chain-of-custody evidence, and unmeasured backlog. The key resilience decision was to continue selectively rather than either stop everything or ship indiscriminately. |
| **T2 — Day 0 afternoon to Day 2: manual workaround** | Emergency triage, hospital fast lane, offline authorization, printed inventory snapshots, manual pick lists, temporary release checks, dispatch without scan, phone proof, spreadsheet invoice holds, and deferral of noncritical orders dominated. Only 1,650 manual packs flowed to temporary release control, while 1,800 went directly to dispatch without scan. | Critical service was partly protected: hospital fill remained **91%** and median cycle time was **10h**, versus 8h baseline. That resilience was purchased with degraded pharmacy service—**61%** fill and 54h median—and major control deterioration: traceability **9.5%**, missing temperature evidence **4.8%**, missing dual control **2.9%**, rework **12.5%**, and cancellations **7.8%**. Fitness fell to **0.71**, with only **44%** fitting traces. This phase contains both effective adaptation and concealed noncompliance. |
| **T3 — Day 3 to Day 6: partial recovery** | Intake returned, but unreliable synchronization and scanning produced a hybrid process: electronic steps mixed with manual records and reconstruction. | Service and controls improved but remained materially degraded. Traceability exceptions were **5.4%**, rework **9.0%**, and cancellations **5.2%**; pharmacy median was 38h and bulk 84h. Fitness recovered to **0.82**, but only **59%** of traces fit. Restoring an interface did not restore trustworthy inventory or end-to-end control. |
| **T4 — Day 7 to Day 21: stabilization** | Most normal routing returned, supplemented by backlog prioritization, manual-reservation reconciliation, proof-of-delivery remediation, repack/recheck, and audit remediation. | Hospital median returned to **8h**, but pharmacy remained at 28h and bulk at 58h; p90 bulk remained **120h** versus 72h baseline. Traceability exceptions (**2.2%**), rework (**5.8%**), and cancellations (**3.0%**) were still above baseline. Fitness reached **0.90**, but fitting traces were only **76%**. The 2,300 invoice-close-to-audit-remediation flows show a long compliance tail: apparent operational recovery preceded evidentiary and control recovery. |

# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**Resilience** preserves an important service while retaining the purpose of required controls through an authorized, auditable substitute. A process need not follow the baseline path to be resilient. For example, a paper pick list can be resilient if it captures order, product, lot, quantity, picker, checker, and time contemporaneously. The baseline scan is absent, but its traceability function survives.

**Concealed noncompliance** preserves apparent throughput by deleting a control, deferring evidence creation, or moving unresolved work outside operational cycle-time reporting. The order appears dispatched or closed, while authorization, custody, temperature, or POD remains uncertain. The T2 move-on-model omissions and the later audit tail illustrate this distinction.

### Predominantly true resilience

- **B — Manual authorization check**, provided the offline source and confirmation are documented.
- **I — Defer noncritical orders**, as controlled load shedding, subject to fairness and aging limits.
- **J — Backlog prioritization**, when based on explicit clinical and product-risk criteria.
- **K — Reconcile manual reservation**, as a necessary recovery control.
- **A — Hospital fast lane**, but only when urgency changes sequence, not required control content.

### Predominantly risky shortcuts

- **C — Skip system authorization check:** customer history is not a substitute for current eligibility.
- **G — Dispatch without scan:** as implemented, there was no sufficiently strong contemporaneous custody substitute.
- **H — Phone POD**, where it was the only evidence for controlled or otherwise high-risk delivery.
- **L — Audit remediation**, if used to treat operational closure as full compliance or to “manufacture” missing evidence retrospectively.

### Ambiguous and dependent on design or risk appetite

- **D — Inventory snapshot:** reasonable immediately after failure, unsafe as the snapshot ages.
- **E — Manual pick list:** resilient with strict lot-level evidence; risky when scans are reconstructed later from memory or incomplete paper.
- **F — Temporary release control:** resilient for orders actually subjected to complete risk-based checks, but inadequate where sampling replaces mandatory controls or omits cold-chain evidence.
- **A, I, and J:** prioritization is defensible, but informal or commercially biased allocation creates fairness and authorization risk.
- **H:** acceptable as corroboration for low-risk ambient deliveries, not as a universal substitute.

The metrics reinforce the distinction. T2 preserved hospital throughput but traceability exceptions rose nearly twelvefold, missing controlled-substance dual control rose from **0.2% to 2.9%**, and rework rose from **3.5% to 12.5%**. Therefore, hospital throughput alone does not demonstrate resilience. Conversely, low baseline-model fitness alone does not prove failure: authorized emergency triage and manual checks are intentionally nonbaseline variants that may retain the control objective.

# 4. Recovery process model

The redesigned process should be an approved **Emergency Distribution Process**, activated and exited by explicit criteria rather than improvised locally.

## A. Trigger and governance

**Activate emergency mode** when any of these occurs:

- WMS, authorization, scanning, temperature integration, or inventory synchronization is unavailable beyond a defined tolerance, such as 15–30 minutes for critical operations.
- Inventory accuracy cannot be trusted across active locations.
- Cyber containment requires system isolation.
- More than a defined percentage of orders cannot complete a mandatory control.
- A control owner declares that electronic evidence is unavailable or unreliable.

On activation:

1. Incident commander records start time, affected functions, and cyber constraints.
2. Compliance and quality approve the permitted emergency variants.
3. Every case receives a unique emergency order ID.
4. A single controlled manual ledger is opened; uncontrolled local spreadsheets are prohibited.
5. A decision log records risk acceptance, scope, owner, expiry, and review time.
6. Emergency rules are reviewed at fixed intervals, with stale inventory snapshots and temporary approvals automatically expiring.

## B. Triage rules

After receipt, classify each order on four dimensions:

1. **Customer criticality:** hospital-critical, pharmacy-standard, wholesale-bulk.
2. **Product control:** controlled substance, cold-chain, recall-sensitive, ambient.
3. **Clinical urgency:** immediate patient need, replenishment at risk, routine.
4. **control feasibility:** evidence complete, substitute control available, or control unavailable.

Suggested priority score:

- Highest: verified urgent hospital demand with all minimum controls feasible.
- Next: controlled/cold-chain/recall-sensitive orders whose handling window or shortage risk is time-sensitive.
- Next: pharmacy orders, with minimum allocation protections to avoid systematic community-care harm.
- Then: routine hospital and pharmacy replenishment.
- Last: deferrable wholesale-bulk orders.

Within each class, use oldest-ready-first. Overrides require a reason, approver, and timestamp. No commercial-value criterion should override clinical urgency, product risk, or backlog age without executive and compliance approval.

## C. Minimum controls by risk

| category | emergency minimum before release/dispatch |
|---|---|
| **All orders** | Unique order ID; customer and ship-to identity; offline authorization result and source; product, quantity, lot where applicable; inventory allocation; picker/packer identity; release decision; courier handoff; exception status. |
| **Hospital-critical ambient** | Expedited sequence is allowed, but offline authorization and contemporaneous custody evidence remain mandatory. A supervisor may prioritize, not waive. |
| **Pharmacy-standard ambient** | Same core controls; may be deferred under published allocation rules. Authorization may use approved cached lists plus authenticated confirmation. |
| **Wholesale-bulk ambient** | Deferral is preferred if manual capacity is constrained. Large quantity requires inventory confirmation to prevent depletion or oversell. |
| **Cold-chain** | Lot and expiry, pre-dispatch temperature, validated packaging, logger/device ID, pack-out time, maximum transit window, courier acceptance, and receipt temperature/evidence. If these cannot be recorded, hold or use an approved alternate channel. |
| **Controlled substance** | Current authorization, two-person pick/release verification, quantity and lot/serial as required, secure storage and handoff, named courier, signed custody transfer, and authenticated recipient. No phone-only POD or undocumented dispatch. |
| **Recall-sensitive** | Exact lot-to-customer mapping before dispatch and retrievable custody record. If lot traceability cannot be assured, do not release. |

A sample-based release check may apply only to explicitly approved low-risk ambient orders. It must never replace mandatory checks for controlled, cold-chain, or recall-sensitive products.

## D. Manual evidence capture

Use pre-numbered paper forms or an offline-capable application with:

- Order and emergency-event IDs.
- Form version and inventory-snapshot version.
- Customer authorization source, result, checker, and time.
- SKU, lot, expiry, quantity, storage condition, and source location.
- Picker, independent checker where required, packer, and release approver.
- Temperature values, device ID, and security seals where applicable.
- Courier, vehicle or route, handoff time, signatures, and package count.
- Recipient identity, delivery time, and POD method.
- Corrections made by single-line amendment or immutable digital history.
- Image capture/upload as soon as a trusted service is available.

Maintain a **live manual reservation ledger**: every allocation, pick, cancellation, return, and adjustment immediately changes the available balance. Printed snapshots must carry a “valid until” time and cannot remain the sole inventory source after material movement begins.

## E. Emergency process flow

1. **Receive order**
2. **Assign emergency ID and classify risk**
3. **Check authorization**
   - If verified: continue.
   - If uncertain: compliance hold or authenticated escalation.
   - If prohibited/unverifiable for a mandatory category: do not ship.
4. **Apply backlog/clinical priority**
5. **Reserve against controlled manual inventory ledger**
6. **Pick using pre-numbered list**
7. **Pack and record lot/temperature/security evidence**
8. **Risk-based release**
   - 100% for controlled, cold-chain, recall-sensitive.
   - Approved sampling only for low-risk ambient.
9. **Dispatch with manual custody event**
10. **Capture authenticated POD**
11. **Place invoice in one of three states**
   - evidence complete;
   - delivered, evidence pending;
   - compliance investigation required.
12. **Reconcile and close only when closure criteria are met**

## F. Backlog and restoration flow

When systems return:

1. Validate system integrity before bulk upload.
2. Freeze or quarantine disputed inventory locations.
3. Import emergency orders in sequence using their unique IDs.
4. Match manual reservations to physical counts and system balances.
5. Detect duplicate orders, duplicate reservations, wrong lots, and unmatched dispatches.
6. Prioritize reconciliation by:
   - controlled/cold-chain/recall-sensitive exposure;
   - patient or customer impact;
   - missing authorization or custody;
   - oldest case;
   - financial value only as a secondary criterion.
7. Separate ordinary backlog from evidence-remediation backlog.

## G. Audit-remediation flow

Each evidence exception should be classified as:

- **Recoverable evidence gap:** reliable contemporaneous evidence exists but was not uploaded.
- **Substantive control failure:** required control was not performed.
- **Indeterminate:** evidence cannot establish whether the control occurred.
- **False exception:** matching or interface error.

For substantive or indeterminate cases, route to quality/compliance for disposition: customer confirmation, stock quarantine, recall assessment, controlled-substance investigation, regulatory reporting, credit/replacement, or corrective action. A reconstructed record must be labeled as reconstructed, with source and confidence. It must not be represented as contemporaneous evidence.

Operational, financial, and compliance closure should be separate timestamps. Recovery is complete only when:

- service levels and tail latency are stable;
- inventory is reconciled;
- high-risk exceptions are resolved;
- remediation backlog is below an approved threshold;
- control-exception rates are near baseline for a sustained period.

# 5. Resilience indicators

Indicators should be segmented by customer class and product type; aggregate averages would conceal the hospital/pharmacy divergence.

## Leading indicators

| dimension | indicator |
|---|---|
| **Throughput capacity** | Orders received, triaged, picked, released, and dispatched per hour; manual processing capacity versus incoming demand; percentage stalled at each control. |
| **Service protection** | Hospital-critical fill rate; urgent-order completion within target; pharmacy minimum-allocation attainment; number of clinically escalated orders awaiting action. |
| **Traceability readiness** | Percentage with unique emergency ID; lot captured at pick; signed custody event at dispatch; matched courier and POD identifiers. |
| **Control completeness** | Authorization verified before release; high-risk orders receiving 100% release; controlled-substance dual-control completion; cold-chain pack-out and logger completion. |
| **Inventory trust** | Age of inventory snapshot; percentage of moves entered into manual ledger within a defined interval; unresolved manual-versus-physical variance. |
| **Backlog** | Volume and age by segment/risk; oldest hospital, pharmacy, and bulk order; percentage crossing class-specific aging limits. |
| **Evidence latency** | Time from physical event to evidence capture or upload; count of dispatches awaiting POD, temperature, or authorization evidence. |
| **Fairness** | Fill-rate and delay gap between hospitals, pharmacies, and wholesale customers; share of capacity by published priority class; override frequency and reason. |
| **Recovery burden** | New remediation cases created per 100 shipments versus cases closed; forecast hours required for reconciliation and audit work. |

## Lagging indicators

| dimension | indicator |
|---|---|
| **Throughput and service** | Completed orders; median and p90/p95 cycle times by segment; fill rate; cancellation rate; shortage or patient-impact complaints. |
| **Traceability** | Traceability exception rate; unmatched lot-to-customer records; missing or weak POD; time required to execute a simulated recall. |
| **Control outcomes** | Missing authorization, temperature record, release check, and dual control; unauthorized shipments; excursions; diversion or security events. |
| **Backlog** | Peak backlog, average and maximum age, time to clear, and percentage cancelled after deferral. |
| **Rework** | Correction/rework rate, duplicate shipment rate, inventory adjustments, credits, replacements, and labor hours per remediated case. |
| **Conformance** | Fitness and fitting-trace percentage against an **approved emergency model**, not only the normal model; unauthorized move-on-log and missing mandatory-control counts. |
| **Hidden workload** | Open remediation cases and labor hours after apparent operational recovery; time from delivery to compliance closure; proportion of invoice-closed cases still under investigation. |
| **Fairness** | Segment-level fill-rate, cancellation, and p90 delay ratios; pharmacy service floor breaches; concentration of deferrals or overrides by customer size or geography. |

Useful trigger limits should be stricter for high-risk products. For example, any controlled-substance dual-control failure or untraceable recall-sensitive lot should cause immediate escalation, rather than waiting for a percentage threshold.

# 6. Recommendations for future disruptions

| priority | recommendation | implementation effort | expected benefit | residual risk |
|---|---|---:|---|---|
| **1** | Define non-waivable controls and stop/hold criteria by product class, especially for controlled, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive items. | Medium | Prevents urgency from silently eliminating legal, safety, or custody requirements. | Critical demand may be delayed when no compliant alternate route exists. |
| **2** | Deploy a controlled offline emergency-workflow kit: unique IDs, pre-numbered forms, offline authorization lists, manual reservation ledger, pick/release forms, custody receipts, and POD templates. | Medium–High | Converts improvisation into auditable control substitution and reduces later reconstruction. | Human entry errors remain; forms and cached data require maintenance. |
| **3** | Replace “dispatch without scan” with a mandatory manual chain-of-custody event; prohibit undocumented dispatch. | Medium | Directly addresses the largest T2 traceability gap and improves recall and diversion response. | Manual signatures may still be illegible or fraudulent without identity verification. |
| **4** | Establish 100% emergency release requirements for high-risk products, including explicit cold-chain temperature and controlled-substance dual-control fields. | Medium | Reduces the observed missing temperature and dual-control failures. | Scarce qualified staff can constrain throughput; cross-training is needed. |
| **5** | Create formal triage and fairness rules with pharmacy service floors, backlog aging limits, and logged overrides. | Low–Medium | Preserves hospital-critical service without allowing indefinite or commercially biased deferral of pharmacies and bulk orders. | Genuine scarcity still produces unequal service and cancellations. |
| **6** | Build time-limited inventory continuity: frequent signed snapshots, offline decrements, lot/location tracking, and automatic expiry of stale views. | High | Reduces overselling, duplicate reservation, and wrong-lot risk during prolonged outages. | Accuracy degrades if physical movements bypass the ledger. |
| **7** | Separate operational delivery, financial close, and compliance close in systems and dashboards. Include remediation work in recovery reporting. | Medium | Makes the audit tail visible and prevents apparent cycle-time recovery from masking unresolved exposure. | Executives may still pressure teams to close queues without sufficient evidence. |
| **8** | Automate post-restoration matching of emergency IDs, orders, lots, reservations, courier records, POD, and temperature evidence. | High | Shortens reconciliation, detects duplicates, and lowers the 9–12.5% rework seen during degraded operation. | Poor source evidence cannot be repaired by matching logic. |
| **9** | Pre-contract compliant alternate couriers, cold-chain lanes, and secure controlled-substance handling options. | Medium–High | Allows continuity when internal scanning or transport integrations fail. | Partner capacity may also be constrained during a broad incident. |
| **10** | Exercise the emergency model quarterly using segmented scenarios and process mining against both normal and emergency reference models. | Medium | Tests whether deviations are authorized adaptations or uncontrolled shortcuts; exposes staffing and evidence bottlenecks. | Exercises may understate real cyber containment and demand pressures. |
| **11** | Size and cross-train compliance, pharmacy, inventory, and reconciliation surge teams. | Medium | Avoids B and F becoming bottlenecks and reduces the long T4 remediation tail. | Surge staff may lack proficiency unless regularly exercised. |
| **12** | Set recovery exit criteria covering inventory accuracy, high-risk exception closure, tail latency, and remediation backlog—not merely system availability. | Low | Prevents premature declaration of recovery during the T3/T4 hybrid-risk period. | Full closure may take longer and require explicit acceptance of residual low-risk cases. |

# 7. Management trade-off statement

Management should accept slower service, controlled deferral, manual processing, and nonbaseline paths when they protect critical supply **and** preserve the control objective through contemporaneous, auditable substitutes. It should not accept shipment based only on customer history, undocumented custody transfer, phone-only proof for controlled products, missing high-risk release or temperature controls, or the presentation of later remediation as equivalent to real-time compliance. The recommendation could be relaxed only with evidence that an alternative control achieves comparable authorization, lot traceability, temperature/security assurance, and chain-of-custody reliability; it should be tightened if pharmacy harm, stale-inventory variance, high-risk exceptions, or the remediation backlog exceed predefined limits.
