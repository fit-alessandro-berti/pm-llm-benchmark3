## 1. Interaction-graph description

A typed interaction graph represents the complex web of dependencies between physical and logical entities in the manufacturing process. The graph is heterogenous, meaning nodes have distinct types and edges represent specific semantic relationships between them.

Important node types:
- `Job` (e.g., J1, J2): The central unit of production tracking and value creation.
- `Component` and `ComponentLot` (e.g., C1, L7): The material inputs and their batch origin, which introduce quality-related dependencies.
- `Machine` (e.g., CNC-2, OVEN-1): Primary capital processing equipment.
- `Tool` (e.g., FIX-A, CAL-3): Reusable auxiliary resources required for specific activities.
- `Batch` (e.g., B1, B2): A logical grouping of Jobs processed simultaneously.
- `Shipment` (e.g., S1): The final consolidation mechanism for dispatch.

Important edge types and qualifiers:
- `requires`: Defines a prerequisite. A Job `requires` a Component (static) or a Tool (dynamic constraint).
- `contains`: A ComponentLot `contains` a Component.
- `batches`: A Batch `batches` multiple Jobs, creating a synchronization barrier.
- `consolidates`: A Shipment `consolidates` Jobs for delivery.
- Shared Event Edges: Event-based relations where objects are linked through simultaneous event participation (e.g., a `Machine Process` event explicitly linking a Job, Machine, Component, and Tool).

Higher-order motifs involving more than two object types:
1. **Batch Synchronization Motif (Job → Batch ← Job, Batch → Machine, Batch → Tool)**: A triad where multiple independent Jobs connect to a single Batch node, which in turn consumes Machine and Tool resources. This motif forces independent Jobs to synchronize their timelines, meaning early-arriving Jobs must wait for late-arriving Jobs.
2. **Auxiliary Contention Motif (Job → Tool ← Job, Job → Machine)**: When multiple Jobs require the same Tool (e.g., J3 and J4 needing FIX-A), a Job may secure the Machine but remain stalled waiting for the Tool. This creates indirect dependencies where a Job's cycle time is dictated by a Tool's lifecycle events rather than the Machine's processing speed.
3. **Upstream Quality Constraint Motif (Job → Component ← ComponentLot → Component → Job)**: If a ComponentLot undergoes a `Quarantine Lot` event, multiple independent Jobs are simultaneously halted. The release of the Lot becomes a coupling point that releases a wave of Jobs downstream simultaneously.

Edges that are direct observations versus inferred:
- **Direct Observations**: Static hierarchical relationships like `batches`, `contains`, `requires`, and `consolidates` are explicitly provided. Shared event participations (e.g., the `Batch Cure` event explicitly logging B1, OVEN-1, RACK-7, J1, J2, J3) are direct observations.
- **Inferred Edges**: Constraints such as "Wait for Tool" (e.g., J4 waiting for FIX-A) or "Wait for Lot Release" are inferred through timestamp analysis. Since the system lacks explicitly logged wait states, these edges are deduced by identifying temporal gaps between Job readiness and process initiation.

## 4. What flattening misses

For each viewpoint:
- **Job flattening**:
  - *Make visible*: End-to-end performance of an individual job and linear progression of processing states.
  - *Hide or duplicate*: Hides cross-job synchronization, batching barriers, and tool contention. Duplicates shared events (e.g., Batch Cure appears independently on multiple jobs). Lot events are inconsistently linked.
  - *Misleading conclusion*: Evaluators conclude that jobs spend excessive time in "staging" due to processing inefficiency, missing that jobs are physically ready but blocked waiting on shared tools or batch mates.
- **Machine flattening**:
  - *Make visible*: Equipment utilization percentages, active processing time, and idle gaps.
  - *Hide or duplicate*: Hides the underlying reasons for idleness (e.g., tool wait, upstream starvation, downstream blocking).
  - *Misleading conclusion*: Evaluators see 44-58% utilization and conclude there is massive excess capacity. This could prompt taking on more orders, causing catastrophic gridlock because the real bottlenecks (tools and batches) are strained.
- **Batch flattening**:
  - *Make visible*: Batch duration and the cycle time from batch start to finish.
  - *Hide or duplicate*: Hides the individual readiness times of the jobs trapped within the batch.
  - *Misleading conclusion*: B1's processing time looks highly efficient, completely obscuring the nearly 4-hour delay inflicted on J1 before the batch officially started.
- **Tool flattening**:
  - *Make visible*: Tool usage frequency and maintenance cycles (cleaning, calibration).
  - *Hide or duplicate*: Hides which specific machines or jobs are currently blocked by the tool's unavailability.
  - *Misleading conclusion*: Tools might show low total active time, but because jobs arrive simultaneously requiring the same tool (contention), the low overall utilization masks severe peak-load blocking.
- **ComponentLot flattening**:
  - *Make visible*: The lifecycle of material lots (creation, quarantine, release).
  - *Hide or duplicate*: Hides the direct causal impact on downstream job schedules unless data integration perfectly maps the relations.
  - *Misleading conclusion*: A lot quarantine might look like a standard procedural quality hold, failing to show how its release triggers a cascading delay across multiple jobs (like J5).
- **Shipment flattening**:
  - *Make visible*: The logistics phase from shipment creation to load and dispatch.
  - *Hide or duplicate*: Hides the time a job spends physically packed and waiting before the formal "Create Shipment" event is logged.
  - *Misleading conclusion*: The Shipment DFG shows a median elapsed time of just 1.1 hours, completely missing the 6 hours J1 spent waiting for J4.

**Why low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting:**
Machines are frequently starved of work despite high physical inventory because jobs are trapped in non-machine queues. A machine like CNC-2 sits idle because the jobs assigned to it are blocked by auxiliary resources (FIX-A). OVEN-1 sits idle because jobs are waiting for their batch peers to arrive or for a shared rack to be cleaned. Thus, jobs accrue massive waiting times blocked by tools and synchronization policies, while machines remain unutilized waiting for those constrained jobs to be released.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

1. **The Fixture Contention and Machine Starvation Narrative (Job → Tool → Machine → Job)**
   - *Temporal precedence*: J3 acquires FIX-A at 09:00. J4 arrives at CNC-2 at 09:50. CNC-2 becomes idle at 10:05. J3 releases FIX-A at 11:40. J4 begins processing on CNC-2 at 11:45.
   - *Resource feasibility*: J4 requires both CNC-2 and FIX-A simultaneously. While CNC-2 is physically available, FIX-A is in use.
   - *Policy constraints*: Tools like fixtures cannot be timeshared or partitioned between active jobs.
   - *Causal speculation*: J4's processing is bottlenecked by J3's prolonged use of FIX-A. This contention artificially depresses CNC-2's utilization and inflates J4's cycle time, demonstrating how a cheap auxiliary tool overrides the availability of a primary capital asset.

2. **The Cascading Upstream Quarantine Narrative (ComponentLot → Job → Batch → Tool → Machine)**
   - *Temporal precedence*: Lot L7 undergoes quarantine at 10:20. L7 releases at 13:50. J5 (requiring C5 from L7) reaches readiness for B2 at 14:40. Meanwhile, RACK-7 undergoes cleaning until 14:35. B2 starts on OVEN-1 at 14:50.
   - *Resource feasibility*: B2 requires OVEN-1, RACK-7, J4, and J5. All conditions must be met to proceed.
   - *Policy constraints*: Safety protocols prevent a job from proceeding if its component lot is quarantined. Batches enforce synchronization.
   - *Causal speculation*: The quarantine of L7 paralyzed J5. Because B2 required J5, J4 was forced to wait. Even if L7 had been released earlier, B2 would have been blocked by RACK-7's cleaning. OVEN-1's idleness from 12:55 to 14:50 is thus a compound symptom of a component quality hold and a tool maintenance cycle.

3. **The Consolidation Cascade Narrative (Job → Tool → Job → Shipment)**
   - *Temporal precedence*: J1 is packed at 13:30. J4 is delayed upstream by FIX-A contention and B2 synchronization, finally getting packed at 19:10. S1 dispatches at 19:25.
   - *Resource feasibility*: S1 requires both J1 and J4 to be packed and loaded.
   - *Policy constraints*: Logistics policies dictate that jobs bound for the same destination or client must consolidate into a single shipment to minimize freight costs.
   - *Causal speculation*: The severe delays inflicted on J4 upstream (by FIX-A and L7 quarantine acting on J5 in B2) propagated to the very end of the line, preventing J1 from leaving the facility. J1's cycle time was ruined not by its own processing, but by the cascading delays of its shipment partner, illustrating how downstream consolidation acts as an amplifier for upstream variability.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

1. **Target Mechanism: Batch synchronization (B1, B2)**
   - *Target mechanism*: Batch synchronization delays.
   - *Expected benefit*: Radically reduce waiting time for early-arriving jobs by eliminating the synchronization barrier.
   - *Jobs/Objects helped*: Early-ready jobs like J1, J2, and J4.
   - *Jobs/Objects potentially harmed*: OVEN-1 capacity utilization; later jobs if the oven becomes a true bottleneck due to excessive changeovers.
   - *New cost or risk*: Increased energy consumption from running partial loads in OVEN-1.
   - *Metric that could create a perverse incentive*: Minimizing "Oven energy cost per job" would force operators right back into large, delay-inducing batches.
   - *Object-centric evaluation design*: Monitor the gap between "Job ready for batch" and "Batch start" across all jobs. If this median gap decreases without an unacceptable spike in total batch cycles, the intervention is successful.

2. **Target Mechanism: Shared-tool contention (FIX-A, CAL-3)**
   - *Target mechanism*: Shared-tool contention and maintenance blocking.
   - *Expected benefit*: Eliminate the indirect waiting time where jobs sit idle before empty machines.
   - *Jobs/Objects helped*: J4, J6, CNC-2, TEST-1.
   - *Jobs/Objects potentially harmed*: Capital expenditure budget.
   - *New cost or risk*: Procurement cost for duplicate fixtures (FIX-B) and calibration tools (CAL-4), plus maintenance costs for the expanded tool fleet.
   - *Metric that could create a perverse incentive*: Maximizing "Tool Utilization" would punish the procurement of duplicates, ensuring tools remain a physical bottleneck.
   - *Object-centric evaluation design*: Track occurrences where `Job reaches Machine` but `Machine Process` is delayed despite the machine being idle. The frequency of this anti-pattern should drop to zero.

3. **Target Mechanism: Shipment consolidation**
   - *Target mechanism*: Shipment consolidation delays.
   - *Expected benefit*: Decrease end-to-end cycle time for jobs that finish early.
   - *Jobs/Objects helped*: Fast-tracked jobs like J1.
   - *Jobs/Objects potentially harmed*: Freight budget; carrier logistics logic.
   - *New cost or risk*: Higher shipping costs due to dispatching partially full trucks or running multiple deliveries.
   - *Metric that could create a perverse incentive*: Minimizing "Shipping cost per unit" strongly incentivizes waiting days to consolidate shipments.
   - *Object-centric evaluation design*: Measure the median duration from a Job's "Pack" event to its "Dispatch" event. Compare the inventory holding cost savings against increased freight spend.

4. **Target Mechanism: Data-integration artifacts**
   - *Target mechanism*: Logging opacity causing data-integration artifacts.
   - *Expected benefit*: Provide management with a veridical map of actual bottlenecks, enabling targeted process engineering.
   - *Jobs/Objects helped*: Process analysts, engineering management.
   - *Jobs/Objects potentially harmed*: Operators tasked with additional manual scanning or data entry.
   - *New cost or risk*: Software development costs to implement explicit `Wait for Tool` events and dynamically link `ComponentLot` releases to all downstream Jobs.
   - *Metric that could create a perverse incentive*: Punishing operators for high "Wait for Tool" times might lead them to game the system by not logging wait states.
   - *Object-centric evaluation design*: Calculate the percentage of "Machine Idle Time" that is deterministically explained by a concurrent `Wait` event on a required auxiliary object.

5. **Target Mechanism: Tool maintenance scheduling (RACK-7)**
   - *Target mechanism*: Tool maintenance interrupting production flows.
   - *Expected benefit*: Prevent tool cleaning or calibration from blocking active production batches.
   - *Jobs/Objects helped*: Batches like B2, and jobs like J5 and J6.
   - *Jobs/Objects potentially harmed*: Maintenance staff who must adjust schedules.
   - *New cost or risk*: Overtime pay or shift differential costs to perform cleaning and calibration during off-hours or overlapping with machine downtime.
   - *Metric that could create a perverse incentive*: "Maintenance department labor efficiency" might drop if technicians are forced to work piecemeal around production schedules.
   - *Object-centric evaluation design*: Track the temporal overlap of "Tool Maintenance" events with "Machine Idle and Jobs Ready" states. A successful intervention drives this overlap to zero.

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Six observations that would weaken the top-ranked explanations (batch sync and tool contention):
1. **Hidden Pre-processing**: If J1 and J2 required undocumented manual preparation steps between 08:10 and 12:00, their "readiness" at 08:10 is an artifact, meaning the batch synchronization delay is illusory.
2. **Scheduled Pickups**: If shipments strictly depart on a fixed daily carrier schedule (e.g., a truck arrives at 19:30 regardless of readiness), the 6-hour delay for J1 in S1 is caused by external logistics, not internal consolidation.
3. **Flexible Maintenance**: If RACK-7 "cleaning" is an opportunistic background activity that could have been instantly interrupted for B2, its overlap with B2's delay is merely a correlation, not a causal bottleneck.
4. **Material Shortages**: If the 1h40m gap at CNC-2 was actually due to a missing physical delivery of Component C4 rather than FIX-A contention, the tool hypothesis is completely falsified.
5. **Operator Constraints**: If CNC-2, OVEN-1, and TEST-1 share a single human operator, the observed delays and idle times are artifacts of operator context-switching rather than tool or batch constraints.
6. **Thermodynamic Batch Requirements**: If OVEN-1 thermodynamically requires a minimum physical mass (e.g., all three jobs) to cure properly, then batch synchronization is a hard physical law of the process, not an alterable policy.

Six additional fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses:
1. **Explicit "Wait for Resource" Events**: Distinct events logged whenever a job reaches a machine but cannot proceed, explicitly identifying the missing tool, material, or operator.
2. **Operator ID Tracking**: Adding an `Operator` object type to track human resource contention across overlapping activities.
3. **Carrier Arrival/Departure Events**: Logging external logistics schedules to distinguish internal packing delays from external transport limits.
4. **Component Delivery Events**: Precise timestamps for when raw components physically arrive at the staging area, verifying true job readiness.
5. **Tool RFID Location Tracking**: Physical telemetry of tools to confirm they are actually in use versus lying idle but unreleased in the system.
6. **Machine Fault Codes**: Telemetry data directly from the machines capturing distinct reasons for idleness (e.g., "Awaiting Parts", "Safety Interlock Open").
