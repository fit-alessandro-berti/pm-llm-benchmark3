# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin:** the timestamp of `Submit`.

**SLA horizon:** \( \tau=72 \) elapsed hours after `Submit`.

**Observed follow-up time:** `duration_or_age_h`.

- For `resolved`, `withdrawn`, `rejected`, and `transferred`, this is time to the recorded terminal/internal disposition.
- For `open`, this is age at the frozen extract cutoff and is therefore a right-censoring time for outcomes not yet observed.

The phrase “probability of breaching SLA” should not be represented by one number without specifying the outcome definition.

## Estimand A: probability of not being resolved by 72 hours

\[
P(T_{\text{Resolve}}>72)
\]

where \(T_{\text{Resolve}}\) is time from `Submit` to `Resolve`. This includes cases resolved late, cases still open at 72, and—depending on the operational definition—cases that have withdrawn, been rejected, or transferred before 72.

A useful operational version is:

> “At 72 hours, what fraction of submitted cases has not received an internal resolution?”

Under that definition, any non-resolution outcome before 72 remains “not resolved by 72,” although it need not be operationally undesirable. For example, a timely `Reject` may be an appropriate disposition even though it is not `Resolve`.

**Best use:** customer/service promises specifically requiring a resolution, backlog exposure, or monitoring of unresolved demand.

**Examples:**

- **S03, resolved at 76 hours:** contributes as not resolved by 72; it is an observed late resolution.
- **S09, open at 92 hours:** known not to have resolved by 72; contributes as not resolved by 72.
- **S06, withdrawn at 27 hours:** not resolved by 72. Whether this is called a “breach” depends on policy; it should normally be reported separately rather than silently treated as failure.
- **S08, transferred at 68 hours:** not internally resolved by 72. But its external resolution status is unknown; whether this is an SLA breach depends on whether the SLA transfers with the case.

A Kaplan–Meier estimator for time to `Resolve` can estimate this only after choosing how non-resolution terminal outcomes are handled. Treating withdrawal, rejection, and transfer as ordinary independent censoring estimates a hypothetical “net” resolution process, not the observed operational probability of internal resolution.

## Estimand B: cumulative incidence of late internal resolution

\[
P(72 < T_{\text{Resolve}} \le t,\ \text{Resolve occurs before another terminal outcome})
\]

for a defined reporting horizon \(t\), such as the observation cutoff or 120 hours if adequate follow-up exists.

This is a competing-risks estimand. `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and possibly `Transfer` prevent a subsequent **internally observed** `Resolve`; they are competing outcomes rather than standard right censoring.

At 72 hours, the cumulative incidence of late resolution is zero by definition if one evaluates exactly at 72; therefore this should be reported over a later horizon, for example:

\[
F_{\text{late Resolve}}(120)
=
P(72<T_{\text{Resolve}}\le120,\ \text{internal Resolve})
\]

or as the observed cumulative incidence curve after hour 72.

**Best use:** capacity planning for work that actually resolves late; identifying process paths that generate late completions; separating “late resolution” from cases appropriately rejected or withdrawn.

**Examples:**

- **S03, resolved at 76 hours:** contributes to late-resolution cumulative incidence at 76 hours.
- **S09, open at 92 hours:** is right-censored at 92 for later resolution incidence; it does not contribute an observed late resolution.
- **S06, withdrawn at 27 hours:** is a competing event at 27; it cannot later contribute an internal resolution in the observed process.
- **S08, transferred at 68 hours:** usually a competing event for internal resolution; alternatively, it may be treated as censoring under a hypothetical external-follow-up assumption.

## Estimand C: probability of no terminal internal disposition by 72 hours

\[
P(T_{\text{terminal}}>72)
\]

where terminal means one of `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or, under an internal-system definition, `Transfer`.

This captures cases still operationally active in the internal workflow at 72 hours.

**Best use:** internal backlog, staffing, queue management, escalation governance, and alerts for cases still needing internal handling.

**Examples:**

- **S03, resolved at 76 hours:** has no terminal disposition at 72, so contributes positively.
- **S09, open at 92 hours:** known to have no terminal disposition at 72, so contributes positively.
- **S06, withdrawn at 27 hours:** does not contribute; it had a terminal disposition before 72.
- **S08, transferred at 68 hours:** does not contribute if `Transfer` is an internal terminal disposition. It would contribute under a business definition in which external transfer does not end organizational responsibility.

## Estimand D: cause-specific cumulative incidence by 72 hours

For each outcome \(k\in\{\text{Resolve},\text{Withdraw},\text{Reject},\text{Transfer}\}\),

\[
F_k(72) = P(T\le72,\ J=k).
\]

This partitions the observed terminal outcomes under a chosen transfer definition. The complement is the probability of remaining without an observed terminal disposition by 72.

**Best use:** SLA governance where “timely disposition” is the relevant promise, and management needs to distinguish timely resolution from timely rejection, withdrawal, or external handoff.

The primary recommendation is to report **Estimand C plus the cause-specific cumulative-incidence decomposition in Estimand D** for internal operations, and report **Estimand B** as the explicit late-resolution measure. Estimand A is valuable for customer-facing “resolved” promises but should not be interpreted as equivalent to internal backlog or process failure.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

The following table distinguishes observed terminal outcomes, right censoring, and alternative transfer assumptions. The term “event” is estimand-specific.

| Observed status | Recorded follow-up time | Internal terminal-process code | For cumulative incidence of internal outcomes | For time to `Resolve` KM / cause-specific model | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| `resolved` | Time to Resolve | 1 = Resolve | Event: Resolve | Event \(=1\) | Not censored | If resolution is at/after 72, classify as observed late resolution for late-resolution analyses |
| `withdrawn` | Time to Withdraw | 2 = Withdraw | Competing event | Competing event in cause-specific/Aalen–Johansen analysis; censoring only for a hypothetical net-resolution KM analysis | Not ordinary non-informative censoring for observed-process estimands | Treat as acceptable timely disposition; treat as adverse non-resolution; or model separately as a competing cause |
| `rejected` | Time to Reject | 3 = Reject | Competing event | Competing event in cause-specific/Aalen–Johansen analysis | Not ordinary non-informative censoring for observed-process estimands | Often appropriate closure rather than SLA failure; report separately from unresolved cases |
| `transferred` | Time to Transfer | 4 = Transfer under internal-system definition | Competing event if transfer ends internal observation/process | Competing event for internal Resolve | Not ordinary censoring unless a defensible independent-external-follow-up assumption is made | Scenario 1: competing event; Scenario 2: right-censor at transfer; Scenario 3: worst-case unresolved/non-resolved at 72; optional Scenario 4: external outcome linkage if obtained |
| `open` | Age at cutoff | 0 = no observed terminal outcome | Right-censored at cutoff age | Right-censored at cutoff age | Administrative right censoring only if cutoff is common and future outcome risk is conditionally independent given modeled history | Use inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting or bounds if long inactivity or data-extract mechanisms make censoring informative |

## Transfer scenarios

At least three transfer treatments should be presented rather than selecting one silently.

1. **Internal-terminal competing event (primary for internal workflow):**  
   `Transfer` ends the internally observed process. It is a competing event in Aalen–Johansen estimation. This estimates the real-world probability of internal resolution before transfer, withdrawal, or rejection.

2. **Right-censor at transfer (hypothetical external-follow-up / net-resolution analysis):**  
   `Transfer` is censored at its transfer time. This is defensible only under a strong assumption: conditional on measured covariates and history, transferred cases would have the same future internal-resolution behavior as non-transferred cases had they remained observable. That assumption is doubtful because transfers often signal complexity or routing needs.

3. **Conservative unresolved-at-SLA treatment:**  
   If the organization remains accountable after handoff and cannot verify external outcome, classify transferred cases before 72 as not confirmed resolved by 72. For the binary “confirmed resolved by 72” metric, S08 at 68 and S21 at 26 would count as not confirmed resolved. This is operationally conservative but is not an estimate of actual external resolution.

4. **External linkage scenario, if later data are available:**  
   Link to external organization outcomes and define transfer either as a state transition, not a terminal event, or as a competing event with an externally observed endpoint. This is the preferred business-wide SLA approach but cannot be implemented from this extract.

`open` rows are not “failures” merely because they are open. S10 at age 47 and S15 at age 35 have not yet reached the SLA horizon at the cutoff. In contrast, S09 at age 92, S11 at 81, S25 at 79, and S26 at 104 are directly known to be active beyond 72 and therefore contribute evidence of 72-hour non-disposition.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive analysis of only `resolved` cases would use the 14 observed resolutions and ignore 18 non-resolved observations. It would incorrectly answer a different question: “Among cases that eventually resolved internally before cutoff, how long did resolution take?” It does not estimate the risk for submitted cases.

## Survivor/selection bias

Among observed resolved cases, late examples include:

- **S03:** Resolve at 76 hours.
- **S13:** Resolve at 110 hours.
- **S28:** Resolve at 88 hours.

But restricting to resolved cases removes:

- **S09:** open at 92 hours;
- **S11:** open at 81 hours;
- **S25:** open at 79 hours;
- **S26:** open at 104 hours;
- **S32:** transferred at 96 hours.

Those cases may represent some of the most delayed or complex work. Excluding them can make the observed resolved duration distribution appear artificially favorable or, depending on which cases resolve late before cutoff, distort it in either direction. More importantly, it changes the target population from all submissions to selected eventual resolvers.

## Right censoring is not the same as an observed failure

Open cases are administratively censored at the extract cutoff. However, their contribution depends on age:

- **Below 72 hours:** S10 (47 hours), S15 (35), S24 (67), S29 (51), and S31 (23) are still at risk before the SLA horizon. They should not be called observed breaches.
- **Above 72 hours:** S09 (92), S11 (81), S25 (79), and S26 (104) are known to have remained open beyond the SLA. They provide direct evidence for no-terminal-disposition or no-resolution-at-72 estimands.

Dropping all open cases loses both types of information.

## Competing risks are not benign censoring

Withdrawal and rejection alter what can happen next in the internal process:

- **S06** withdrew at 27 hours.
- **S12** withdrew at 15 hours.
- **S05** was rejected at 9 hours.
- **S14** was rejected at 63 hours.

Treating these as independent right-censoring in a Kaplan–Meier estimate of internal resolution assumes that, if they had not withdrawn or been rejected, their eventual internal-resolution distribution would match the still-observed risk set. That is a hypothetical net-risk quantity, not the observed probability of resolution. For the actual process, they should generally be competing events.

## Transfers may be informative

Transfers are not random loss to follow-up. Several transferred cases have escalation in their prefix:

- **S08:** `...Review>Escalate>Transfer`, transferred at 68 hours.
- **S21:** `...Screen>Escalate>Transfer`, transferred at 26 hours.
- **S32:** `...Screen>Escalate>Transfer`, transferred at 96 hours.

The common escalation/transfer pattern suggests that transfer may be associated with route complexity, workload, or inability to complete internally. Censoring them as if they were ordinary independent losses can bias a resolution survival curve. The direction depends on the unobserved external trajectory: transferred cases might resolve quickly externally, or they might be hard cases transferred late.

## Long event gaps raise informative-observation concerns

For open cases, `last_event_gap_h` identifies how long since the last recorded activity:

- S11: age 81, gap 55;
- S26: age 104, gap 72;
- S25: age 79, gap 48;
- S09: age 92, gap 31.

A long gap can indicate inactivity, queue delay, missing events, external work not logged internally, or an impending unrecorded terminal event. Thus even administrative cutoff censoring may be conditionally informative unless the model accounts for current stage, escalation, workload, and activity recency.

## Cohort imbalance and non-random rollout

Cohort B began after an automated-screening and escalation-policy change, but assignment was not randomized and compliance was incomplete.

Cohort A has 15 cases and cohort B has 17. Their observed process patterns differ: B commonly begins with `Submit>AutoCheck...`, while A commonly begins `Submit>Screen...`; nevertheless B still has non-AutoCheck paths such as S20, S23, S26, and S30. Therefore a simple A-versus-B difference mixes calendar time, eligibility, staffing, demand mix, policy compliance, routing, and priority composition. It cannot be read as the causal effect of automated screening or escalation policy.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

The sample is very small (32 cases), has multiple terminal outcomes, open cases, and uncertain transfer semantics. Therefore the primary analysis should be descriptive, nonparametric, and transparent about denominators and assumptions; regression should be exploratory.

## Recommended primary analysis: Aalen–Johansen competing-risks curves

Use time from `Submit` to first observed terminal disposition, with:

- Resolve,
- Withdraw,
- Reject,
- Transfer,

as distinct causes under the primary internal-system scenario, and `open` as right-censored at cutoff age.

Estimate cumulative-incidence functions \(F_k(t)\) for each cause and derive:

\[
P(\text{no internal terminal disposition by }72)
=
1-\sum_k F_k(72).
\]

Also report:

- timely Resolve cumulative incidence \(F_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\);
- timely non-resolution dispositions \(F_{\text{Withdraw}}(72)\), \(F_{\text{Reject}}(72)\), and \(F_{\text{Transfer}}(72)\);
- late Resolve cumulative incidence after 72;
- counts at risk near 72 hours.

**Why primary:** it respects competing terminal outcomes and retains open cases. It answers operational questions about what actually happens in the observed process, rather than creating a hypothetical world in which withdrawal, rejection, and transfer never occur.

For this dataset, uncertainty intervals will be wide. Report point estimates with confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals, but emphasize event counts and avoid overinterpreting cohort-specific curves.

## Secondary analysis: Kaplan–Meier under clearly labeled hypothetical assumptions

Fit Kaplan–Meier curves for time to `Resolve` with non-Resolve terminal outcomes censored. This estimates a **net/hypothetical resolution survival** only if censoring is conditionally independent.

Use it as a sensitivity analysis, not as the primary estimate. It can be useful for a planning question such as:

> “If cases remained eligible for internal resolution rather than withdrawing, being rejected, or transferring, what would the conditional internal-resolution timing look like?”

That question is often not the same as observed SLA performance.

A second Kaplan–Meier analysis can model time to **any internal terminal disposition**, treating Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer as events and open cases as censored. Its survival at 72 estimates no recorded terminal disposition by 72, subject to right-censoring assumptions.

## Cause-specific hazard models

Cause-specific Cox models can model, for example, the instantaneous hazard of Resolve among cases still under internal observation and not yet having any terminal event:

\[
\lambda_{\text{Resolve}}(t\mid X(t)).
\]

Separate models can be fit for Resolve, Transfer, Withdraw, and Reject. Covariates may include priority, cohort, current state, rework, and time-varying escalation.

**Use:** exploratory process diagnosis, especially whether escalation or repeated review is associated with later transfer or reduced/greater resolution hazard.

**Caution:** with 32 cases and few events by cause, multivariable Cox estimates will be unstable. Prefer parsimonious models, penalization, descriptive stratification, or pooled/regularized approaches only if more data become available.

## Subdistribution-hazard models

Fine–Gray models directly target covariate associations with cumulative incidence of a cause, such as internal Resolve or Transfer.

**Use:** if the decision concerns population-level incidence, for example “which baseline groups have higher cumulative incidence of transfer by 72 hours?”

**Caution:** coefficients are not cause-specific process effects and are difficult to interpret with time-varying covariates. With this sample, use only as a secondary exploratory model, if at all. Do not make causal claims from cohort coefficients.

## Restricted mean time

For an all-terminal-disposition endpoint, estimate restricted mean time without terminal disposition through 72 hours:

\[
\text{RMST}_{\text{active}}(72)
=
\int_0^{72} P(T_{\text{terminal}}>t)\,dt.
\]

This translates directly into expected active case-hours before SLA and can be more stable and operationally meaningful than a hazard ratio.

For Resolve specifically, a restricted mean time to resolution must define how competing events are handled. A multistate expected time in “unresolved internal states” is generally more interpretable than treating competing outcomes as conventional censoring.

## Landmarking

Use landmarks before 72 hours, for example 24, 48, and 60 hours. At each landmark, consider only cases still active and use information observed by that time:

- current stage;
- whether escalation has occurred by the landmark;
- number of Reviews/Reworks completed by the landmark;
- time since last event;
- priority;
- cohort;
- possibly age since Submit.

Estimate the conditional risk of no terminal disposition at 72 or resolution after the landmark.

**Why useful:** this avoids immortal-time bias. For example, “ever escalated” cannot be a baseline predictor, but “escalated by hour 48” can be used in a 48-hour landmark model.

## Multi-state model

A multi-state model is the most process-faithful longer-term approach. States could include Submitted, Screen/AutoCheck, Review, Rework, Escalated, and terminal outcomes Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer.

This supports:

- state occupancy at 72 hours;
- transition hazards such as Review \(\rightarrow\) Escalate;
- expected time spent in Review or Escalated states;
- separation of process delay from terminal disposition mix.

It requires exact event timestamps to estimate transition timing properly. With only ordered prefixes and terminal/cutoff time, this dataset can motivate the state model but cannot support a defensible fully timed transition analysis.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The prefixes encode control flow but not event timestamps. Therefore features derived from the full prefix must be classified carefully.

| Feature | Example | Classification | Safe use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Priority | P1/P2/P3 | Baseline | Baseline stratification/model covariate, subject to sufficient data |
| Cohort | A/B | Baseline but non-random policy/calendar proxy | Descriptive adjustment; not causal treatment effect |
| Initial routing pattern | `Submit>AutoCheck...` vs `Submit>Screen...` | Baseline only if determined at/near Submit; otherwise early process feature | Use as a baseline routing variable only if timestamp/order confirms it occurs before prediction time |
| Ever AutoCheck | S01, S16, etc. | Post-start; may be early but not necessarily baseline | Landmark/time-varying only |
| Current last observed stage | S10 currently Escalate; S15 currently Review | Time-varying | Suitable at a landmark using only events observed before the landmark |
| Number of Review occurrences | S03 has two Reviews; S13 has two Reviews | Time-varying cumulative count | Count only up to prediction time |
| Rework count | S07, S13, S27 | Time-varying / post-start | Use as time-varying or landmark feature; not a baseline covariate |
| Escalation occurred | S03, S04, S08, etc. | Time-varying; potential mediator | Do not use “ever escalated” at Submit; model as state transition or landmark exposure |
| Escalation timing | Time from Submit to Escalate | Time-varying | Requires actual timestamps; unavailable here |
| Path pattern | `Screen>Review>Escalate`; `AutoCheck>Screen>Review`; rework loop | Control-flow history, post-start | Use for descriptive variant analysis or landmark state/history features |
| Last-event gap | S26 has 72-hour gap | Time-varying and cutoff-dependent | Potential staleness/inactivity marker at a landmark; not available as a baseline feature |
| Terminal action in prefix | `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, etc. | Outcome leakage | Never use to predict that terminal outcome |

## Immortal-time and leakage risks

An “ever escalated” baseline model would be biased because a case must survive without terminal disposition long enough to reach `Escalate`. For example, S04 escalated and resolved at 29 hours, while S05 was rejected at 9 hours and could never later become “escalated.” Assigning S04 to an escalated group from time zero credits the group with survival time before escalation.

Similarly, using final `rework_count`, a terminal `Transfer`, or the entire completed prefix as a feature at Submit leaks future information. A process-mining classifier trained on full traces may predict late resolution accurately for the wrong reason: it has seen activities that occurred only after the outcome became likely or after the SLA horizon.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

The following analyses should be reported together. Their disagreement is information about uncertainty, not a nuisance to hide.

| Analysis | Transfer treatment | Withdrawal/rejection treatment | Open cases | Main target | Conclusions potentially affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Internal observed-process analysis | Competing event | Competing events | Right-censored at cutoff | Aalen–Johansen incidence of Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer; no-terminal-disposition at 72 | Internal capacity, timely disposition mix, transfer burden |
| B. Net internal-resolution sensitivity | Right-censored at transfer | Right-censored for time-to-Resolve KM | Right-censored at cutoff | Hypothetical time to internal Resolve if competing outcomes did not remove cases | Estimated probability of delayed resolution can rise relative to competing-risk incidence |
| C. Conservative customer-accountability analysis | Not confirmed resolved unless external proof exists | Report separately; may count as timely disposition if policy allows | Cases open beyond 72 count as known unresolved; younger open remain uncertain | Probability not confirmed resolved by 72 | Customer-facing breach rate may be higher, especially due to transfers S08 and S21 |
| D. Transfer worst/best-case bounds | Worst case: all transfers unresolved at 72; best case: all pre-72 transfers externally resolved by 72 | Same as chosen business rule | Administrative censoring/bounds for open cases | Bounds on organization-wide confirmed resolution by 72 | Transfer interpretation can materially reverse apparent SLA performance |
| E. Informative-censoring stress test | As A or C | As A or business rule | Weight or stratify by last-event gap/current stage; compare complete-case assumptions | Robustness to open-case observation mechanism | Long-gap open cases may make simple independent-censoring estimates optimistic |
| F. Cohort-policy descriptive analysis | As A | As A | Retained | Stratified A/B and priority-specific curves, plus AutoCheck-path descriptives | Apparent cohort advantage/disadvantage can reverse after priority/routing mix changes |

## Informative-censoring sensitivity

Because open cases with large gaps may differ systematically from recently active cases, compare:

1. standard administrative right-censoring;
2. stratified descriptive curves by `last_event_gap_h` categories, for example 0–24, 25–48, and over 48 hours, recognizing this is post-start information;
3. an inverse-probability-of-censoring approach only if larger data and additional predictors of observation/censoring become available;
4. practical bounds at 72: cases known open beyond 72 are definitely active at 72; cases censored before 72 cannot be assumed either resolved or breached without further information.

In this extract, all `open` cases have an age recorded at cutoff; none is censored before its listed age. Therefore cases such as S09 and S26 give firm evidence of persistence beyond 72, whereas S31 at age 23 cannot establish status at 72.

## Cohort and policy sensitivity

Compare:

- unadjusted cohort A versus B;
- priority-stratified cohort comparisons;
- comparisons restricted descriptively to common route families where feasible, such as Screen-first versus AutoCheck-first;
- an “intent-to-observe-policy-era” cohort analysis versus an “observed AutoCheck path” analysis.

Neither is causal: cohort is non-random, and observed AutoCheck is a post-assignment/compliance variable potentially affected by case characteristics. A perceived B improvement could reverse after accounting for priority, route, calendar workload, or incomplete compliance. Conversely, a lack of aggregate cohort improvement could mask improvement among cases actually routed through AutoCheck.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Survival outputs should support decisions, not produce unsupported causal claims.

## Alerts

At a 48- or 60-hour landmark, generate an alert based on a calibrated conditional probability of:

- no internal terminal disposition by 72;
- no confirmed resolution by 72;
- transfer before resolution;
- remaining in a high-delay state such as repeated Review or Escalated.

The alert should use only information available at that landmark: priority, cohort, current state, prior Review/Rework counts, whether escalation has occurred by then, and time since last event. It must not use later `Resolve`, `Transfer`, final duration, or the completed prefix.

A practical alert policy can be tiered:

- **High urgency:** high predicted probability of still being active at 72, especially P1 or cases with long inactivity gaps.
- **Process intervention:** repeated Review/Rework or Escalated state near the SLA threshold.
- **Data-quality intervention:** unusually long gap since last event, which could indicate missing logging rather than work delay.
- **Transfer coordination:** cases approaching a transfer pathway should trigger external handoff tracking if the organization remains accountable.

## Workload actions

Operational actions should be tied to process states rather than only a black-box risk score:

- move aging Review cases to a dedicated review queue;
- require escalation triage before a specified landmark;
- create a handoff confirmation workflow for transfers;
- investigate repeated Review/Rework loops;
- monitor queue-level state occupancy and expected active case-hours through 72.

For example, an increase in the probability of occupying `Escalated` or `Review` at 60 hours is more actionable than a generic “late risk” score.

## SLA governance dashboard

A governance dashboard should show, by priority and cohort:

1. cumulative incidence of Resolve by 72;
2. cumulative incidence of Withdraw, Reject, and Transfer by 72;
3. probability of no internal terminal disposition at 72;
4. incidence of late Resolve after 72;
5. number at risk at key landmarks;
6. transfer scenario sensitivity;
7. open-case count and long-gap open-case count.

This prevents a favorable “resolution time among completed cases” statistic from concealing growing unresolved inventory.

## Calibration and fairness checks

Risk models should be evaluated separately by:

- **priority:** P1, P2, P3;
- **cohort:** A, B;
- where data permit, priority-by-cohort cells;
- route/state groups, such as AutoCheck-first versus Screen-first.

Check:

- calibration at a fixed horizon, e.g. predicted versus observed no-terminal-disposition probability at 72;
- calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope;
- discrimination only as secondary evidence, because the sample is too small for stable estimates;
- false-alert and missed-alert rates by priority and cohort;
- whether an intervention reallocates resources away from lower-priority cases in ways that create systematic disadvantage;
- whether differing logging completeness or transfer propensity creates apparent performance differences.

A cohort coefficient or AutoCheck-path association should be described as an association with observed outcomes, not evidence that the policy caused improvement.

"""

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(csv_text))
SLA_H = 72

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Estimand encodings
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------

# Estimand 1: observed internal terminal disposition.
# event_code: 0=open/right-censored, 1=Resolve, 2=Withdraw, 3=Reject, 4=Transfer
status_to_code = {
    "open": 0,
    "resolved": 1,
    "withdrawn": 2,
    "rejected": 3,
    "transferred": 4,
}
df["terminal_code_internal"] = df["observed_status"].map(status_to_code)
df["any_internal_terminal_event"] = (df["terminal_code_internal"] != 0).astype(int)

# Estimand 2: "known no terminal disposition by 72":
# cases open beyond 72 or terminal after 72 were active at 72.
df["known_active_at_72"] = (
    (df["observed_status"] == "open") & (df["duration_or_age_h"] >= SLA_H)
) | (
    (df["observed_status"] != "open") & (df["duration_or_age_h"] > SLA_H)
)

# A conservative "not confirmed internally resolved by 72" indicator is known
# for all rows whose observed path establishes the status by 72. Open cases under
# 72 are unknown at 72 and are left as NA rather than fabricated as failures.
def confirmed_not_resolved_by_72(row):
    if row["observed_status"] == "resolved" and row["duration_or_age_h"] <= SLA_H:
        return 0
    if row["duration_or_age_h"] >= SLA_H:
        return 1
    if row["observed_status"] in {"withdrawn", "rejected", "transferred"}:
        return 1
    return pd.NA

df["not_confirmed_resolved_by_72"] = df.apply(confirmed_not_resolved_by_72, axis=1)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Three transfer scenarios for time-to-Resolve analysis
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1) Competing event: retained as cause 4 in terminal_code_internal.
# 2) Independent-censoring sensitivity: transfer is censored at transfer time.
# 3) Conservative customer-accountability metric: transferred before 72 is
#    not confirmed resolved by 72, already encoded above.

# KM-ready event indicator for a hypothetical net time-to-Resolve analysis:
# Resolve = event; withdrawal/rejection/open/transferred = censoring.
df["resolve_event_net"] = (df["observed_status"] == "resolved").astype(int)
df["resolve_time_net_h"] = df["duration_or_age_h"]

# Alternative where transfers are treated as competing outcome rather than censoring:
df["resolve_competing_code_transfer_competes"] = df["terminal_code_internal"]

# Explicit scenario labels for interpretable reporting.
df["transfer_scenario_1_internal_competing"] = df["terminal_code_internal"]
df["transfer_scenario_2_transfer_censored"] = df["resolve_event_net"]
df["transfer_scenario_3_conservative_not_confirmed_resolved_72"] = (
    df["not_confirmed_resolved_by_72"]
)

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Process-aware features from prefixes, using order only, not timestamps
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
df["activities"] = df["prefix"].str.split(">")
df["initial_after_submit"] = df["activities"].apply(
    lambda x: x[1] if len(x) > 1 else pd.NA
)
df["current_observed_stage"] = df["activities"].apply(
    lambda x: x[-1] if len(x) else pd.NA
)
df["n_review_in_full_prefix"] = df["activities"].apply(lambda x: x.count("Review"))
df["has_autocheck_in_full_prefix"] = df["activities"].apply(
    lambda x: int("AutoCheck" in x)
)
df["has_escalate_in_full_prefix"] = df["activities"].apply(
    lambda x: int("Escalate" in x)
)

# These full-prefix features are descriptive only. For prediction at a landmark,
# recompute them from events observed by that landmark, not from complete prefixes.
print("\n=== Data overview ===")
print(df["observed_status"].value_counts().sort_index())
print("\nKnown active at 72 hours:")
print(df.loc[df["known_active_at_72"], ["case", "observed_status", "duration_or_age_h",
                                        "prefix", "last_event_gap_h"]].to_string(index=False))

print("\n=== Cause counts by cohort and priority (internal competing-risk encoding) ===")
print(pd.crosstab([df["cohort"], df["priority"]], df["observed_status"], margins=True))

print("\n=== Cohort summary at the SLA horizon (descriptive, not causal) ===")
cohort_summary = df.groupby("cohort").agg(
    n=("case", "size"),
    resolved_by_72=("observed_status",
                    lambda s: ((s == "resolved") &
                               (df.loc[s.index, "duration_or_age_h"] <= SLA_H)).sum()),
    known_active_at_72=("known_active_at_72", "sum"),
    transfers_by_72=("observed_status",
                     lambda s: ((s == "transferred") &
                                (df.loc[s.index, "duration_or_age_h"] <= SLA_H)).sum()),
)
print(cohort_summary)

# Empirical observed process counts by 72; not a censoring-adjusted estimator.
print("\n=== Observed disposition status by 72 hours (raw observed counts) ===")
by72 = df.assign(
    observed_by_72=df.apply(
        lambda r: r["observed_status"]
        if r["observed_status"] != "open" and r["duration_or_age_h"] <= SLA_H
        else ("open_or_not_yet_terminal_by_72"
              if r["duration_or_age_h"] >= SLA_H else "unknown_at_72_due_to_cutoff"),
        axis=1
    )
)
print(pd.crosstab(by72["cohort"], by72["observed_by_72"], margins=True))

# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
# Optional Kaplan-Meier demonstration
# ---------------------------------------------------------------------
try:
    from lifelines import KaplanMeierFitter

    print("\n=== Kaplan-Meier sensitivity: hypothetical net time to Resolve ===")
    print("Interpretation: Withdraw/Reject/Transfer are censored; this requires "
          "a strong independent-censoring assumption and is not the primary "

    # Cohort-level sensitivity comparison
    for cohort, g in df.groupby("cohort"):

        print(f"Net-resolution KM, cohort {cohort}: S_resolve(72)="
              f"{float(kmf.predict(SLA_H)):.3f}")

print("\n=== Transfer scenario comparison ===")
transfer_rows = df[df["observed_status"] == "transferred"]

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The most important limitation is that prefixes provide activity order but not activity timestamps. This prevents estimation of actual stage dwell times, escalation timing, time-varying covariate histories, landmark features at precise hours, transition-specific hazards, and valid multi-state transition intensities.

Priority additions are:

1. **Exact timestamps for every event**, including Submit, Screen, AutoCheck, Review, Rework, Escalate, Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer, and any queue-entry/queue-exit events.
2. **External outcomes after Transfer**, including external receipt, external resolution/rejection/withdrawal dates, ownership, and whether the organization remains SLA-accountable.
3. **Actual extract cutoff timestamp and case start dates**, allowing validation that `duration_or_age_h` is consistently measured and that censoring is administrative.
4. **Data-quality and logging information**, including whether external work, manual work, or delayed event posting can explain long event gaps.
5. **Time-varying workload and capacity data**, such as queue sizes, staffing, shift, backlog, assignment delays, system outages, and external partner capacity.
6. **Assignment mechanism for cohort/policy exposure**, including rollout dates, eligibility criteria, geographic/team differences, implementation exceptions, and policy compliance at the case level.
7. **Richer case-mix variables**, such as issue type, complexity, customer segment, required documentation, language, jurisdiction, and prior contacts.
8. **Clear SLA policy definitions**, especially whether Reject, Withdraw, and Transfer constitute timely disposition, whether the SLA pauses, and whether external resolution counts as resolution.
9. **Intervention records**, including when alerts, escalations, manual reviews, or staffing actions occurred; otherwise risk-model evaluation can confound prediction with intervention response.
10. **Larger samples and repeated cohorts**, needed for stable uncertainty estimates, priority/cohort calibration checks, and any credible policy-effect analysis.

With those additions, the analysis could progress from descriptive competing-risk monitoring to timestamped landmark models, multistate occupancy forecasting, and—only with a defensible assignment or quasi-experimental design—causal evaluation of the automated-screening and escalation-policy changes.
