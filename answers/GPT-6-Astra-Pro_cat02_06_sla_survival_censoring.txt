# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**The primary distinction is between failure to achieve resolution and continued internal workload.** A withdrawn case can fail a resolution-based SLA without remaining in the work queue. A transferred case can leave that queue while its end-to-end outcome remains unknown.

All numerical observations and calculations below use the supplied 32 cases.

**Time definitions.** Set elapsed time zero at `Submit`, and use a horizon \(h=72\) hours. Resolution exactly at 72 hours counts as timely. Let:

* \(T_D\) be time to the first **internal disposition**, counting `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer`.
* \(J\) identify that disposition.
* \(C\) be available administrative follow-up to the observation cutoff.
* \(F_j(t)=P(T_D\le t,J=j)\) be the cumulative incidence of disposition \(j\).

For terminal rows, the supplied time is \(T_D\); for open rows, it is \(C\). The potential cutoff age of an already-terminal case need not be known to use its observed event time.

**Scope assumption for the primary analysis:** transfer ends the internal episode. This does not imply that the case can never resolve externally. Recorded open statuses are initially taken as accurate through cutoff; Section 6 examines that assumption.

**Estimand A — no internal resolution by the SLA:**

$$
q_R(72)=1-F_R(72).
$$

This includes cases still active at 72 and cases that have already exited without `Resolve`. It best matches governance of a strict resolution-based SLA across all submitted cases. Counting withdrawal or rejection here is a business-definition choice, not a claim that those decisions were inappropriate.

**Estimand B — still internally active at the SLA:**

$$
q_D(72)=P(T_D>72)=1-\sum_j F_j(72).
$$

This best matches backlog, staffing, ownership, and overdue-work management. Under this definition, transfer removes internal workload.

**Estimand C — resolution in a specified late window:**

$$
q_{\mathrm{late}}(72,\tau)=F_R(\tau)-F_R(72),\qquad \tau>72.
$$

For illustration, use \(\tau=96\) hours, an analytical reporting choice rather than another supplied SLA. This measures late recovery, not all SLA failures. An eventual, unrestricted “probability of resolving after 72” would require follow-up beyond the observed tail.

**Estimand D — no end-to-end resolution by 72:** this includes external resolution after transfer. Unlike the internal estimands, it is not directly identified from these records.

The requested examples contribute as follows. Entries describe known horizon outcomes, not their censoring-adjusted estimator weights.

| Case                       | A: No internal resolution by 72                | B: Internally active at 72                   | C: Internal resolution in \((72,96]\)                                    | D: No end-to-end resolution by 72                             |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **S03: resolved at 76**    | Yes: resolution was late.                      | Yes: no internal disposition occurred by 72. | Yes.                                                                     | Yes, on the supplied record.                                  |
| **S09: open at 92**        | Yes: the deadline failure is already observed. | Yes.                                         | Unknown: resolution could occur between 92 and 96; follow-up ends at 92. | Yes, assuming the open status is accurate.                    |
| **S06: withdrawn at 27**   | Yes: withdrawal is not resolution.             | No: internal work ended at 27.               | No: withdrawal is a competing terminal outcome.                          | Yes, treating withdrawal as terminal for this case.           |
| **S08: transferred at 68** | Yes: no internal resolution occurred.          | No: the internal episode ended at 68.        | No internally.                                                           | Unknown: external resolution between 68 and 72 is unobserved. |

An open case at 92 is therefore **right-censored for its eventual outcome but not uncertain about whether it met the 72-hour deadline**.

There are also assumption-light bounds for the **finite extract**. Nine cases resolved by 72; five open cases have not yet reached 72; the remaining 18 cannot achieve timely internal resolution. Consequently,

$$
q_R^{\mathrm{extract}}(72)\in[18/32,23/32]
=[56.3\%,71.9\%].
$$

Nine cases are known to have remained internally active through 72, giving

$$
q_D^{\mathrm{extract}}(72)\in[9/32,14/32]
=[28.1\%,43.8\%].
$$

These are bounds on the eventual proportions among these 32 cases, **not population confidence intervals**.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Use mutually exclusive cause codes. Let \(d_R\) indicate an observed resolution and \(d_D\) indicate any observed internal disposition.

| Observed status | Primary \(d_R\) | Primary \(d_D\) | Competing-risk code | Primary event/censoring rule                                                                    | Sensitivity alternatives                                                                                                                      |
| --------------- | --------------: | --------------: | ------------------: | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `resolved`      |               1 |               1 |                   1 | Resolution event at the supplied duration, including resolutions after 72.                      | Include in a broader acceptable-disposition endpoint, without changing the observed event.                                                    |
| `withdrawn`     |               0 |               1 |                   2 | Competing terminal event at the supplied duration; not ordinary loss to follow-up.              | Count as acceptable closure for a different business endpoint; or censor only to examine a clearly labelled net/hypothetical curve.           |
| `rejected`      |               0 |               1 |                   3 | Competing terminal event at the supplied duration.                                              | Same alternatives as withdrawal. Excluding rejected cases from the denominator changes the target population.                                 |
| `transferred`   |               0 |               1 |                   4 | Competing exit from the internal episode at transfer time. External resolution remains unknown. | Independent-loss assumption, optimistic external resolution, pessimistic external non-resolution, or an explicitly accepted-handoff endpoint. |
| `open`          |               0 |               0 |                   0 | Administrative right censoring at supplied age, conditional on accurate status.                 | Informative-censoring analysis; observation-quality audit; censor at last **verified** active time only if later status is genuinely unknown. |

Three principal transfer scenarios are required:

**Internal competing exit.** Transfer permanently prevents a subsequently observed internal `Resolve` within this episode. This is the primary scope definition, not an assumption about external success.

**Censor at transfer.** For an end-to-end target, transfer can be treated as loss of outcome observation. Estimation then needs the strong assumption that transferred cases’ future outcome process is comparable to that of cases still observed, conditional on sufficient history. The data cannot verify this. For a hypothetical “no-transfer” internal process, still stronger causal assumptions would be necessary.

**Optimistic external completion at transfer.** Recode transfer as resolution at its transfer time as an extreme sensitivity scenario. S08 and S21 then resolve by 72; S32, transferred at 96, still misses the deadline. This is **hypothetical outcome encoding**, not an observed resolution or a new event inserted into the process log.

The opposite external extreme assumes transferred cases do not resolve by 72. For this horizon it agrees with the primary internal non-resolution encoding.

A common cutoff does not automatically guarantee independent censoring. Calendar time, rollout, workload, and case assignment may jointly affect follow-up and outcomes. Aalen–Johansen estimation requires suitable independence of loss to observation, **not independence between competing causes**.

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

**Completion/survivor selection.** Only 13 cases have an observed `Resolve`. Four are late: S03, S13, S20, and S28. Thus \(4/13=30.8\%\) is a description of the observed resolved subset, not either primary SLA probability.

Resolved-only mean durations are 54.7 hours in A and 45.6 in B. These omit unresolved tails such as S09 at 92 hours and S26 at 104 hours, as well as unsuccessful terminal outcomes. Conditioning on resolution observed before cutoff preferentially retains cases that reached that particular outcome soon enough to be seen.

**Competing outcomes change the meaning of success.** S05’s rejection at 9 hours and S06’s withdrawal at 27 hours are fast dispositions, but neither is operational resolution. Counting every terminal row as SLA success measures closure speed instead. Treating these outcomes as ordinary independent censoring does not recover the actual-world probability of resolution.

**Transfers are selected losses, not obviously random losses.** All three transfers—S08, S21, and S32—are P1 and escalated. They are not a visibly representative sample of the remaining cases. External resolution might be faster or slower; neither direction is established.

**Open cases below and above 72 carry different information.** S09, S11, S25, and S26 are already overdue. Their deadline outcome is known even though their eventual disposition is censored. S10, S15, S24, S29, and S31 are still below 72 and could either meet or miss the deadline. Dropping either group wastes information; labelling the younger group as failures invents outcomes.

**Cohort composition and observation differ.** A contains 15 cases, including 3 P1 cases; B contains 17, including 5 P1 cases. `AutoCheck` appears in 2 A prefixes and 13 B prefixes, but observed activity occurrence is not equivalent to randomized policy assignment or verified compliance.

Nor can B simply be assumed to have shorter follow-up. At the common cutoff, B’s S26 is 104 hours old while A’s S15 is 35 hours old. A strictly non-overlapping “all A starts before all B starts” interpretation would conflict with those ages. Overlapping rollout or another cohort definition could explain them; start dates and assignment rules are needed.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

**Primary recommendation:** estimate competing-risk cumulative incidences for resolution, withdrawal, rejection, and internal transfer using Aalen–Johansen, reporting \(1-F_R(72)\) alongside finite-extract bounds.

**Secondary recommendation:** report internal-disposition survival and restricted mean active time for workload planning. Use cohort and recorded-priority strata descriptively; do not fit a large process-feature regression to 32 cases.

For every estimated curve, independent censoring within its analysis group is an assumption. A pooled curve requires an appropriate marginal assumption; standardization of subgroup curves is preferable when censoring differs across groups.

With the primary encoding, the calculations give:

| Group | Cases | Observed risk set at 72 | Resolution CIF by 72 | No internal resolution by 72 | Internally active at 72 |
| ----- | ----: | ----------------------: | -------------------: | ---------------------------: | ----------------------: |
| All   |    32 |                       9 |                30.4% |                    **69.6%** |               **38.0%** |
| A     |    15 |                       4 |                29.2% |                        70.8% |                   33.9% |
| B     |    17 |                       5 |                31.6% |                        68.4% |                   41.6% |

These are assumption-dependent estimates, not directly observed percentages. In particular,

$$
69.6\%\approx
38.0\%\ \text{active}
+14.0\%\ \text{withdrawn}
+10.1\%\ \text{rejected}
+7.5\%\ \text{transferred by 72}.
$$

**A 69.6% non-resolution estimate does not mean 69.6% of cases remain in the internal queue.**

The methods serve different purposes:

| Method                                    | Role and suitability here                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Kaplan–Meier**                          | Appropriate for time to any internal disposition, censoring only open cases. Its survival curve estimates internal active probability. Censoring all non-resolution outcomes instead produces a net resolution-survival curve, not the observed-world complement of resolution incidence.                                           |
| **Aalen–Johansen / cumulative incidence** | Best primary choice because it separates actual outcome types and preserves their competition. It directly provides resolution probability and the decomposition above. Sparse subgroup risk sets remain a major uncertainty.                                                                                                       |
| **Cause-specific hazards**                | Useful for the instantaneous resolution or transfer rate among cases still internally active. Removing competing events from the relevant risk set is correct hazard bookkeeping; interpreting a resulting KM curve as actual-world resolution probability is not. With only 13 resolutions, rich adjusted models would be fragile. |
| **Subdistribution hazards**               | Can model associations with a particular CIF. Its modified risk set retains competing-event subjects mathematically, so it is not a live-workload model. A subdistribution hazard ratio is neither a probability ratio nor a causal policy effect. Internal time-varying covariates also become problematic after competing exits.  |
| **Restricted mean time**                  | Through 72, \(\int_0^{72}P(T_D>t)\,dt\) estimates **50.4 hours of internal episode occupancy per case**, including waiting. In contrast, \(\int_0^{72}[1-F_R(t)]\,dt\approx60.6\) hours includes time after unsuccessful exit and must not be called internal workload.                                                             |
| **Landmarking**                           | Preferred next step for actionable prediction: at, for example, 24, 48, or 60 hours, predict deadline outcomes using only history available then among still-active cases. The current snapshots cannot reconstruct all those histories.                                                                                            |
| **Multi-state model**                     | Appropriate once transition timestamps are available. It could distinguish waiting after review, escalation, and rework, using case age and time since state entry. Prefix order plus total duration is insufficient to estimate stage-specific exposure times.                                                                     |

For the illustrative late window, the primary estimate is

$$
F_R(96)-F_R(72)=14.3\%.
$$

This is a subset of cases active at 72, not an additional probability to add to SLA failure. The tail is poorly supported: only three cases remain under observation immediately before 96 hours, and only S13 remains at risk at 110. An unrestricted eventual-resolution probability should not be inferred from the curve’s endpoint.

Uncertainty reporting should include risk-set sizes, confidence intervals or bands, and the deterministic bounds. A case-level bootstrap preserving cohort structure is possible, but sparse strata can produce unstable or unsupported replicates. No numerical confidence intervals are asserted here.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

The prefixes support control-flow descriptions, but a feature’s **availability time** determines whether it is valid for prediction.

| Feature                  | Evidence or proposed derivation                                                      | Timing and leakage treatment                                                                                                                                                              |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cohort and priority      | Supplied columns.                                                                    | Cohort is an era/assignment descriptor. Priority is a baseline covariate **only under the additional assumption that it was recorded at Submit**; otherwise priority history is required. |
| Last observed milestone  | Last activity in an active prefix: `Screen`, `Review`, or `Escalate`.                | Valid at that observation time, not automatically at an earlier landmark. A milestone does not prove that a task has been continuously executing.                                         |
| Repeated review          | S03, S07, and S13 each contain two `Review` events.                                  | Accumulated time-varying history. Repeated review is not identical to rework: S03 has `rework_count=0`.                                                                                   |
| Rework history           | S07 and S13 return to `Review`; S27 goes from `Rework` to `Resolve`.                 | Use the count observed so far. The final count leaks future history into earlier predictions.                                                                                             |
| Escalation               | Occurrence, time since first escalation, number of escalations, return to review.    | Post-start and potentially post-treatment. Escalation timing is unavailable for most traces.                                                                                              |
| Automated-check path     | S16 has `AutoCheck` before `Screen`; S17 has it after; S18 has no observed `Screen`. | Useful routing descriptors, but not established compliance indicators. No authoritative expected workflow is supplied.                                                                    |
| Inactivity               | Current `last_event_gap_h`, interpreted together with case age and last milestone.   | Potential time-varying predictor for active cases; not a safe baseline feature.                                                                                                           |
| Variant or prefix family | Ordered activity sequence; 27 distinct observed-prefix variants occur.               | Useful descriptively. Full sequences containing outcomes are direct leakage if used for earlier prediction.                                                                               |

**Immortal-time risk.** Labelling S03 “escalated” from Submit attributes its pre-escalation period to the escalated group, although the case had to remain unresolved long enough to receive escalation. Comparison with “never escalated” cases mixes treatment timing with outcome timing. All eight P1 cases are escalated, further entangling escalation with priority.

A time-aware design would switch escalation exposure from zero to one at its actual event time, or use only escalation already observed at a fixed landmark. Even then, causal interpretation requires handling why escalation was chosen, including delay or severity that may also predict resolution.

**The gap field has an especially strong leakage trap:** every terminal row has gap zero, while every open row has a positive gap. Training on these final snapshots could primarily learn extraction status.

For open cases, the final recorded event’s relative age is derivable:

$$
\text{last-event age}=\text{case age}-\text{last-event gap}.
$$

For S11 it is \(81-55=26\) hours; for S26 it is \(104-72=32\) hours. These are valid derived ages, but they do not establish that a person was idle, that processing stopped, or that status remained correctly synchronized.

An observed-edge state skeleton is:

> Submit → Screen | AutoCheck
> Screen → AutoCheck | Review | Escalate | Resolve | Withdraw | Reject
> AutoCheck → Screen | Review | Escalate | Resolve
> Review → Rework | Escalate | Resolve | Withdraw | Reject
> Rework → Review | Resolve
> Escalate → Review | Resolve | Transfer
> Resolve, Withdraw, Reject, Transfer → absorbing within the internal episode.

This is an **observed-edge skeleton**, not a validated process model: combining edges can permit paths never observed. State augmentation with review counts, escalation history, and elapsed state time would reduce reliance on a simple Markov assumption.

For an end-to-end model, transfer would instead lead to an external-active state with subsequently unobserved transitions.

Open prefixes must retain their incomplete-trace metadata. Their endings must not teach a discovery method that `Review`, `Screen`, or `Escalate` are legitimate completed outcomes. Nor should equal event spacing be fabricated to produce a performance directly-follows graph.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

The following calculations illustrate how assumptions and endpoints change the answer:

| Analysis                                         | Interpretation                                                                                                           | Estimated 72-hour non-resolution |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------: |
| Transfers, withdrawals, and rejections compete   | Actual internal episode, primary definition.                                                                             |                        **69.6%** |
| Transfers censored; withdrawal/rejection compete | End-to-end extrapolation under an independent-transfer-loss assumption.                                                  |                        **68.7%** |
| Transfers hypothetically resolve immediately     | Optimistic external-completion scenario.                                                                                 |                        **62.1%** |
| Withdrawal/rejection censored; transfer competes | Net curve obtained by removing withdrawal/rejection hazards; not actual-world risk or an identified intervention effect. |                        **65.0%** |

The optimistic and pessimistic transfer calculations are scenario results conditional on the administrative-censoring assumptions, **not confidence bounds**.

For the finite extract, allowing unknown external success after S08 and S21 widens the no-resolution bounds to **50.0–71.9%**, assuming withdrawal and rejection remain terminal. S32 transferred after the deadline and cannot become a timely success under these scenarios.

**Alternative withdrawal/rejection governance.** Counting all internal dispositions as acceptable SLA completion changes the target to internal active probability: 38.0% overall. The cohort comparison reverses relative to resolution failure: B has slightly lower estimated non-resolution, but higher estimated continued internal activity. This is an endpoint change, not contradictory statistical evidence.

**Informative censoring and long gaps.** First distinguish true ongoing cases from stale records. A long gap alone does not justify moving censoring backward. If an open status is authoritative through cutoff, S26’s breach remains observed despite its silence. If status merely means “no update received,” the real outcome may be missing after the last verified contact. Subsequent discovery of a resolution within a known interval would require interval-censoring treatment.

With richer records, compare conditional independent-censoring estimates against inverse-probability-of-censoring weighting and explicit departures from the assumed post-loss resolution rate. Such adjustments need a defensible observation model and adequate probability of continued observation. They cannot be reliably learned from these 32 snapshots alone.

**Cohort-policy sensitivity.** Standardizing cohort-specific, priority-stratified estimates to the pooled priority proportions gives internal non-resolution estimates of **68.8% for A and 67.5% for B**, versus crude estimates of 70.8% and 68.4%. This remains descriptive adjustment on recorded priority, not a causal policy estimate.

The finite-extract cohort bounds overlap substantially: A is 60.0–73.3%, and B is 52.9–70.6%. Different eventual outcomes for the five immature cases could reverse their ranking even without changing the endpoint. Differential external success after transfer could also reverse it.

Finally, any apparent escalation or automated-screening advantage could change after accounting for case severity, treatment timing, calendar workload, and rollout eligibility. Observed activity occurrence should not be substituted for randomized or baseline-assigned treatment.

# 7. Operational interpretation

**Separate prevention alerts from recovery alerts.** S11 and S25 are P1, already overdue, and already escalated. Their probability of missing this deadline is no longer a forecasting problem: the breach is observed. They need ownership confirmation and a recovery plan, not merely another high-risk score.

S24 is different: it is 67 hours old, leaving five hours before the deadline. Its four-hour activity gap does not reset the SLA or establish safety. For such cases, the useful future target is

$$
P(T_R>72\mid T_D>L,\mathcal H_L),
$$

where \(\mathcal H_L\) contains only information available at current age \(L\). For cases already beyond 72, forecast remaining resolution time or recovery within a new operational window.

S09 and S26 warrant investigation of both process delay and record freshness. Silence may indicate a queue, dependency, unrecorded work, or an observation problem; the table does not distinguish them.

**Use the correct output for workload.** Forecast staffing demand from expected active-state occupancy, not from \(1-F_R\), which also includes cases already withdrawn, rejected, or transferred. Transfer should close internal ownership only under a defined handoff rule; external success requires its own follow-up record.

**Keep governance explicit.** Publish resolution-by-72, active-at-72, withdrawal/rejection incidence, and transfer incidence together. A policy that accelerates rejection or transfer can improve closure speed without improving resolution. Neither a low predicted success probability nor a high breach risk justifies manipulating the endpoint to improve the dashboard.

**Calibration and fairness checks must use the same estimand as the alerts.** On larger, later cohorts, evaluate horizon calibration, censoring-adjusted prediction error, alert burden, and missed breaches separately by priority and cohort. Do not label open cases below 72 as observed failures or successes. Competing outcomes are observed outcomes for the primary target, not missing labels.

The current subgroup sizes are too small to establish reliable calibration. P1 comparisons also require particular caution because every P1 case has observed escalation and every transfer is P1. Different underlying risks do not automatically imply unfairness; assess whether alert thresholds, ownership responses, and resource allocation impose avoidable disadvantages within each priority and cohort.

These predictions describe risk under the observed operating environment. They do not estimate how much escalation, automation, or additional staffing would causally reduce it.

# 8. Python or precise pseudocode

The executable Python below requires **pandas** and implements Aalen–Johansen directly, so no survival library is assumed. Its numerical calculations and pandas fallback were checked; the optional PM4Py branch was not executed here because PM4Py is unavailable.

When installed, PM4Py receives ordered `EventLog` traces without fabricated timestamps and derives variants through `get_variants`. Otherwise, the fallback retains a PM4Py-compatible activity table and computes the same sequence-count features. Process features remain descriptive rather than leakage-prone baseline predictors.

```python

from numbers import Integral

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(DATA), sep="|")
STATUS_CODE = {
    "resolved": 1, "withdrawn": 2, "rejected": 3,
    "transferred": 4, "open": 0,
}


def encode(frame, transfer="competing", wr="competing"):
    """Retain every case. Codes: 0=censor; 1=R; 2=W; 3=Reject; 4=Transfer."""
    if transfer not in {"competing", "censor", "immediate_resolution"}:
        raise ValueError("Unknown transfer scenario")
    if wr not in {"competing", "censor"}:
        raise ValueError("Unknown withdrawal/rejection scenario")
    out = frame.copy()
    out["time"] = out.duration_or_age_h.astype(float)
    out["code"] = out.observed_status.map(STATUS_CODE).astype(int)
    transferred = out.observed_status.eq("transferred")
    if transfer == "censor":
        out.loc[transferred, "code"] = 0
    elif transfer == "immediate_resolution":
        # Hypothetical outcome only; observed prefixes remain unchanged.
        out.loc[transferred, "code"] = 1
    if wr == "censor":
        out.loc[out.observed_status.isin(["withdrawn", "rejected"]), "code"] = 0
    out["resolve_event"] = out.code.eq(1).astype(int)
    out["any_event"] = out.code.gt(0).astype(int)
    # Primary KM for disposition uses time + any_event.
    # Primary resolution probability uses cause codes, not resolution-only KM.
    return out


def aj(encoded):
    """Aalen-Johansen: one initial state, four absorbing causes.
    At tied times, subjects censored then remain in that time's event risk set.
    S is the KM survival for any coded event.
    """
    survival = 1.0
    cif = {k: 0.0 for k in range(1, 5)}
    rows = [dict(time=0.0, risk=len(encoded), censored=0, d_all=0,
                 S=survival, **{f"F{k}": cif[k] for k in cif})]
    for time in sorted(encoded.time.unique()):
        risk = int(encoded.time.ge(time).sum())
        codes = encoded.loc[encoded.time.eq(time), "code"]
        counts = {k: int(codes.eq(k).sum()) for k in cif}
        previous = survival
        for k in cif:
            cif[k] += previous * counts[k] / risk
        all_events = sum(counts.values())
        survival *= 1.0 - all_events / risk
        rows.append(dict(time=float(time), risk=risk,
                         censored=int(codes.eq(0).sum()), d_all=all_events,
                         S=survival, **{f"F{k}": cif[k] for k in cif}))
    curve = pd.DataFrame(rows)
    mass = curve.S + curve[[f"F{k}" for k in cif]].sum(axis=1)
    assert ((mass - 1).abs() < 1e-10).all()
    return curve


def at(curve, horizon):
    return curve.loc[curve.time.le(horizon)].iloc[-1]


def restricted_mean(curve, horizon):
    points = curve.loc[curve.time.lt(horizon), ["time", "S"]].copy()
    points["stop"] = points.time.shift(-1).fillna(horizon)
    return float(((points.stop - points.time) * points.S).sum())


def summary(frame, transfer="competing", wr="competing"):
    encoded = encode(frame, transfer, wr)
    curve = aj(encoded)
    if encoded.time.max() < 72 and curve.iloc[-1].S > 1e-12:
        raise ValueError("Follow-up does not support estimation through 72 hours")
    x = at(curve, 72)
    return dict(
        n=len(frame), risk_at_72=int(encoded.time.ge(72).sum()),
        F_resolve_72=x.F1, no_resolution_72=1.0 - x.F1,
        event_free_72=x.S, F_withdraw_72=x.F2,
        F_reject_72=x.F3, F_transfer_72=x.F4,
        restricted_event_free_h=restricted_mean(curve, 72),
    )


def horizon_bounds(frame, horizon=72):
    """Finite-extract status bounds, NOT population confidence intervals."""
    t, status = frame.duration_or_age_h, frame.observed_status
    immature = status.eq("open") & t.lt(horizon)
    timely = status.eq("resolved") & t.le(horizon)
    bad = (~immature & ~timely).sum()
    active = (t.gt(horizon) | (status.eq("open") & t.eq(horizon))).sum()
    early_transfer = (status.eq("transferred") & t.le(horizon)).sum()
    n, unknown = len(frame), immature.sum()
    return dict(
        n=n, immature=int(unknown),
        internal_noR_lower=bad / n,
        internal_noR_upper=(bad + unknown) / n,
        active_lower=active / n,
        active_upper=(active + unknown) / n,
        external_noR_lower=(bad - early_transfer) / n,
        external_noR_upper=(bad + unknown) / n,
    )


def control_flow_features(frame):
    """Observed-prefix features only; do not use these as baseline predictors."""
    out = frame.copy()
    out["activities"] = out.prefix.str.split(">")
    events = pd.DataFrame([

        for row in out.itertuples()
        for j, activity in enumerate(row.activities)
    ])  # Activity order is observed. No timestamps are fabricated.

    try:
        import pm4py
        from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace, Event
    except ImportError:
        variant_counts = out.activities.map(tuple).value_counts().to_dict()
        print("\nPM4Py unavailable: pandas ordered-sequence fallback.")
    else:
        log = EventLog()
        for case, group in events.groupby("case:concept:name", sort=False):
            status = group["case:observed_status"].iloc[0]
            trace = Trace(attributes={
                "concept:name": case,
                "observed_status": status,
                "internal_episode_complete": status != "open",
            })
            for activity in group.sort_values("event_index")["concept:name"]:
                trace.append(Event({"concept:name": activity}))
            log.append(trace)
        variants = pm4py.get_variants(log)
        variant_counts = {
            (key if isinstance(key, tuple) else tuple(key.split(","))):
            (int(value) if isinstance(value, Integral) else len(value))
            for key, value in variants.items()
        }
        print("\nPM4Py EventLog/get_variants executed without timestamps.")

    out["variant_n"] = out.activities.map(lambda a: variant_counts[tuple(a)])
    out["review_count"] = out.activities.map(lambda a: a.count("Review"))
    out["auto_seen"] = out.activities.map(lambda a: "AutoCheck" in a)
    out["escalation_seen"] = out.activities.map(lambda a: "Escalate" in a)
    assert out.escalation_seen.eq(out.escalated.eq("yes")).all()
    assert out.activities.map(lambda a: a.count("Rework")).eq(out.rework_count).all()
    out["last_event_age_h"] = (
        out.duration_or_age_h - out.last_event_gap_h
    ).where(out.observed_status.eq("open"))
    print("Distinct observed-prefix variants:", len(variant_counts))
    return out, events
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

**First priority: outcome and observation integrity.** Obtain linked external outcomes after transfer, dates of external resolution or other disposition, confirmation of handoff acceptance, and the last date on which each case was verified active. Clarify whether `open` is an authoritative operational state or merely absence of an update. These determine whether uncertainty is administrative censoring, informative loss, or potentially interval-censored outcome occurrence.

**Second priority: exact event timestamps and lifecycle semantics.** Obtain timestamps for screening, automated checks, review, escalation, rework, and disposition; distinguish task start, completion, queue entry, and reassignment. These enable actual landmark histories, time-varying exposure encoding, and stage-duration analysis. Activity order alone does not identify where elapsed time was spent.

**Third priority: cutoff, Submit dates, and extraction rules.** Recover the common cutoff, absolute case-start dates, inclusion window, and cohort definition. Clarify the age overlap between A and B. Establish whether this is an incident-case cohort or an extract selected partly because cases remained active; otherwise prevalent-case selection, length bias, or delayed entry may matter.

**Fourth priority: assignment, eligibility, and rollout compliance.** Record baseline policy assignment, which cases were eligible for automated screening, policy version, actual execution times, exceptions, and reasons for escalation or transfer. The presence or absence of `AutoCheck` is not enough to distinguish compliance from legitimate routing differences.

**Fifth priority: time-varying workload and case mix.** Obtain queue sizes, staffing, external dependencies, complexity indicators, resource assignments, and priority changes. These may explain both outcomes and selection into escalation, transfer, or prolonged silence. They are needed for credible prediction and are particularly important for causal policy analysis.

Finally, obtain a larger cohort with longer, reasonably complete follow-up and an explicit SLA eligibility policy for withdrawal, rejection, and transfer. The present extract supports a transparent descriptive competing-risk analysis and concrete overdue-case triage. It does **not** establish that cohort B’s policy caused an improvement, that escalation causes delay, or that long inactivity gaps identify a particular bottleneck.
