## 1. Interaction-graph description

The object-centric graph is not a set of six independent job paths. It is a typed, multi-layer interaction graph in which jobs are tied through components, lots, batches, tools, machines, and shipments.

Important node types are: `Job`, `Component`, `ComponentLot`, `Batch`, `Machine`, `Tool`, `Shipment`, and time-stamped `Event` nodes. Directly observed edges come from supplied O2O relations and event-to-object relations. Inferred edges are job-to-job couplings created when two jobs share a batch, lot, tool, shipment, or capacity-constrained event.

Compact typed edge list:

```text
Job requires Component:
  J1-C1, J2-C2, J3-C3, J4-C4, J5-C5, J6-C6          [direct O2O]

ComponentLot contains Component:
  L7-C2, L7-C5; L8-C1, L8-C3                         [direct O2O]

Batch batches Job:
  B1-J1, B1-J2, B1-J3; B2-J4, B2-J5                  [direct O2O]

Shipment consolidates Job:
  S1-J1,J4; S2-J2,J3; S3-J5,J6                       [direct O2O]

Batch Cure event at 12:05:
  event-B1-OVEN-1-RACK-7-J1-J2-J3-C1-C2-C3           [direct E2O]

B2 cure:
  B2-OVEN-1-RACK-7-J4-J5                             [partly direct from evidence]

Fixture use:
  J3-FIX-A 09:00-11:40; J4-CNC-2-FIX-A at 11:45      [direct/inferred hold interval]

Lot quarantine/release:
  L7-C2-C5-J2-J5 at 10:20 and 13:50                  [direct E2O, but integration caveat]

Final test:
  J5-TEST-1-CAL-3 16:10-16:35;
  CAL-3 recalibration 16:35-17:20;
  J6-TEST-1-CAL-3 17:25-17:50                        [direct/inferred from testing rule]

Shipment waiting:
  J1 packed 13:30; J4 packed 19:10; S1 dispatch 19:25 [direct timing, inferred waiting]
```

Higher-order motifs matter more than simple pairwise resource sharing.

First, `Job–Component–Batch–Machine–Tool`: B1 joins J1, J2, J3, C1, C2, C3, OVEN-1, and RACK-7 in one `Batch Cure` event. The early-ready jobs J1 and J2 cannot be understood without J3’s readiness and the batch/rack/oven combination.

Second, `Job–Tool–Machine–Job`: J3 holds FIX-A until 11:40, while J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and only starts at 11:45. CNC-2 appears idle for part of the interval, so the hidden coupling is not machine capacity alone but the missing tool relation.

Third, `ComponentLot–Component–Job–Batch–Policy`: L7 contains C2 and C5, linking J2 and J5. Lot quarantine/release events relate to both jobs, but J2 proceeds before release through a supervisor override while J5 does not. This is both an operational dependency and a data-integration issue.

Fourth, `Job–Batch–Tool–Machine–Shipment`: J4 is delayed through FIX-A/CNC and B2/RACK-7/OVEN-1, then its late packing holds J1’s S1 dispatch. That creates a cascade from a production-side dependency into logistics.

Edges such as `J1 waits for J3`, `J4 waits for FIX-A`, and `J1 waits for J4 shipment partner` are inferred from timestamps plus feasibility rules. They should be treated as hypotheses, not direct causal facts.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition

1. **B1 synchronization wait for J1.**
   J1 is ready for batch at 08:10; B1 starts at 12:05. Waiting is 3 h 55 min, or 235 minutes. Counterfactual assumption: J1 could have proceeded immediately or in a smaller batch once ready. This is synchronization-induced waiting under that assumption, not proof that all 235 minutes were avoidable.

2. **B1 synchronization wait for J2.**
   J2 is ready at 08:25; B1 starts at 12:05. Waiting is 3 h 40 min, or 220 minutes. Counterfactual assumption: J2 did not need to wait for J3 or for a full B1 batch. If the batch rule is mandatory for quality or cost reasons, this is policy-imposed waiting rather than waste.

3. **B1 total job-time waiting.**
   J1, J2, and J3 wait 235 + 220 + 10 = 465 job-minutes, or 7 h 45 min of cumulative waiting before one cure event. Counterfactual assumption: readiness timestamps are trusted and the only relevant barrier is batch start. The total highlights why a low-frequency batch event can dominate job experience.

4. **B2 waiting and RACK-7 ambiguity.**
   J4 is ready at 13:10; J5 is ready at 14:40; B2 starts at 14:50. J4 waits 100 minutes, J5 waits 10 minutes. RACK-7 is unavailable until 14:35, so up to 85 minutes of J4’s wait overlaps with rack quarantine, but J5’s readiness at 14:40 also blocks a two-job batch. Counterfactual assumption: B2 could start when both rack and required jobs are available. Attribution is ambiguous: the binding constraint shifts from rack to J5 readiness around 14:35–14:40.

5. **OVEN-1 idle despite job waiting.**
   OVEN-1 is idle from 12:55 to 14:50, or 115 minutes. During 13:10–14:50, J4 is waiting for B2, so there are 100 minutes where at least one job is ready while the oven is not processing it. Counterfactual assumption: OVEN-1 could process a partial batch or an alternative batch without RACK-7 constraints. This calculation argues against pure oven capacity as the explanation.

6. **FIX-A delay plausibly associated with J4.**
   J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and starts `Machine Process` at 11:45 using FIX-A. J3 holds FIX-A from 09:00 to 11:40. The broad J4 delay from reaching CNC-2 to start is 115 minutes. A tighter fixture-unavailability estimate is 09:50–11:40 = 110 minutes, plus 5 minutes handover/setup. A conservative lower bound is the observed CNC idle interval 10:05–10:47, or 42 minutes, during which J4 could not start despite machine idleness if FIX-A was required. Counterfactual assumption: J4 was otherwise process-ready and no unrecorded operator or quality hold existed.

7. **CAL-3 delay plausibly associated with J6.**
   J6 is ready for final test at 16:05 and tests at 17:25. Total wait is 80 minutes. J5 occupies TEST-1/CAL-3 from 16:10 to 16:35. CAL-3 recalibration runs 16:35–17:20, and J6 starts at 17:25. If J6 would have tested immediately after J5 with a 5-minute changeover, it would start at 16:40. Actual start at 17:25 implies a 45-minute calibration-associated delay. Counterfactual assumption: the recalibration was not mandatory before J6 or could have been scheduled earlier.

8. **S1 consolidation delay imposed on J1.**
   J1 is packed at 13:30. J4 is packed at 19:10. S1 dispatches at 19:25. J1 waits 5 h 55 min, or 355 minutes, from pack to dispatch. If J1 could dispatch 25 minutes after being packed, matching S2’s post-last-pack timing, it would leave at 13:55. Excess delay is 19:25 − 13:55 = 330 minutes. Counterfactual assumption: shipment consolidation is optional or can be split without unacceptable transport cost.

## 4. What flattening misses

A **Job** flattening makes the apparent control flow visible: release, staging, machine processing, inspection, rework, pack, dispatch. It hides batch partners, shared tools, lot status, and shipment partner readiness. It can misleadingly conclude that no job is exceptional because no job exceeds 1.8 standard deviations, even though several jobs experience large hidden waits at different places.

A **Machine** flattening makes setup, processing, release, load, cure, unload, calibrate, and final test visible. It hides jobs waiting away from the machine because a tool, batch partner, lot release, or shipment partner is missing. The misleading conclusion is that CNC-2, OVEN-1, and TEST-1 are not bottlenecks because utilization is moderate. That may be true for machine capacity, but not for the object network around the machines.

A **Batch** flattening would reveal B1 and B2 as synchronization objects. It would show that one cure event can hold multiple jobs. It may hide individual job waiting if readiness is not represented as a batch-related event. It may also duplicate delay across jobs if every job in a batch inherits the same cure duration.

A **Tool** flattening would reveal FIX-A, RACK-7, and CAL-3 lifecycles: acquisition, release, cleaning, quarantine, recalibration. It may hide the affected job’s downstream shipment delay. A misleading tool DFG could show ordinary acquire/release sequences while missing the fact that CNC-2 and TEST-1 are idle because the relevant tool is unavailable.

A **ComponentLot** flattening would reveal L7 quarantine and release. It would hide the operational asymmetry that J2 proceeds through override while J5 waits. Depending on integration policy, it can either omit job impact or overstate it by attaching lot events to every related job.

A **Shipment** flattening shows create, load, and dispatch. It hides the waiting of packed jobs for other jobs in the same shipment. The misleading conclusion is that shipment processing is fast because `Load -> Dispatch` is only 0.3 h, while J1 waits almost six hours after packing.

Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting because the binding resources are not always machines. Jobs can wait for a batch cohort, a rack to exit quarantine, a fixture to be released, a calibration tool to be recalibrated, a lot to be released, or a shipment partner to be packed. Machine idle time is therefore not evidence of no bottleneck; it can be evidence that the bottleneck lies upstream or orthogonal to the machine.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

Narrative 1: B1 synchronization.
Temporal precedence: J1 is ready at 08:10, J2 at 08:25, and J3 at 11:55. B1 starts at 12:05 on OVEN-1 using RACK-7 and relates to J1, J2, J3, C1, C2, and C3. Resource feasibility: the cure requires both OVEN-1 and RACK-7, and the event is a single multi-object batch event. Policy constraints: if B1 must contain J1–J3 together, early jobs must wait for the last ready member. Causal speculation: J3’s later readiness is a plausible proximate cause of J1/J2’s long pre-cure waiting, but unrecorded rack, operator, or recipe constraints could also explain part of the delay.

Narrative 2: FIX-A to J4 to S1 cascade.
Temporal precedence: J3 acquires FIX-A at 09:00 and releases it at 11:40. J4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and starts `Machine Process` at 11:45 with CNC-2 and FIX-A. J4 later becomes part of B2 and is packed at 19:10. S1 contains J1 and J4; J1 was packed at 13:30 but dispatches only at 19:25. Resource feasibility: J4 requires FIX-A for its CNC operation, and S1 requires both shipment jobs before dispatch. Policy constraints: the system has no explicit `Wait for Tool`, and shipment consolidation keeps J1 tied to J4. Causal speculation: FIX-A contention may have delayed J4, and J4’s lateness then propagated into S1, delaying J1. The full cascade is plausible, but the exact share of J4’s lateness caused by FIX-A is not proven.

Narrative 3: L7, B2, CAL-3, and S3.
Temporal precedence: L7 contains C2 and C5. L7 is quarantined at 10:20 and released at 13:50, relating to J2 and J5. J2 enters B1 before release through an override, while J5 does not proceed until after release. J5 is ready for final test at 16:00, tests 16:10–16:35 using TEST-1 and CAL-3, then CAL-3 is recalibrated until 17:20. J6, also in S3 with J5, waits to test until 17:25. Resource feasibility: J5 and J6 share CAL-3 as well as TEST-1, and J5/J6 share shipment S3. Policy constraints: lot release governs component use unless overridden; calibration governs test validity; shipment consolidation governs dispatch. Causal speculation: L7 may have delayed J5, J5’s test may have triggered or preceded CAL-3 recalibration, and CAL-3 then delayed J6. The evidence supports coupling, but not a single deterministic cause.

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py
from collections import Counter, defaultdict

# Assumption: `ocel` is an already loaded PM4Py OCEL object.

# 1) Object-centric summaries and graphs
interactions_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)        # object-type graph
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)      # event types related to object types
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)

# Object interaction graph: shared events between concrete objects.
try:
    oi_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(ocel, graph_type="object_interaction")
except TypeError:
    oi_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(ocel)

# Optional NetworkX conversion for graph motifs.
try:
    nx_graph = pm4py.convert_ocel_to_networkx(ocel)
except Exception:
    nx_graph = None

# 2) Compare object-centric frequency metrics.
# Metric A: PM4Py interaction summary, usually object-type-pair based.
print("Object interactions summary")
print(interactions_summary)

# Metric B: object-type graph / event-type-to-object-type frequencies.
print("Object type graph")
print(otg)
print("Event type to object type")
print(etot)

# A lightweight normalizer for graph-like outputs.
def edge_count(obj):
    if hasattr(obj, "edges"):
        return len(list(obj.edges()))
    if isinstance(obj, dict):
        return sum(len(v) if hasattr(v, "__len__") else 1 for v in obj.values())
    return None

print({
    "object_interaction_graph_edges": edge_count(oi_graph),
    "object_type_graph_edges": edge_count(otg),
    "etot_entries": edge_count(etot),
})

# 3) Flatten several viewpoints, but do not treat any one DFG as sufficient.
object_types = ["Job", "Machine", "Batch", "Tool", "ComponentLot", "Shipment"]
flattened_perf = {}

for ot in object_types:
    try:
        flat = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, ot)
        # PM4Py flattening usually returns a dataframe/event log with standard columns.
        if isinstance(flat, pd.DataFrame):
            if {"case:concept:name", "concept:name", "time:timestamp"}.issubset(flat.columns):
                flat = pm4py.format_dataframe(
                    flat,
                    case_id="case:concept:name",
                    activity_key="concept:name",
                    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
                )
        flattened_perf[ot] = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(flat)
    except Exception as exc:
        flattened_perf[ot] = f"Could not flatten {ot}: {exc}"

for ot, dfg in flattened_perf.items():
    print(f"\nPerformance DFG for {ot}")
    print(dfg)

# 4) Extract object-level features.
# These features can be used to model waiting risk using object relations,
# not just using machine idle gaps.
features_by_type = {}
for ot in object_types:
    try:
        features_by_type[ot] = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, ot)
    except TypeError:
        try:
            features_by_type[ot] = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, obj_type=ot)
        except Exception as exc:
            features_by_type[ot] = f"Feature extraction failed for {ot}: {exc}"
    except Exception as exc:
        features_by_type[ot] = f"Feature extraction failed for {ot}: {exc}"

# 5) Explicit wait analysis using event-to-object relations, when events exist.
# This intentionally uses object states such as Ready/Acquire/Release/Cure/Test,
# not machine-event gaps alone.
events = getattr(ocel, "events", None)
relations = getattr(ocel, "relations", None)

if events is not None and relations is not None:
    ev = events.copy()
    rel = relations.copy()

    def pick_col(df, candidates):
        for c in candidates:
            if c in df.columns:
                return c
        raise KeyError(f"Missing one of {candidates}")

    eid = pick_col(ev, ["ocel:eid", "event_id", "eid"])
    act = pick_col(ev, ["ocel:activity", "concept:name", "activity"])
    ts = pick_col(ev, ["ocel:timestamp", "time:timestamp", "timestamp"])
    rid = pick_col(rel, ["ocel:eid", "event_id", "eid"])
    oid = pick_col(rel, ["ocel:oid", "object_id", "oid"])
    otype = pick_col(rel, ["ocel:type", "object_type", "type"])

    ev_rel = rel.merge(ev[[eid, act, ts]], left_on=rid, right_on=eid, how="left")
    ev_rel[ts] = pd.to_datetime(ev_rel[ts])

    # Example: ready-to-batch waiting by Job, requiring explicit Ready and Batch Cure events.
    job_events = ev_rel[ev_rel[otype].eq("Job")]
    ready = job_events[job_events[act].str.contains("Ready", case=False, na=False)]
    cure = job_events[job_events[act].str.contains("Batch Cure|Cure", case=False, regex=True, na=False)]

    waits = []
    for job_id, rgrp in ready.groupby(oid):
        cgrp = cure[cure[oid].eq(job_id)]
        if rgrp.empty or cgrp.empty:
            continue
        r_time = rgrp[ts].min()
        c_after = cgrp[cgrp[ts] >= r_time]
        if not c_after.empty:
            c_time = c_after[ts].min()
            waits.append({
                "job": job_id,
                "ready_time": r_time,
                "next_cure_time": c_time,
                "ready_to_cure_minutes": (c_time - r_time).total_seconds() / 60,
            })

    ready_to_cure = pd.DataFrame(waits)
    print("\nExplicit ready-to-cure waits")
    print(ready_to_cure.sort_values("ready_to_cure_minutes", ascending=False))

    # Tool wait proxy: Request/Acquire/Release/Calibration event analysis if logged.
    tool_related = ev_rel[ev_rel[otype].eq("Tool")]
    print("\nTool-related activity counts")
    print(tool_related.groupby([oid, act]).size().sort_values(ascending=False))

# 6) Suggested comparison:
# - High Job-Tool interaction frequency with low Machine utilization suggests hidden tool gating.
# - High Job-Batch and Batch-Tool interaction frequency with high ready-to-cure waits suggests synchronization.
# - Job-Shipment interactions must be evaluated using packed-job-to-dispatch waits, not shipment DFG alone.
```

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken the top-ranked batch-synchronization explanation:

1. J1 and J2 readiness timestamps were backfilled incorrectly and they were not truly ready before 12:05.
2. B1 could not legally or physically be split because J1–J3 required the same validated cure cycle.
3. OVEN-1 had unrecorded maintenance, operator absence, or recipe setup from 08:10 to 12:05.
4. RACK-7 was unavailable before B1 as well, making rack availability rather than synchronization the true binding constraint.
5. J1 and J2 were doing value-adding work between readiness and B1 start, so the interval was not waiting.
6. In a larger sample, ready-to-batch waiting is rare and B1 is an isolated exception.
7. Similar batches with late members do not delay early-ready jobs because dispatching logic usually resequences them.
8. Quality or rework outcomes worsen sharply when batches are split, meaning the “delay” is an intentional control.

Additional data fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses:

1. Explicit `Ready for Batch`, `Wait for Batch Partner`, `Batch Formed`, and `Batch Rule Applied` events.
2. Tool `Request`, `Reserved`, `Acquire`, `Release`, `Cleaning`, `Quarantine`, and `Available` timestamps for FIX-A, RACK-7, and CAL-3.
3. Machine queue-entry, operator assignment, setup, maintenance, and downtime events.
4. Lot status events consistently linked to lots, components, jobs, overrides, approvers, and source systems.
5. Supervisor override reason, timestamp, authorization level, and whether override bypassed a hard or soft control.
6. Shipment policy fields: consolidation rule, carrier cutoff, customer promise, split-shipment permission, and cost.
7. Packed-job waiting events, not just shipment `Create/Load/Dispatch`.
8. Calibration due reason for CAL-3: time-based, use-count-based, failure-triggered, or product-specific.
9. Relation provenance: whether each event-to-object relation came from the source system, inference, backfill, or manual integration.
10. Planned schedule versus actual schedule for jobs, tools, machines, batches, and shipments.
