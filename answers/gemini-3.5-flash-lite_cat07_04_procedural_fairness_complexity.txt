# 1. Fairness dimensions

Assessing a public-benefits case-management process requires going beyond raw approval rates to evaluate procedural fairness across multiple dimensions:

- **Outcome parity:** Raw approval rates vary widely, from 78% for Group A down to 58% for Group D and 63% for Group B. However, raw outcome parity is a weak test of fairness because applicant groups differ substantially in baseline risk, completeness, channel usage, and support needs.
- **Adjusted outcome disparity:** Even after controlling for risk score, documentation completeness, channel, household size, prior case count, region, and month, regression models show residual gaps. Group B faces a 5.5 percentage point drop in approval probability and Group D a 3.1 percentage point drop compared to the reference Group A. These adjusted gaps indicate that controls do not fully account for outcome differences.
- **Procedural burden:** Procedural burden measures the administrative friction imposed on applicants. Groups B, C, and D face much higher rates of incomplete documentation, more routing steps (e.g., V2, V3, V4, V5), and higher reopen/rework rates (20–24% vs. 11% for Group A).
- **Timeliness:** Median and 90th percentile cycle times reveal stark inequalities. While Group A's median cycle time is 8.1 days, Groups B, C, and D experience medians of 13.7, 15.9, and 12.1 days respectively. 90th percentile wait times exceed 34–38 days for Groups B and C.
- **Consistency of routing:** Routing differs sharply by group. Group A predominantly follows straight-through processing (Variant V1: 42%). Group B is funneled into document requests (V2: 34%) and language support (V4: 24%). Group C is heavily routed through accommodation reviews (V5: 31%). Group D is concentrated in fraud and risk reviews (V3: 28%). 
- **Quality and accessibility of support:** Rights-preserving steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review) are intended to ensure accessibility. However, because they are bottlenecked by severe resource constraints (only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists), they transform into sources of severe delay (3.8-day to 4.7-day wait times before eligibility assessment).
- **Appeal and rework burden:** Appeal rates (6% for Group A vs. 13–16% for Groups B and D) and rework rates (11% vs. 20–24%) indicate that disadvantaged groups bear a disproportionate share of administrative friction after initial decisions are made.
- **Resource-allocation fairness:** Workload distribution is highly uneven. Group B cases are concentrated among 3 overloaded regional officers and 7 bilingual caseworkers. Group C cases rely on 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also handle supervisor reviews. This structural bottleneck concentrates vulnerable populations under resource-constrained staff.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To rigorously distinguish between legitimate case-mix differences, procedural discrimination, and statistical uncertainty, we employ a multi-faceted analytical strategy:

- **Matching or stratification variables:** Stratify cases using exact matching or propensity score weighting across `risk_score_band`, `documentation_completeness`, `channel`, `household_size_band`, `prior_case_count`, `region`, and `month` to create comparable risk-and-complexity strata.
- **Path/variant comparison:** Compare the frequency and duration of specific process variants (e.g., V1 vs. V4 and V5) within matched strata to isolate the structural time penalty introduced by rights-preserving support steps.
- **Resource-assignment comparison:** Evaluate workloads, queue lengths, and processing times conditional on assigned resource pools (e.g., general caseworkers vs. bilingual caseworkers vs. accommodation specialists) to test whether resource scarcity drives disparities.
- **Waiting-time decomposition:** Use PM4Py waiting-time insertion functions to decompose total cycle time into active service time and queuing waiting time across every transition, identifying specific bottlenecks (e.g., `Language Support Contact` $\rightarrow$ `Eligibility Assessment`).
- **Sensitivity analysis for possibly biased covariates:** Test models with and without `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` to measure how much of the outcome gap is "explained" by covariates that may themselves be artifacts of historical bias or linguistic barriers.
- **Subgroup checks by region and channel:** Run interacted models (Group $\times$ Region, Group $\times$ Channel) to determine if procedural burdens are localized in specific regional offices or offline intake channels.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

Analysis of the process variants and operational data reveals specific procedural disparities:

- **V1 Straight-Through Processing (42% Group A vs. 18–22% Groups B/C/D):** Favors Group A, enabling rapid 5.8-day median cycle times without administrative friction.
- **V4 & V5 Support-Heavy Paths:** Group B relies heavily on V4 (24%), which introduces a Language Support Contact step and a 3.8-day wait for eligibility assessment. Group C relies on V5 (31%), which introduces an Accommodation Review step and a 4.7-day wait. These paths are structurally legitimate (serving rights-preserving needs), but their operational implementation creates severe time penalties.
- **V3 & V6 Risk/Appeal Paths:** Group D is disproportionately routed into V3 (28%, Fraud/Risk/Supervisor review) and V6 (13%, leading to appeals), reflecting higher risk scores and intensive scrutiny.
- **Justified vs. Concerning Disparities:** Routing applicants requiring language support or disability accommodation through specialized steps is *justified* and legally necessary. However, the resulting 3.8 to 4.7-day bottlenecks, increased handover counts, and higher downstream appeal rates are *concerning*. They indicate that the agency burdens applicants for exercising their rights to accommodation and language access.

---

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Four primary mechanisms explain the observed operational patterns:

1. **Legitimate risk and complexity matching:** Higher-risk applicants (Group D) and those with missing documentation genuinely require fraud screening and document remediation, naturally producing longer cycle times and more review steps.
2. **Resource capacity constraints (Bottleneck mechanism):** The agency employs only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 5 accommodation specialists for hundreds of Group B and C applicants. This acute scarcity creates artificial queuing delays that inflate cycle times independently of case complexity.
3. **Structural handover friction:** Disadvantaged groups experience more handovers between general caseworkers, specialists, and supervisors. Each handover introduces handover lag, context loss, and opportunities for miscommunication, increasing rework and appeal rates.
4. **Proxy feedback loops in risk scoring and documentation:** If `risk_score_band` or `documentation_completeness` penalize minority-language or offline applicants (e.g., due to untranslated forms or digital exclusion), automated routing flags them for intensive fraud review, creating a compounding feedback loop of disadvantage.

---

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available dataset, while rich, has notable analytical limitations:

- **Measurement bias:** `documentation_completeness` is likely confounded by language barriers. An applicant who cannot read a poorly translated online form will submit "incomplete" documents, which is a failure of form design rather than applicant negligence.
- **Missing applicant needs:** Unobserved variables include underlying health conditions, caregiving responsibilities, digital literacy, and broadband access, which interact with channels and drive completion times.
- **Unobserved resource workload:** Resource workload is observed only at daily granularity. We cannot see intra-day queue surges, concurrent case loads per caseworker, or cognitive fatigue.
- **Appeal quality:** `appeal_flag` and `case_outcome` record binary results but capture nothing about the substantive justice, clarity, or dignity of explanations provided to applicants.
- **Historical bias in risk scores:** `risk_score_band` is treated as an objective covariate, but if it was trained on historical data containing systemic biases, controlling for it masks discrimination rather than explaining it.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation option | Intended benefit | Potential side effects or trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| **Add capacity for language support & accommodation review** | Reduce the 3.8–4.7 day wait times; ease bottlenecks for Groups B and C. | Requires budget reallocation; hiring and credentialing bilingual/specialist staff takes time. |
| **Establish service-level targets for support steps** | Ensure rights-preserving steps do not languish in queues. | May induce staff rushing, leading to superficial reviews or administrative errors. |
| **Redesign routing to reduce handovers** | Eliminate general $\rightarrow$ specialist $\rightarrow$ general handover loops; streamline ownership. | Requires cross-training general caseworkers in basic language/accommodation protocols, risking initial quality drops. |
| **Improve decision-explanation clarity** | Reduce rework, appeal rates, and applicant confusion by providing transparent rationales. | Drafting individualized explanations increases active case handling time per worker. |
| **Monitor adjusted disparities continuously** | Detect emerging bias across groups, regions, and channels in real time. | Can lead to defensive administrative behavior or metric gaming if not paired with qualitative oversight. |
| **Audit risk scores and documentation completeness** | Remove proxies for language and channel from automated risk models. | Short-term disruption to automated triage rules while models are recalibrated. |

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The public-benefits case-management process is **procedurally unfair**, exhibiting systematic structural disadvantage for minority-language applicants (Group B) and applicants requiring disability accommodations (Group C), even after controlling for measured case complexity. 

While differential routing into specialized support paths is legally and operationally justified, the severe resource bottlenecks governing those paths (producing multi-day queuing delays, excessive handovers, and elevated rework/appeal rates) impose an unjustifiable administrative burden on vulnerable applicants. The process fails to provide equitable procedural due weight to rights-preserving accommodations.
