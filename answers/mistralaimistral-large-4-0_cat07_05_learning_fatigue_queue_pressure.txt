# 1. Integrated explanation

The observed pattern is not one mechanism. It is a changing mixture of case mix, warm-up, queue pressure, circadian effects, batching, automation, and resource experience.

Early in a shift, service time is high and errors are moderately high because the work is harder: complexity is 0.72, high-priority share is 41%, queue length is 46, and the shift inherits backlog from the previous shift. At the same time, resources are still warming up, especially juniors. So the early-shift pattern is partly learning/warm-up and partly case-mix/selection. It would be wrong to say “people are slow because they are tired” at the start of a shift; the opposite is more plausible—they are slow because the cases are complex and the system is clearing inherited backlog.

From positions 6–50, service time falls and errors fall. This looks like learning, but it is also explained by declining complexity and priority. The cleanest evidence for warm-up is not the raw sequence-position table; it is the within-resource pattern: seniors and mid-level resources are faster later in the shift, and juniors improve from 5.1% error in the first 20 events to 4.4% later under low WIP. Even so, the improvement is modest compared with the case-mix gradient.

Late in the shift, the raw pattern changes character: service time keeps falling, but errors and rework rise sharply. This is not learning. Complexity is lower late—0.43 at positions 76+—so the late-shift error rise cannot be explained by harder cases. The more plausible explanation is queue pressure: WIP rises from 31 at positions 31–50 to 88 at positions 76+, and the WIP table shows a clear speed-quality tradeoff. Under high WIP, low-complexity cases drop from 12.7 to 9.4 minutes while errors rise from 2.1% to 5.8%; high-complexity cases drop from 22.4 to 16.8 minutes while errors rise from 4.2% to 10.9%. In other words, the system becomes faster and more error-prone at the same time because pressure induces rushing, shorter checks, batching, delayed Quality Check, and more corrections of Bot_Risk.

Fatigue may contribute, but it is not the only explanation and may not even be the dominant one. The strongest fatigue-like evidence is the deterioration in the last two hours of Day and Late shifts. But those are also the highest-pressure periods: Day queue rises from 38 to 75, and Late inherits a queue of 70. The fact that errors rise while complexity falls is consistent with fatigue, but it is equally consistent with queue pressure, batching, and end-of-shift Quality Check compression. The data cannot separate these without controls.

Circadian effects are plausible but entangled with staffing and case mix. Night shift has slower mean service time, 15.2 minutes, and a high-complexity share in the first hour, 56%. Night staff have fewer cases but still show 8.2% errors later under high WIP. That pattern could reflect circadian strain, fewer staff, more urgent exceptions, or selection of difficult cases into night hours. Early shift clears the night backlog, which suggests handover and queue ownership matter as much as clock time.

Resource experience changes the slope of the pressure response. Seniors under high WIP later in the shift have 4.9% errors; mid-level 6.8%; juniors 12.4%. Under low WIP later, seniors are stable at 2.2%, mid-level 2.9%, juniors 4.4%. This means the same queue pressure does not produce the same quality loss for everyone. Seniors appear able to absorb complexity and pressure; juniors are exposed to both early complexity and later pressure. That is a selection and capability interaction, not a simple “junior = bad” conclusion.

Batching and automation create local distortions. Document Review by Junior_H and Junior_I shows long consecutive runs with very short service times late in the Day shift. Those events may be legitimate batch work, but they coincide with the period where errors and rework rise. Quality Check is often delayed until the end of shift, creating correction bursts in the last 45 minutes. If Quality Check is compressed or deferred, measured service time for earlier activities may look artificially fast while defects move downstream. Bot_Risk is instant, but human corrections of bot classifications increase when queue exceeds 70. That suggests automation is not simply “good”; it interacts with human review under pressure.

The key integrated statement is: service time falls over the shift partly because cases become less complex and partly because of warm-up; errors fall early for the same reasons; but late in the shift, service time falls further while errors rise because queue pressure produces a speed-quality tradeoff, amplified by batching, delayed quality checks, automation overrides, lower experience, and possibly fatigue/circadian strain. Errors rise only in some conditions because the risk is concentrated where high WIP meets high complexity, junior/night resources, end-of-shift compression, and automation correction. Under low WIP, even late-shift work can be fast and accurate—seniors later under low WIP have 2.2% errors—so “late shift” itself is not the cause.

# 3. Proposed analyses

A defensible analysis should combine process mining with risk-adjusted statistics. The event log is observational, so the goal is not to “prove” one cause but to compare mechanisms, quantify interactions, and identify leverage points.

**Within-resource comparisons.** Compare each resource against themselves across shift position, WIP band, complexity band, and hour. This removes stable worker ability and selection. Use resource fixed effects or random intercepts. Compare first 20 events versus later events within the same resource-shift, matched on complexity and WIP.

**Within-shift position effects.** Model `minutes_since_shift_start` and `sequence_position_within_shift` with splines or bins, not a linear term. Expect non-linear warm-up and late-shift deterioration. Include interactions with WIP and complexity. Do not interpret position effects without case-mix adjustment.

**WIP/queue stratification.** Stratify by `wip_at_start` and `queue_length_at_start`: <30, 30–70, >70. Estimate service time, error rate, and rework by WIP × complexity × experience group. Look for threshold effects around 70. Use lagged queue and previous-shift end queue as instruments or controls.

**Complexity adjustment.** Include `case_complexity_score`, `priority`, high-complexity share, and predicted difficulty. Compare raw and adjusted late-shift error rates. If adjusted late-shift risk remains elevated, queue pressure/fatigue/batching are more plausible. If it disappears, case mix explains most of the pattern.

**Shift and time-of-day controls.** Include `shift_id`, hour-of-day, day-of-week, weekend/holiday flags, and interactions. Separate Early/Day/Late/Night from clock hour. Night effects should be modeled as shift × hour × staffing × case urgency.

**Batching detection.** Use `pm4py.discover_batches` and custom run detection for same resource/activity. Measure batch size, share of events in batches, inter-event gap, and downstream rework. Compare Document Review and Quality Check batch vs non-batch events.

**Resource-experience interactions.** Model experience group × WIP, experience × complexity, and experience × shift position. Test whether juniors degrade more under pressure and whether seniors absorb complex cases early.

**Rework/error downstream analysis.** Do not rely only on immediate `error_flag`. Use `rework_flag`, Rework Correction events, and 7-day rework attribution. Trace whether late-shift fast cases generate rework in the next shift or after closure. Use `pm4py.get_rework_cases_per_activity` and path-performance analysis from Quality Check to Rework Correction.

Key analytical rules: never compare raw late-shift error rates across workers without adjusting for WIP, complexity, priority, shift, hour, and case mix; treat queue length as endogenous; attribute rework to the originating shift/activity, not only to the correcting resource; and use event-log indicators to evaluate interventions before and after, with a control group where possible.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Worker-level conclusions from this log can be unfair or misleading. The log is observational, and the most visible patterns are exactly the ones most confounded.

**Selection bias.** Seniors appear to take complex cases early, especially after weekend or night backlog. Juniors may receive difficult first cases or late-shift pressure. Night staff handle fewer but more urgent exceptions. Comparing raw error rates across these groups compares different jobs, not different effort or skill.

**Simpson’s paradox and confounding.** A resource can look worse because they work when WIP is high, cases are complex, or Quality Check is compressed. A shift can look worse because it inherits backlog. A group can look better because it receives easier cases. Raw rankings can reverse after adjustment.

**Measurement error.** `error_flag` may be immediate and incomplete; rework within 7 days may be attributed to the wrong shift or resource. `case_complexity_score` may be assigned after the fact or correlated with unobserved difficulty. `start_timestamp` may be missing or inconsistent, making some batched events look artificially fast. Queue length at start may reflect policy and arrivals, not worker behavior.

**Queue pressure created by management.** WIP >70 is not only a worker problem. It is produced by staffing levels, shift design, arrival surges, handover practices, automation rules, and capacity decisions. Blaming workers for errors under high WIP punishes them for a system state they did not create.

**Automation quality.** Bot_Risk is fast, but fast is not the same as correct. Human corrections of bot classifications rise when queue >70. This may indicate bot errors, human rushing, or both. Automation can also change the case mix reaching humans by handling easy cases and leaving hard cases for review.

**Punitive use of error rates.** Penalizing workers based only on raw late-shift error rates is unsafe. It can suppress reporting, encourage gaming, hide rework, increase turnover, damage trust, and create legal/HR and disparate-impact risks. It can also shift attention away from queue design, staffing, batching, and automation governance.

**Fair process.** Any worker-level metric should be risk-adjusted, shown with uncertainty, compared within similar working conditions, and used for coaching and system improvement. Workers should be able to contest classifications, see the data, and understand how case mix and WIP are controlled. Do not remove Quality Check to improve throughput; improve QC capacity, timing, and downstream learning instead.
