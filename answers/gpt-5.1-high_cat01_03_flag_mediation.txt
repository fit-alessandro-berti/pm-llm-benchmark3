## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | P0 (current bundle) vs P1 (flag hidden, no priority lane, discretionary manual review) | Case-level; all incoming cases after stabilization of each policy | arrival-to-close cycle_time | Total policy effect on cycle time | Partly: policies are clear, but interference and time-varying congestion blur units | Unmeasured underlying risk and queue interference; policy also changes manual/escalation, not only visibility of flag |
| E2 | P0 vs P1 | Case-level; all cases with overlapping risk_score and complexity | arrival-to-triage_done time | Controlled direct effect of flag/priority_lane on triage completion, holding downstream handling as under each policy | Moderately: CDE can be framed via protocol for manual/escalation under each policy | Exposure-induced confounding because manual/escalation routing can feed back into triage behaviour and staffing |
| E3 | P0 vs P2 (keep priority lane, remove mandatory manual review) | Case-level; flagged-eligible cases | probability(manual_review=1) | Interventional indirect effect via manual-review occurrence (flag-induced change in whether manual_review happens) | Yes conceptually, but hard empirically | Manual-review decision depends on unobserved case features and reviewers; need model for manual under altered policy |
| E4 | P0 vs P3 (add protected manual-review capacity for flagged) | Case-level; flagged cases | manual_queue_wait | Interventional indirect effect through manual waiting time, holding manual occurrence high in both arms | Mostly: manual capacity expansion is a schedulable intervention | Need queueing/operational model; interference because one case’s routing changes others’ waits |
| E5 | P2 vs P4 (same triage/manual but different escalation criteria/capacity) | Case-level; cases entering manual review | escalation_queue_wait and cycle_time | Interventional indirect effect via escalation occurrence and waiting | Conceptually clear; empirically noisy | Small escalation counts; severe selection into escalation on post-triage/post-manual information |
| E6 | P0 vs P2 among high-risk cases | Case-level; high risk_score & high complexity | arrival-to-close cycle_time | Total effect of mandatory manual review (given priority triage) on cycle time | Partly: well-defined for eligible cases | Unmeasured underlying_risk and reviewer beliefs drive both escalation and service times; strong treatment–confounder feedback |
| E7 | P0 vs P3 | Case-level; flagged cases | decision_outcome quality (e.g., avoidable rework, adverse events, or ex-post error proxy) | Total policy effect on decision quality; interventional analogue to natural indirect effect via additional review depth | Poorly identified with current data but conceptually well-defined | Lack of direct error labels; outcome only “Approve/Reject”; confounding by underlying risk and external information |
| E8 | Within P0, hypothetical shift from current flag policy to “priority triage only” (flag used solely for triage order, not manual/escalation routing) | Case-level; flagged-eligible cases | cycle_time and triage_wait | Separable-like effect: contrast of flag’s triage component while holding downstream handling as if unflagged | Only as a thought experiment; operationally difficult | Cross-world assumptions about holding downstream paths fixed while changing only priority_lane; impossible to verify without a dedicated experiment |

I avoid natural direct/indirect effects because they require cross-world independence and no interference, which are implausible with shared queues and exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding. Instead, I use controlled direct effects and interventional path-specific effects defined through explicit policy changes (P0–P4) and queue-management rules.

```text
[Arrival / Intake]
U_underlying_risk, complexity, arrival_load
      |
      v
Register -> risk_score -> flag -> priority_lane

[Triage]
arrival_load, priority_lane, U_other_cases_load
      -> triage_wait -> triage_service

[Manual Review Layer]
flag, risk_score, complexity, U_reviewer_pref
      -> manual_review
manual_review, arrival_load, U_other_cases_load
      -> manual_queue_wait -> manual_service

[Escalation Layer]
manual_service, complexity, U_reviewer_pref
      -> escalation
escalation, arrival_load, U_other_cases_load
      -> escalation_queue_wait -> senior_service

[Closure]
triage_wait, triage_service,
manual_queue_wait, manual_service,
escalation_queue_wait, senior_service
      -> cycle_time -> decision_outcome
```

Interference arises because `U_other_cases_load` and shared queues mean one case’s routing and service times affect others’ waits at manual review and escalation. Exposure-induced confounding appears when `flag` influences `manual_review` and `escalation`, which then affect subsequent queue states and even triage behaviour. Treatment–confounder feedback occurs as manual and escalation decisions depend on updated beliefs about `U_underlying_risk`. Arrival-time patterns and `arrival_load` also create time-varying confounding between flag-driven routing and waiting times. These features make natural direct/indirect effects ill-posed and favour interventional, policy-defined contrasts and queue-aware models.

Stages that do not occur for a given case (e.g., no manual review or escalation) are treated as structurally absent and excluded from that stage’s mean; they are never coded as zero-duration events.

Flagged cases have dramatically shorter arrival-to-triage-start (≈0.2h vs ≈2.5h) and somewhat shorter triage service, consistent with effective priority triage. However, they almost always receive manual review and frequently escalation, with long manual_queue_wait (≈6.5h vs ≈1.9h for unflagged with manual) and long escalation waits among escalated, yielding roughly double total cycle time. High-complexity and flagged–escalated subgroups drive the longest cycles, while low-complexity unflagged cases rarely enter manual review and finish quickly. These contrasts are consistent with acceleration at triage but delay downstream due to extra review and scarce senior capacity. They are not automatically causal because flagged cases differ in underlying risk and complexity, reviewers may use unrecorded information, and shared queues introduce interference: shortening waits for one subgroup can lengthen waits for another even if their intrinsic processing requirements are identical.

## 4. Direct and mediated pathway analysis

| pathway | candidate interpretation | observable evidence | required assumptions | competing explanation | intervention targeting this pathway | expected downstream side effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| flag → priority_lane → triage timing | Priority lane moves flagged cases to the front, shortening triage waits without changing case mix | Mean arrival-to-triage-start ≈0.2h for flagged vs ≈2.5h for unflagged; triage service also modestly shorter | After conditioning on arrival_load, complexity, risk_score, triage resources behave similarly for flagged/unflagged; little anticipatory batching | Triage staff may also pre-screen or shortcut low-risk unflagged cases when queues are long, partly masking flag effect | Adjust priority rules (e.g., P1 vs P2) or add triage capacity for high-load periods | Faster triage for flagged may increase arrival rate into manual queues, worsening downstream congestion |
| flag → manual-review occurrence | Flagged cases are systematically routed to manual review, increasing depth of scrutiny | 10/10 flagged vs 4/10 unflagged enter manual review; flagged cases more often high complexity | Manual-review routing is governed primarily by flag/risk/complexity, not by unmeasured staff preferences unrelated to risk | Humans might still send some unflagged but suspicious cases to manual, making flag only one of several triggers | P2 (no automatic mandatory manual) or allow threshold-based skipping of manual review for low-risk flagged cases | Fewer manual reviews can shorten cycle time but may reduce error catching or regulatory compliance |
| flag → manual queue | Extra flagged cases saturate the shared manual-review queue, extending waits for all manuals | Manual_queue_wait ≈6.5h for flagged vs ≈1.9h for unflagged with manual; many waits spill overnight | Queue length and service discipline are reasonably stable; load changes are primarily driven by flag-based routing and arrival patterns | Manual_reviews may be scheduled into specific shifts regardless of load, so long waits partly reflect staffing patterns, not just flag volume | P3: add protected manual-review capacity or shifts dedicated to flagged cases | If not carefully sized, protected capacity may displace unflagged cases or leave senior staff idle in low-flag periods |
| flag → escalation | Flagged-plus-manual cases are more likely to be escalated to seniors, adding another scarce stage | Escalation in 6/10 flagged vs 2/10 unflagged; escalated cases show long escalation_queue_wait and longest cycle times | After adjusting for risk_score, complexity, and manual findings, remaining difference in escalation is attributable to flag policy | Underlying_risk and subtle manual-review signals (U_underlying_risk, U_reviewer_pref) may drive both flag and escalation decisions | P4: refine escalation criteria or add senior capacity for specific risk/complexity bands | Relaxed criteria may reduce cycle time but risk under-escalation; stricter criteria or more capacity may pull more cases into senior queue |
| complexity/risk confounding | Higher underlying_risk and complexity both increase probability of flag and of longer, multi-stage processing | Flagged set is enriched for High complexity and very high risk_score; high-complexity unflagged cases also exhibit long cycles | Risk_score and complexity sufficiently summarize relevant pre-treatment risk; residual U_underlying_risk is weak | There may be strong unrecorded drivers (narrative notes, external data) that influence both flag and handling | Stratified or matched analyses within risk_score and complexity bands; within-band randomization where feasible | Even within bands, sparse data and interference may leave residual confounding, limiting causal interpretation |
| shared-queue interference | Because manual and escalation queues are shared, treatment of one case shifts others’ waits | Long overnight waits cluster when many flagged, high-load cases enter; unflagged manual cases on those days also wait long | Queue state variables (arrival_load, queue lengths) capture most cross-case dependence | Hidden operational changes (e.g., temporary reallocation of staff) could explain wait differences instead of pure volume | P3 with explicit capacity guardrails and monitoring of queue lengths for all subgroups | Easing waits for flagged may lengthen waits for unflagged if total capacity is fixed; must monitor spillovers |
| reviewer/resource assignment | Specific reviewers or resources may be preferentially assigned to flagged or complex cases, affecting service times | Some reviewers (e.g., M2) appear more often on high-risk or escalated cases; triage resources T1/T2 handle many flagged | Resource assignment is driven by protocol, not unmeasured case difficulty | Informal hand-offs (e.g., “expert on tricky cases”) may cause correlated risk and service times | Train more reviewers to handle complex flagged cases or rotate assignments to balance load | Rebalancing may smooth waits but could temporarily reduce expertise concentration on the most complex cases |
| decision-quality tradeoffs | Additional manual review and escalation may improve decision quality at the cost of longer cycles | Escalated and high-complexity flagged cases have higher reject rates; unflagged low-complexity cases are almost all approved quickly | Approve/Reject is a reasonable proxy for quality (e.g., rejects correspond to true high risk) and there is no unmeasured reversal | Approvals and rejections could both be error-prone; we lack ex-post truth, so reject rate may mislead | P2 (fewer manuals) or P4 (targeted escalation) tuned using downstream adverse-event or rework data | Tighter quality focus may further lengthen high-risk pathways; lighter-touch policies may improve speed but degrade safety or compliance |

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Does priority triage causally reduce triage completion time, holding risk profile fixed? | Within-risk-score / local-threshold design: compare cases just above vs just below the flagging threshold (or narrow risk_score bands) under otherwise identical conditions | Risk_score (narrow window), complexity, arrival_load, time-of-day/day-of-week | Risk_score threshold is sharp; no other policies change discontinuously at the threshold; local comparability within bands | Check for continuity of baseline covariates and arrival patterns around the threshold; test for pre-policy symmetry | If triage timing shows no discontinuity at the flag threshold after adjustment, I would downweight the role of the priority lane in observed triage-time differences |
| How much of flagged cases’ extra cycle time is due to queue congestion rather than inherent case complexity? | Queue-state-aware analysis using time-varying covariates: regress waiting times on flag status, current queue lengths, and arrival_load, or simulate counterfactual queues under alternative routing | Risk_score, complexity, arrival_load, time-of-day, observed queue length / number in system at each stage | Queue metrics are accurately measured; unobserved shocks do not systematically favour flagged vs unflagged; model correctly captures service discipline | Plot residual waits vs queue length by flag; assess whether flagged status adds little once queue state is controlled | If flagged status remains strongly associated with waiting times even after detailed queue adjustment, I would infer that routing and prioritization rules, not just congestion, drive delays |
| What is the net impact of mandatory manual review on cycle time and quality? | Policy-change or randomized encouragement design: stagger rollout of P2 vs P0 across teams or days, or randomize a subset of flagged medium-risk cases to skip mandatory manual | Baseline risk_score, complexity, arrival_load; team indicators; calendar time | Assignment of policy arm (P0 vs P2) is as good as random within strata; no major spillovers across arms beyond measured queues | Compare cycle_time and decision_outcome across arms; test for pre-intervention trend differences and cross-arm queue contamination | If randomized or quasi-experimental evidence shows little quality loss but large cycle-time reductions when mandatory manual is relaxed, I would favour P2-like policies more strongly |
| Through which mediators (manual occurrence, manual waits, escalation) does the flag bundle lengthen total cycle time? | Mediation-oriented analysis using longitudinal g-computation or structural models with interventional indirect effects for manual_review and escalation | Baseline confounders: risk_score, complexity, arrival_load; time-varying: current queue state, partial outcomes of manual review | No major unmeasured confounders of mediator–outcome relationships given measured history; model correctly captures dynamics; interference approximated via queue covariates | Compare observed vs model-implied mediator distributions; perform sensitivity checks by perturbing mediator models | If plausible mediator models cannot reproduce observed long waits and escalation patterns, I would question any detailed path decomposition and treat estimates as highly speculative |
| Are apparent flag effects actually due to hidden risk? | Negative-control outcome/exposure analysis: use outcomes that should not be affected by flag policy (e.g., very early administrative steps) and exposures unrelated to risk (e.g., arbitrary case IDs) | Same as primary models plus negative-control variables | Negative-control exposure truly does not affect the outcome; negative-control outcome is unaffected by flag | Test whether negative-control exposure predicts outcomes; test whether flag predicts negative-control outcomes | Strong associations in negative-control analyses would suggest residual confounding by unmeasured risk or operational factors, weakening causal claims about the flag |
| How sensitive are conclusions to unobserved underlying risk? | Quantitative sensitivity analysis for unobserved risk (e.g., bias formulas, e-values, or latent-risk models) on key contrasts such as flagged vs unflagged cycle_time within risk_score bands | Observed baseline covariates; hypothetical parameters for U_underlying_risk | Unobserved risk can be parameterized as a scalar factor influencing both selection into flagging and waiting times | Vary plausible strengths of U_underlying_risk and plot resulting adjusted effects | If modest unobserved confounding could fully explain the longer flagged cycle times, I would avoid interpreting any residual differences as causal policy effects |
| How large is shared-queue interference and can we design around it? | Interference-aware design: cluster-randomize policies (e.g., days, shifts, or teams) or use agent-based simulation calibrated to event logs | Cluster identifiers (day/shift/team), queue metrics, baseline case mix | Interference is mostly within, not across, clusters; cluster-level randomization or simulation adequately represents queue mechanics | Compare within-cluster vs between-cluster variation; check for spillover of policy changes across clusters | If strong cross-cluster interference or mis-specified queues are detected, I would interpret any cluster-level policy estimates as descriptive only and rely more on simulation and localized pilots |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P0: keep current system | Very fast triage for flagged via priority lane | Heavy loading of shared manual and escalation queues for flagged; potential spillover delays to unflagged manuals | Long cycles for high-risk flagged, moderate for others | Likely highest review depth and senior oversight for high-risk cases | High risk that flagged load crowds manual/escalation stages, especially at high arrival_load | Moderate: supported by current data but confounded | Maintain as baseline but not clearly optimal; treat as reference arm for pilots |
| P1: suppress display of flag | Triage reverts to generic FIFO/urgency rules; slower triage for high-risk flagged cases | Fewer automatic manuals for flagged, easing manual queues; escalation patterns become more discretionary | Shorter cycles for some high-risk cases, longer for others; overall effect ambiguous | Risk that truly high-risk cases no longer receive timely manual/escalation attention | Could free capacity but also misallocate to lower-risk cases | Low: no direct evidence here; counterfactual only | Not recommended as primary policy; consider only with strong safeguards and monitoring of adverse events |
| P2: priority triage, no automatic mandatory manual review | Maintains fast triage for flagged | Reduces manual volume for flagged, easing manual queues and downstream escalation load | Likely shorter cycles for many flagged cases; more similar to unflagged durations | Increased probability of missed issues if manual review is skipped for some high-risk, especially with imperfect risk_score | Frees capacity for the remaining manual and escalation work; may improve waits for unflagged manuals | Low–moderate: partially informed by current manual vs no-manual contrasts, but strong selection | Consider as an experimental arm for medium-risk flagged cases only, with close monitoring of quality outcomes |
| P3: current routing plus protected manual-review capacity for flagged | Keeps current fast triage | Extra, possibly dedicated manual capacity for flagged reduces manual_queue_wait without eliminating review | Substantial cycle-time reduction for flagged manuals; modest impact on total cycle if escalation remains scarce | Maintains or improves review depth; risk of over-review remains similar to P0 | If capacity is truly additive, spillover on unflagged is neutral or positive; if reallocated, unflagged manuals could worsen | Moderate: queue decomposition strongly suggests manual waiting is a major delay driver | Recommended for a limited pilot focusing on high-risk flagged cases, with explicit capacity and queue guardrails |
| P4: retain manual review but change escalation criteria or capacity | Triage unchanged | Either fewer escalations (tighter criteria) or shorter escalation waits (more senior capacity) | Could shorten cycles sharply for cases currently escalated; minimal effect on non-escalated | Looser escalation may reduce safety; more capacity with current criteria may improve both speed and quality but costs more | Reallocating senior resources could delay other tasks outside this process | Low–moderate: escalation data are sparse and highly selected | Consider as a follow-on experiment after better understanding escalation benefits and risks |

For a limited pilot, I would select **P3**. **Eligibility**: flagged cases with Medium or High complexity arriving during business hours, excluding rare edge-case categories where manual review is legally mandatory. **Randomization or assignment rule**: randomize eligible cases 1:1 to (a) current-routing manual queue vs (b) a protected manual lane with additional reviewer capacity; assign at the case level within strata of risk_score and arrival_load. **Queue-capacity guardrail**: cap the proportion of reviewers devoted to the protected lane and automatically redirect new flagged cases to the standard queue if unflagged manual waits exceed a pre-specified threshold. **Primary outcomes**: manual_queue_wait and total cycle_time for flagged cases. **Secondary outcomes**: decision_outcome proxies (approve/reject patterns, rework or appeal rates), escalation frequency, and waiting times for unflagged manuals. **Minimum process data**: all existing timestamps, risk_score, flag, complexity, arrival_load, resource IDs, and explicit queue length snapshots at manual and escalation. **Stopping conditions**: any statistically or operationally significant degradation in quality proxies, escalation backlogs, or unflagged manual waiting times; or clear evidence that additional capacity is underutilized.

    if pd.notna(row["manual_start"]) and pd.notna(row["manual_done"]):
        events.append(
            {
                "case:concept:name": case_id,
                "concept:name": "Manual review",
                "time:timestamp": row["manual_done"],
                "start_timestamp": row["manual_start"],
                "resource": row["review_resource"],
                "flag": row["flag"],
                "risk_score": row["risk_score"],
                "complexity": row["complexity"],
                "arrival_load": row["arrival_load"],
            }
        )
    # Escalation event
    if pd.notna(row["escalation_start"]) and pd.notna(row["escalation_done"]):
        events.append(
            {
                "case:concept:name": case_id,
                "concept:name": "Escalation",
                "time:timestamp": row["escalation_done"],
                "start_timestamp": row["escalation_start"],
                "resource": "SeniorTeam",
                "flag": row["flag"],
                "risk_score": row["risk_score"],
                "complexity": row["complexity"],
                "arrival_load": row["arrival_load"],
            }
        )
    # Closing event (optional, not used for DFG but for cycle time)
    events.append(
        {
            "case:concept:name": case_id,
            "concept:name": "Close",
            "time:timestamp": row["close"],
            "start_timestamp": row["close"],
            "resource": "System",
            "flag": row["flag"],
            "risk_score": row["risk_score"],
            "complexity": row["complexity"],
            "arrival_load": row["arrival_load"],
        }
    )

events_df = pd.DataFrame(events)

# 3. PM4Py formatting with start timestamp
parameters = {
    pm4py.objects.log.util.xes.DEFAULT_TIMESTAMP_KEY: "time:timestamp"
}
formatted_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events_df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    start_timestamp_key="start_timestamp",
)
log = log_converter.apply(formatted_df)

# 4. Discover DFGs for flagged and unflagged cases
def discover_dfg_for_flag(flag_value):
    sub_df = formatted_df[formatted_df["flag"] == flag_value]
    if sub_df.empty:
        print(f"No events for flag={flag_value}")
        return
    sub_log = log_converter.apply(sub_df)
    dfg_freq = pm4py.discover_dfg(sub_log)
    dfg_perf = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(sub_log)
    print(f"\n=== Frequency DFG for flag={flag_value} ===")
    print(dfg_freq)
    print(f"\n=== Performance DFG (mean seconds) for flag={flag_value} ===")
    print(dfg_perf)

discover_dfg_for_flag(1)
discover_dfg_for_flag(0)

# 5. Case-level durations and stage waiting/service times
def td_hours(delta):
    return delta.total_seconds() / 3600.0

df["cycle_time_h"] = (df["close"] - df["arrival"]).apply(td_hours)
df["triage_wait_h"] = (df["triage_start"] - df["arrival"]).apply(
    lambda x: td_hours(x) if pd.notna(x) else np.nan
)
df["triage_service_h"] = (df["triage_done"] - df["triage_start"]).apply(
    lambda x: td_hours(x) if pd.notna(x) else np.nan
)
df["manual_queue_wait_h"] = (df["manual_start"] - df["triage_done"]).apply(
    lambda x: td_hours(x) if pd.notna(x) else np.nan
)
df["manual_service_h"] = (df["manual_done"] - df["manual_start"]).apply(
    lambda x: td_hours(x) if pd.notna(x) else np.nan
)
df["escalation_queue_wait_h"] = (df["escalation_start"] - df["manual_done"]).apply(
    lambda x: td_hours(x) if pd.notna(x) else np.nan
)
df["escalation_service_h"] = (df["escalation_done"] - df["escalation_start"]).apply(
    lambda x: td_hours(x) if pd.notna(x) else np.nan
)

# Residual time from last completed stage to close
def residual_to_close(row):
    last_time = row["triage_done"]
    if pd.notna(row["manual_done"]):
        last_time = row["manual_done"]
    if pd.notna(row["escalation_done"]):
        last_time = row["escalation_done"]
    return td_hours(row["close"] - last_time)

df["residual_to_close_h"] = df.apply(residual_to_close, axis=1)

# 6. Print stage summaries and support counts
def summarize_by(group_cols, label):
    grouped = df.groupby(group_cols)
    print(f"\n=== Stage summaries by {label} ===")
    for name, g in grouped:
        n_cases = len(g)
        print(f"\nSubgroup {label}={name}, n={n_cases}")
        print(
            g[
                [
                    "cycle_time_h",
                    "triage_wait_h",
                    "triage_service_h",
                    "manual_queue_wait_h",
                    "manual_service_h",
                    "escalation_queue_wait_h",
                    "escalation_service_h",
                    "residual_to_close_h",
                ]
            ].mean()
        )

summarize_by(["flag"], "flag")
summarize_by(["complexity"], "complexity")
summarize_by(["flag", "arrival_load"], "flag&arrival_load")
summarize_by(["flag", (df["escalation_start"].notna())], "flag&escalation_occurrence")

# Small subgroup and causal-interpretation warnings
print("\n=== Warnings ===")
for subgroup, count in df.groupby(["flag", "complexity"]).size().items():
    if count < 5:
        print(f"WARNING: Small subgroup {subgroup} has n={count}; summaries are unstable.")

print(
    "WARNING: DFG performance and waiting-time differences are descriptive only; "
    "they do not identify natural direct or indirect effects without strong, likely violated assumptions."
)

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk severity**: If richer clinical or contextual data showed that flagged cases are dramatically higher-risk than suggested by `risk_score` and `complexity` alone, such that long manual and escalation queues are primarily justified safety measures, I would view long cycle times as appropriate rather than a target for reduction, weakening support for P3.
2. **Queue interference dominance**: If more detailed queue logs revealed that manual and escalation congestion is driven mainly by other processes or work types (outside the flagged pathway), not by the flag-driven routing examined here, I would downgrade the importance of flag-induced manual loading and reconsider whether P3 meaningfully addresses the bottleneck.
3. **Decision-quality benefits**: If a better outcome proxy (e.g., adverse events, rework) showed that mandatory manual review plus escalation under P0 yields substantially fewer serious downstream problems than any lighter-touch alternative, I would prioritize maintaining or even strengthening P0/P4 over P3 despite longer cycle times.
4. **Timestamp semantics errors**: If validation uncovered that some timestamps represent batch updates or logging delays (e.g., `manual_start` recorded long after work actually began), making waiting times appear much longer than true, my queue-based mediation story and the motivation for extra manual capacity would both weaken.
5. **Positivity and overlap failure**: If, in larger data, flagged and unflagged cases scarcely overlap in risk_score, complexity, or arrival_load (e.g., all very high-risk cases are always flagged), identification of contrasts like P0 vs P2 within shared strata would become impossible, forcing a more descriptive, non-causal interpretation.
6. **Reviewer behaviour change**: If introducing protected manual capacity (P3) led reviewers to broaden escalation or manual-review criteria (e.g., “we now escalate more because we have slots”), eroding the assumed link between current queues and risk, the pilot might increase both cycle time and escalation without quality gains, making P3 unattractive and shifting preference toward careful tuning of P4 instead.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed**: Flagged cases receive much faster triage (about 0.2 hours from arrival to triage start versus roughly 2.5 hours for unflagged), but they almost always undergo manual review and are frequently escalated. These extra stages, coupled with long manual and escalation waits—often spanning many hours or overnight—produce roughly double the total cycle time for flagged versus unflagged cases, especially among high-complexity cases.  
**Plausible mechanisms**: The flag functions as part of a policy bundle: priority triage, near-mandatory manual review, and higher escalation propensity of complex, high-risk work into a scarce senior queue. Shared manual and escalation queues, limited capacity, and possibly specialized reviewer assignment create congestion effects that amplify downstream delays even as front-end triage is accelerated.  
**Unidentified aspects**: We cannot cleanly separate the impact of the flag from underlying unmeasured risk, time-varying congestion, or reviewers’ changing beliefs. Natural direct and indirect effects are ill-posed because of exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounding and interference; decision quality is only coarsely captured by approve/reject. Quantitative mediation decompositions are therefore fragile and should be treated as descriptive.  
**Most informative next intervention**: A carefully guarded pilot of P3—preserving priority triage and manual review while adding protected manual capacity for flagged, high-risk cases—offers the best opportunity to reduce long waits, measure queue spillovers, and collect richer outcome data. This design can sharpen understanding of queue-mediated mechanisms while directly testing whether targeted capacity relieves delays without compromising decision quality.
