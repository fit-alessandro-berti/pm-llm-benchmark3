# 1. Objectives and constraints

The policy is a **guardrailed, explainable multi-objective assignment policy**, not a single-score optimizer that blindly maximizes throughput. It makes safety and compliance decisions first, then balances competing operational goals.

## Objectives

For each assignment, balance:

1. **SLA protection**
   - Prioritize urgent and near-deadline work.
   - Avoid assigning work to people unlikely to start or complete it in time.

2. **Quality and correct skill use**
   - Match the activity’s required skill to an appropriately skilled resource.
   - Use specialists where the cost of error is high, but avoid making specialists the default for every case.

3. **Continuity of ownership**
   - Preserve the current owner when that person is competent and the handover cost is high.
   - Reduce unnecessary reassignment, especially after previous handovers.

4. **Throughput and queue flow**
   - Reduce waiting time and avoid queues at scarce skills such as Security and Approval.
   - Use batching only where it is operationally safe.

5. **Fair workload and overtime protection**
   - Spread additional work across qualified people.
   - Avoid adding work to high-overtime-risk resources except when there is a documented SLA or safety reason.

6. **Development and cross-training**
   - Allocate at least 20% of safe normal/low-priority work to documented training priorities.
   - Use supervision rather than unsafe independent trainee assignment.

7. **Resilience**
   - Avoid concentrating all knowledge or all difficult work in one specialist.
   - Build backup capability while protecting urgent cases.

## Hard safety and compliance constraints

These constraints cannot be overridden by ordinary scoring:

- A resource with required skill level `0` is ineligible.
- No resource receives more than four new assignments in this batch.
- High-priority or urgent work must be assigned at skill level `2` or above unless explicitly supervised.
- Security Review:
  - Skill level `3` may work independently.
  - Skill level `1` or `2` may work only under explicit supervision by `R7_Gita` or `R8_Hugo`.
- Final Approval:
  - Only skill level `2` or `3` may finalize.
  - Skill level `1` may prepare the approval, but cannot be named as the final approver.
- High-complexity cases with high handover cost should remain with the current owner where feasible.
  - An exception is allowed when the owner has skill `0` or `1` and suitable supervision or safe reassignment is needed.
- Quality Check batching is allowed only when:
  - priority is normal or low; and
  - SLA time remaining is greater than 24 hours.
- High-overtime-risk resources should not receive additional work unless:
  - the case is urgent or severe SLA risk exists; and
  - no lower-risk qualified alternative can safely take it.
- Supervisory assignments must be recorded explicitly in `assignment_reason`.

## Soft preference trade-offs

These are scored and explained; they are not absolute rules:

- Prefer the current owner when they are competent.
- Prefer higher skill for high-complexity, customer-facing, or rework-prone work.
- Prefer lower workload and greater remaining capacity.
- Prefer development assignments for normal/low-risk work.
- Avoid unnecessary use of scarce specialists.
- Prefer a feasible backup or supervised pairing if it strengthens resilience.
- Prefer batching for low-risk Quality Check work where the batch does not create SLA risk.
- Prefer fewer handovers when reassignment count is already high.

A useful conceptual score is:

\[
Score(r,c) =
SLA(c,r) + Skill(c,r) + Continuity(c,r) + Fairness(r) +
Training(c,r) + Resilience(c,r) - Risk(r,c)
\]

The policy does not need to expose this formula to workers as a rigid “ranking”; it is an auditable decision structure.

# 2. Assignment policy

## Decision sequence

1. **Classify case risk**
   - Determine urgency from priority, SLA time remaining, complexity, rework/reassignment history, and activity risk.
   - Mark cases as:
     - `critical`: urgent and less than 4 hours remaining, or severe safety/compliance activity;
     - `high risk`: high priority, less than 8 hours remaining, high complexity, or repeated rework;
     - `standard`: normal priority with adequate SLA room;
     - `development-safe`: normal/low priority, sufficient SLA buffer, lower complexity, and no critical compliance restriction.

2. **Apply hard eligibility filters**
   - Exclude skill level `0`.
   - Apply Security and Final Approval rules.
   - Exclude high-overtime-risk resources unless the exception criteria are met.
   - Exclude resources already at four new assignments.
   - For urgent/high-priority work, exclude skill level `1` unless a valid supervision pair is available.

3. **Calculate SLA urgency score**
   - Higher score for:
     - urgent priority;
     - less remaining SLA time;
     - high complexity;
     - customer-facing work;
     - prior rework/reassignment;
     - activities historically associated with long service or waiting time.
   - Example classification:
     - `Critical`: urgent and <4h;
     - `High`: urgent <8h, high priority <10h, or high complexity >0.75;
     - `Moderate`: normal/high with 10–24h;
     - `Low`: ample SLA buffer.

4. **Calculate skill-match score**
   - Skill level 3: strongest independent-fit score.
   - Skill level 2: competent-fit score.
   - Skill level 1:
     - development score only where allowed;
     - requires supervision for high-priority work;
     - cannot finalize Approval;
     - cannot independently perform Security Review.
   - Penalize cases where historical data shows a poor skill match leads to rework or excessive service time.

5. **Calculate continuity and handover score**
   - Add a strong bonus if the current owner has adequate skill and capacity.
   - Add a stronger bonus when:
     - handover cost is high;
     - complexity is high;
     - reassignment count is already greater than two.
   - Apply a strong penalty to changing owner for high-complexity/high-handover cases.
   - Override continuity when the current owner is unsafe or clearly underqualified and supervision is not viable.

6. **Calculate workload and overtime fairness score**
   - Prefer resources with lower open-case load and sufficient working hours.
   - Penalize high overtime risk.
   - Penalize assigning several new items to the same person when qualified alternatives exist.
   - Use relative workload by team and role, not only raw case count, because Security and Approval cases can differ substantially in effort.

7. **Calculate development score**
   - For normal/low-priority cases with adequate SLA buffer:
     - reward assignment to a resource whose declared training priority matches the activity;
     - require appropriate supervision or review if the recipient is a trainee;
     - document the learning objective.
   - Ensure at least 20% of normal/low-priority assignments are genuine development opportunities if safe.

8. **Calculate resilience/bottleneck score**
   - Penalize repeatedly assigning every Security case to the same specialist if another qualified specialist exists.
   - Preserve capacity for imminent urgent Security and Approval work.
   - Prefer supervised work that builds backup capability only when it does not increase material SLA or quality risk.
   - Monitor whether specialist queues, rather than general workload, are becoming the bottleneck.

9. **Tie-breaking**
   In order:
   1. lower SLA risk;
   2. stronger skill fit;
   3. continuity;
   4. lower overtime exposure;
   5. lower relative workload;
   6. documented training benefit;
   7. resilience benefit;
   8. deterministic alphabetical/resource-ID tie-break for auditability.

10. **Exception handling**
   - If no eligible resource exists:
     - escalate to the shift lead;
     - create an explicit `assignment_reason` such as `SLA_EXCEPTION`, `SUPERVISED_SECURITY_EXCEPTION`, or `CAPACITY_ESCALATION`;
     - capture supervisor, decision owner, and expected review time.
   - The system must not silently assign an ineligible resource just to clear the queue.
   - Workers may raise factual corrections: inaccurate workload, unrecorded absence, skill matrix error, case context not in the log, or a known relationship/continuity consideration.

## Pseudocode

```text
for each case in queue ordered by:
    safety/compliance criticality,
    SLA urgency,
    priority,
    complexity,
    reassignment_count descending:

    risk = classify_case_risk(case)

    eligible = []
    for resource in resources:
        if resource.new_assignments >= 4:
            continue
        if skill(resource, case.activity) == 0:
            continue
        if case.activity == "Security Review":
            if skill(resource, "Security") == 3:
                eligible.add(independent(resource))
            elif skill(resource, "Security") in [1, 2] and supervisor_available(R7 or R8):
                eligible.add(supervised(resource, supervisor))
            continue
        if case.activity == "Final Approval" and skill(resource, "Approval") < 2:
            continue
        if case.priority in ["urgent", "high"] and skill(resource, case.activity) < 2:
            if not valid_supervision(resource, case):
                continue
        if resource.overtime_risk == "high" and not urgent_exception(case, eligible):
            continue
        eligible.add(resource)

    if eligible is empty:
        escalate(case)
        continue

    for candidate in eligible:
        candidate.score =
            sla_urgency_score(case, candidate) +
            skill_match_score(case, candidate) +
            continuity_score(case, candidate) +
            fairness_score(candidate) +
            training_score(case, candidate) +
            resilience_score(case, candidate) -
            handover_penalty(case, candidate) -
            overtime_penalty(candidate)

    chosen = select_highest_score_with_tiebreaks(eligible)

    if chosen changes owner on high-complexity/high-handover case:
        require documented reason and lead confirmation

    record:
        assigned_resource
        supervisor if applicable
        assignment_reason
        policy_score_components
        exception flag
```

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

| Dimension | First-available routing | Specialist-only routing | Proposed explainable dynamic policy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Expected throughput | Often improves immediate queue movement because low-load people receive work quickly. Can later lose throughput through rework, handovers, and poor skill fit. | Can produce high first-pass quality for complex specialist work, but may create queues behind scarce specialists. | Seeks stable throughput: urgent work moves quickly, while continuity, batching, and selective specialist use reduce downstream rework and waiting. |
| SLA risk | May reduce average waiting time, but can miss SLAs when a low-skilled assignee takes longer or requires reassignment. | Protects SLA for cases that reach specialists quickly, but Security and Approval queues can create substantial waiting. | Uses explicit SLA urgency and estimated capability; escalates genuine urgent exceptions while avoiding avoidable specialist queues. |
| Error/rework risk | Higher risk because it ignores specialization except skill level 0. Skill level 1 may be used too freely without safeguards. | Usually low immediate error risk, but can create handovers and specialist overload. It may also discourage capable level-2 staff from building experience. | Uses high skill for high-risk work, preserves ownership when appropriate, and applies supervised development only where safe. |
| Fairness of workload | Can appear fair based on current open-case counts, but may unfairly give difficult work to lower-skilled workers and create hidden workload. | Often unfairly concentrates work in the most skilled people, especially Security and Approval specialists. | Uses workload, hours, overtime risk, assignment caps, and role scarcity. Fairness is measured by burden and risk, not merely case count. |
| Skill development | Incidental and poorly controlled. People may get learning opportunities, but without supervision or documentation. | Weak development: specialists retain almost all complex work, limiting growth and resilience. | Planned and documented development assignments, with supervision and SLA safeguards. |
| Continuity of ownership | Weak: cases may move whenever another person is available. This can increase reassignments and waiting. | Mixed: continuity is retained only when the owner is also the highest-skilled option. | Strong but not absolute: continuity is favored especially for high-complexity, high-handover, or repeatedly reassigned work. |
| Resilience to absence or bottlenecks | May distribute work broadly, but not necessarily build real capability. It can create unsafe dependence on underqualified people. | Low resilience: specialist absence can stop the flow, and queues accumulate around a small number of experts. | Builds controlled backup capability while protecting critical work. Monitors specialist queues and avoids unnecessary concentration. |
| Explainability | Simple but incomplete: “the person with the fewest open cases got it.” This can be perceived as arbitrary or unsafe. | Simple but potentially demoralizing: “the expert always gets it.” | Explains decisions in human terms: urgency, capability, continuity, workload protection, development, and documented exceptions. |

## Overall trade-off

- **First-available routing** is attractive when immediate waiting time is the only concern, but it is likely to increase mismatch, reassignments, and later rework.
- **Specialist-only routing** is attractive where error prevention dominates, but it risks making Security and Approval permanent bottlenecks and limiting development.
- The proposed policy accepts that some urgent work must go to specialists and some continuity must be preserved even when workload is uneven. It compensates by using supervision, assignment caps, workload monitoring, and development-safe assignments.

There is no universal optimum. The appropriate balance should be reviewed using observed SLA, quality, workload, and worker-experience outcomes.

# 5. Explanation to workers

Assignments are made to support good service, safe decisions, manageable workloads, and opportunities to develop skills.

The policy does not assume that the fastest assignment is always the best assignment. For example:

- Urgent work is prioritized when a customer or service deadline is close.
- Security and final approval work go to people who are authorized to make those decisions.
- Where a case already has an owner who understands its history, we usually keep that ownership—especially if the case is complex or has already changed hands several times.
- We try not to add work to people already at high risk of overtime when another safe option exists.
- Some normal or low-risk work is deliberately assigned as supervised development so that skills are not concentrated in only a few people.
- A specialist may be asked to review or supervise rather than take over the whole case. This protects quality while helping colleagues build capability.

The assignment reason should be visible in the work system. Examples include:

- “Urgent SLA and specialist authorization.”
- “Current owner retained because handover risk is high.”
- “Supervised development assignment; specialist retains final accountability.”
- “Workload balancing after checking skill and SLA.”
- “Exception approved because no lower-risk qualified option was available.”

Workers can challenge an assignment when important information is missing or wrong, such as an unavailable colleague, incorrect skill record, a case relationship that is not visible in the log, or an unrecorded workload constraint. The policy supports professional judgment; it does not replace it.

Performance should not be judged solely by how many cases someone receives or closes. The organization should also recognize quality, careful handovers, mentoring, prevention of rework, and appropriate escalation.

# 6. Process-mining audit indicators

The policy should be audited at case, resource, team, activity, and policy-version level. Indicators should be reviewed by priority class, complexity band, and activity, because averages can hide unfair or unsafe behavior.

| Indicator | Definition | Why it matters | Suggested breakdown |
|---|---|---|---|
| Handover frequency | Number of distinct resources per case minus one, or owner changes per case. | Detects excessive fragmentation of ownership. | Activity, priority, complexity, handover-cost band, team. |
| Reassignment count | Recorded `reassignment_count` at assignment and final count at closure. | Historical evidence links more than two reassignments to higher rework and appeal risk. | Cases with 0, 1–2, and >2 reassignments. |
| Skill mismatch rate | Share of assignments where assigned skill is below required level, including supervised exceptions. | Detects unsafe routing and identifies training needs. | By required skill, priority, supervisor, and outcome. |
| Supervision compliance | Share of trainee or sub-threshold assignments with documented supervisor and supervisor event/review. | Ensures “supervision” is real rather than only a label. | Security, Approval preparation, high-priority work. |
| Workload distribution | Distribution of open cases, new assignments, estimated work hours, and complexity-weighted workload by resource/team. | Case counts alone can hide uneven difficult work. | Gini coefficient, percentile spread, team and role. |
| Overtime risk exposure | Number and share of assignments given to high-overtime-risk resources, especially non-urgent work. | Tests whether the policy protects sustainable work. | Urgent versus non-urgent; exception reasons. |
| SLA misses | Percentage of cases completed after `sla_deadline`; also waiting-time and service-time contribution to misses. | Measures whether routing decisions protect customer commitments. | Activity, priority, assignee skill level, assignment reason. |
| SLA near-miss rate | Cases completed within a small margin before deadline, such as <10% remaining SLA buffer. | Detects fragile performance even if formal SLAs are met. | Specialist activities, urgent work, owner changes. |
| Rework after assignment | Rate of later `Rework Correction`, loopbacks, repeated diagnosis, or re-open events after an assignment. | Measures downstream quality, not just first completion time. | Skill level, continuity choice, supervised versus independent. |
| Appeal after assignment | Appeal or complaint rate following decision activities, particularly Approval, Eligibility, and Security. | Tests whether assignment quality affects customer outcomes. | Reassignment band, handover band, skill match. |
| Training assignment share | Share of normal/low-priority assignments aligned with documented training priorities. | Tests the 20% development objective. | Resource, activity, supervision status, SLA outcome. |
| Training outcome quality | Trainee/supervised assignment completion quality, rework rate, SLA performance, and later independent competency. | Prevents training assignments from becoming hidden performance risk. | Trainee, supervisor, activity, time period. |
| Specialist bottleneck utilization | Queue length, waiting time, assignment concentration, and utilization for Security and Approval specialists. | Identifies overdependence on scarce experts. | R7/R8 for Security; R6 and other approval-capable resources. |
| Assignment concentration | Share of an activity performed by top one or two resources. | Measures resilience and single-point-of-failure risk. | Security, Approval, Diagnosis, Quality. |
| Continuity retention rate | Share of cases kept with current owner when owner was qualified. | Tests whether the policy avoids avoidable handovers. | Complexity, handover-cost band, reassignment count. |
| Continuity exception rate | Share of high-complexity/high-handover cases reassigned, with documented reason. | Ensures exceptions are rare, justified, and reviewable. | Skill deficit, capacity emergency, SLA emergency. |
| Waiting-time impact of handover | Median waiting time before/after owner change versus retained-owner cases. | Verifies the reported 2.7-hour high-complexity handover effect locally. | Complexity and handover-cost bands. |
| Policy deviation rate | Assignments that differ from recommended eligible candidates or violate a soft rule. | Human overrides can be appropriate, but should be visible. | Override reason, approver, outcome. |
| Hard-rule violation rate | Security, Approval, supervision, assignment-cap, and high-priority skill violations. | Safety/compliance metric; target should be zero except approved data corrections. | Rule type, team, policy version. |
| Worker-experience signals | Survey/feedback on fairness, explainability, workload, learning value, and ability to challenge decisions. | Process logs do not capture all social and operational realities. | Team, role, tenure, policy period. |

## Audit interpretation

The policy should not be judged solely by lower average waiting time. A favorable deployment should show a balanced pattern such as:

- SLA misses do not increase;
- rework and appeals decline or remain stable;
- high-risk cases have fewer unnecessary handovers;
- high-overtime-risk staff receive fewer non-urgent assignments;
- training share reaches the target without increasing trainee-related quality failures;
- Security and Approval queues do not become increasingly concentrated;
- documented overrides reveal legitimate operational information rather than systematic bias.

Use pre/post comparisons and, where possible, compare similar cases by activity, priority, complexity, and SLA band. Avoid interpreting a raw outcome difference as proof that the policy caused it; case mix and staffing conditions matter.

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

The following sketch shows how to prepare the log, calculate indicators, discover organizational patterns, and audit routing decisions. It is illustrative: production implementation also needs validated skill-matrix data, availability data, role authorization, supervisor records, privacy controls, and human review.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pm4py

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Load and format the event log
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

df = pd.read_csv("service_event_log.csv")

# Expected columns include:
# case:concept:name, concept:name, time:timestamp, org:resource,
# required_skill, case_priority, sla_deadline, case_complexity,
# current_owner, team, assignment_reason, reassignment_count,
# workload_snapshot, handover_cost_band

df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)
df["sla_deadline"] = pd.to_datetime(df["sla_deadline"], utc=True)

# PM4Py dataframe formatting. Depending on PM4Py version, timestamp
# parameters may be supplied explicitly.
log_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp"
)

# Ensure chronological ordering for case-level calculations.
log_df = log_df.sort_values(
    ["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]
).reset_index(drop=True)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Resource-network and organizational discovery
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

# Handover-of-work network:
# identifies how frequently work moves from one resource to another.
handover_sna = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log_df)

# Working-together network:
# identifies resources frequently involved in the same cases.
working_together_sna = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(log_df)

# Activity-based resource similarity:
# helps identify people with similar operational capability,
# useful for backup candidates and resilience analysis.
resource_similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(log_df)

# Organizational roles:
# can validate or complement the maintained skill matrix.
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log_df)

# Visualize or save the SNA depending on deployment environment.
# pm4py.view_sna(handover_sna)
# pm4py.save_vis_sna(handover_sna, "handover_network.html")

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Performance and flow analysis
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

# Service time by case/activity/resource can help estimate
# whether a candidate can reasonably meet the SLA.
service_time = pm4py.get_service_time(log_df)

# Add case service and waiting time where supported by installed version.
# This is useful for separating delay caused by queues/handovers
# from active work duration.
timed_df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
    log_df,
    case_id_column="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_column="time:timestamp"
)

# Performance DFG: highlights activity transitions with high duration.
performance_dfg, start_acts, end_acts = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(
    timed_df
)

# Variant analysis: inspect repeated diagnosis/rework loops.
variants = pm4py.get_variants(log_df)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Useful filters for audit slices
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

# Example: filter Security Review events.
security_events = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log_df,
    "concept:name",
    ["Security Review"],
    level="event",
    retain=True
)

# Example: filter urgent cases.
urgent_cases = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log_df,
    "case_priority",
    ["urgent"],
    level="case",
    retain=True
)

# Example: inspect long-running or high-risk cases.
# Parameter naming can differ by PM4Py version; this is conceptual.
slow_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
    log_df,
    min_performance=24 * 60 * 60,
    max_performance=10**18
)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Case-level handover and reassignment measures
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

def calculate_case_handover_metrics(events: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    events = events.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).copy()

    # Resource changes between consecutive events.
    events["previous_resource"] = (
        events.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"].shift(1)
    )

    events["is_handover"] = (
        events["org:resource"].notna()
        & events["previous_resource"].notna()
        & (events["org:resource"] != events["previous_resource"])
    )

    summary = events.groupby("case:concept:name").agg(
        distinct_resources=("org:resource", "nunique"),
        observed_handovers=("is_handover", "sum"),
        max_logged_reassignment_count=("reassignment_count", "max"),
        case_priority=("case_priority", "first"),
        complexity=("case_complexity", "first"),
        handover_cost_band=("handover_cost_band", "first")
    ).reset_index()

    return summary

handover_metrics = calculate_case_handover_metrics(log_df)

# Compare high-reassignment cases with rework or appeal outcomes.
```

## Implementation notes

- `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network` supports analysis of actual handover patterns and whether the policy is reducing unnecessary ownership changes.
- `pm4py.discover_working_together_network` can reveal effective supervisory or collaborative relationships, but should not be used as proof that a particular pairing is always appropriate.
- `pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` and `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` can identify potential backups and validate gaps in the formal skill matrix.
- `pm4py.get_service_time` and `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time` help distinguish active work time from time spent waiting for assignment, handover, or specialist availability.
- `pm4py.discover_performance_dfg` can identify slow transitions, such as Diagnosis-to-Rework or Security-to-Approval, where assignment choices may create delay.
- `pm4py.get_variants` is useful for identifying repeated diagnosis or rework loops, such as cases similar to C003 and C007.
- `pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values` and `pm4py.filter_case_performance` support focused audits of urgent cases, Security work, high-complexity cases, long-running cases, and cases with repeated reassignment.
- `pm4py.view_sna` or `pm4py.save_vis_sna` can make organizational-network results understandable to operational leads, provided the visualization is used for process improvement rather than punitive individual surveillance.

The audit should be governed jointly by operations, quality/compliance, worker representatives, and process owners. Logs can reveal patterns, but they do not capture all legitimate reasons for reassignment, workload strain, customer context, or informal mentoring; those need structured feedback and review.
