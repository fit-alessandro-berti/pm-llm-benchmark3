## 1. Stress scenarios

### Evidence baseline used to build the scenarios

Converting the two logs to weekly rates changes the picture given by the averages. "Implied load" below is normal utilization multiplied by the growth in weekly activity; it assumes unchanged capacity and service time, so it is an estimate, not a measurement.

| Signal (per week) | Normal | Peak | Change | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| All orders | 2,300 | 3,300 | x1.43 | Volume shock is moderate |
| Routine orders | 1,403 | 1,386 | x0.99 | Routine volume is flat, yet its p90 goes from 68h to 120h: pure crowding-out |
| Urgent orders | 414 | 957 | x2.3 | The surge is Urgent, Complex-panel and Repeat only |
| Complex-panel orders | 299 | 660 | x2.2 | |
| Assay runs (incl. re-runs) | 2,283 | 3,417 | x1.50 | Implied load about 106% (reported 98%) |
| Pre-analytical checks | 2,265 | 3,243 | x1.43 | Implied load about 106% (reported 96%) |
| Technical validations | 1,986 | 2,413-2,693 | x1.2-1.36 | Implied load 92-103% (reported 101%) |
| Medical validations | 530 | 1,367 | x2.6 | Implied load about 150% (reported 93%) |
| Holds for specialist review | 36 | 203 | x5.6 | Implied load about 240% (reported 87%) |
| Accession corrections | 63 | 140 | x2.2 | Implied load about 139% (reported 91%), only 2 specialists |

Other facts that drive the design:

- **Rates deteriorate, not only volumes.** Recollection per checked sample goes from 4.6% to 9.1%, repeat assays per assay exit from 6.7% to 14.8%, and Batch wait per preparation from 4.4% to 12.7%. Holds grow x5.6 while Complex-panel volume grows x2.2, so hold probability per case rises under pressure.
- **Reported utilization hides overload.** It saturates near 100%, and the peak window ends with 13.3% of orders neither released nor cancelled (10.7% in normal). The worst implied overloads are in the small, mix-driven pools (specialist review, medical validation, accession correction), not at the machines.
- **Urgent compliance is already marginal.** Normal p90 is 19h against an 18h limit. With a peak median of 17h and p90 of 33h, I estimate that 40-45% of peak Urgent orders breach.
- **Single loops and queues exceed the Urgent budget.** A recollection takes 42h (normal) or 60h (peak), and 35-42% of recollections end in cancellation. A peak repeat adds 8h + 26h. A peak Batch wait adds 14h + 22h. The peak medical-validation queue is 22h.
- **Data caveats.** The DFG is not flow-conserving: Assay run has more incoming than outgoing arcs, and peak Technical validation has more outgoing than incoming. The log is flattened by order, so tube-level splits are invisible. The peak covers one 3-week wave, and medians hide tails. Complex-panel has no stated target; I assume an internal p90 target of about 120h, its normal level.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

**S1 - Mix shift.** The chain runs front to back:

1. Arrival waves hit courier intake (7.5h) and the pre-analytical check (9.5h). Defects are found late, so recollection doubles and clerks spend time on physician calls. Registration overtime beyond 9h raises errors, which feeds accession correction (14h queue, 26% of corrections now end in recollection).
2. Preparation (11h) batches more, and assay (18h) carries 10.5% of its runs as re-runs (5.4% in normal).
3. Technical validation (101%) feeds medical validation (24h edge) and holds (48h edge).

Relief migrates as follows:

- Strict Urgent-first at preparation and assay (I1) delivers Urgent cases sooner into a 16h technical queue and a 22h medical queue. Release times barely move, while Routine and Complex-panel work-in-process grows.
- More assay throughput through larger batches (I2) lands on technical validators who are already at 101%, in lumps of 24.
- Relieving technical validation lets Routine exit directly (50-74% of outputs go straight to release), but Urgent and Complex-panel then reach medical validation faster, where implied load is about 150%.
- Relieving specialist review releases held cases into medical validation in bursts.

For Urgent, five consecutive stations each have a peak median queue of at least half the 18h budget. No single-station fix restores compliance.

**S2 - Assay degradation.**

- *M1 down.* All work goes to M2, which is faster but fails more. Repeats rise, and each repeat consumes a second run, so effective capacity falls further. Preparation at 89% keeps producing, so prepared samples age in front of the machine. The peak log shows 480 more exits from Test preparation than entries, which may indicate re-preparation.
- *Recovery.* After repair the flush hits technical validation, then medical validation. Recovery speed is set by validators, not by the machine.
- *M2 drift.* This is a slow version of the same loop: repeat rate rises, Repeat -> Assay grows from 10h to 26h, and Repeat-after-failure cases reach a 145h median.
- *Interaction with I3.* Under strict dedication, an M2 outage leaves Routine with no machine, and an M1 outage puts Urgent and Complex-panel on the failure-prone machine.

**S3 - Validator or specialist absence.**

- The lab keeps producing, and finished analytical work piles up between technical and medical validation. This is the most expensive place to hold work-in-process, and Urgent penalties accrue despite healthy machines.
- A Friday specialist absence turns a 36h median hold into a weekend-long one. The Monday release of holds then collides with the Monday 10:00 intake wave at medical validation.
- Speeding assay (I1, I3) is useless here. Outsourcing (I10) moves load to courier intake (88%) and adds a result re-import step that ends at medical validation.
- The useful move is upstream: favour work that does not need the missing resource, which is Routine with direct release.

**S4 - Courier compression.**

- A merged wave overloads intake and then the pre-analytical check. Urgent orders lose 8h or more in Register -> Receive, then 9h before the check.
- Aged samples raise rejections, which raises recollections (60h, 42% cancelled), clerk phone time, and accession corrections.
- The wave then travels as one block: large preparation batches, night assay runs with clustered repeats, a morning technical-validation wave, then medical validation.
- Staffing the intake spike moves the block to the night shift, where failure rates are worst.

**S5 - Routine-heavy backlog.** The volume-driven stations bind (pre-analytical, preparation, assay, technical validation), while medical validation and specialist review sit idle. Urgent reservations and M1 affinity designed for S1 would strand capacity. Larger batches help here, but technical validation becomes the next constraint.

**S6 - Compound.** Flexible people are claimed twice (accession and validation), M1 affinity concentrates load on the one healthy machine, and the specialist queue has no recovery day. This scenario needs explicit load shedding, not optimization.

## 3. Robust redesign package

These elements are always on. They are chosen because they reduce load created by failure (recollections, repeats, fragmentation, late defect discovery) or control flow. They do not add capacity at one station and pass the queue downstream.

**R1. Slack-based, class-aware flow control (replaces I1).**

- Dispatch at preparation, assay, technical and medical validation by remaining slack (due time minus expected remaining time), not by class label.
- Urgent gets stage budgets that sum to about 15h: transit 5h, intake and check 1.5h, preparation 1.5h, assay 3.5h, technical validation 1.5h, medical validation 1.5h, release 0.5h.
- Routine and Complex-panel get guaranteed minimum shares of assay and validation capacity, plus aging promotion (for example Routine older than 48h, Complex-panel older than 96h).
- Preparation is released against assay queue-hours, so preparation never builds more than about 8h of machine work.
- The priority order under overload is explicit: Urgent 18h first, then aged Routine and Complex-panel, then the rest. Routine may slide to a declared ceiling that is communicated to clients, instead of degrading silently.
- *Why robust:* priority follows the order through every station and adapts to any mix (S1, S5).
- *Assumptions:* trustworthy due dates and remaining-time estimates per class, and a LIMS that can sequence by slack.
- *Where it fails:* when total load exceeds capacity (S6) it only chooses who is late. It moves waiting upstream to the pre-analytical buffer, so sample stability must allow that.

**R2. Fixed-cadence Urgent runs plus time-capped batching (modified I2).**

- Replace ad hoc Batch-wait bypasses with scheduled Urgent runs, for example every 2-3h. They depart on time and are topped up with the lowest-slack other samples.
- Routine batches close at 24 samples or when the oldest sample has waited about 6h, whichever comes first.
- *Why robust:* it removes the Urgent Batch-wait path. The bypass rate falls from 74% to 58% exactly when it is needed. Fragmentation becomes a predictable schedule.
- *Assumptions:* assay chemistry allows mixed-priority fill, and run length is compatible with the cadence.
- *Where it fails:* part-filled runs waste capacity when Urgent volume is low (S5), so unused slots must be released automatically. Larger batches make a failed run costlier and deliver results to technical validation in lumps of 24, so batch 24 is allowed only on a machine with a healthy repeat rate.

**R3. Soft machine affinity and repeat containment (modified I3).**

- M1 is preferred for Urgent, Complex-panel and every re-run. M2 is preferred for Routine. Overflow is allowed both ways, with an M2 load cap.
- QC is run at shift handover, and night-shift use of M2 is restricted.
- Re-runs keep their original due date instead of rejoining at the back.
- *Why robust:* it keeps pooling when a machine is lost (S2) and attacks the largest recoverable capacity. Holding repeats at the normal ratio would return about 5% of peak assay runs, which is roughly the size of the overload.
- *Assumptions:* repeats are driven by machine and shift, not by sample quality. This needs decision mining on machine, shift, batch size, operator and sample age.
- *Where it fails:* M1 becomes the critical single point and wears faster. Fewer repeats mean more first-pass results reaching technical validation.

**R4. Front-end quality gate and arrival shaping (I6, I7, I8).**

- A visual triage within 2h of receipt checks label, volume, tube type and visible integrity. It has three outcomes: pass, obvious reject, or fast-track to the full check.
- Orders with complete digital metadata are registered automatically, with validation at order entry.
- Recollection notices are sent digitally instead of by clerk phone call. Defect rates are fed back per collection site.
- Courier slots are staggered, and digital orders pre-advise incoming Urgent samples.
- *Why robust:* it shortens the most expensive loop in every scenario and removes defective samples from the full check, whose implied load is about 106%.
- *Assumptions:* defects are detectable at intake, a meaningful share of orders has complete metadata, and courier contracts and clinic collection rounds can move.
- *Where it fails:* triage under time pressure may reject good samples, and each false reject costs 42-60h. Automated registration removes a human sanity check. Time saved at registration (2.8h) becomes queue at a 96% pre-analytical check unless R1 meters the release. Earlier, more frequent deliveries can shift load into the night shift.

**R5. Validation-end demand reduction and pre-trained flexibility (modified I4).**

- Rule-based auto-verification for unflagged Routine results, which are 50-74% of technical-validation output and go straight to release.
- Tiered medical validation: protocol-based sign-off for unflagged Urgent results, full review for flagged and Complex-panel results.
- Explicit hold criteria, plus a rule to clear holds on the Thursday before each specialist absence.
- Cross-training is completed before the season, because the 4-week lead time exceeds a 3-week peak. Accession backup comes from clerks freed by automated registration for simple corrections. Technical validators are used for accession only in normal periods (76% utilization), never in peak (101%).
- *Why robust:* it shrinks the two worst implied overloads without assuming that hiring is possible.
- *Assumptions:* accreditation and the medical director accept the rules, and held cases are partly pressure-driven escalations.
- *Where it fails:* faster technical validation pushes Urgent and Complex-panel cases into medical validation sooner, so it must be paired with tiering or the evening shift. Tighter hold criteria carry clinical risk.

**Rejected or limited candidates.**

- I1 and I3 are rejected in their strict form, for the starvation and pooling reasons above.
- I9 is rejected as a default. Release is not a constraint (2h/5h), and normal Routine p90 (68h) is within 4h of target. It is acceptable only as short release batching, every 2 hours or less, for cases with more than 24h of slack.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

Triggers use a higher on-threshold than off-threshold, and off-conditions must hold for several days, so policies do not flip daily. Thresholds are starting values set between the normal and peak figures and should be recalibrated.

| Policy | Activate when | Actions | Deactivate when |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 Surge validation (I5) | Urgent plus Complex-panel arrivals above 1.5x baseline (about 150/day against about 100) for 3 days, or medical-validation queue above 6h for 2 days | Evening medical-validation shift aligned with the 17:00 wave; widen protocol sign-off; shorten the Urgent run cadence | Arrivals below 1.25x and queue below 3h for 5 days |
| P2 M2 containment | M2 rolling 24h repeat rate above 10% or above 2x M1 | M2 limited to re-testable Routine samples; batch size back to 12; extra QC; no M2 night runs | M2 repeat rate below 7% for 48h after maintenance |
| P3 Assay outage | A machine is down for more than 2h, or assay queue-hours exceed 12h | Throttle preparation; Urgent cadence on the surviving machine; batch 24 for Routine only if that machine's repeat rate is healthy; send new Complex-panel orders to the partner lab, since they use several assays each; announce the Routine ceiling | Both machines up and assay queue below 6h for 24h; ramp the flush at the technical-validation rate |
| P4 Specialist or validator absence | Planned absence within 24h, or oldest hold above 24h, or medical queue above 12h | Clear holds beforehand; remote review of data by the partner's specialist where permitted; upstream favours Routine with direct release beyond the Urgent lane; cap Complex-panel starts | Oldest hold below 12h and medical queue below 4h for 2 days |
| P5 Courier compression | More than 45% of daily samples arrive in one 2h window, or Register -> Receive median above 8h, or a route is delayed by more than 2h | Pull pre-advised Urgent samples at the dock; move intake and triage staff to the wave; hold Routine in cold storage and release by slack; on-demand Urgent courier | Concentration below 35% and transit below 6h for 3 days |
| P6 Complex-panel outsourcing (I10) | Specialist queue above 24h and forecast in-house completion exceeds 12h transport plus partner turnaround plus a 12h margin | Decide at accession, before preparation; whole orders only, never split tubes; panel types with high historical hold rates; capped daily volume | Specialist queue below 12h for 3 days |
| P7 Routine backlog (S5) | Urgent share below 20% and more than 15% of Routine work-in-process is older than 48h | Release unused Urgent slots; batch 24 on both machines; relax M1 affinity; flex staff to technical validation | Aged Routine share below 5% |

In the compound scenario (S6), policies stack in the order P3, P1, P4, P6. If assay queue-hours stay above 12h after 48h, the lab declares load shedding: an extended Routine ceiling and maximum outsourcing.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

**Not solved by the redesign**

- **True overload.** Implied assay load is about 106% and specialist-review load about 240% in the observed peak. An M1 outage during a wave (S6) cannot be absorbed. The redesign only decides who is late.
- **Specialist review.** It remains a single point of failure. Demand reduction depends on clinical acceptance of tighter hold criteria.
- **Urgent labelling.** If Routine visibly slides, clinicians may order more as Urgent. The 29% peak share may already include some of this.
- **Evidence limits.** The findings rest on one 3-week wave, medians, an order-flattened log and a DFG that does not balance. Tube-level behaviour and a second wave may differ.
- **Recovery.** After any outage, recovery is limited by validators. A second disruption during the flush is poorly covered.

**Created by the redesign**

- **Rule complexity.** Slack dispatch, cadence runs, affinity and seven triggers invite manual overrides and trigger thrash. Conformance to the rules must itself be mined.
- **Reservation waste and priority inflation.** Urgent slots strand capacity if the forecast is wrong. Aging promotion can make nearly everything high priority late in a wave.
- **M1 concentration.** Soft affinity makes M1 the critical asset and increases its wear.
- **Front-end errors.** Triage under a 2h limit may reject good samples. Automated registration can pass errors that a clerk would have caught.
- **Clinical risk.** Auto-verification and protocol sign-off rules can be wrong and need audit sampling.
- **Double-booked flexibility.** In compound scenarios the same cross-trained people are claimed by accession and validation.
- **Outsourcing.** Chain of custody is harder with multi-tube orders, and the lab depends on a partner that may be overloaded in the same wave.
- **Evening shift.** If the same validators cover it, fatigue raises error risk and daytime capacity suffers.

**Lagging outcome metrics (weekly, by case type, never pooled)**

- Urgent released within 18h, plus p90 and penalty count. References: normal p90 19h, peak 33h.
- Routine p90 against 72h (normal 68h, peak 120h) and against the declared peak ceiling.
- Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure p90 (normal 118h and 132h, peak 210h and 240h).
- Rework rate (7.5% / 11.8%), recollection rate (4.6% / 9.1%) and share of recollections cancelled (35% / 42%).
- Share of cases with any queue above 12h (22% / 54%), and open orders at week end (10.7% / 13.3%).
- Policy activation days and manual overrides of dispatch rules.

A red lagging metric with green leading indicators means the thresholds or the process model are wrong. That should trigger a fresh process-mining review, not another local fix.
