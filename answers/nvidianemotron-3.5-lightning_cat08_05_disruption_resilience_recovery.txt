# 3. Resilience versus concealed noncompliance

**True resilience** adaptations protected critical service outcomes while embedding a planned path back to full compliance. Pattern **A** (hospitalcritical fast lane) preserved lifesaving supply when paired with mandatory retroactive authorization checks; **B** (manual authorization check) maintained the eligibility gate without system access, provided coverage was rotated and limited; **F** (temporary release control) upheld controlledsubstance and coldchain checks using formalized manual checklists; **J** (backlog prioritization) restored orderly service recovery when rules were explicit and documented. These adaptations *increased* throughput without *eliminating* core controls, and each had a defined reintegration mechanism.

**Risky shortcuts** preserved throughput at the expense of traceability, authorization, or control completeness, creating hidden exposure that amplified postincident remediation workload. Pattern **C** (skip system authorization check) assumed standing eligibility that could be stale, directly inflating traceability exceptions to 9.5%. Pattern **D** (manual inventory snapshot) became risky as inventory aged, increasing mispick and expiration risk. Pattern **E** (manual pick list) caused downstream traceability reconstruction and doubled rework. Pattern **G** (dispatch without scan) generated the largest DFG deviation and the biggest auditremediation tail. Pattern **H** (phone proof of delivery) was weak for controlled products, compromising dualcontrol compliance. Pattern **I** (defer noncritical order) reduced capacity pressure but drove cancellations from 2.1% to 7.8% and damaged customer relationships. In each case, the shortcut *traded* control for speed, and the cost was deferred into the stabilization phase as a remediation backlog.

**Ambiguous adaptations** depend on risk appetite, implementation detail, and the rigor of postincident fixes. Pattern **I** (defer noncritical order) can be resilience (protecting capacity with communication) or risk (high cancellations) depending on whether a formal deferment queue with ETA and autoreengagement exists. Patterns **K** (reconcile manual reservation) and **L** (audit remediation) are necessary catchup mechanisms but become concealed noncompliance if treated as systemic posthoc patches rather than exceptiondriven, timebound processes; their value hinges on whether reconciliation/remediation SLAs are enforced and visible, or whether they remain “hidden workload” that masks ongoing process weakness. Management’s risk tolerance for delayed compliance, and the resources allocated to transparent remediation, determine whether these are resilient recovery steps or concealed noncompliance.
