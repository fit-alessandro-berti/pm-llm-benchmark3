## 1. What is observed

Automation became available at Alpha and Beta in April and at Gamma in July; Delta and Epsilon remained without it through August. Availability is a branch-month attribute. The recorded automated fast-path share is a completed-path share, rising from 4–6% before rollout to 25–50% after rollout in adopting branches. It captures one realized route through the process, not every use of automated triage: an automatically triaged case can be escalated. Nonzero shares before availability also call for a data-definition check.

Alpha and Beta were chosen for high volume and poor historical cycle times, while Gamma entered after a readiness pilot. At the same time, the shared fraud desk began in March; routing shifted more arrivals to adopters; FTE changed in four branches; a May product change altered complexity; and managers could change local practice. The post-rollout months also contain learning and increasing fast-path completion. These changes overlap the adoption dates and differ by branch.

The cycle-time figures are monthly medians and p90s, so they do not reveal individual-case distributions or yield a pooled case-level median. Rework and escalation are branch-month percentages without event-level paths or queue waits. CSAT is a respondent mean with response rates around 20–45% and no counts, preventing precision weighting or an assessment of respondent composition. Only five branches and eight months are observed.

## 2. Causal estimands

Here, a branch-month quantile effect means the average difference between *potential branch-month quantiles*, not the quantile of all pooled cases. All availability estimands require a specified rollout version and a specified level of adoption elsewhere, because routing and the shared fraud desk can transmit effects between branches.

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | population | time horizon | outcome | unit of analysis | interpretation | identifiable from supplied data? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A, availability ITT-like | Make the system available with the observed training and fallback policy versus retain manual triage, holding the wider rollout regime fixed | Branches eligible for rollout, especially the adopting cohorts | First 0–2 months | `median_cycle_h` | Branch-month | Total policy effect of offering automation, including uptake and local adaptation | No; staggered comparisons need unverified trends and no interference |
| B, sustained availability | Same offer versus no offer | Adopting branches | Months 3–4 after adoption | `median_cycle_h` | Branch-month | Dynamic effect after more learning, potentially different from A | No; only April adopters reach this horizon and co-changes persist |
| C, actual automated triage | Randomly route a baseline-eligible registered case to automated triage versus manual triage, retaining specialist fallback | Cases meeting a prespecified eligibility rule at registration | Registration to close | Individual cycle hours | Case | Effect of actual route assignment; completed automated fast-path status is a post-assignment outcome, not a valid treatment definition | No; eligibility, assignment, and case outcomes are unavailable |
| D, tail | Offer automation versus no offer at the same policy saturation | Eligible branches | First 0–2 months | `p90_cycle_h` | Branch-month | Effect on the long-wait tail, separate from the median | No; same identification and queue-interference problems |
| E, rework | Offer automation versus no offer | Eligible branches | First 0–2 months | `rework_pct` | Branch-month | Change in repeated-review or information-request loops, allowing for displacement between steps | No; conditional comparison only |
| F, escalation | Offer automation versus no offer | Eligible branches and shared fraud-desk catchment | First 0–2 months | `escalation_pct` and specialist/fraud wait | Branch-month and queue-week | Effect on referral load and downstream queue state | No; desk waits and cross-branch exposure are missing |
| G, customer experience | Offer automation versus no offer | All served customers, including survey nonresponders | First 0–2 months | Population mean satisfaction on the 1–5 scale | Case/customer | Customer effect, rather than change among respondents | No; survey selection and counts are unknown |
| H, heterogeneity | Offer automation versus no offer within prespecified registration-risk strata | Low- and high-complexity cases | First 0–2 months and later | Case cycle time and rework | Case, aggregated within risk stratum | Whether automation helps simple cases while burdening complex ones | No; only the branch-month high-complexity share is supplied |

## 3. Identification strategy

| strategy | treatment timing representation | comparison group | adjustment variables | estimand targeted | identifying assumptions | diagnostic checks | main failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Primary: cohort-specific, calendar-aligned group-time difference-in-differences and event-time summaries | April cohort (Alpha, Beta) and July cohort (Gamma), estimated separately by months since availability; never use already-treated branches as controls | Gamma only before July, plus Delta and Epsilon; after July, Delta and Epsilon only | Calendar month; baseline outcome, arrival/FTE, and risk mix for matching or weighting; later workload, FTE, and mix only in clearly labeled sensitivity analyses | Cohort-specific availability effects on branch-month median, p90, rework, escalation, and respondent CSAT | Conditional parallel untreated trends, no anticipation, stable outcome definitions, and limited or modeled fraud-desk/routing spillovers | Plot branch levels and pre-trends; placebo adoption dates; vary pre/post windows; leave one control out; report cohort and horizon separately | Selection and concurrent branch-specific changes make untreated trends unobservable |
| Alternative: controlled interrupted time series with branch-specific slopes | April and July level/slope interruptions, with March fraud-desk and May product-change indicators | Delta and Epsilon contemporaneously; Gamma before July | Baseline branch level/slope, calendar shocks; measured staffing, load, mix in sensitivity models | Change in level or slope after availability | Pre-period slopes forecast the untreated path and no other discontinuity coincides with rollout | Backcast placebo breaks, residual and autocorrelation plots, varying trend forms | Three pre-April observations cannot establish a credible branch-specific trend |
| Alternative: constrained synthetic-control/matched-donor sensitivity | Separate synthetic counterfactual at each adoption date | Nonadopters and not-yet adopters only, excluding contaminated donor months | Pre-adoption outcomes, load, complexity, and staffing | Adopter-specific availability contrast | Donors span the untreated adopter trajectory; donor exposure is unaffected | Pre-fit error, placebo assignments, donor-weight sensitivity | Very few donors and pre-periods make excellent fit or placebo inference unlikely |
| Prospective alternative: randomized phase-in of stable intake queues in Delta and Epsilon | Randomly assigned early versus delayed availability, with prespecified saturation and crossover date | Delayed queues in the same calendar weeks | Randomization strata, registration risk, queue load; analyze assigned availability first | ITT for the next rollout under the tested saturation | Assignment is respected; queue/routing spillovers measured and incorporated | Balance, crossover, contamination, fraud-desk occupancy, subgroup harm | Shared specialist/fraud queues may transmit treatment effects to controls |

The primary retrospective analysis preserves the rollout cohorts and compares them in the same calendar months. That matters because all branches experienced the March fraud-desk change, the May complexity shift, and a common rise and fall in demand. A conventional two-way fixed-effects coefficient would mix early- and late-cohort comparisons, potentially using already-treated Alpha and Beta as controls for Gamma, while effects and uptake evolve. Cohort-specific contrasts avoid that particular weighting problem and keep the first-month and later-month questions distinct. Baseline matching can make Alpha/Beta and Gamma more comparable to Delta/Epsilon, but five branches provide little room to match on both levels and trends; any weights and failed pre-fit should be shown. For the total availability effect, post-rollout fast-path share, escalation, staffing, and arrivals are possible mediators or co-interventions, so adjusting for them does not automatically remove confounding. They belong in sensitivity analyses with their interpretation stated. The primary design is thus the clearest *description under explicit assumptions* available from this panel, not a persuasive stand-alone causal estimate. A prospective random phase-in is stronger for the next decision, provided queue interference is measured.

## 4. Process-aware causal mechanisms

| mechanism | process artifact or metric affected | expected signature if beneficial | expected signature if harmful | evidence in supplied data | unresolved ambiguity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fast-path substitution | Share completing `Register → Automated Triage → Resolve → Close`; time in triage | More eligible cases bypass manual triage with shorter total time and no downstream crowding | Easy cases leave the manual pool, making remaining manual cases look slower; inappropriate bypass harms quality | Share rises from 4–6% to 25–50% in adopters; medians do not improve uniformly | No case-level eligibility, automated-triage usage, or path durations |
| Specialist-queue congestion | Automated escalated path; specialist/fraud arrivals and wait | Accurate referrals with stable or shorter specialist wait | Borderline referrals grow and wait increases for both treated and untreated branches | Escalation rises about 1 percentage point at adoption; all branches rose 2 points in March | Shared fraud-desk waits and referral composition are absent |
| Learning and implementation fidelity | Month-since-rollout and automated fast-path share | Growing use accompanied by falling cycle time and stable rework | Uneven training causes rework or delayed escalation | Alpha/Beta fast-path shares keep rising; their May–June tails worsen, then ease | Use and learning coincide with changing load, staffing, and mix |
| Case-mix or routing changes | Registration risk class, branch arrivals, route assignment | Risk-adjusted outcomes improve within comparable intake strata | More or harder arrivals overload queues or create misleading aggregate shifts | April adopters get more arrivals; complexity rises in May; Gamma's pre-July times worsen | Risk share is coarse and routing can change unmeasured severity |
| Staffing pressure | Arrivals per productive FTE; work in progress | Freed triage capacity reduces queue occupancy despite load | FTE losses or load growth lengthen waits | Alpha loses two FTE in May; Beta gains one in April; Alpha load rises from 31.2 to 40.2 arrivals/FTE across its windows | FTE productivity, overtime, and specialist capacity are unknown |
| Rework displacement | Repeated-review loops, information requests, specialist returns | Fewer total loops without moving them later | Apparent triage savings become specialist rework or customer callbacks | Rework falls at each adoption month, then rebounds in Alpha/Beta after May | Aggregate rework cannot locate the loop or capture unrecorded callbacks |
| Tail amplification | p90 cycle time, age of open cases, specialist wait | p90 falls alongside the median | A small escalated group has much longer waits despite a faster median | Alpha/Beta full-post p90 rises about 9.7 h versus pre, while median changes little | Monthly p90 lacks case mix, queue state, and uncertainty |
| Survey selection | Response rate, respondent risk/path mix, `csat_1to5` | Stable response and better scores across paths/risk groups | Faster satisfied customers respond more, masking harmed nonresponders | Gamma respondent mean rises after July; rates vary 20–45% overall | No branch-month response counts or nonrespondent outcomes |

## 5. Quantitative evidence

All figures below are arithmetic on **unweighted monthly branch summaries**. April adopters use January–March as pre and April–August as post; Gamma uses April–June as pre and July–August as post. A percentage-point change is marked “pp.” These are not pooled case-level medians or causal estimates.

| adopter and windows | fast-path share Δ, pp | median Δ, h | p90 Δ, h | rework Δ, pp | escalation Δ, pp | CSAT Δ | arrivals/FTE Δ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Alpha, Jan–Mar → Apr–Aug | +34.0 | −0.40 | +9.65 | +0.54 | +2.91 | +0.02 | +9.05 |
| Beta, Jan–Mar → Apr–Aug | +34.0 | +1.86 | +9.81 | −0.56 | +2.53 | −0.02 | +5.86 |
| Gamma, Apr–Jun → Jul–Aug | +24.5 | −6.47 | −9.27 | −1.23 | +1.17 | +0.14 | +1.86 |

**Calendar-aligned controls.** For January–March to April–August, Alpha/Beta average changes are +0.73 h median, +9.73 h p90, −0.01 pp rework, +2.72 pp escalation, and +0.002 CSAT. Delta/Epsilon average changes are +5.22 h, +16.05 h, +1.92 pp, +1.82 pp, and −0.084. Subtracting control changes gives **descriptive** contrasts of −4.49 h median, −6.32 h p90, −1.93 pp rework, +0.90 pp escalation, and +0.087 CSAT. For Gamma's April–June to July–August window, subtracting Delta/Epsilon changes yields −3.84 h median, −7.05 h p90, −2.12 pp rework, +0.90 pp escalation, and +0.097 CSAT. These comparisons require untreated parallel trends and negligible shared-queue spillovers to be interpreted causally.

**Intensity and timing.** From April to August, Alpha's completed automated fast-path share rises 25%→48% while its median falls 48.3→43.3 h but p90 rises 97.3→101.2 h; Beta's share rises 27%→50%, median falls 47.3→45.0 h, and p90 rises 95.6→99.2 h. Gamma's share rises 25%→32% in July–August while median falls 38.4→36.0 h and p90 falls 87.1→82.7 h. These associations cannot be treated as dose response: completed path is selected after triage, and load, mix, learning, and calendar time change alongside it.

**Pre-trends and window sensitivity.** In January–March, Alpha's median goes 50.4→46.9→49.4 h and Beta's 45.4→45.9→46.4 h; the controls have different levels and paths. Gamma worsens from 36.4 h in January to 45.9 h in June before adoption, then falls in July; Delta and Epsilon also fall in July. Weak branches were selected for April, so regression to the mean could create an apparent gain. Using only March→April, the Alpha/Beta versus Gamma/Delta/Epsilon contrast is −1.57 h median and −2.85 h p90, smaller than the full-window figures. June→July gives Gamma versus Delta/Epsilon −3.65 h median and −6.70 h p90. No single window captures a stable percentage reduction.

**Sensitivity, not confidence intervals.** Write each comparison as effect plus an unobserved differential untreated trend. If that trend could be anywhere within ±5 h for median and ±10 h for p90, the April-cohort full-window effects would lie in scenario ranges **−9.49 to +0.51 h median** and **−16.32 to +3.68 h p90**; Gamma's would be **−8.84 to +1.16 h median** and **−17.05 to +2.95 h p90**. These chosen drift limits are illustrative, not empirically identified bounds. For rework, allowing ±2 pp differential drift around the contrasts gives April **−3.93 to +0.07 pp** and Gamma **−4.12 to −0.12 pp**; coding changes or displaced loops could widen that range. CSAT additionally has unbounded selection within its 1–5 response scale given the missing response counts and respondent mix.

## 6. Threats, tests, and decision consequences

| threat | why it matters here | direction of likely bias | test or additional evidence | consequence if threat is severe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection into adoption | Alpha/Beta had weak historical times; Gamma passed readiness screening | Regression to mean can exaggerate apparent benefit; readiness can favor Gamma | Longer pre-history, assignment records, placebo start dates | Do not generalize cohort contrasts to Delta/Epsilon |
| Nonparallel untreated trends | Branch levels, demand, and January–March paths differ | Either direction | Cohort-specific pre-trend plots, matched pre-fit, negative-control outcomes | DiD contrast is not an effect |
| Time-varying confounding | Routing campaign, May product change, and manager actions overlap adoption | Either; higher demand may mask benefit | Daily routing and policy logs, risk-stratified trends, sensitivity models | Separate co-interventions or randomize future rollout |
| Treatment intensity/endogenous path | Fast-path completion is a post-treatment route, rising over time | Selection of easy cases can exaggerate per-case benefit | Record eligibility, actual triage assignment, override, failure, and completion | Do not call the share a causal dose |
| Shared fraud-desk spillovers | More automated borderline referrals may delay controls too | Harm to controls exaggerates apparent adopter benefit; adopter wait can hide it | Desk arrivals, waits, occupancy, origin, and priority | Estimate network-wide policy effect, not isolated branch effect |
| Staffing | Alpha loses two FTE, Beta gains one, Gamma loses one pre-rollout, Delta gains one | Alpha loss masks benefit; Beta gain exaggerates it | Shift-level productive hours, skills, overtime, absences | Re-estimate within stable-capacity strata or redesign capacity |
| Case mix | High-complexity share rises in May and differs among branches | More complexity can mask benefit; routing may sort beyond recorded risk | Case-level registration risk and severity, standardization | Aggregate medians may reverse within risk groups |
| Anticipation/readiness | Training or managerial changes may precede recorded availability | Pre-improvement can attenuate measured effect; pre-harm can inflate it | Training and workflow dates; event-time leads | Move treatment start or define a bundled intervention |
| Survey nonresponse | Only 20–45% respond, with missing counts and mix | Either; happy fast-path customers may overrespond | Counts, response propensity by path/risk, follow-up sample | No claim about all-customer satisfaction |
| Measurement aggregation | Monthly medians/p90s are nonlinear and cannot be pooled; path loops are aggregated | Unknown | Case timestamps, censoring and case-closure rules, event logs | Use case-level estimands and uncertainty |
| Small number of branches | Five clusters, only three adopters, short pre-periods | Point bias unknown; conventional standard errors likely overconfident | Randomization inference in future, leave-one-branch-out, more sites | Treat current intervals and p-values as fragile |
| Dynamic effects and treatment versions | Learning, changing share, overrides, and local practices evolve | Early estimates may understate mature benefit or miss later harm | Record version and fidelity; report event-time effects | Do not export a single constant effect to all months |
| Queue displacement/unfinished cases | Faster closures can leave difficult cases aging in open inventory | Closed-case medians can exaggerate benefit | All arrivals, active-case age, censoring, backlog, wait at every step | Guardrails must include open-case and tail measures |

## 7. Effect assessment

The scenario intervals below concern the **availability policy at the observed rollout conditions**, not a universal per-case effect. Confidence is a judgment about the *direction of a causal effect* from these data, not a statistical probability or a confidence-interval coverage level.

## 8. Rollout recommendation

**expand with an embedded evaluation**

Make Delta and Epsilon the next eligible branches, with a randomized phase-in across stable intake teams or queues within *both* branches. Stratify assignment by branch, baseline workload, and registration-risk mix; randomize half to receive availability first and delay the other half for 12 weeks. Keep the eligibility rule, staffing plan, and routing rules documented and as stable as service needs allow. Analyze assignment to availability first, regardless of actual use, and record overrides and crossovers. If teams share specialist or fraud capacity, the comparison measures the policy at the tested saturation; it cannot be described as an isolated direct case effect. Do not use just two branch-level assignments as the sole experiment.

Use at least the eight existing pre-rollout branch-months, preferably extracting case-level timestamps for that entire period; collect a minimum of 12 post-assignment weeks for each wave and follow all waves for at least six months to examine learning and delayed queue harm. Before launch, verify registration risk, arrival source, assigned team, treatment eligibility, actual triage route, decision version, specialist/fraud referral, rework loops, timestamps for every wait and service step, closure, and open-case age. Measure queue arrivals, staffing hours, backlog, occupancy, and wait distributions at each shared desk by originating branch and treatment wave. Report case-level risk-standardized median and p90, rework, escalation, open inventory, and both respondent CSAT and response rate with counts. Audit especially high-complexity customers and cases escalated after automation.

Set safety guardrails before the first wave: pause new assignments and investigate if a monitored wave shows a sustained p90 increase above 5 hours, rework increase above 1 percentage point, respondent CSAT decrease above 0.1 with stable response, or a material rise in aged open cases or fraud-desk waits. These are decision thresholds, not effects inferred from the current panel. Scale only if the randomized availability comparison shows a practically useful, risk-standardized median gain (for example at least 3 hours), rules out material p90 and subgroup harm, and the shared desks remain within capacity; otherwise extend measurement, fix the bottleneck, or stop expansion. This staged rollout can deliver benefit while making the next estimate substantially more credible.

```python
    changes = post[outcomes + ["automated_fast_path_pct"]].mean() - \
              pre[outcomes + ["automated_fast_path_pct"]].mean()
    print(branch, changes.round(3).to_dict())

# Relative time is defined only for adopters. Never-treated branches stay missing.
def relative_month(row):
    if row.branch not in adoption:
        return pd.NA
    start, current = adoption[row.branch], row.month
    return 12 * (current.year - start.year) + current.month - start.month

df["relative_month"] = df.apply(relative_month, axis=1).astype("Int64")
for k in range(-3, 5):
    name = f"event_{'m' + str(-k) if k < 0 else 'p' + str(k)}"
    df[name] = df.relative_month.eq(k).fillna(False).astype(int)
print("\nStaggered event-time indicators:")
print(df[["branch", "month", "relative_month", "event_m1", "event_p0"]].to_string(index=False))

# DESCRIPTIVE associations only. Identification would require conditional
# untreated parallel trends, no anticipation, stable policy versions, and
# absent or explicitly modeled routing/fraud-desk interference. Those
# assumptions are not established by this panel. Post-treatment path share
# may be a mediator/collider and is not an exogenous treatment dose.
df["month_str"] = df.month.astype(str)
for outcome in ["median_cycle_h", "p90_cycle_h"]:
    for regressor, label in [("automation", "availability association"),
                             ("automated_fast_path_pct", "fast-path-share association per percentage point")]:
        fit = smf.ols(
            f"{outcome} ~ {regressor} + C(branch) + C(month_str)", data=df
        ).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df.branch})
        print(f"\n{outcome}: {label}: coefficient={fit.params[regressor]:.3f}, "
              f"cluster SE={fit.bse[regressor]:.3f}")

warnings.warn("Only five branch clusters: cluster-robust SEs, p-values, and normal intervals are unreliable.")
warnings.warn("Branch-month medians/p90s are aggregated quantiles; averaging or regressing them does not estimate a pooled case-level median or p90.")
warnings.warn("Completed automated-fast-path share is selected after triage; its regression coefficient is not a causal dose response.")
```

## 10. Final claim ladder

- Observed: Availability began in April for Alpha/Beta and July for Gamma; completed automated fast-path shares rose, while branch outcomes moved differently across cohorts and months.
- Suggestive: Calendar-aligned adopter comparisons show roughly 4 h lower branch-month medians and 6–7 h lower p90s than controls, alongside about 2 pp less recorded rework and 0.9 pp more escalation.
- Conditionally causal: Those contrasts estimate availability effects only if untreated trends were parallel, rollout versions comparable, and routing and shared-queue spillovers negligible or correctly modeled; the supplied panel cannot verify these conditions.
- Not supported: “Automation reduces cycle time by X%” for all branches or cases, or “automation improves customer satisfaction,” is not justified by these data.
