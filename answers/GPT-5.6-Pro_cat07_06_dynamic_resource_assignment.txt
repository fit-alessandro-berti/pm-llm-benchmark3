# 1. Objectives and constraints

This is a **constrained, multi-objective assignment problem**, not a pure throughput optimization. Several objectives conflict:

* Keeping a case with its owner preserves context, but may be slower when the owner has weak skill or little remaining capacity.
* Using specialists reduces immediate error risk, but can create Security and Approval bottlenecks.
* Assigning the first available worker reduces initial waiting, but can increase service time, rework, and subsequent handovers.
* Training assignments improve future resilience, but consume supervision time and may slightly reduce current throughput.
* Equal assignment counts are not necessarily fair when cases differ in complexity, urgency, handover burden, and expected service time.

A useful design distinction is between:

1. **Case ownership**: who remains accountable for the case and customer context.
2. **Activity execution**: who performs the next specialist or operational task.

A specialist can therefore execute one activity while the current owner remains accountable. This is particularly useful for C001 and C003: it satisfies skill or workload requirements without necessarily causing another full ownership reassignment.

## Safety and compliance constraints

These rules are applied before any resource is scored:

| Constraint                    | Treatment                                                                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Skill level 0                 | The resource cannot execute the activity. They may observe for training, but cannot prepare a decision, perform the controlled work, or sign off.           |
| Urgent and high-priority work | Treat both `urgent` and `high` as high-priority. The executor must have skill 2–3, unless an activity explicitly permits supervised skill-1 execution.      |
| Security Review               | Skill 3 may execute independently. Skill 1–2 may execute only with explicit supervision by R7_Gita or R8_Hugo. A skill-0 observer is not an executor.       |
| Final Approval                | The finalizer must have skill 2–3. Skill 1 may prepare supporting material but cannot finalize.                                                             |
| Quality batching              | Only normal/low cases with more than 24 hours remaining may be batched.                                                                                     |
| Per-resource batch cap        | No worker receives more than four new duties. Primary work, supervision, and structured shadowing all count, so coaching is not treated as invisible labor. |
| High overtime risk            | No new execution assignment unless the case is urgent, no safer qualified alternative exists, and the exception is recorded.                                |
| Missing supervisor            | A supervised candidate becomes ineligible if the named supervisor cannot commit capacity.                                                                   |
| Safety versus SLA             | Authorization rules are never waived merely to avoid an SLA miss. An infeasible case is escalated rather than assigned illegally.                           |

## Strong preferences and trade-offs

These are not absolute prohibitions, but departures require stronger justification:

1. **SLA risk:** minimize expected negative slack and prioritize cases with little time remaining.
2. **End-to-end throughput:** minimize waiting plus service time plus avoidable rework, not merely time to first assignment.
3. **Skill fit:** use specialists where complexity or control risk requires them, but do not consume scarce specialist capacity for every routine case.
4. **Continuity:** prefer the current owner, particularly for high-complexity or high-handover-cost cases.
5. **Workload fairness:** compare projected hours and complexity-weighted workload, not just open-case counts.
6. **Training:** use normal/low cases with sufficient slack for documented development priorities.
7. **Resilience:** preserve backup capacity in scarce skills and avoid routing all Security or Approval work to one person.
8. **Worker agency:** permit workers and dispatchers to correct stale workload, skill, or case-complexity data. Overrides should be logged and reviewed, not automatically treated as misconduct.

The supplied historical values should initially be used as policy priors rather than permanent universal constants:

* Add approximately **2.7 hours of expected waiting** when ownership changes on a high-complexity case.
* Add approximately **3.3 hours of expected service** when a skill-1 owner remains the unsupervised executor.
* Apply a substantial quality-risk penalty when a proposed ownership change would move a case above two reassignments, because the observed rework and appeal rates rise sharply in that group.

# 2. Assignment policy

## Decision modes

For each case, the policy evaluates several explainable modes:

1. **Owner executes:** the current owner performs the activity.
2. **Ownership transfer:** another qualified resource executes and becomes the owner.
3. **Specialist execution without ownership transfer:** another resource performs the activity, while the current owner retains accountability and supplies a bounded handover.
4. **Supervised execution:** a level-1 or level-2 worker executes under an authorized named supervisor.
5. **Shadow assignment:** the qualified worker executes; a skill-0 learner observes without production rights.

The third mode is important because not every resource change should be treated as a full case reassignment.

## SLA urgency and predicted slack

Cases are first ordered by urgency:

[
U(c)=0.6P(c)+0.4\max\left(0,1-\frac{T_c}{24}\right)
]

where:

* (P(c)) is 1.00 for urgent, 0.75 for high, 0.35 for normal, and 0.10 for low.
* (T_c) is the SLA time remaining in hours.

For every feasible assignment mode (m), calculate:

[
\text{slack}(c,m)=T_c-
\left(
\widehat W_m+
\widehat S_{c,m}+
\widehat H_{c,m}+
\widehat V_{c,m}
\right)
]

where:

* (\widehat W_m): predicted queue waiting for the executor.
* (\widehat S_{c,m}): predicted service time based on activity, complexity, skill level, workload, and team.
* (\widehat H_{c,m}): handover delay.
* (\widehat V_{c,m}): supervision or coordination time.

The SLA-feasibility component can be normalized as:

[
A(c,m)=
\operatorname{clip}
\left(
0.5+0.5\frac{\text{slack}(c,m)}{T_c},
0,1
\right)
]

A score of 0.5 means predicted completion is approximately at the deadline; below 0.5 indicates predicted negative or weak slack.

## Skill-match scoring

Set a target skill:

* Target 3 for Security and cases with complexity at least 0.75.
* Target 2 for most other controlled work.
* Target 1 only for deliberately selected low-risk training work with supervision or review.

Then:

[
M(c,m)=\min\left(1,\frac{\text{executor skill}}{\text{target skill}}\right)
]

A supervised assignment one level below target is capped below a fully qualified assignment, for example at 0.8. Excess skill receives little or no extra reward once the target is met; otherwise the policy would collapse into specialist-only routing.

## Continuity and handover scoring

An explainable continuity scale is:

| Assignment mode                        | Initial continuity score |
| -------------------------------------- | -----------------------: |
| Current owner executes                 |                     1.00 |
| Different executor, owner retained     |                     0.85 |
| Ownership change, low handover cost    |                     0.65 |
| Ownership change, medium handover cost |                     0.40 |
| Ownership change, high handover cost   |                     0.15 |

Subtract an additional penalty when:

* `reassignment_count >= 2`;
* complexity is at least 0.75;
* the proposed change would move the case above two reassignments.

Continuity is not absolute. Keeping a skill-1 owner as the unsupervised executor may be worse than a well-managed specialist intervention. C001 illustrates this distinction.

## Workload and overtime fairness

Use projected workload pressure rather than open-case count alone:

[
\text{pressure}(r)=
\frac{
\text{estimated remaining work hours}
+
\text{new assigned hours}
+
\text{supervision hours}
}{
\text{hours remaining today}
}
]

The fairness score is the inverse normalized pressure across eligible resources. Apply:

* a moderate penalty for medium overtime risk;
* a strong exclusion or penalty for high overtime risk;
* a hard maximum of four new duties;
* an additional concentration penalty when one worker receives several high-complexity or urgent cases, even if their raw assignment count remains low.

## Training and development scoring

A training score is awarded only when all of the following hold:

* the activity matches the worker’s documented training priority;
* the case is normal or low priority, or the learner is only observing;
* predicted SLA slack remains acceptable after supervision;
* an authorized and available supervisor or qualified performer is named;
* the learning objective and assignment mode are logged.

Suggested values are:

* 1.0 for qualified supervised execution aligned with the training priority;
* 0.5 for structured shadowing with no production decision rights;
* 0 otherwise.

Shadowing and hands-on execution should be reported separately even when both count as “training support.”

## Resilience and bottleneck avoidance

The resilience component rewards assignments that:

* leave at least one qualified backup for a scarce activity;
* use a competent level-2 worker for routine work rather than a scarce specialist;
* distribute Security work between Gita and Hugo;
* distribute Approval work between Farid and competent level-2 finalizers;
* create supervised future capacity without compromising current controls.

It penalizes:

* assigning R8_Hugo to routine Quality or Rework while Security work is queued;
* routing every Approval to R6_Farid;
* using one specialist for more than four cases;
* consuming the last available specialist slot for a routine case;
* treating observed activity similarity as formal authorization.

## Candidate scoring

After hard filtering, use different weights by case class.

For urgent/high cases:

[
\text{Score}=
0.30A+
0.15F+
0.20M+
0.18C+
0.10L+
0.00T+
0.07B
]

For normal/low cases:

[
\text{Score}=
0.15A+
0.15F+
0.18M+
0.18C+
0.16L+
0.10T+
0.08B
]

where:

* (A): SLA feasibility;
* (F): predicted flow or completion-time score;
* (M): skill match;
* (C): continuity;
* (L): workload fairness;
* (T): training value;
* (B): resilience.

The weights are governance choices, not an objectively correct utility function. They should be reviewed using observed outcomes and worker feedback.

## Pseudocode

```text
INPUT:
    queue, resource snapshot, versioned skill matrix,
    historical service/wait estimates, policy version

Separate "case owner" from "activity executor".

For every case:
    calculate urgency and process cases from highest to lowest urgency

    enumerate assignment modes:
        owner executes
        qualified new executor with ownership transfer
        qualified new executor while owner remains
        supervised executor + authorized supervisor
        qualified executor + shadow observer

    hard-filter modes:
        reject skill 0 execution
        enforce high/urgent minimum skill
        enforce Security and Approval rules
        enforce Quality batching conditions
        reject unavailable supervisors
        reject projected assignment count > 4
        reject high-overtime-risk resources unless urgent and unavoidable

    for every remaining mode:
        estimate queue wait, service time, handover delay,
        supervision time, and SLA slack

        score:
            SLA feasibility
            expected throughput
            skill match
            continuity
            projected workload and overtime
            documented training value
            specialist scarcity and resilience

    choose highest-scoring mode

After provisional assignment:
    repair the batch if necessary:
        enforce max-four cap
        ensure urgent work has feasible positive slack where possible
        ensure Security/Approval reserve capacity
        enforce Quality batch rules
        seek at least 20% training-supported normal/low assignments
        do not force training if it would violate skill, SLA, or overtime rules

Tie-breaking:
    1. greater predicted SLA slack
    2. owner retained
    3. lower overtime risk
    4. lower complexity-weighted projected workload
    5. lower consumption of scarce specialist capacity
    6. transparent rotating tie-break among operationally equivalent workers

Log:
    chosen executor, retained/transferred owner, supervisor or observer,
    score components, primary reason, alternatives, policy version,
    predicted slack, and any human override
```

## Exception handling

When no compliant assignment exists, the policy should not silently relax a safety rule. It should:

1. Mark the case `unassigned-escalated`.
2. Identify the binding constraint, such as “no Security specialist available.”
3. Permit controlled overtime only for an urgent case with no safer option.
4. Notify the dispatcher and affected owner.
5. Record the expected SLA consequence and exception reason.
6. Re-evaluate when workload, supervisor availability, or case information changes.

# 4. Comparison with baseline policies

Exact throughput cannot be calculated because no activity-specific service-time distribution is supplied. The comparison is therefore directional and should be validated after deployment.

For an illustrative baseline calculation, assume:

* cases are considered in C001–C016 order;
* open-case counts are incremented after each assignment;
* every selected worker has enough remaining hours, because task duration is unknown.

Under those assumptions, literal first-available routing would, among other assignments:

* give C001 Security to Celine at skill 1 without an explicit supervisor;
* give C002 Final Approval to Jonas at skill 1;
* give high-priority C007 Rework to Farid at skill 1;
* give C008 and C015 Security to Celine at skill 1 without authorized supervision;
* give urgent C011 Negotiation to Jonas at skill 1;
* assign five new cases to Jonas.

Thus, literal first-available routing is not deployable under the stated hard constraints. Adding the required safety filters would make it a **guard-railed first-available policy**, not the baseline as originally defined.

Illustrative specialist-only routing would concentrate approximately:

* five assignments on Celine: C003, C007, C009, C014, and C016;
* four assignments on Farid;
* all three Security cases on Hugo;
* both Quality cases on Jonas.

It would exceed the four-assignment cap for Celine and transfer C016 away from a competent current owner despite high complexity and high handover cost.

# 5. Explanation to workers

> Assignments are made by first checking who is permitted and qualified to do the work. We then consider the case deadline, whether changing the owner would lose important context, each person’s current workload and remaining hours, and whether a lower-risk case can provide a planned learning opportunity.
>
> Being assigned a case does not mean that the system considers someone “better,” and not receiving a case is not a negative performance judgment. Some workers are temporarily protected because of overtime risk. Specialists are sometimes held back so they remain available for Security, Approval, or difficult escalations.
>
> We normally keep the current owner when continuity matters. In some cases another person performs a specialist activity while the owner remains responsible for the case. This is intended to preserve customer and case knowledge without asking an underqualified owner to perform controlled work.
>
> Training assignments are identified explicitly. A supervised learner has a named coach and a defined review point. A shadow observer may watch and discuss the work but does not make or sign the decision.
>
> Workers may flag an assignment when the recorded workload, skill level, case complexity, or ownership information is wrong. A dispatcher can override the recommendation, but the reason is recorded so that the policy and its data can be improved.

Each assignment explanation shown to a worker should contain four items:

* **Why this case was assigned:** for example, “current owner, competent skill, seven-hour SLA.”
* **What trade-off was made:** for example, “continuity was preferred over a specialist transfer.”
* **What safeguard applies:** supervisor, escalation checkpoint, batch rule, or owner-retention arrangement.
* **When it will be reviewed:** an explicit time or remaining-slack threshold.

The visible explanation should use a small number of reason codes and plain language rather than displaying an unexplained decimal score.

This requires a `decision_batch_id`.                                                                                                                |
| **Quality batch compliance**          | For every batched Quality assignment, verify normal/low priority and more than 24 hours of remaining SLA time at the assignment timestamp. Compare waiting and service time for batched versus non-batched Quality cases.                                                                                                              |
| **Resilience and skill coverage**     | Number of resources with level 2+ and level 3 for each skill, plus the number who have completed supervised development assignments. Monitor whether the process remains executable after the absence of each specialist.                                                                                                              |
| **Reason-code completeness**          | Percentage of assignments with a valid `assignment_reason`, owner-change status, supervisor or observer where relevant, predicted slack, and policy version. Missing explanations undermine both worker trust and auditability.                                                                                                        |

## Recommended additional decision fields

The supplied log is sufficient for several retrospective indicators, but robust policy auditing should add:

* `assignment_id`
* `decision_batch_id`
* `assignment_timestamp`
* `policy_version`
* `recommended_resource`
* `actual_resource`
* `assignment_mode`
* `supervisor_resource`
* `observer_resource`
* `owner_changed`
* `skill_level_snapshot`
* `overtime_risk_snapshot`
* `predicted_service_time`
* `predicted_waiting_time`
* `predicted_sla_slack`
* component scores
* `override_reason`

Skill and overtime data must be versioned by time. Joining all historical events to the current skill matrix would introduce hindsight bias.

Observed outcome differences should initially be treated as associations. Cases are not randomly assigned: specialists receive harder cases, and training cases are deliberately selected for lower risk. Comparative evaluation should therefore stratify or match on activity, priority, complexity, prior reassignments, and initial SLA slack.

# 7. PM4Py implementation sketch

PM4Py’s current API documentation exposes organizational-network functions for handover, working-together relationships, activity-based resource similarity, and organizational roles. It also provides service/waiting-time enrichment, performance DFGs, variants, batching, and event/case filtering. Depending on the installed version, functions may be available through top-level aliases or modules such as `pm4py.org`, `pm4py.analysis`, `pm4py.stats`, `pm4py.discovery`, and `pm4py.filtering`. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][1])

The handover network is useful for identifying frequent resource-to-resource transitions, while the working-together network identifies case collaboration. Activity-based similarity and discovered roles can suggest possible backups, but they should never override the authoritative skill matrix or turn historical activity into automatic permission. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][2])

```python
from __future__ import annotations

from pathlib import Path
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"
START_TIMESTAMP = "start_timestamp"

REQUIRED_COLUMNS = {
    CASE,
    ACTIVITY,
    TIMESTAMP,
    RESOURCE,
    "required_skill",
    "case_priority",
    "sla_deadline",
    "case_complexity",
    "current_owner",
    "team",
    "assignment_reason",
    "reassignment_count",
    "workload_snapshot",
    "handover_cost_band",
}


def load_and_format_log(path: str | Path) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Load and validate the event log."""

    path = Path(path)
    if path.suffix == ".parquet":
        df = pd.read_parquet(path)
    else:
        df = pd.read_csv(path)

    missing = REQUIRED_COLUMNS.difference(df.columns)
    if missing:
        raise ValueError(f"Missing required columns: {sorted(missing)}")

    df[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(df[TIMESTAMP], utc=True, errors="raise")
    df["sla_deadline"] = pd.to_datetime(
        df["sla_deadline"], utc=True, errors="raise"
    )

    if START_TIMESTAMP in df.columns:
        df[START_TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(
            df[START_TIMESTAMP], utc=True, errors="raise"
        )

    df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
        df,
        case_id=CASE,
        activity_key=ACTIVITY,
        timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
        start_timestamp_key=(
            START_TIMESTAMP if START_TIMESTAMP in df.columns else TIMESTAMP
        ),
    )

    return df.sort_values([CASE, TIMESTAMP], kind="stable").reset_index(drop=True)


events = load_and_format_log("service_log.parquet")

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Organizational networks
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

handover_network = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    events,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

working_together_network = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    events,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

resource_similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    events,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

organizational_roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    events,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

# Use view_sna interactively or save an auditable HTML artifact.
pm4py.save_vis_sna(handover_network, "handover_network.html")
pm4py.save_vis_sna(working_together_network, "working_together_network.html")

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Performance, waiting, variants, and batches
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

performance_dfg, start_activities, end_activities = (
    pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(events)
)

variants = pm4py.get_variants(events)

quality_events = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    events,
    attribute_key=ACTIVITY,
    values=["Quality Check"],
    level="event",
    retain=True,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

quality_batches = pm4py.discover_batches(
    quality_events,
    merge_distance=900,
    min_batch_size=2,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE,
    resource_key=RESOURCE,
)

security_events = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    events,
    attribute_key=ACTIVITY,
    values=["Security Review"],
    level="event",
    retain=True,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

normal_low_cases = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    events,
    attribute_key="case_priority",
    values=["normal", "low"],
    level="case",
    retain=True,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

# Case performance uses elapsed seconds between first and last event.
slow_cases = pm4py.filter_case_performance(
    events,
    min_performance=72 * 3600,
    max_performance=float("inf"),
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
    case_id_key=CASE,
)

# True service-time analysis needs interval timestamps.
if START_TIMESTAMP in events.columns:
    service_time_by_activity = pm4py.get_service_time(
        events,
        aggregation_measure="median",
        activity_key=ACTIVITY,
        timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
        start_timestamp_key=START_TIMESTAMP,
        case_id_key=CASE,
    )

    events_with_case_times = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
        events,
        activity_key=ACTIVITY,
        timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
        start_timestamp_key=START_TIMESTAMP,
        case_id_key=CASE,
    )
else:
    # This is an inter-event gap, not true activity service time.
    # It should be labelled as a waiting/sojourn proxy.
    events_with_case_times = events.copy()
    events_with_case_times["next_timestamp"] = (
        events_with_case_times.groupby(CASE)[TIMESTAMP].shift(-1)
    )
    events_with_case_times["inter_event_gap_hours"] = (
        events_with_case_times["next_timestamp"]
        - events_with_case_times[TIMESTAMP]
    ).dt.total_seconds() / 3600
    service_time_by_activity = None

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Basic handover and SLA indicators
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

audit = events.sort_values([CASE, TIMESTAMP], kind="stable").copy()

audit["previous_resource"] = audit.groupby(CASE)[RESOURCE].shift()
audit["executor_handover"] = (
    audit["previous_resource"].notna()
    & audit[RESOURCE].notna()
    & audit[RESOURCE].ne(audit["previous_resource"])
)

handover_frequency = (
    audit["executor_handover"].sum()
    / audit["previous_resource"].notna().sum()
)

case_summary = (
    audit.groupby(CASE, as_index=False)
    .agg(
        case_end=(TIMESTAMP, "max"),
        sla_deadline=("sla_deadline", "max"),
        priority=("case_priority", "last"),
        complexity=("case_complexity", "max"),
        max_reassignments=("reassignment_count", "max"),
    )
)

case_summary["sla_margin_hours"] = (
    case_summary["sla_deadline"] - case_summary["case_end"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 3600

case_summary["sla_miss"] = case_summary["sla_margin_hours"] < 0
case_summary["near_miss"] = case_summary["sla_margin_hours"].between(0, 2)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Skill and hard-rule audit
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

# The production skill table should be versioned with valid_from/valid_to.
# Expected long format:
# resource, required_skill, skill_level, training_priority,
# overtime_risk, valid_from, valid_to
skills = pd.read_parquet("resource_skill_history.parquet")

# For brevity, this sketch assumes the correct timestamp-versioned skill
# row has already been selected. In production, perform an interval join.
skill_snapshot = skills[
    [
        "resource",
        "required_skill",
        "skill_level",
        "training_priority",
        "overtime_risk",
    ]
].drop_duplicates(["resource", "required_skill"])

audit = audit.merge(
    skill_snapshot,
    left_on=[RESOURCE, "required_skill"],
    right_on=["resource", "required_skill"],
    how="left",
    validate="m:1",
)

if audit["skill_level"].isna().any():
    bad_rows = audit.loc[
        audit["skill_level"].isna(),
        [CASE, ACTIVITY, RESOURCE, "required_skill"],
    ]
    raise ValueError(
        "Missing skill snapshots for some assignments:\n"
        + bad_rows.head(20).to_string(index=False)
    )

# These fields should be logged explicitly. Defaults permit legacy logs.
for column in [
    "assignment_mode",
    "supervisor_resource",
    "observer_resource",
    "policy_version",
    "decision_batch_id",
    "owner_changed",
]:
    if column not in audit.columns:
        audit[column] = pd.NA

audit["is_supervised"] = audit["assignment_mode"].eq("supervised")
audit["is_high_priority"] = audit["case_priority"].isin(["urgent", "high"])

audit["skill_zero_violation"] = audit["skill_level"].eq(0)

audit["high_priority_skill_violation"] = (
    audit["is_high_priority"]
    & audit["skill_level"].lt(2)
    & ~audit["is_supervised"]
)

authorized_security_supervisors = {"R7_Gita", "R8_Hugo"}

security_row = audit[ACTIVITY].eq("Security Review")
security_valid = (
    audit["skill_level"].eq(3)
    | (
        audit["skill_level"].between(1, 2)
        & audit["is_supervised"]
        & audit["supervisor_resource"].isin(
            authorized_security_supervisors
        )
    )
)
audit["security_rule_violation"] = security_row & ~security_valid

approval_row = audit[ACTIVITY].eq("Final Approval")
audit["approval_rule_violation"] = (
    approval_row & audit["skill_level"].lt(2)
)

audit["hard_rule_violation"] = audit[
    [
        "skill_zero_violation",
        "high_priority_skill_violation",
        "security_rule_violation",
        "approval_rule_violation",
    ]
].any(axis=1)

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Training and overtime indicators
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

training_reason = audit["assignment_reason"].str.contains(
    r"training|supervised|shadow",
    case=False,
    na=False,
)

normal_low = audit["case_priority"].isin(["normal", "low"])
training_assignment_share = (
    (normal_low & training_reason).sum()
    / max(normal_low.sum(), 1)
)

high_overtime_exposure = audit["overtime_risk"].eq("high").mean()

# A more useful "avoidable exposure" indicator requires replaying the
# candidate set at the historical assignment timestamp and checking
# whether a qualified lower-risk alternative existed.

# -------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Rework and appeal proxies
# -------------------------------------------------------------------

audit["is_rework_activity"] = audit[ACTIVITY].eq("Rework Correction")
audit["is_appeal_event"] = audit[ACTIVITY].str.contains(
    "Appeal", case=False, na=False
)

case_outcomes = (
    audit.groupby(CASE, as_index=False)
    .agg(
        had_rework=("is_rework_activity", "max"),
        had_appeal=("is_appeal_event", "max"),
        max_reassignments=("reassignment_count", "max"),
        complexity=("case_complexity", "max"),
        priority=("case_priority", "last"),
    )
)

outcomes_by_reassignment_band = (
    case_outcomes.assign(
        reassignment_band=pd.cut(
            case_outcomes["max_reassignments"],
            bins=[-1, 0, 2, np.inf],
            labels=["0", "1-2", ">2"],
        )
    )
    .groupby("reassignment_band", observed=True)
    .agg(
        cases=(CASE, "nunique"),
        rework_rate=("had_rework", "mean"),
        appeal_rate=("had_appeal", "mean"),
    )
    .reset_index()
)

print("Handover frequency:", handover_frequency)
print("Training-supported share:", training_assignment_share)
print("High overtime exposure:", high_overtime_exposure)
print("Hard-rule violations:", int(audit["hard_rule_violation"].sum()))
print(outcomes_by_reassignment_band)
```

`get_service_time` computes service time by activity when suitable start and completion information is available, while `insert_case_service_waiting_time` adds case service, waiting, and sojourn measures. With only a single timestamp per event, inter-event durations must be labelled as waiting or sojourn proxies rather than true active service. `discover_batches` identifies repeated activity execution by a resource within a time window. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][3])

Performance DFGs can identify slow transitions around Security, Approval, Rework, or handovers. Variants can expose repeated diagnosis, rework, and appeal paths. Event-attribute and case-performance filters support controlled comparisons by activity, priority, assignment reason, and elapsed case duration. ([Process Intelligence Solutions][2])

For learning the policy, the PM4Py outputs should be combined with transparent statistical models:

* Estimate p50 and p80 service time by activity, complexity band, skill level, workload band, and assignment mode.
* Estimate waiting added by ownership transfer separately from executor-only collaboration.
* Estimate rework and appeal risk after controlling for priority, complexity, prior reassignment count, and activity.
* Use resource similarity and role discovery to identify candidates for review, never to grant authorization.
* Replay historical decisions using the exact skill and workload snapshot available at the time.
* Evaluate the policy at team and process level rather than turning case duration into an unadjusted individual productivity score.
