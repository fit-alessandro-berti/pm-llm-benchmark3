## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk**: If `risk_score` misses critical risk dimensions that the flag algorithm captures, suppressing the flag (P1) or making review discretionary (P2) causes high-risk cases to skip necessary review/escalation, worsening decision quality and reversing the recommendation that P2 is safe.
2. **Queue interference reversal**: If shared-queue dynamics are such that flagged cases actually reduce others’ waits (e.g., by triggering staff reallocation or enabling batching), then reducing flagged reviews under P2 could lengthen waits for unflagged cases, reversing the expected positive spillover.
3. **Decision quality reversal**: If manual review primarily corrects false approvals and escalation prevents severe errors, the faster cycle times under P2 come at the cost of increased false approvals/reject errors, reversing the quality-risk assessment and making P0 or P3 preferable despite longer cycles.
4. **Timestamp semantics reversal**: If `triage_done` for flagged cases marks preliminary tagging rather than completed triage work, and actual triage labor continues into the `manual_start` interval, then the observed “faster triage” is artifactual; the mechanism shifts to hidden service overlap, undermining the priority-lane interpretation.
5. **Positivity / overlap failure**: If no unflagged cases share the same `complexity` and `risk_score` profile (e.g., all high-complexity, high-risk cases are flagged), comparing flag=0 vs 1 within strata is impossible; all observed differences become purely confounded by case mix, reversing any causal claim.
6. **Behavior change by reviewers**: If reviewers work faster or more carefully on flagged cases solely because the visible flag triggers a Hawthorne or labeling effect (not because of queue priority), then suppressing the flag (P1) changes `manual_service` and `triage_service` directly, not just routing; this reverses the interpretation that organizational pathways (priority, mandatory review) drive the observed delays.

## 9. Final synthesis

**Directly observed:** Flagged cases (`n=10`) start triage in approximately 14 minutes versus 2 hours 29 minutes for unflagged (`n=10`) and finish triage in about 36 minutes versus 57 minutes. Despite this acceleration, flagged total cycle time is about 16.5 hours versus 8.2 hours for unflagged. All flagged cases enter manual review versus 40% of unflagged; 60% of flagged are escalated versus 20% of unflagged. Queue intervals after triage are long: about 6.5 hours to manual start for flagged (all present) versus 1 hour 53 minutes for the four unflagged with review; escalation waits average about 7 hours with 2 hours of senior service. High-complexity flagged cases reach about 22 hours; medium-complexity flagged cases are closer to 10.6 hours, suggesting case mix matters.

**Plausible mechanism:** The flag operates as a policy bundle, not a single causal lever. One pathway is direct: the priority triage lane accelerates arrival-to-start and service. Another is mediated through occurrence: the flag routes most cases into manual review, which then feeds escalation. A third is queue-mediated: shared manual and senior pools mean that high volumes of flagged cases extend waiting times for all in those pools, especially under high `arrival_load`. Resource assignment (`T2`, `M2`) and complexity (`High` vs `Medium`) further confound comparisons because they are not randomly distributed across flag status.

**What remains unidentified:** The counterfactual cycle time for flagged cases if they received standard triage but discretionary review (P2) is unknown; we have not decomposed how much delay is due to review occurrence versus queue interference versus reviewer behavior. Unobserved risk (`U_unobserved_risk`) may drive both flag assignment and reviewer effort, and timestamp interpretation is ambiguous: long `triage_done``manual_start` gaps include overnight hours, mixing true queue delay with off-hours closure. Without cluster-randomized variation or local-threshold identification, natural direct and indirect effects are not recoverable.

**Intervention for next evidence:** A cluster-randomized pilot of P2—retaining priority triage but removing mandatory manual review, with guardrails (`risk_score`  0.90 mandatory, protected senior slot, audit sample)—produces the most useful evidence. It isolates the occurrence-mediation pathway while preserving speed, allows measurement of spillover to unflagged queues, and includes quality safeguards. Comparing P2 to P0 within the same organizational context, with full timestamp and queue-state data, can separate routing effects from case-mix confounding far better than observational decomposition.
