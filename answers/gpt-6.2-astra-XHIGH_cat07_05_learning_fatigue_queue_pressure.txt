# 2. Integrated explanation

There are two bends in the shift profile. Early on, service gets faster **and** quality improves. The first few positions include complex, high-priority carryover. As that backlog clears, workers can finish subsequent cases faster even with unchanged skill. Repeated work may also help them settle into an activity. The amount of warm-up is the early improvement that survives a comparison of similar assignments under similar conditions.

Later, service keeps falling while quality reverses direction. Between positions 31–50 and 76+, mean service falls from 12.9 to 10.8 minutes and measured complexity falls further, yet the mean queue grows from 31 to 88, errors rise from 2.7% to 7.9%, and seven-day rework rises from 5.0% to 13.7%. Pressure is the best-supported explanation for this divergence because the same speed–quality pattern appears *within both complexity bands*. Later junior error rates are 4.4% at low WIP and 12.4% at high WIP. Seniors also show a pressure gradient, from 2.2% to 4.9%. That points to a shared operating condition with different consequences across assignments and experience levels. A shorter recorded touch can also create more work before a claim is finally resolved.

Elapsed work and clock hour may modify that pressure response. Day ends with a mean queue of 75 and 7.5% errors in the final two hours. Early ends with a queue of 42 and 4.2% errors over the corresponding hours on duty. Equal scheduled shift lengths can thus end under quite different conditions. Selective routing, batch recording and delayed checks influence the size and timing of the observed differences. The summaries support testing a combination of mechanisms but cannot apportion the changes causally.

# 3. Proposed analyses

1. **Build the event and case timelines.** Use service start for position, elapsed time, clock hour and exposure to WIP or queue length. Use completion minus start for recorded service. Verify timezone, overnight shift membership, ties, negative durations, rounded or shared timestamps, and overlapping human intervals. Check whether the recorded sequence counts an individual's starts or a whole team's starts. Preserve full case traces across shifts, including Bot_Risk and subsequent quality checks. Analyze human service separately. A claim's waiting time, elapsed time and sum of event durations answer different questions, especially when work overlaps.
2. **Estimate comparable within-worker contrasts.** For each worker, compare positions 1–5 with 16–30 and 31–50 with later bins within activity, shift type, WIP band, complexity and priority. Show event counts, distinct workers and shifts per cell. Standardize to a shared distribution of activity, continuous complexity, priority and carryover status. Use information available before service. Repeat on workers and shifts represented in both bins. Report how much of the raw change survives and how many shifts the restriction excludes. Reaching position 76 depends partly on pace, task duration and batching.
3. **Model speed and quality separately.** Fit a log-time or median regression for positive human durations, with a separate analysis of recorded zeros. Fit event-level binary models for errors and seven-day rework. Include worker effects, activity, case mix, calendar day or weekday, inherited backlog and shift type. Allow nonlinear effects of position or minutes since start. Compare the position model with the elapsed-time model, then estimate their separate contributions only where pace varies enough. At a given elapsed time, position can distinguish many short tasks from a few long ones. Report adjusted minutes and percentage-point differences, with uncertainty accounting for repeated cases and worker-shifts.
4. **Separate pressure, experience and clock time where data allow.** Plot the position curves within low (<30), medium (30–70), and high (>70) WIP, then use continuous WIP to see whether risk rises gradually or changes near 70. Repeat for queue length, which counts waiting work under the local queue definition. Examine pressure by complexity, pressure by experience in months, and pressure by elapsed time. Estimate how each worker's pressure response varies with experience, allowing for differences in assignments. Compare Early and Day at 10:00, for example, four hours versus one hour into duty. Use local clock-hour curves that wrap at midnight. Clock time equals shift start plus elapsed time, so unrestricted effects for all three are not separately identifiable. Limit claims to supported contrasts, especially for dedicated Night staff. Compare models before and after pressure adjustment, since pressure may be one route through which late-shift conditions affect quality.
5. **Trace defects to their source.** Follow each eligible index decision forward seven days across handovers. Count it once for rework if a linked corrective event or validated rework flag occurs. Use decisions with a full follow-up window or a censoring-aware analysis. Also report distinct affected claims. Keep original decision time and resource, detection time, correction time and resource, and bot involvement. When several decisions could be responsible, retain an unattributed category. Report review coverage and detection lag by shift and pressure. Independently sampled audits can estimate missed errors when routine checks are deferred or selective.

# 4. Intervention options

| Proposed action | Expected benefit and decision | Risk | Event-log impact measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Strictly cap shift length for all | A cap could reduce sustained exposure if actual shifts overrun. First check actual duty spans against the eight-hour schedules and locate any excess risk. | Additional handovers or an understaffed peak could move the backlog and errors to the next shift. | Compare errors by elapsed hour at similar load, overtime exposure using roster data, handover waits and next-shift opening queue. |
| 2. Move complex cases away from the first hour | Selective, staffed routing could give difficult carryover timely specialist attention. | Blanket deferral can delay urgent claims and move complex work into the afternoon pressure peak. | Track time to first qualified touch, priority deadline breaches, age of carryover and seven-day outcomes for complex claims across both shifts. |
| 3. Alert when WIP >70 | Pilot an alert with a named responder who can add coverage or rebalance work. High WIP and high complexity coincide with 10.9% errors. | Repeated alerts can add pressure to hurry. The coarse bands do not establish an optimal threshold. | Measure alert frequency, response time, minutes above threshold, queue age, and adjusted error and rework rates before and after response. |
| 4. Force random assignment | Random allocation among equally eligible workers can help estimate selection effects and distribute comparable work. | Unrestricted allocation can violate skill requirements, disrupt continuity, or delay urgent cases. | In a restricted pilot, check balance of incoming complexity and priority, transfers, deadline breaches and risk-adjusted quality. |
| 5. Mandate micro-breaks in the last two hours | Pilot staggered, covered breaks to test whether recovery improves end-of-shift accuracy. | Simultaneous breaks can raise queues and transfer the load to colleagues. | Link actual breaks to matched pre/post decision cohorts. Track audited errors, rework, team WIP and covering workers' loads. |
| 6. Defer low-priority quality checks | Redistributing scheduled checks could smooth the end-of-shift burst if the receiving shift has capacity and a review deadline. | Delayed discovery can allow a wrong decision to stand, inflate inherited backlog and make current-shift error counts look better. Preserve required coverage and audit sampling. | Measure review coverage, time from decision to check and correction, age of unchecked claims, next-shift load and mature defect rates by decision cohort. |
| 7. Add a senior reviewer at Day peaks | Pilot added review capacity when Day demand rises. Target complex decisions and escalations where delay or an error is costly. | Extra sign-offs can become a bottleneck, or relocating a senior can expose another queue. | Compare matched peak periods on arrivals, WIP, time to decision, complex-case defects and rework. Track waits and quality in any team supplying coverage. |
| 8. Penalize higher late-shift error rates | Managers may seek accountability. Use reviewed cases for coaching and system improvement. Raw rates provide no defensible basis for penalties. | Punishment encourages under-reporting and avoidance of hard cases and penalizes workers assigned to pressured queues. | Compare reported errors with independently audited defects, review coverage, changes in routing and complex-case acceptance. Investigate team-level patterns with workers. |

For pilots, compare matched shifts or stagger implementation across eligible teams, accounting for arrivals and opening backlog. Follow claims across the pilot boundary for seven days and measure waiting in adjacent queues so that displaced work counts in the evaluation.

# 5. Ethical and operational cautions

Assignment is part of the process being evaluated. A senior accepting a difficult weekend carryover, a junior reviewing a batch, and a night worker handling an urgent exception face different opportunities for both error and detection. Position 76+ disproportionately represents shifts that reached that volume. Worker estimates need comparable assignments, sufficient observations and uncertainty intervals. Unmeasured complexity can still defeat adjustment.

Recorded time may reflect bulk entry, multitasking or handover. A worker who corrects an error can appear in the log as the last person to touch a defective case. Likewise, a higher override rate may reveal diligent review of Bot_Risk. Verify attribution against traces and sample audits, including uncorrected bot decisions. Give workers a way to correct erroneous records and explain local practices.

Staffing, routing, throughput targets and review schedules are management decisions that shape queue pressure. Examine those decisions alongside individual work. Restrict identifiable data to those responsible for case review, suppress small cells in shared views and avoid worker rankings or punitive use of raw rates. Track whether a change improves outcomes for claimants as well as conditions for the workers inheriting the queue.

# 6. Monitoring dashboard specification

Show operational load in 15-minute intervals and quality by **originating decision cohort**, with seven-day maturity marked. Provide filters for shift, local clock hour, elapsed-time and position bins, activity, experience group, complexity, priority, pressure band and human/bot origin. Show counts and uncertainty beside rates, and raw and case-mix-standardized trends where comparisons have adequate support.

| Indicator | Definition and use |
|---|---|
| Human service time | Median and 90th percentile of completion minus start per human event, by activity and position. Show zero-time and batched shares separately so changes in recording are visible. |
| Waiting time | Median and 90th percentile from ready-for-service to service start. Where ready time is missing, label predecessor-completion to next-start gaps on sequential paths as a proxy. Report carryover age and priority deadline breaches separately. |
| WIP and queue pressure | Plot open work and ready-but-waiting work with their operational definitions, arrival and completion counts, and opening and closing backlog. Report share of starts above 70 for each measure. Time above threshold requires timed snapshots or reconstructed queue history. |
| Error rate and review coverage | Flagged eligible decisions divided by eligible decisions, by origin time. Alongside this show fraction reviewed, defects per reviewed decision and independently audited defect rate, with denominators. |
| Seven-day rework | Mature eligible decisions with at least one linked correction within seven days divided by all mature eligible decisions. Display affected claim count, correction minutes and detection lag. |
| Incoming case mix | Mean and distribution of pre-start complexity, high-priority share and inherited-case share by shift phase and assignment group. Include missing-score share. |
| Batching | Fraction of Document Review starts in confirmed batches, median run size and within-run service time. Separately show fraction of Quality Checks completed in the final 45 minutes and the age of decisions checked. |
| Handovers and queue ownership | From assignment history, count ownership changes per claim and measure time to the receiving team's first touch. With only the supplied fields, use cross-resource or cross-shift transitions as a labeled proxy and distinguish planned activity transitions. |
| Worker-load fairness | Compare distributions of high-complexity, urgent and high-WIP assignments per staffed hour across similarly qualified workers. Use roster hours for exposure and show counts of workers and shifts. Examine concentration of inherited work within each group. |
| Automation quality | For bot-origin classifications, show human review coverage, overrides per reviewed classification, audited defect rate and correction effort by queue pressure at classification and review. |
| End-to-end resolution | Show elapsed time from receipt to final resolution, its 90th percentile, and claims resolved without seven-day rework per staffed hour. Track this beside touch time to reveal the cost of faster handling that produces repeat work. |
