# 1. Fairness dimensions  

1. Outcome parity  
   • Raw approval rates differ (78 % vs. 58 %). These numbers alone cannot diagnose unfairness because groups differ in documented risk, documentation completeness, etc.  
2. Adjusted outcome disparity  
   • After controlling for known covariates, Groups B, C, D still face 2–6 pp lower approval probability. These residual gaps may reflect remaining bias, unobserved complexity, or noise.  
3. Procedural burden (number of activities, handovers, rework)  
   • Groups B and C go through more activities (V2, V4, V5) and more handovers; re-open/rework rates are almost double those of Group A.  
4. Timeliness  
   • Even after adjustment, Groups B ( +2.6 d) and C ( +4.1 d) wait longer. Waiting concentrates in queues that are supposed to help them (language support, accommodation).  
5. Consistency of routing  
   • Legitimate rules explain part of the routing (riskfraud review, languagesupport). However, 24 % of Group B cases enter V4 although only 57 % request language support—suggesting over-routing.  
6. Quality / accessibility of support  
   • Language support and accommodation steps lack explicit service-level targets; small specialized teams create bottlenecks.  
7. Appeal / rework burden  
   • Appeal rates are higher for Groups B and D even after adjustment (+4–6 pp). Delays and handovers may undermine perceived fairness.  
8. Resource-allocation fairness  
   • Group B is funneled to seven bilingual caseworkers and three overloaded officers, raising workload and delay equity issues; Group A is spread across 52 workers.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy  

1. Matching / stratification  
   ```python
   controls = ["risk_score_band","documentation_completeness","channel",
               "household_size_band","prior_case_count","region","month"]
   ```  
   Propensity-score matching or coarsened exact matching to compare similar cases across groups.  
2. Path / variant comparison  
   • After matching, compare probability of entering each variant or activity (e.g., `Request Missing Documents`) using logistic regression with cluster-robust SEs.  
3. Resource-assignment comparison  
   • For matched cases, test whether group predicts assignment to particular resources, high-workload resources, or higher handover count.  
4. Waiting-time decomposition  
   • Insert service and waiting times (pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time) then run Oaxaca-Blinder-style decomposition: how much of the cycle-time gap is explained by (a) extra activities, (b) slower service at the same activity, (c) waits for specialist resources.  
5. Sensitivity analysis  
   • Vary the functional form and omit potentially biased covariates (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness) to see whether disparities widen.  
6. Subgroup checks  
   • Run the whole analysis separately by region and by submission channel to detect localized or channel-specific issues.

# 3. Path and resource disparities  

1. Extra steps  
   • 34 % of Group B follows V2 vs. 24 % of Group A; 24 % of Group B follows V4 although only 57 % request language support, hinting at possible over-use of the support path.  
2. Specialist queues as bottlenecks  
   • Language support adds median 3.8 d; accommodation review adds 4.7 d. These waits explain most of Groups B and C extra cycle time.  
3. Handovers  
   • Group B experiences generalbilingualgeneral pattern in two regions, increasing coordination cost and error risk.  
4. Resource load  
   • Seven bilingual workers carry 1 200+ Group B cases (170 each) vs. 52 general workers with 70 each—objective capacity gap.  
5. Supervisor review  
   • After controls, Group B still +8 pp likelihood of Supervisor Review; some may be legitimate (language or policy check) but the magnitude suggests inconsistent practice across groups.  
Conclusion: part of the disparity is justified by declared rules, but capacity constraints and inconsistent routing likely create avoidable inequities.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms  

Legitimate  
1. Higher measured risk (Group D)  Fraud Review  V3 path; adds delay but aligns with policy.  
2. Disability accommodation requests (Group C)  Accommodation Review  V5; adds steps but required by law.

Concerning or uncertain  
3. Capacity shortage among bilingual and accommodation staff  queues that penalize the very groups the support is meant to help.  
4. Over-routing of Group B to language support even when language_preference is majority language (possible stereotyping by intake staff).  
5. Additional Supervisor Review triggered by unfamiliar document formats from minority-language or accommodation cases (could be bias or training gap).  
6. Handovers in two regions driven by organizational silo rather than policy, increasing processing errors for Groups B and C.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes  

• Risk_score_band may embed historic bias; treating it as a control may "explain away" discrimination.  
• Documentation_completeness is recorded at intake; language barriers may drive incompleteness, so controlling for it could mask unfairness.  
• org:resource only at daily granularity; we cannot see true workload or intra-day prioritization.  
• case_outcome lacks qualitative dimension (e.g., sufficiency of explanation, respectful treatment).  
• disability_accommodation_requested is binary; we don’t know type or urgency.  
• No direct measure of applicant socio-economic status or digital literacy.  
Hence, residual bias could still hide behind “explained” factors.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects  

1. Add bilingual and accommodation capacity  
   + Cuts waiting time; – Budget cost, risk of under-utilization if demand fluctuates.  
2. Introduce service-level targets for support steps (e.g., 2 d for Language Support Contact queue)  
   + Makes rights-preserving steps time-bound; – May shift delay elsewhere if downstream capacity untouched.  
3. Routing redesign to reduce handovers  
   + Single-owner model for cases needing language or accommodation; – Requires cross-training, may initially slow overall throughput.  
4. Embedded decision-explanation templates in multiple languages  
   + Could lower appeal rates; – Development cost, requires maintenance with policy changes.  
5. Continuous disparity monitoring (after-controls)  
   + Early warning for emerging inequities; – Risk of focusing on metrics over substantive fairness.  
6. Audit risk_score_band and documentation rules for bias  
   + Could reveal systemic issues; – Complex, may yield politically sensitive findings.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment  

The evidence suggests that, after accounting for observable complexity, minority-language applicants (Group B) and applicants needing disability accommodation (Group C) experience materially higher procedural burden and slower service, largely driven by capacity bottlenecks in the very support mechanisms intended to protect them. Some of the delay for Group D aligns with higher recorded risk, but the reliance on a possibly biased risk score tempers that conclusion. Because part of the disparities remain unexplained after adjustment—and because support-step queues and handovers appear avoidable—the process cannot be declared procedurally fair. At the same time, legitimate routing rules and data limitations prevent us from definitively labeling it discriminatory. The current assessment: procedurally concerning, warranting remediation and ongoing audit rather than outright condemnation.
