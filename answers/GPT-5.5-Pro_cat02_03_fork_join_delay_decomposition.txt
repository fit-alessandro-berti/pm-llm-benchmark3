# 1. Delay-decomposition definitions

Let case be indexed by (c), and branch (i \in {A,B,C}).

Use these timestamps:

* (a_c): process arrival
* (f_c): fork release
* (s_{c,i}): branch (i) start
* (e_{c,i}): branch (i) completion
* (S^F_c): final activity start
* (E^F_c): final activity completion

All durations below are in minutes.

**Upstream elapsed before fork release**

[
U_c = f_c - a_c
]

This is elapsed time before the parallel branch work is allowed to begin.

**Branch-specific queue waiting**

[
Q_{c,i} = s_{c,i} - f_c
]

This captures branch-local delay after the fork is released but before branch (i) starts. It is not synchronization waiting.

**Branch active service**

[
V_{c,i} = e_{c,i} - s_{c,i}
]

This is elapsed active interval for the branch, using the given start and complete timestamps. With only interval timestamps, this is elapsed service time, not necessarily hands-on effort.

**Branch completion offset from fork**

[
X_{c,i} = e_{c,i} - f_c = Q_{c,i} + V_{c,i}
]

This is the branch’s full contribution to readiness for the join, including both branch queue and service.

**Join-ready time**

Because F may start only after all three branches have completed:

[
J_c = \max_{i \in {A,B,C}} e_{c,i}
]

The critical branch set is:

[
K_c = {i : e_{c,i} = J_c}
]

In these observed data there are no critical-branch ties, but ties are possible in principle.

**Branch-specific synchronization waiting after completion**

[
W^{sync}*{c,i} = J_c - e*{c,i}
]

A branch with (W^{sync}_{c,i}=0) is critical or tied critical. A positive value means the completed branch waited for another branch to finish.

**Elapsed fork-join stage time**

[
L^{FJ}*c = J_c - f_c = \max_i (Q*{c,i} + V_{c,i})
]

This is the elapsed duration of the parallel stage. It is not the sum of the three branch elapsed durations.

**Downstream queueing before F**

[
D_c = S^F_c - J_c
]

This is downstream queueing only under the stated process model: F is allowed immediately once all branches are complete. If there is an unlogged release rule, batching rule, priority policy, or resource calendar, this value may not be a pure queue.

**Final service**

[
R_c = E^F_c - S^F_c
]

**Total case flow time**

[
T_c = E^F_c - a_c
]

The additive elapsed-time decomposition is:

[
T_c = U_c + L^{FJ}_c + D_c + R_c
]

Equivalently:

[
T_c = (f_c-a_c) + (\max_i e_{c,i}-f_c) + (S^F_c-J_c) + (E^F_c-S^F_c)
]

**Why summing all branch synchronization waits double counts elapsed time**

For each branch:

[
Q_{c,i} + V_{c,i} + W^{sync}*{c,i}
= (s*{c,i}-f_c) + (e_{c,i}-s_{c,i}) + (J_c-e_{c,i})
= J_c-f_c
= L^{FJ}_c
]

So if we sum this expression over A, B, and C:

[
\sum_i (Q_{c,i}+V_{c,i}+W^{sync}_{c,i})
= 3 \times L^{FJ}_c
]

That would count the same parallel calendar time three times. Synchronization waits are therefore diagnostic branch-level quantities, not additive case-flow components. The elapsed fork-join contribution to the case is the maximum branch completion offset, not the sum of branch queues, services, and synchronization waits.

# 3. Aggregate critical-path analysis

Descriptive statistics are based on 12 cases, so the tail values should be interpreted as descriptive rather than stable estimates.

| branch | mean service | p90 service | mean queue | p90 queue | mean queue+service from fork | p90 queue+service from fork | critical frequency | mean sync wait | p90 sync wait | influence assessment                                                                             |
| :----- | -----------: | ----------: | ---------: | --------: | ---------------------------: | --------------------------: | :----------------- | -------------: | ------------: | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| A      |         50.0 |        50.0 |        0.0 |       0.0 |                         50.0 |                        50.0 | 2/12               |           11.0 |          24.0 | Longest service, but not usually latest because it has no queue. Important secondary constraint. |
| B      |         37.5 |        45.0 |       19.2 |      30.0 |                         56.7 |                        74.0 | 8/12               |            4.3 |          16.3 | Strongest controller: most often critical and highest tail completion from fork.                 |
| C      |         20.2 |        21.8 |       10.4 |      24.0 |                         30.6 |                        53.0 | 2/12               |           30.4 |          51.0 | Usually finishes early and waits, but queue spikes make it critical in F04 and F08.              |

The strongest controller of total completion time is **B, the Machine Stress Test**, but not because B has the largest service time. It does not: A has a constant 50-minute service time, while B’s mean service is only 37.5 minutes. B controls because its queue plus service is usually larger. B is critical in 8 of 12 cases, has the highest mean completion offset from fork, and has the highest tail completion offset from fork.

A and C are secondary but in different ways. A is structurally long: it always consumes 50 minutes after fork release and becomes the replacement bottleneck when B is improved in several cases. C is usually not influential, as shown by its high mean synchronization wait of 30.4 minutes, but it creates local tail risk when its queue is large. F04 and F08 are the important examples: C controls the join despite being the administrative branch with short service.

The observed final-station queue also matters. Every case has positive downstream queueing before F, with a mean of 14.7 minutes and a maximum of 20 minutes. Therefore, branch improvements do not automatically become final completion improvements. If the final station behaves as a fixed downstream queue or release policy, earlier join-ready times may merely increase waiting before F. If the final station is responsive and can start cases earlier, then branch-side gains can reduce total flow time.

A useful sensitivity check is a 20% branch-service acceleration while leaving starts, queues, final service, and downstream queueing policy otherwise unchanged. Under that mechanical assumption, reducing A service by 20% saves 15 total join-ready minutes across all cases; reducing B service by 20% would save 62 total join-ready minutes; reducing C service by 20% would save about 9.4 total join-ready minutes. This reinforces the main conclusion: **B is more influential than A even though A has the largest mean service time**.

Influence ranking:

1. **B: highest influence.** It is critical in two-thirds of cases, has the largest queue-plus-service tail, and B improvements affect many cases.
2. **A: secondary structural constraint.** It ties C on observed critical frequency, but its constant 50-minute duration makes it the next bottleneck once B improves.
3. **C: episodic tail-risk constraint.** It is usually early, but administrative queue spikes can dominate individual cases.

The ranking between A and C should not be overstated. They are tied on observed critical frequency, but their operational meanings differ: A is a persistent near-critical technical duration; C is a less frequent administrative queue spike.

# 4. Counterfactual redesign analysis

Counterfactuals below are first-order mechanical calculations. They recompute the fork-join maximum and/or downstream queue while holding other quantities fixed. This is useful for identifying bottleneck logic, but it does not prove what would happen after real operational changes.

| counterfactual                      | direct modeled effect              | nonzero direct-benefit cases                                    | zero direct-benefit cases | total modeled saving | mean saving per case | max saving | main interpretation                                                          |
| :---------------------------------- | :--------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------ | -------------------: | -------------------: | ---------: | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Reduce A service time by 20%        | A service becomes 40 instead of 50 | F01 saves 5; F11 saves 10                                       | 10 cases                  |                 15.0 |                 1.25 |       10.0 | Weak aggregate effect because A is critical in only 2 cases.                 |
| Reduce B queue delay by 50%         | B queue is halved before B starts  | F02 10; F03 10; F05 10; F06 15; F07 15; F09 12.5; F10 5; F12 15 | F01, F04, F08, F11        |                 92.5 |                 7.71 |       15.0 | Strong branch-side effect because B is commonly critical.                    |
| Eliminate downstream queue before F | F starts at join-ready             | All cases benefit                                               | None                      |                176.0 |                14.67 |       20.0 | Largest direct flow-time effect, if the waiting is truly removable queueing. |

**Counterfactual 1: reduce A service time by 20%**

A’s service falls from 50 minutes to 40 minutes. The direct fork-join benefit appears only when A was critical or close enough to critical.

F01 saves 5 minutes, not 10, because after A is shortened, B becomes the latest branch. F11 saves 10 minutes, after which A and B tie at the join. The remaining 10 cases receive zero branch-side benefit because B or C remains later than A.

This is the central warning against equating largest service time with strongest control. A has the largest service time, but most cases are waiting for B, not A.

A likely secondary effect is that A acceleration can increase A’s synchronization wait in cases where B or C remains critical. Operationally, staff may see A finish earlier, but the case still cannot proceed. Also, if final starts remain fixed, even the 15 minutes of join-ready improvement may be absorbed as longer downstream queueing rather than shorter final completion time.

**Counterfactual 2: reduce B queue delay by 50%**

This is more powerful because B’s influence comes from queue plus service. The total direct fork-join saving is 92.5 minutes across 12 cases.

Cases F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, and F12 benefit. Cases F01, F04, F08, and F11 do not benefit because another branch is critical: A in F01 and F11, C in F04 and F08.

This redesign changes the bottleneck pattern. In F05 and F10, A becomes critical after B’s queue is reduced. In F02 and F06, A and B tie. That means B-queue reduction is highly valuable, but it will expose A as the next constraint in some cases.

The likely secondary effect is increased synchronization waiting for B in cases where A or C becomes critical. It may also shift congestion to the final station if more cases become join-ready earlier.

**Counterfactual 3: add final-station capacity or otherwise eliminate downstream queueing**

If every case could start F immediately at join-ready, total modeled saving equals the current downstream queue. The total saving is 176 minutes, with every case benefiting. The largest case-level savings are F10 and F11, each at 20 minutes.

This counterfactual has the largest direct total-flow effect in the table. However, it depends on a strong assumption: that `final_start - join_ready` is truly avoidable queueing. If the delay reflects a policy hold, batching rule, missing unlogged control, resource calendar, priority decision, or other cases outside the supplied data, simply adding capacity may not eliminate it.

This redesign would also make the fork-join bottleneck more visible. Once downstream queueing is removed, B’s branch completion would more directly control final start for most cases.

**Masking by final-station congestion**

For branch-side counterfactuals, there are two defensible interpretations:

* Under a responsive final-station assumption, earlier join-ready can move final start earlier, so branch-side join savings become total-flow savings.
* Under a fixed final-start or congested-release assumption, branch improvements may produce no observed final-end reduction; they merely increase `downstream_queue`.

Because all observed cases have positive downstream queueing, this masking risk is material. Therefore, B improvement and final-station redesign should be evaluated together rather than independently.

# 5. Recommended redesigns and risks

**Redesign 1: reduce B-branch queueing before the Machine Stress Test**

The most targeted branch redesign is to reduce B queueing, not necessarily B service time. B’s mean queue is 19.2 minutes, its p90 queue is 30 minutes, and B is critical in 8 of 12 cases. A 50% reduction in B queue has a modeled direct saving of 92.5 join-ready minutes, much larger than the 15 minutes from reducing A service by 20%.

Operationally, this could mean reserving B capacity shortly after fork release, changing dispatch priority so B does not wait behind less urgent work, smoothing releases into B, or cross-training enough technical capacity to prevent B queue spikes. The exact intervention cannot be chosen from these timestamps alone, because B resource calendars and competing workload are not observed.

Expected throughput effect: cases should become join-ready earlier, especially the current B-critical cases. If B is a true capacity bottleneck, reducing its queue should improve effective throughput through the fork-join stage.

Expected flow-time tail effect: this should reduce the long fork-join cases, especially F07 and F12, where B completion from fork is 75 minutes. It should also reduce synchronization waiting for A and C in B-critical cases.

Resource-utilization risk: if B capacity is increased too much, B may become underutilized while A becomes the next bottleneck. The counterfactual already shows A becoming critical or tied critical in several improved-B cases.

Possible new bottlenecks: A is the likely next technical bottleneck because its 50-minute service is constant. C remains a sporadic administrative queue risk in cases like F04 and F08. The final station may also absorb the gains as downstream queueing.

Synchronization effect: reducing B queue reduces waiting by already-completed A and C branches in B-critical cases. But if A becomes critical after B improves, B may itself start waiting at the join.

What to pilot or measure first: measure B resource availability, B queue length at fork release, competing workload, dispatch rules, and whether B start delay is capacity-driven or policy-driven. During a pilot, track the critical-branch distribution before and after the change. A successful B intervention should reduce B-critical frequency, but if total flow does not improve, the final station is probably masking the gain.

**Redesign 2: reduce downstream queueing before Release Decision F**

The second redesign should address `final_start - join_ready`, because every case waits after all branches are complete. The mean downstream queue is 14.7 minutes, with a maximum of 20 minutes. Eliminating it mechanically saves 176 minutes across 12 cases, more than the branch-side counterfactuals evaluated here.

This does not automatically mean “hire more final resources.” The data say the final station has two resources, F1 and F2, but the timestamps alone do not prove that both are continuously available or that there is no outside work. The downstream delay could also be due to batching, a release policy, priority handling, or an unlogged control. The redesign should therefore start with queue-discipline and release-policy diagnosis before capacity is added.

Expected throughput effect: if F is a real capacity bottleneck, more effective final capacity or better scheduling should improve throughput after the join. If the delay is a policy hold, capacity will not help unless the policy changes.

Expected flow-time tail effect: reducing downstream queue directly shortens all observed cases, including F10 and F11, which each wait 20 minutes before F. It also prevents branch improvements from being hidden after join-ready.

Resource-utilization risk: adding final capacity may lower utilization if the current delays are not caused by capacity scarcity. It may create idle final resources waiting for B-critical cases to become join-ready.

Possible new bottlenecks: once downstream queueing is reduced, B becomes even more clearly the dominant upstream controller. In other words, final-station improvement may reveal rather than solve the fork-join bottleneck.

Synchronization effect: this redesign does not reduce branch synchronization waits. A, B, and C will still wait for the latest branch. It only reduces waiting after all branches are complete.

What to pilot or measure first: log actual final-resource calendars, other work assigned to F1 and F2, queue order, release rules, batching rules, and whether F could have started earlier for each case. A good pilot would test an “immediate pull when join-ready” rule for a subset of cases and compare actual final-start delays.

import pandas as pd
from io import StringIO
import pm4py

RAW = """case,arrival,fork_release,A_start,A_end,B_start,B_end,C_start,C_end,final_start,final_end,final_resource
F01,07:55,08:10,08:10,09:00,08:15,08:55,08:20,08:38,09:08,09:28,F1

F05,08:50,09:10,09:10,10:00,09:35,10:10,09:15,09:35,10:20,10:40,F1
F06,09:05,09:25,09:25,10:15,09:55,10:30,09:40,10:00,10:45,11:05,F2
F07,09:20,09:40,09:40,10:30,10:10,10:55,09:45,10:03,11:10,11:30,F1
F08,09:35,09:55,09:55,10:45,10:00,10:30,10:20,10:50,11:05,11:25,F2
F09,09:50,10:10,10:10,11:00,10:35,11:15,10:15,10:35,11:30,11:50,F1
F10,10:05,10:25,10:25,11:15,10:45,11:20,10:30,10:50,11:40,12:00,F2
F11,10:20,10:40,10:40,11:30,10:50,11:20,10:45,11:03,11:50,12:10,F1
F12,10:35,10:55,10:55,11:45,11:25,12:10,11:00,11:18,12:20,12:40,F2
"""

BRANCHES = ["A", "B", "C"]
BASE_DATE = "2026-05-19 "

df = pd.read_csv(StringIO(RAW.strip()))

time_cols = [
    "arrival", "fork_release",
    "A_start", "A_end",
    "B_start", "B_end",
    "C_start", "C_end",
    "final_start", "final_end",
]
for col in time_cols:
    df[col] = pd.to_datetime(BASE_DATE + df[col])

def minutes(delta):
    return delta.dt.total_seconds() / 60.0

# Core elapsed-time decomposition.
df["upstream_elapsed"] = minutes(df["fork_release"] - df["arrival"])

for br in BRANCHES:
    df[f"{br}_queue"] = minutes(df[f"{br}_start"] - df["fork_release"])
    df[f"{br}_service"] = minutes(df[f"{br}_end"] - df[f"{br}_start"])
    df[f"{br}_completion_from_fork"] = minutes(df[f"{br}_end"] - df["fork_release"])

# Fork-join quantities are custom pandas calculations.
# A standard directly-follows graph orders events by timestamps and does not itself recover
# the max-based join_ready timestamp or the synchronization waiting of completed parallel branches.
df["join_ready_dt"] = df[[f"{br}_end" for br in BRANCHES]].max(axis=1)
df["join_ready"] = df["join_ready_dt"].dt.strftime("%H:%M")
df["fork_join_elapsed"] = minutes(df["join_ready_dt"] - df["fork_release"])

for br in BRANCHES:
    df[f"{br}_sync"] = minutes(df["join_ready_dt"] - df[f"{br}_end"])

def deterministic_critical_from_values(row, value_cols):
    values = {br: row[value_cols[br]] for br in BRANCHES}
    max_value = max(values.values())
    tied = [br for br in BRANCHES if abs(values[br] - max_value) < 1e-9]
    return "+".join(tied)

df["critical_branch"] = df.apply(
    deterministic_critical_from_values,
    axis=1,
    value_cols={br: f"{br}_completion_from_fork" for br in BRANCHES},
)

df["downstream_queue"] = minutes(df["final_start"] - df["join_ready_dt"])
df["final_service"] = minutes(df["final_end"] - df["final_start"])
df["total_flow_time"] = minutes(df["final_end"] - df["arrival"])

# Compact display table.
case_cols = [
    "case", "upstream_elapsed",
    "A_queue", "A_service", "B_queue", "B_service", "C_queue", "C_service",
    "join_ready", "critical_branch", "fork_join_elapsed",
    "A_sync", "B_sync", "C_sync",
    "downstream_queue", "final_service", "total_flow_time",
]
case_table = df[case_cols].copy()
for col in case_table.columns:
    if col not in ["case", "join_ready", "critical_branch"]:
        case_table[col] = case_table[col].round(1)

print("\nCASE-LEVEL DECOMPOSITION")
print(case_table.to_string(index=False))

# Branch summary.
summary_rows = []
for br in BRANCHES:
    summary_rows.append({
        "branch": br,
        "mean_service": df[f"{br}_service"].mean(),
        "p90_service": df[f"{br}_service"].quantile(0.90),
        "mean_queue": df[f"{br}_queue"].mean(),
        "p90_queue": df[f"{br}_queue"].quantile(0.90),
        "mean_queue_plus_service": df[f"{br}_completion_from_fork"].mean(),
        "p90_queue_plus_service": df[f"{br}_completion_from_fork"].quantile(0.90),
        "critical_count": df["critical_branch"].str.split("+").apply(lambda xs: br in xs).sum(),
        "mean_sync": df[f"{br}_sync"].mean(),
        "p90_sync": df[f"{br}_sync"].quantile(0.90),
    })
branch_summary = pd.DataFrame(summary_rows)
print("\nBRANCH SUMMARY")
print(branch_summary.round(2).to_string(index=False))

def run_counterfactual(label, service_reduction=None, queue_reduction=None, downstream_reduction=0.0):
    service_reduction = service_reduction or {}
    queue_reduction = queue_reduction or {}

    cf = df[["case", "critical_branch", "fork_join_elapsed", "downstream_queue"]].copy()

    for br in BRANCHES:
        q = df[f"{br}_queue"].astype(float).copy()
        s = df[f"{br}_service"].astype(float).copy()

        if br in queue_reduction:
            q = q * (1.0 - queue_reduction[br])
        if br in service_reduction:
            s = s * (1.0 - service_reduction[br])

        cf[f"{br}_completion_cf"] = q + s

    cf["new_fork_join_elapsed"] = cf[[f"{br}_completion_cf" for br in BRANCHES]].max(axis=1)
    cf["new_critical_branch"] = cf.apply(
        deterministic_critical_from_values,
        axis=1,
        value_cols={br: f"{br}_completion_cf" for br in BRANCHES},
    )

    cf["join_saving"] = cf["fork_join_elapsed"] - cf["new_fork_join_elapsed"]

    old_downstream = cf["downstream_queue"].astype(float)
    new_downstream = old_downstream * (1.0 - downstream_reduction)
    cf["downstream_saving"] = old_downstream - new_downstream

    # This is an unmasked first-order flow saving:
    # it assumes that earlier join_ready can translate into earlier final_start,
    # unless the counterfactual explicitly changes downstream queueing.
    # If actual final_start timestamps are treated as fixed, branch-side join_saving
    # may be absorbed as additional downstream waiting instead of lowering final_end.
    cf["unmasked_flow_saving"] = cf["join_saving"] + cf["downstream_saving"]

    per_case = cf[
        [
            "case", "critical_branch", "new_critical_branch",
            "join_saving", "downstream_saving", "unmasked_flow_saving",
        ]
    ].copy()

    aggregate = pd.DataFrame([{
        "counterfactual": label,
        "total_join_saving": cf["join_saving"].sum(),
        "total_downstream_saving": cf["downstream_saving"].sum(),
        "total_unmasked_flow_saving": cf["unmasked_flow_saving"].sum(),
        "mean_unmasked_flow_saving": cf["unmasked_flow_saving"].mean(),
        "max_unmasked_flow_saving": cf["unmasked_flow_saving"].max(),
        "zero_benefit_cases": int((cf["unmasked_flow_saving"].abs() < 1e-9).sum()),
    }])

    return per_case, aggregate

counterfactual_specs = [
    ("A service -20%", {"A": 0.20}, {}, 0.0),
    ("B queue -50%", {}, {"B": 0.50}, 0.0),
    ("Downstream queue eliminated", {}, {}, 1.0),
]

all_aggregates = []
for label, service_red, queue_red, downstream_red in counterfactual_specs:
    per_case, aggregate = run_counterfactual(
        label,
        service_reduction=service_red,

    )

    print(f"\nCOUNTERFACTUAL PER-CASE: {label}")
    print(per_case.round(2).to_string(index=False))

print("\nCOUNTERFACTUAL AGGREGATES")
print(pd.concat(all_aggregates, ignore_index=True).round(2).to_string(index=False))

# 7. Limits of inference

These timestamps support a clean mechanical delay decomposition, but they do not identify the full operational mechanism.

First, causality is not identifiable. B is most often critical, but the data do not prove why B queues are long. The cause could be insufficient B capacity, dispatching rules, batch releases, resource calendars, priority rules, rework outside the table, or another unobserved workload.

Second, branch resource capacity cannot be inferred. The table does not provide resources for A, B, or C, nor does it provide competing cases outside this sample. Therefore, queue delay cannot be safely translated into utilization or staffing requirements.

Third, service intervals may include preemption, interruptions, or passive waiting. The timestamps are start-to-completion intervals accurate to one minute. They measure elapsed interval service, not necessarily continuous hands-on processing effort.

Fourth, `join_ready` is derived rather than directly logged. Under the stated rule, it is the latest branch completion. If there are hidden validation steps or unlogged joins, the derived value would be incomplete.

Fifth, `final_start - join_ready` is labeled downstream queueing only under the model assumption that F may start immediately after all branches complete. It could instead reflect a release policy, batching rule, priority queue, missing authorization, planned review window, resource calendar, or unobserved work by F1/F2.

Sixth, final-station utilization cannot be reliably estimated from this table alone. Although two final resources are named, the observation window, their availability, breaks, outside work, and scheduling rules are unknown.

Seventh, counterfactuals are first-order mechanical calculations. They hold other branch starts, queues, service times, downstream queues, and final service times fixed unless explicitly changed. Real redesigns may create feedback: reducing B queue could shift the bottleneck to A or F; adding F capacity could expose B more strongly; reducing C queue spikes could matter only for a small subset of cases.

Finally, this is a single-day, 12-case sample. Frequencies such as “B critical in 8 of 12 cases” are strong descriptive evidence for this table, but not a stable long-run estimate without more days, resource calendars, and competing workload data.
