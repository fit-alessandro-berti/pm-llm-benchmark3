## 1. Interaction-graph description

The appropriate model is a typed, temporal interaction graph, not six independent Job DFGs. Its object nodes are **Job, Component, ComponentLot, Batch, Machine, Tool, and Shipment**. Events provide temporal event-to-object (E2O) evidence; they should not be confused with causal edges.

A compact edge list is:

- **Observed O2O:** `Ji —requires Ci` for `i=1…6`; `L7 —contains {C2,C5}`; `L8 —contains {C1,C3}`.
- **Observed O2O:** `B1 —batches {J1,J2,J3}`; `B2 —batches {J4,J5}`; `S1 —consolidates {J1,J4}`; `S2 —consolidates {J2,J3}`; `S3 —consolidates {J5,J6}`.
- **Observed E2O/resource use:** the 12:05 `Batch Cure` connects B1, OVEN-1, RACK-7, J1–J3, and C1–C3. B2 also uses OVEN-1 and RACK-7. J4’s 11:45 process connects J4, C4, CNC-2, and FIX-A. Every `Final Test` requires TEST-1 and CAL-3.
- **Observed intervals, partly inferred continuous holds:** J3 acquires/releases FIX-A at 09:00/11:40; RACK-7 is cleaning/quarantined until 14:35; CAL-3 is recalibrated at 16:35–17:20. Continuous occupancy between acquire and release is an interpretation.
- **Inferred dependency edges, not observed activities:** `J1,J2 —synchronization exposure B1/J3`; `J4 —possible tool blocking FIX-A`; `J5 —possible release gating L7`; `J1 —shipment-partner waiting J4`; and `J6 —possible calibration blocking CAL-3`.

Important higher-order motifs include:

1. **Complementary-resource batch barrier:** Job–Component–Batch–Machine–Tool. A cure may require not merely an idle OVEN-1, but RACK-7, released components, and the required job set.
2. **Lot fan-out with heterogeneous outcomes:** ComponentLot–Component–Job–Batch. L7 couples J2 and J5 through C2/C5, yet J2 receives an override while J5 waits.
3. **Serial tool handoff:** Job–Tool–Machine–Component. J3’s FIX-A hold precedes J4’s CNC-2 processing; similarly, J5’s test precedes CAL-3 recalibration and J6’s test.
4. **Consolidation across otherwise separate branches:** Shipment–Job–Batch. S1 joins J1 from B1 to J4 from B2; S3 joins J5 to J6 after their shared-test-tool interaction.

Structurally, there is even an undirected cycle  
`J1–B1–J2–C2–L7–C5–J5–B2–J4–S1–J1`.  
This is not a temporal cycle, but it shows why local delays can cross supposed job boundaries.

2. **B2 and RACK-7.** J4 waits 13:10–14:50 = **100 minutes**; J5 waits 14:40–14:50 = **10 minutes**. OVEN-1 is idle for 12:55–14:50 = **115 minutes**. RACK-7 becomes available at 14:35, before J5 is ready, so it is not demonstrably the critical constraint on the observed 14:50 start. The oven idle time is therefore not 115 minutes of proven avoidable delay.

3. **FIX-A and J4.** J4’s CNC arrival-to-process-start interval is 09:50–11:45 = **115 minutes**. 4. **L7 release.** Quarantine-to-release is 10:20–13:50 = **210 minutes**. J2 enters B1 at 12:05, **105 minutes before release**, consistent with the separately recorded override. No such readiness timestamp is supplied; 210 minutes is a lot-state duration, not a measured J5 delay.

5. **S1 consolidation.** J1 waits 13:30–19:10 = **340 minutes** for J4 to be packed, and 13:30–19:25 = **355 minutes** from packing to dispatch. The last-pack-to-dispatch portion is only 15 minutes. 6. **CAL-3 and J6.** J6’s ready-to-test-start interval is 16:05–17:25 = **80 minutes**, decomposable under the observed sequence into 30 minutes until J5 finishes, 45 minutes of recalibration, and five minutes after recalibration. ## 4. What flattening misses

- **Job:** Shows activity order, rework, and per-job performance. It hides partner identity and resource complementarity; shared events can be omitted or repeated across jobs depending on E2O extraction. The normal medians and absence of a 1.8-standard-deviation duration outlier do not refute severe waiting. In particular, a 1.1-hour median PackDispatch can coexist with J1’s 355-minute interval.
- **Machine:** Shows setups, processing, utilization, and recorded event sequences. It hides jobs that are ready but lack a tool, released material, batch partners, or calibration. Concluding “44% OVEN-1 utilization means ample effective capacity” is misleading: physical capacity was not necessarily usable capacity.
- **Batch:** Shows B1/B2 lifecycle and cure performance. Without member readiness, it collapses J1/J2/J3 into one instance and hides unequal within-batch waiting. Two Batch–Machine interactions may look insignificant despite large member-level exposure.
- **Tool:** Can expose acquire/release, cleaning, quarantine, and recalibration lifecycles. It hides unsuccessful requesters when no request/wait event exists and can overstate certainty when holds are inferred. RACK-7’s cleaning interval alone does not prove it controlled B2’s start.
- **ComponentLot:** Shows quarantine/release and the C2/C5 fan-out. It can hide the override and different J2/J5 responses. Alternatively, attaching one lot event to both jobs can duplicate event frequency and create two misleadingly independent job-local holds.
- **Shipment:** Shows CreateLoadDispatch execution. It hides packed-job dwell before consolidation; S1’s 15-minute last-pack-to-dispatch performance looks excellent while J1 has already waited 340 minutes.

Low utilization coexists with long waiting because processing requires **combinations** of objects, not machine capacity alone. A machine can be idle precisely because RACK-7, FIX-A, CAL-3, a released lot, or a batch/shipment partner is unavailable. These policies can also be non-work-conserving. Waiting is therefore not recoverable from machine-event gaps alone.

)

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken the top-ranked batch-synchronization explanation include:

1. Validated corrections showing J1 or J2 were not genuinely ready until shortly before 12:05.
2. Evidence that their supposed waiting intervals contained mandatory transport, inspection, or other processing.
3. A common material or quality gate affecting all B1 members until approximately 12:00, making J3’s readiness non-critical.
4. OVEN-1/RACK-7 calendars proving that neither an earlier complete batch nor feasible partial batches could run.
5. A larger sample showing no association between batch readiness skew and member waiting after controlling for resource calendars.
6. Repeated cases where relaxing batch synchronization reduces ready-to-cure waiting but leaves end-to-end lead time unchanged because downstream buffers absorb the saving.
7. Natural experiments in which B1-like starts remain unchanged after batch-policy relaxation, with tool or material constraints instead binding.

Discriminating additional data would include:

- Validated `Ready for Batch` events with input checklists, timestamp provenance, and revisions.
- `Batch Decision` records: planned members, minimum fill, maximum wait, capacity/compatibility, and scheduler reason.
- OVEN-1/RACK-7 reservation and availability calendars, including cleaning/quarantine reason codes.
- `Tool Requested`, `Tool Granted`, and `Tool Released` events for FIX-A/CAL-3, plus requester queues and calibration-due rules.
- Machine-state events distinguishing idle-available, setup, blocked, down, and reserved states.
- Actual component-consumption timestamps, J5’s pre-release processing attempts, and lot-release conditions.
- E2O relation provenance and integration-pipeline version, plus supervisor-override ID, approver, reason, and quality disposition.
- Test-request queues and complete shipment events: job packed, shipment eligible, cutoff decision, load start/end, and dispatch constraint.
