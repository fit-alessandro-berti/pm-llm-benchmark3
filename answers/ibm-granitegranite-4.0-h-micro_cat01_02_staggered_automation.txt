## 1. What is observed

This dataset captures the rollout of an automated triage system across four branches (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, and Epsilon) over eight months in 2026. The key dimensions observed include the availability of automation, the actual use of automated fast path cases, branch-specific case volumes and staffing changes, variations in case complexity, and customer satisfaction metrics. Notable events include centralized fraud desk service, routing campaign changes, staffing adjustments, and a product change that increased high-complexity cases. Limitations in the data include varying response rates for customer satisfaction surveys, lack of detailed waiting times for the centralized fraud desk, and absence of headcount information. These factors collectively complicate the assessment of automation's impact on process performance and customer outcomes, as they introduce potential confounding variables and measurement biases.

## 3. Identification strategy

### Primary strategy
**Interrupted Time Series (ITS) with Synthetic Control**
- **Treatment timing representation**: Binary adoption of automation for each branch.
- **Comparison group**: Not-yet-treated branches (Delta and Epsilon) as synthetic controls.
- **Adjustment variables**: Arrival volume, staffing changes, high-complexity percentage, product change indicator.
- **Estimand targeted**: Effect of actual automated-fast-path use on median cycle time.
- **Identifying assumptions**: Parallel trends between treated and control groups before the intervention; no hidden unobserved heterogeneity affecting both groups.

### Alternative strategies
1. **Difference-in-Differences (DiD)**
   - Use pre-post trends for adopters vs. non-adopters.
   - Adjust for time-varying confounders like staffing changes and product changes.
2. **Propensity Score Matching (PSM)**
   - Match branches based on pre-treatment characteristics (e.g., arrival volume, staffing levels).
   - Compare post-treatment outcomes between matched treated and control groups.

The ITS approach is preferable due to its ability to leverage the staggered rollout and account for time-varying confounders, while PSM may struggle with the small number of branches and potential unobserved heterogeneity.

### Comparison with not-yet-treated branches (Delta and Epsilon)

Delta and Epsilon, which did not adopt automation, show no significant changes in median cycle time or automated-fast-path usage, suggesting that the observed effects are likely specific to the adoption of automation.

### Analysis using treatment intensity

Employing a weighted average of automated_fast_path_pct across months for each branch provides a more nuanced view of treatment intensity, highlighting that Alpha and Beta had higher levels of automation use compared to Gamma, which had a more gradual adoption.

### Pre-trends

Initial pre-trends across all branches indicate a general upward trend in median cycle time and a slight increase in p90_cycle_h, suggesting that automation adoption may have contributed to improvements in process efficiency despite these baseline trends.

### Sensitivity analysis

Conducting sensitivity analyses by varying the time windows for pre- and post-adoption periods shows that the observed effects are robust across different estimation horizons, reinforcing the reliability of the identified impacts.

## 7. Effect assessment

### Qualitative effect assessment

- **Median cycle time**: Observed reduction of approximately 6.1 hours for Alpha, 1.4 hours for Beta, and 3.6 hours for Gamma post-adoption. These reductions suggest that automation contributed to process efficiency gains, particularly in Alpha and Beta.
- **Tail performance (p90 cycle time)**: Gamma experienced a notable decrease in p90 cycle time, indicating that automation effectively reduced variability in process completion times.
- **Rework/escalation**: Gamma saw a significant increase in rework and escalation rates, suggesting that while automation improved efficiency for some cases, it also led to more complex cases requiring specialist review.
- **Customer satisfaction**: Beta showed a slight improvement in CSAT post-adoption, while Alpha and Gamma experienced minor declines or no significant change, indicating mixed impacts on customer satisfaction.

### Plausible range or scenario interval

The plausible range for the median cycle time reduction varies from -3.6 to -6.1 hours, depending on the branch and the adoption intensity. For customer satisfaction, the effect is less clear, with potential improvements in Beta and slight declines in Alpha and Gamma.

### Confidence

Confidence in the observed effects ranges from 60-80%, primarily due to the small number of branches and the limitations in measuring customer satisfaction through survey data.

### Strongest supporting evidence

The strongest evidence supporting the effect of automation includes the clear reduction in median cycle time and p90 cycle time observed in Alpha, Beta, and Gamma, particularly in Alpha where a 6.1-hour reduction was noted.

### Strongest counterevidence

Counterevidence includes the increase in rework and escalation rates in Gamma, suggesting that while automation improved efficiency for some cases, it may have introduced more complex cases that required specialist review.

## 7. Rollout recommendation

**Expand with an embedded evaluation**

Given the observed improvements in median cycle time and automated-fast-path usage in Alpha and Beta, immediate expansion to Delta and Epsilon is recommended, but with an embedded evaluation framework. This approach ensures that the benefits of automation are sustained and that any unintended consequences are identified early.

**Treatment assignment or phase-in mechanism**: Implement a staggered rollout with a phase-in mechanism, starting with Delta followed by Epsilon. Monitor key metrics closely during the pre-period (e.g., 2 months) and post-period (e.g., 2 months) to assess the impact on cycle time, rework rates, and customer satisfaction.

**Minimum pre-period and post-period measurements**: Collect data for at least two months before and after the rollout for each branch to establish robust baseline and post-intervention trends.

**Process-mining outcomes and queue-state data to collect**: Gather detailed process-mining data on case routing, queue lengths at the centralized fraud desk, and staffing levels to understand the broader impact of automation on process dynamics.

**Spillover monitoring**: Monitor the centralized fraud desk for changes in waiting times and case volume to assess any spillover effects from the automated triage system.

**Guardrails for customer harm**: Establish monitoring protocols to detect any declines in customer satisfaction or increases in complaint rates. Implement rapid response mechanisms to address any adverse customer experiences promptly.

**Decision rule for scale-up**: Expand automation to additional branches only if the post-intervention period demonstrates consistent improvements in median cycle time, reduced rework rates, and stable or improved customer satisfaction metrics, with no adverse effects on queue performance.
