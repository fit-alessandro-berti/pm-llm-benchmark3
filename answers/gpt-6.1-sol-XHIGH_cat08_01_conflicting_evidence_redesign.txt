# 1. Current-process diagnosis

The objective should be **durable, policy-compliant resolution at an acceptable total cost**, measured by segment. Optimizing the time to first approval or first close would reward the very shortcuts that generate later complaints. Equally, sending every claim through the slow reviewed/repair route would create avoidable delay, especially for Premium accessories.

- **The dominant route has a real speed advantage, but its economics weaken downstream.** The frequency DFG shows 3,600 of 5,000 claims entering auto screening, 2,550 Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval transitions, and 2,300 Fast approval → Dispatch replacement transitions. The performance DFG gives medians of 0.4 hours for auto screen → approval and 1.8 hours for approval → dispatch. However, the case-level rework statistics show 14.1% complaint/reopen incidence for Fast approval versus 3.3% for the complete quote/approval/repair route. Fast approval's cost advantage falls from 31% before rework to 9% after complaints, reshipments, and credits. It remains cheaper in the aggregate supplied here; that does not establish that it is cheaper for every segment.
- **The trade-off changes sharply by customer and product.** Premium accessories take a median 8 hours on the fast route versus 46 hours manually, with complaints of 6% versus 3%. Moving all of them to manual review would threaten the 24-hour service-credit boundary. Standard accessories have more room for additional checks: medians are 13 versus 52 hours, and customers tolerate 72 hours with good visibility. For Premium high-value devices, 20 versus 96 hours comes with 22% versus 5% complaints; for Standard high-value devices, 28 versus 120 hours comes with 18% versus 6%. Wrong replacements also cost much more for high-value devices: EUR 410 versus EUR 95 for accessories. High-value shortcuts therefore warrant a different decision from accessory shortcuts.
- **Evidence collection is a repeated interaction problem, not simply a slow review task.** The frequency DFG contains 890 Request missing evidence → Manual eligibility review transitions and 320 transitions to abandonment. Among cases entering evidence requests, 26% receive at least two requests. The corresponding return-to-review edge has a median of 26 hours and p90 of 88 hours. The unclear-defect/missing-evidence group accounts for 22% of cases, has a 140-hour median cycle, and the highest abandonment. Repeated requests, fragmented instructions, and customer waiting plausibly reinforce one another.
- **The slower route contains avoidable waits as well as useful controls.** Manual review → Repair quote has a mean of 22 hours and p90 of 60 hours; temporal diagnostics identify this as particularly problematic for Premium cases. Quote → Customer approval adds a mean of 30 hours and p90 of 80 hours. Part-price confirmation is the stated main source of quote delay. Repair nevertheless has its own quality problem: repaired high-value devices have 12.5% rework, versus 4.0% for repaired low-risk devices. More repair is not automatically better repair.
- **Early completion hides a substantial tail.** Dispatch → Complaint/reopen has a mean of 96 hours and p90 of 180 hours, with temporal deviations linked to incorrect replacement selection. After a complaint, returning to manual review takes a mean of 40 hours and p90 of 100; escalation takes a mean of 54 hours and p90 of 130. The frequency DFG also shows 310 complaint → manual-review and 120 complaint → escalation transitions. These are delayed second journeys that must enter cycle-time and cost measures.
- **Conformance is insufficient despite apparently high fitness.** Token replay fitness of 0.91 and only 68% fitting traces measure different things: many events can fit while whole claims still deviate. Alignment diagnostics identify approval before evidence completion, complaints after close, and high-value dispatch without risk review. Apply the actual rules precisely: R1 requires either manual eligibility or fraud/risk review before Fast approval for claims above EUR 400; it does not require fraud review for every high-value device. R2 requires confirmed evidence before dispatch, with the specific Premium low-risk accessory exception. That exception does not waive R1. R3 requires manual review after a reopen before subsequent final close or denial. A legitimate later complaint also needs a linked reopen episode; an overly restrictive lifecycle model should not make it disappear.
- **The discovered model is an unsafe redesign specification.** Low precision permits combinations of auto approval, review, and repair that policy does not permit. A redesigned model needs explicit decision conditions and event ordering, rather than reproducing every observed edge. The 350 Fast approval → Quality/compliance close transitions also need outcome-level inspection: some may represent valid resolutions without dispatch, while others may reflect premature closure or missing events.
- **Capacity constrains the apparent solution.** Seven reviewers provide 252 productive hours per week; the 82% queue-growth threshold corresponds to about 207 occupied hours. A rule update can add 15–35% to review inflow. Under unchanged effort per case, absorbing that increase below 82% would require baseline utilization of roughly 61–71%, or additional capacity/less work per claim. The two fraud specialists already face Monday/campaign peaks; the three quote technicians wait for prices; the two inspectors are usually underused but become busy after repair campaigns. The daily 45-minute escalation board cannot become the normal recovery route. DFG elapsed times must not be treated as labor hours for capacity or costing.

These artifacts establish priorities, not causal effects. The reviewed and fast cohorts may differ in defect complexity, value, evidence completeness, and customer behavior. The displayed DFG also does not form a complete conserved case flow: its END counts total 3,625, rather than 5,000. Check missing events, observation boundaries, and unfinished cases before calculating route probabilities or completed-case averages. Use the supplied case-level outcome rates as evidence, while rebuilding comparable cohorts from traces.

# 2. Redesign A: Guarded replacement with segment-specific routing

**Redesigned flow.** Intake records customer segment, claim value, product identity, defect, and evidence status → updated auto screen performs provisional eligibility/risk routing → complete evidence where required → obtain manual eligibility review or fraud/risk review when required → make an explicit technical decision → validate replacement selection → dispatch → record fulfillment and quality/compliance close. Technically unsuitable replacements enter the repair route. Every reopen enters manual review before its next final close or denial.

Clear accessory claims retain automated processing. Premium low-risk accessories may use the documented R2 exception, provided product identity is sufficient to select the correct replacement. Standard accessories require completed evidence. Claims above EUR 400 cannot Fast approve without the R1 review; ordinary eligibility questions go to reviewers, while actual fraud indicators go to specialists. Unclear defects enter an evidence/diagnostic queue instead of receiving default approval.

Use an automated serial/SKU/compatibility match for clear accessories and a lightweight human pre-dispatch gate for high-value or ambiguous replacement selections. The human gate checks the diagnosed defect, exact part/device variant, and selected replacement. It should use scheduled inspector capacity and clear dispatch cutoffs, with repair-campaign capacity reserved. The supplied 6–18-hour gate delay is a design constraint to measure, not a guaranteed improvement. Final-close controls verify the required evidence exception, review, decision, and fulfillment events.

**Targets.** Clear replacement-appropriate claims, especially high-value devices currently exposed to wrong replacement costs; Premium accessories that need fast service; Standard accessories where additional checking can fit within their tolerance. Routing uses monetary value separately from the product-risk label.

**Mechanism.** This design preserves the dominant route's short approval/dispatch sequence while removing specific sources of downstream failure: outdated routing, missing evidence, and incorrect part selection. It creates a reviewed replacement route that may be faster than a full reviewed/repair journey. The economics are strongest where prevented wrong replacements avoid EUR 410 losses. Gate cost must be compared with the actual probability of preventing a wrong replacement and avoided handling/credit costs; complaint incidence alone is not that probability.

**Sacrifices and unresolved issues.** Initial review and dispatch become slower and more expensive for some claims; the remaining aggregate 9% cost advantage could disappear. A blanket 18-hour gate would jeopardize Premium accessory service, so that gate is selective. This design does not remove repeated evidence collection or price-confirmation delays, and correct part selection does not prove that the technical decision to replace was appropriate. Its quality benefits must be demonstrated rather than assumed to equal the repair cohort's outcomes.

# 3. Redesign B: Repair-led resolution cells with an early binding quote

**Redesigned flow.** Intake and provisional eligibility → a named case owner coordinates evidence and diagnostic assessment → qualified reviewer confirms eligibility where needed → technician makes a repair/replacement decision → offer a reliable quote and repair slot → customer approval → repair → defect-specific functional quality check → fulfillment and compliance close. If repair is uneconomic, unavailable within the agreed service window, or technically inappropriate, use a compliant replacement route. Replacement fallback still observes R1 and R2; reopened cases still receive a fresh manual review under R3.

Create a coordinated service cell using reviewer, technician, and inspector time, without assuming that these roles are interchangeable. While evidence is collected, retrieve part prices and availability provisionally. Finalize the quote using verified information and an approved, refreshed price catalogue for standard parts; send unusual pricing to a defined exception owner. Reserve feasible repair capacity when issuing the offer, and present a clear price, timing, and repair/replacement choice. Avoid quoting a completion promise before checking parts availability.

Diagnosis, repair instructions, and final testing share the same serial number and reported fault. For high-value devices, the quality check must reproduce the original symptom where possible and verify that the repair resolved it. Premium cases receive protected appointments and rapid quote handling; an optional temporary device can reduce disruption, with its cost measured separately. Age-based scheduling protects Standard cases from indefinite displacement.

**Targets.** Repairable Standard high-value devices and suitable mid-risk devices, especially where replacement errors are costly. Premium high-value devices qualify when a credible repair promise and acceptable interim service can be offered. Cheap accessories remain on a controlled replacement route. Unknown defects require diagnosis before being assigned to repair.

**Mechanism.** This changes the fulfillment strategy and ownership structure, rather than merely adding a check to Fast approval. It uses the observed lower complaint rate of the complete repair route as a hypothesis, while attacking that route's actual delays: part-price confirmation, handoffs, customer uncertainty, and unavailable repair slots. Better diagnosis and fault-specific testing address the 12.5% high-value repair rework rate. A credible early offer may shorten the quote-to-approval wait, although customers retain the right to decline.

**Sacrifices and unresolved issues.** Repair consumes skilled labor and can remain slower than replacement, particularly at the tail. Coordinated staffing, parts reservations, and temporary devices add cost; reducing wrong replacements does not guarantee lower total cost. Repair is unattractive for low-value products, unavailable parts, and customers who need immediate restoration. This design does not justify forcing Premium customers into the historical 96-hour reviewed path or assuming every high-value defect is repairable.

# 4. Redesign C: Assisted evidence-first resolution with one case owner

**Redesigned flow.** Intake → segment/defect-specific evidence checklist and provisional eligibility screen → choose self-service or assisted evidence collection → confirm a complete, versioned evidence bundle → route once to the qualified eligibility/risk and technical decision resources → appropriate repair or replacement → quality/compliance close. Preliminary price/availability checks can run during collection, but definitive decisions depend on verified information. Dispatch obeys R2, Fast approval obeys R1, and every reopen receives manual review before final disposition under R3.

The portal requests a consolidated bundle: purchase/coverage information, product serial/variant, relevant fault description, and only the images or diagnostics needed for that defect. It checks completeness and flags unreadable or contradictory submissions. Customers can instead use a scheduled phone/video callback or assisted submission. A named owner carries the evidence history across teams so a reviewer does not ask again for information already supplied. A second request triggers assisted resolution and an explanation of the remaining gap, rather than another generic request or automatic denial.

Customers receive clear status, the specific next action, and an expected contact time. Record separately when the organization is waiting for evidence and when it is working, while continuing to report full elapsed time. The Premium accessory exception remains available where appropriate; it is not expanded to other segments to improve the portal's statistics.

**Targets.** The 22% unclear-defect/missing-evidence group, evidence-request loops in both auto and manual paths, and customers with low digital literacy. This is an information and interaction redesign across fulfillment routes, not a decision to favor replacement or repair.

**Mechanism.** It intervenes before the 26-hour median/88-hour p90 return from evidence collection to manual review, reduces duplicate contacts, and makes downstream decisions better informed. The proposed portal's 30–45% reduction in repeated requests is a testable potential. If applied to the same cohort and definition, a 26% repeat-request incidence would become approximately 14–18%; this is not a forecast of a 30–45% cycle-time reduction. Assisted access addresses the stated risk that a portal improves internal efficiency by causing customers to abandon.

**Sacrifices and unresolved issues.** Portal development, customer assistance, and case ownership add upfront and ongoing costs. Some straightforward claims will receive unnecessary interaction unless the checklist is proportional. Better evidence cannot by itself fix wrong replacement selection, repair workmanship, fraud-specialist peaks, or parts availability. The design may increase completed claims and downstream workload by retaining customers who previously abandoned; that can be a customer benefit even if aggregate processing cost rises.

“No” means no demonstrated dominance in the design's best-fit cohort, not proof of a true Pareto frontier. A sacrifices some initial speed for safer replacement; B trades speed and skilled effort for repair durability; C trades interaction investment for less evidence friction. The current process is conditionally dominated only where a redesign proves equal or better speed/cost with better outcomes. Policy-violating routes are infeasible choices irrespective of their position on a speed/cost chart. Compare dominance within similar segments and defect types, using total cost and durable resolution rather than first close.

# 6. Failure modes and displaced bottlenecks

**A — guarded replacement.** Outdated or inaccurate value/segment data can send claims through an illegal shortcut, including misuse of the Premium exception. Review inflow can increase faster than available capacity, producing nonlinear queues above 82% utilization. A superficial part check can become a costly rubber stamp; a thorough but poorly staffed check can threaten Premium timing. The likely bottleneck moves from complaint/escalation recovery to manual eligibility and pre-dispatch inspection. Inspector allocation must also preserve post-repair quality checks during campaigns.

**B — repair-led cells.** A stale price catalogue or unavailable parts can make the “early binding quote” unreliable, converting price waiting into cancellation or broken promises. Repair volume can exceed the three technicians' capacity, while rushed testing allows repeat failures despite more formal quality checks. Premium priority can starve Standard claims, and temporary-device costs can erase replacement savings. The bottleneck moves toward parts supply, technician appointments, and the two inspectors; removing quote waiting may expose these constraints rather than reduce end-to-end time.

**C — assisted evidence-first.** Digital collection can increase abandonment among less confident customers, yielding an apparently faster completed-case cohort through exclusion. False completeness checks can pass unusable evidence, while rigid checklists can demand unnecessary information and create new loops. Assistance and validation can consume reviewer capacity without sufficient duplicate-work savings. The bottleneck moves first to assisted contact/evidence validation and then potentially to fraud, repair, or dispatch as more customers successfully complete intake. Monitor retained volume rather than treating higher downstream demand as automatic failure.

# 7. Recommended experiment sequence

**Stage 0: establish a compliant, measurable baseline.** Reconstruct case and reopen episodes, observation windows, and the unexplained END shortfall. Log evidence confirmation, claim value, review type, technical decision, selected part, actual dispatch, repair tests, close, and subsequent complaints. A completeness event must represent verified information, not a renamed request event. Encode R1–R3 and the narrowly defined exception in the normative model; inspect deviations at case level. Implement mandatory safeguards for treatment and control cohorts rather than preserving known violations for an experiment.

Measure reviewer effort and utilization, specialist/inspector workloads, arrival peaks, and queue age before scaling. During the feasible two-week rule-update period, run proposed routing in shadow mode to estimate added review demand. Use observed handling times for capacity calculations, not DFG elapsed times.

**Stage 1: first pilot — A on a limited Standard high-value replacement cohort.** Start with clear, technically replacement-appropriate claims, initially about 10–15% of eligible arrivals or less if measured capacity requires it. This exposes the EUR 410 wrong-replacement problem without initially placing Premium service promises at risk. Provide required eligibility/risk review and evidence confirmation, then test replacement validation and the revised routing. Do not generalize the result to unclear defects excluded from this cohort.

Compare with a contemporaneous compliant usual-process cohort. Randomize allocation where both approaches are acceptable; stratify by defect, value, evidence status, and campaign/day effects. Track shared-queue spillovers so an improved treatment group cannot hide delayed controls or nonpilot cases. Retain customer choice and required specialist referral.

Monitor first-resolution and durable-resolution median/p90, wrong replacements, complaints/reopens, repeat shipments, actual total claim cost, R1–R3 violations, gate delay, productive utilization, backlog age, and customer satisfaction. Suggested decision thresholds are design targets, not estimates from the prompt:

- Any confirmed policy breach pauses the affected route for correction and audit. Apparent conformance improvement without verified events is insufficient.
- Maintain reviewer utilization around or below 80%, with no sustained excursion above 82%; require stable queue age across pilot and nonpilot work. Review the inspector plan if gate delay repeatedly exceeds the stated 18-hour upper range.
- Seek roughly a 20–30% relative reduction in complaints/wrong replacements against comparable controls. The historical 18% Standard high-value fast-path complaint rate is a planning reference, not a guaranteed control rate after compliance correction.
- Require no material worsening of durable-resolution p90, with a predeclared tolerance such as 10%. Scale on cost only when total cost is non-inferior, or when the sponsor explicitly values and budgets the demonstrated quality improvement. Mandatory compliance does not depend on meeting a savings target.

If these conditions hold, expand A gradually. Before applying human gates to Premium accessories, test whether approximately 90–95% can still finish within 24 hours, alongside complaint and credit exposure. This is a proposed pilot target; the actual service contract may require a stricter threshold. Premium high-value targets must come from their own promise, not the accessory 24-hour rule.

**Stage 2: test C on the evidence-heavy cohort before further review-intensive expansion.** Compare assisted portal/ownership against existing assisted collection under the same compliance controls. Offer help in both arms to customers who need it. Track actual customer requests across channels, rather than reducing the count by changing event labels. Look for repeat-request incidence moving from 26% toward approximately 14–18% in comparable cases, shorter evidence-wait p90, and fewer total reviewer/contact minutes per incoming claim. Require no material abandonment increase; an increase around 1–2 percentage points, particularly within low-digital-literacy groups, should trigger investigation and redesign of assistance. Check eligibility-confirmation accuracy and downstream workload. Capacity released by fewer loops should be measured before being allocated to A or B.

**Stage 3: test B on repairable high-value devices as a competing fulfillment strategy.** Establish refreshed pricing, reserved appointments, and functional-test capacity before enrolling customers. Where both repair and replacement are suitable, compare the service-cell offer with a compliant replacement offer, respecting customer agreement. Report outcomes by assigned offer as well as actual route so selection into repair does not manufacture a benefit.

Test whether quote waiting and Premium quote-delay deviations fall, whether durable-resolution p90 improves against comparable reviewed cases, and whether high-value repair rework can move from 12.5% toward a proposed 8–10% range. Distinguish rework caught internally from defects escaping to customers: better testing can initially increase recorded internal rework while improving customer outcomes. Stop expansion for growing repair/parts queues, broken promises, or higher total cost without a valued outcome gain. Do not demand that B match the shortest accessory fast path; assess it against a relevant high-value alternative.

**Follow each cohort beyond first close and decide by segment.** Use at least a 30-day complaint/reopen follow-up, with longer device monitoring where needed. The dispatch-to-complaint p90 of 180 hours already makes a dispatch-only or one-day assessment inadequate. Include all incoming claims, abandonments, open cases, and service credits; report censored cases and backlog age rather than excluding slow unfinished claims. Cost every review, contact, replacement, repair, reshipment, rework, and credit using actual effort. Inspect late complaints and verify R3 on their linked episodes.

Evaluate confidence intervals, case mix, campaign effects, and queue spillovers before declaring success. A short pilot with too few late failures is inconclusive. Keep the interventions distinguishable until their effects are understood, then combine only demonstrated complements: guarded fast service for suitable replacements, repair cells for repairable devices, and assisted evidence collection where uncertainty dominates. That portfolio should follow measured segment-level trade-offs rather than a single organization-wide average.
