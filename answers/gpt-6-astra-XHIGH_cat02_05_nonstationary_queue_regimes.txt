# 1. Data audit and estimands

The strongest descriptive interpretation is a calendar-dependent queue with an outage and recovery episode, a marked change in recorded case mix and handling times around April 20, and a staffing transition on April 29. These are plausible operating regimes, not demonstrated stationary queues. Two internal inconsistencies also prevent accepting all supplied measures as observations of one conserved population without reconciliation.

**Quantities and conventions.** Let $A_d$ and $D_d$ be daily arrivals and completions. Every listed day, including weekends, has 12 opening hours, 07:00–19:00. For a set of $n$ days, estimate opening-hours arrival intensity as $\hat\lambda=\sum A_d/(12n)$ and completion intensity as $\hat\delta=\sum D_d/(12n)$, both in cases per open hour. These are exposure averages, not constant instantaneous rates. Completion intensity is realized output, not a service-rate parameter or maximum capacity.

$L$ is mean cases in the system, including waiting and active handling. Average the daily `mean_wip` values only under the assumption that snapshots have comparable coverage and approximate time averages. $S$ denotes active handling time in minutes; $T$ denotes total case flow time, including waiting. Neither $E[S]$ nor $E[T]$ is supplied. A range of daily medians or p90s describes reported daily quantiles; it is not the corresponding pooled quantile.

Let $c_d$ be available FTE. Nominal daily resource capacity is $720c_d$ FTE-minutes. Using that as processing capacity additionally assumes compatible skills, one FTE per active case, and handling times that represent total resource work. Those assumptions are needed only for the capacity sensitivity calculation below. They are not established by the table.

Define the **count residual** $r_d=A_d-D_d$. It equals the change in backlog only if these counts cover the same queue and exhaust its entries and exits. Unknown starting backlog does not prevent computing a change under that assumption, but does prevent recovering absolute backlog.

**Source inconsistencies.** All four hourly vectors sum to their corresponding daily arrivals and completions. Their stated 08:00–09:59 shares nevertheless disagree:

| Representative date | Arrivals in bins 08–09 and 09–10 | Share from hourly vector | Daily `peak_08_10_pct` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| April 7 | 17 of 101 | 16.8% | 31% |
| April 15 | 19 of 105 | 18.1% | 32% |
| April 21 | 11 of 115 | 9.6% | 47% |
| April 25 | 6 of 51 | 11.8% | 22% |

These differences exceed rounding. The daily peak field can suggest a change in its own recorded series, but cannot establish the clock time of that change. Use the hourly vectors for their explicitly labeled hours, keep both sources visible, and investigate time labels, definitions and extraction versions.

There is also a stronger inventory check. **Conditional assumption:** all arrivals enter this queue, completions are its only exits, and WIP counts all its cases. With nonnegative initial backlog, cumulative residual through April 24 gives at least 88 cases at opening on April 25. Even if all 43 completions occurred before any arrivals, WIP could never fall below $88-43=45$, whereas reported mean WIP is 23. The same conservative check finds:

| Date | Minimum opening inventory from earlier residuals | Whole-day completions | Lower bound on WIP at every instant | Reported mean WIP |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| April 25 | 88 | 43 | 45 | 23 |
| April 26 | 96 | 39 | 57 | 20 |
| May 2 | 110 | 51 | 59 | 22 |
| May 3 | 112 | 47 | 65 | 19 |

Thus, that conservation interpretation is incompatible with the supplied WIP. Snapshot timing alone cannot explain violation of a pointwise lower bound. Possible explanations include unrecorded exits or transfers, a WIP population that excludes carried cases, or inconsistent count definitions; none is observed. An unknown *positive* initial backlog would strengthen the contradiction. Accordingly, the total residual of +110 is an accounting discrepancy to investigate, not an established absolute backlog or an unconditional estimate of its growth.

Further threats to naive inference include:

- **Calendar composition:** weekends have substantially fewer arrivals and fewer staff; changing weekday proportions alters pooled comparisons.
- **Intraday variation:** daily rates conceal concentrated arrivals and zero-output outage hours. Fifteen-minute snapshots can miss short peaks.
- **Cohort mismatch and censoring:** completed cases can come from earlier days; the service-summary population is unspecified. Arrival mix and completed-case handling cannot automatically be paired.
- **Closures and carry-over:** opening-hours exposure excludes overnight waiting. End-of-day backlog and its age distribution are unobserved.
- **Capacity confounding:** staffing may respond to pressure; mean FTE hides deployment by hour, skill, breaks and outage effectiveness. Recovery output is not normal productivity.
- **Ambiguous mix change:** the complex label may change because of routing, coding or both. Aggregate service changes cannot identify variant-specific service distributions.
- **Dependence and limited replication:** daily rows share calendar structure, backlog and interventions. There are only four weeks and very few observations in some regimes.
- **Uncertain treatment timing:** April 29 is partially treated at an unknown time. Daily before/after analysis cannot identify an immediate staffing effect.

**Capacity pressure without inventing a service mean.** Under the resource assumptions in Section 1, an aggregate workload balance would require

\[
\rho_R=\frac{A_R E[S_R]}{720\sum_{d\in R}c_d}<1,
\qquad
E[S_R]<s_R^*=\frac{720\sum_{d\in R}c_d}{A_R},
\]

where $A_R$ is total arrivals and $E[S_R]$ is their arrival-weighted mean total labor requirement. $s_R^*$ is a break-even sensitivity threshold, not an estimate of service time. For baseline weekdays it is 42.6 minutes; for higher-mix weekdays before staffing it falls to 37.4; after staffing it rises to 42.4. Weekend thresholds are 45.0, 38.3 and 44.1 minutes respectively. Peaks, skill constraints, interruptions and recovery work require additional margin even when average offered workload fits.

The higher-mix weekday median of 34–36 minutes is close to its 37.4-minute threshold, while p90 is 78–88. This supports concern about workload, but does not prove utilization above one. Medians and p90s do not determine a mean, variance or upper bound on the mean: the unobserved upper tail matters. Reported daily p90/median ratios rise from roughly 1.8–1.9 in baseline weekdays to 2.3–2.4 before the intervention, indicating that the upper quantile increased relative to the center.

# 3. Alternative segmentation

A materially different approach groups days by **observed flow balance**, with calendar and mix as explanatory covariates rather than primary boundaries. It represents an operational story of accumulation, discharge and small daily changes; recovery after an outage and recovery after staffing belong to the same discharge state.

A transparent, deliberately provisional rule is: classify an outage first; otherwise call a day “accumulating” if $A-D\geq8$, “discharging” if $A-D\leq-5$, and “near balance” otherwise. Thresholds are operational choices, not inferred natural constants.

| Alternative state | Dates | Days | Arrivals/day | Completions/day | Total residual |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Outage | April 15 | 1 | 105.0 | 58.0 | +47 |
| Accumulating | April 20–25 and 27–28 | 8 | 106.1 | 93.4 | +102 |
| Discharging | April 16–17 and April 29–May 1 | 5 | 111.2 | 120.8 | −48 |
| Near balance | April 6–14, 18–19, 26 and May 2–3 | 14 | 72.1 | 71.4 | +9 |

This alternative is useful for response rules and prevents interpreting recovery output as equilibrium throughput. It also pools similar flow responses across different causes, increasing observations per state.

Its weaknesses are substantial. It conditions on the outcome, so it cannot estimate the causal effect of staffing or explain the classified output difference. Absolute thresholds treat weekdays and weekends differently. April 26 is “near balance” despite a +4 residual and earlier accumulation; changing the threshold would move it. A small daily change says nothing about backlog level, backlog age or stationarity. The WIP audit further means these are accounting states until population boundaries are reconciled.

The primary segmentation better separates demand, recorded mix and capacity mechanisms, but fragments a small dataset. For prediction, a related alternative would model hour-of-day and day-of-week demand, variant workload, available skill capacity and evolving backlog jointly, with an outage indicator and uncertain treatment time. Such a model could share information across days instead of assuming ten independent steady states. The supplied aggregates are insufficient to fit and validate that richer model.

# 5. Burstiness, autocorrelation, and stationarity

For the twelve equal one-hour bins, define the descriptive index of dispersion $F=s^2/\bar x$, using sample variance with denominator 11, and $CV=s/\bar x$. Also use maximum hourly arrivals divided by mean hourly arrivals. Fano factor and CV reuse the same variance and are not independent evidence. They describe hourly counts, not the coefficient of variation of individual interarrival times.

| April 15 | 4.14 | 0.69 | 2.25 | 2.51 | 0.71 |

April 21 is particularly uneven: 34 arrivals in 10:00–11:00 and 24 in 13:00–14:00 account for 50.4% of the day's arrivals. The first of those bins has 34 arrivals against 13 completions, a +21 within-bin residual. The vector therefore indicates pressure at different clock times from the daily field labeled 08:00–10:00. April 7 and April 25 show smoother daily humps, which themselves generate positive adjacent-bin correlation. Negative lag-1 correlation on April 21 does not mean absence of bursts; separated spikes can create that result.

Hourly cumulative arrivals minus completions reach maxima, relative to opening inventory, of +6, +55, +27 and +8 cases respectively. These are hour-end increments, not observed queue lengths or maxima within the hour. Their minima are −3, −2, −2 and 0; under complete event accounting, the first three vectors require at least that many carried cases at opening. April 15 has 37 arrivals during the three zero-completion hours alone.

A homogeneous Poisson count model has equal mean and variance under repeated identical exposures. These twelve bins do not have identical expected demand. A smooth time-varying Poisson rate can yield a large empirical Fano factor when different hours are pooled; the outage also changes the completion process. Consequently, these Fano factors demonstrate unevenness but do not isolate stochastic overdispersion after conditioning on time. Completion counts are departures from a queue, not independent arrival-like observations.

At the daily scale, arrival Fano is 10.03 pooled, but 0.66 for weekdays and 0.73 for weekends. It falls further to 0.26 and 0.29 for baseline and higher-mix pre-intervention weekdays. This shows how calendar mixing can create apparent overdispersion. The small within-group values do not prove a deterministic or underdispersed arrival law: group selection, small samples and fixed daily totals can coexist with strong intraday clustering.

**Serial dependence.** Pearson correlations between the original daily series and its lags are descriptive:

Even subtracting each day-of-week's four-week mean leaves arrival lag-1 correlation about 0.80, consistent with remaining trend or regime structure, not proof of an AR model. Ordinary independent-day standard errors would be misleading. More weeks would be needed for meaningful seasonal residual diagnostics or block-based uncertainty estimates.

**Change points and stationarity.** A transparent one-break least-squares scan of the recorded complex percentages, requiring at least five days on each side, selects April 20 as the first day of the second segment. Daily mean percentage changes from 22.43% to 43.29%, reducing within-segment squared error by about 95.1% relative to one constant level. Nearby April 21 and April 19 splits fit less well. This is a descriptive heuristic on the coding series, not a significance test, proof of a causal mix change, or confidence interval for the transition.

April 15 is an externally documented transient interruption. April 16–17 is a response with changed staff and likely inherited work. April 29 is an externally identified but intraday-uncertain intervention. April 27–28 might be split off as a later pressure escalation—WIP averages 49.5 and arrival intensity 9.96/hour—but two days do not establish another lasting regime.

Baseline demand is at best approximately periodic rather than homogeneous. Higher-mix weekdays have positive residuals on every observed day, inconsistent with claiming demonstrated rate balance under conserved-queue assumptions. The brief post-intervention sequence cannot establish a new equilibrium. Four purposively labeled representative days provide no replication at a given regime/hour, no individual interarrival gaps, and no service-time second moment. They cannot validate Poisson arrivals, exponential service, a renewal process, a Hawkes process, or an M/M/c model.

# 6. Little's-Law applicability matrix

Little's Law relates compatible long-run averages, $L=\lambda E[T]$; it does not require Poisson arrivals or exponential service. Rate stability, finite relevant averages and consistent system/time boundaries matter. Periodic operations can support a long-run cycle-average relationship even though their hourly rates vary. The local shortcut $L(t)/\lambda(t)$ generally ignores the history of arrivals and outstanding work.

For a finite observation window $W$, the accounting identity is instead

\[
\int_{W\cap\mathcal O}N(t)\,dt
=\sum_i \operatorname{duration}\bigl([a_i,d_i)\cap W\cap\mathcal O\bigr),
\]

where $\mathcal O$ is the set of opening intervals and cases crossing either boundary remain in the sum. Dividing this truncated occupancy area by departures does not generally produce the mean full flow time of departed cases. Small net count change does not guarantee small boundary-age effects.

The two numerical windows below are explicitly chosen **conditional sensitivity calculations**. They assume comparable WIP and count populations, representative snapshots, and modest boundary effects. They are the least problematic windows in these data, but the wider audit means those assumptions require verification. Completions are the primary throughput proxy because they measure realized exits; arrivals provide a balance sensitivity check, not a different estimate of service duration.

# 7. Operational implications

1. **Reconcile the queue boundary before using backlog targets.** Capture opening and closing inventory, cancellations, transfers, reopenings and all queue entry/exit events. Reconcile the count equation interval by interval against snapshots. Correct the peak-share discrepancy and document whether WIP includes overnight cases. Count residuals remain useful alerts, but the current data cannot support an exact clearance date or stock target.

2. **Plan staffing by demand timing, variant workload and skills.** Retain different weekday/weekend capacity assumptions and allow for longer complex handling. The pre-intervention weekday mean-service budget of 37.4 minutes is a warning threshold under stated labor assumptions, not a measured utilization estimate. Collect mean total resource work and availability by interval before sizing servers or applying a staffing formula. Validate the apparent 10:00 and 13:00 bursts on additional days before fixing shifts around them.

3. **Treat restoration and recovery as separate operating tasks.** During an outage, reduce avoidable release of new work, communicate delays and preserve work state. After restoration, provide compatible recovery capacity and prioritize by age and service commitments. Monitor incoming work, completions, backlog age and residuals together. High recovery throughput does not establish sustainable ordinary-day capacity.

4. **Evaluate the staffing intervention with matched exposures.** The within-calendar improvements support continued observation of the temporary capacity while pressure is assessed. Record exact implementation and roster times; compare equivalent weekdays, hours and variant cohorts, and account for opening backlog. With more observations, use an interrupted time-series or matched comparison that accommodates serial dependence. The four post-transition days cannot isolate a causal effect.

5. **Smooth peaks where operationally possible.** Stagger appointments, batch releases or upstream handoffs around verified demand peaks. The April 21 vector suggests that two separated surges deserve attention. Avoid assuming that the daily field's 08:00–10:00 label identifies the intervention window; first resolve its conflict with the hourly source.

6. **Guard against a policy encouraged by pooling.** Withdrawing temporary staff because pooled completions fell from 87.33 to 85/day would ignore improvement within both weekdays and weekends. Likewise, leveling every day's schedule around 5.14 FTE and 7.50 arrivals/hour would hide weekday peaks and mix pressure. A smaller weekend WIP alone is not evidence that further weekend cuts are safe.

7. **Collect an event log suitable for process mining.** Record case ID, queue entry/exit, activity, lifecycle start/complete, resource, variant version, routing and rework, plus interruption intervals and skill-specific rosters. Retain cases still open at extraction and distinguish active work from waiting and suspended work. Compare actual activity sequences around April 20 to distinguish routing changes from relabeling; examine variant-specific workload and completion selection. A completion-only log would bias long-case and boundary analysis. Process discovery and conformance analysis can then identify extra steps and loops, while interval accounting reconstructs WIP and actual business/calendar flow times.
