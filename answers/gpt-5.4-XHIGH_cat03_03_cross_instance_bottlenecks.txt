## 1. Interaction-graph description
The useful graph is not Job-only. The important node types are `Job`, `Component`, `ComponentLot`, `Batch`, `Machine`, `Tool`, and `Shipment`. The key idea is that several objects create shared eligibility constraints without appearing as conventional machine queues.

Directly observed edges come from the O2O relations and from multi-object event co-reference. Inferred edges are blocking or dependency edges reconstructed from timestamps, because the log has no explicit `Wait for Tool`, `Wait for Batch`, or `Wait for Shipment Partner` activities.

Compact typed edge list:

- `J1 -requires-> C1`, `J2 -requires-> C2`, `J3 -requires-> C3`, `J4 -requires-> C4`, `J5 -requires-> C5`, `J6 -requires-> C6` (direct).
- `L7 -contains-> C2`, `L7 -contains-> C5`, `L8 -contains-> C1`, `L8 -contains-> C3` (direct).
- `B1 -batches-> J1,J2,J3`; `B2 -batches-> J4,J5` (direct).
- `S1 -consolidates-> J1,J4`; `S2 -consolidates-> J2,J3`; `S3 -consolidates-> J5,J6` (direct).
- `Batch Cure@12:05` relates `B1`, `OVEN-1`, `RACK-7`, `J1`, `J2`, `J3`, `C1`, `C2`, `C3` (direct event hyperedge).
- `Batch Cure@14:50` relates `B2`, `OVEN-1`, `RACK-7`, `J4`, `J5`, and their components (direct event hyperedge, with partial detail supplied).
- `J3 -acquire/release-> FIX-A`; `J4 -Machine Process@11:45-> CNC-2` with `FIX-A` and `C4` (direct).
- `Quarantine Lot@10:20` and `Release Lot@13:50` relate `L7`, `C2`, `C5`, `J2`, `J5` (direct in the richer object-centric view, omitted from the current Job flattening).
- `J5 -Final Test-> TEST-1` with `CAL-3`; `CAL-3 -Recalibrate-> 16:35-17:20`; `J6 -Final Test-> TEST-1` with `CAL-3` (direct).
- `J1 -waits_for_batch_partner-> J3 via B1`, `J2 -waits_for_batch_partner-> J3 via B1`, `J4 -waits_for_tool_release-> J3 via FIX-A`, `J1 -waits_for_shipment_partner-> J4 via S1`, `J6 -waits_for_tool_requalification-> J5/CAL-3` (inferred).

Higher-order motifs:

1. `Job-Component-Batch-Machine-Tool`: `J1/J2/J3` require distinct components, then meet at `B1`, which can start only when `OVEN-1` and `RACK-7` are jointly feasible. This is more than “shared machine”; it is a synchronization hyperedge.
2. `Job-Tool-Machine-Job`: `J3` holds `FIX-A`; `J4` reaches `CNC-2` but cannot start while the fixture is unavailable, even though the machine itself becomes idle. The effective coupling is `J3 -> FIX-A -> CNC-2 eligibility -> J4`.
3. `ComponentLot-Component-Job-Batch`: `L7` governs both `C2` and `C5`, coupling `J2` and `J5`. Because `J2` uses a supervisor override and `J5` does not, the same lot state propagates asymmetrically.
4. `Job-Shipment-Job`: `S1` couples `J1` and `J4` after production has finished. This creates a downstream cross-instance dependency invisible in the shipment DFG.
5. `Job-Tool-Machine-Job` at test: `J5` and `J6` both require `CAL-3` to use `TEST-1`; recalibration of the tool, not busy machine time, plausibly explains part of `J6`’s wait.

So the interaction graph is a set of overlapping shared-object motifs. Jobs that look independent in a flat lifecycle become coupled through `B1/B2`, `RACK-7`, `FIX-A`, `CAL-3`, `L7`, and `S1`.

## 2. Quantitative delay decomposition
These are not causal proofs. Each number depends on a stated counterfactual.

| Calculation | Result | Counterfactual assumption | Interpretation |
|---|---:|---|---|
| `J1` ready for `B1` at 08:10, `B1` starts 12:05 | `3 h 55 m = 235 min` | `J1` could have entered cure once itself ready, if batch policy or resource policy allowed | Upper-bound estimate of synchronization-related wait for `J1` before cure |
| `J2` ready for `B1` at 08:25, `B1` starts 12:05 | `3 h 40 m = 220 min` | Same assumption as above | Same for `J2` |
| `J1` extra wait relative to the late partner `J3` ready at 11:55 | `11:55 - 08:10 = 225 min` | The main reason `J1` cannot start earlier is waiting for the last batch member, not hidden oven work | Most of `J1`’s pre-batch time is explained by partner synchronization, with only `10 min` remaining after `J3` is ready |
| `J2` extra wait relative to `J3` | `11:55 - 08:25 = 210 min` | Same assumption | Same structure for `J2` |
| `J4` and `FIX-A`: `J4` reaches `CNC-2` at 09:50; `CNC-2` is idle 10:05-10:47; `FIX-A` remains held until 11:40 | Conservative lower bound `42 min`; plausible upper bound `100 min` from 10:05 to actual start 11:45 | Lower bound assumes only the known idle machine window is safely attributable; upper bound assumes no hidden blocker besides `FIX-A` after 10:05 | The machine is not the limiting object during at least part of the wait; the fixture plausibly is |
| `B2` with `RACK-7`: `J4` ready 13:10, `OVEN-1` idle from 12:55, but `RACK-7` quarantined until 14:35 | `1 h 25 m = 85 min` non-machine delay before the rack becomes feasible | `J4` is otherwise batch-eligible and oven availability is real | Shared rack state, not oven occupancy, plausibly explains much of `J4`’s post-CNC waiting |
| `J5` then sets `B2` start at 14:50 after being ready at 14:40 | `10 min` after last member is ready | Once both jobs and rack are feasible, batch launch is prompt | Suggests `B2` delay is mostly pre-readiness and rack-status, not slow dispatch into the oven |
| `L7` quarantine duration | `13:50 - 10:20 = 3 h 30 m = 210 min` | Lot quarantine really blocked dependent jobs unless overridden | Strong candidate coupling window for `J5`; weaker for `J2` because override breaks the link |
| `J1` packed at 13:30, `S1` dispatches at 19:25 | Total packed-to-dispatch wait `5 h 55 m = 355 min` | `J1` could leave independently once packed | Large downstream dwell on an already finished job |
| Partner-induced portion of `J1`’s shipment wait: `J4` packed at 19:10 | `19:10 - 13:30 = 5 h 40 m = 340 min` | `S1` truly waits for all consolidated members before dispatch prep begins | Most of `J1`’s post-pack wait is waiting for `J4`, not final loading |
| Excess `J1` dwell versus `S2/S3` style dispatch lag | `355 - 25 = 330 min` or `355 - 35 = 320 min` | If a solo `J1` shipment would behave like `S2` or `S3` after last pack | Consolidation, not loading itself, dominates `J1`’s shipment delay |
| `J6` ready for final test at 16:05; `J5` finishes test at 16:35; `J6` starts 17:25 | `50 min` after the prior test finishes | If `J6` could have started immediately after `J5` absent recalibration | Plausible `CAL-3`-related delay for `J6` |
| Pure recalibration window on `CAL-3` | `16:35-17:20 = 45 min` | The 5 minutes from 17:20 to 17:25 is ordinary setup, not extra waiting | Conservative lower bound attributable to calibration itself |

The decomposition points away from simple machine saturation. The largest observed waits are synchronization waits around batches and shipments, plus shared-tool qualification/availability.

If forced to name one dominant pattern, it is not “the oven” or “the tester.” It is synchronized eligibility on shared non-job objects.

## 4. What flattening misses
| Viewpoint | Visible in a flattening | Hidden or duplicated | Misleading DFG or performance conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Job | Lifecycle order such as `Release -> Stage -> Machine -> Inspect -> Pack -> Dispatch`; rework loop frequency | Shared lot events may disappear; batch start reason is hidden; tool waits are absorbed into long inter-event times; shipment partner wait after `Pack` is invisible | “Jobs look broadly normal and none is an outlier, so there is no severe bottleneck.” |
| Machine | Utilization and event gaps on `CNC-2`, `OVEN-1`, `TEST-1` | Jobs that are waiting but not machine-eligible; batch and tool prerequisites; already-packed jobs waiting for shipment | “Machines are only 44-58% utilized, so capacity is fine and waiting must be random noise.” |
| Batch | Common batch start and multi-job synchronization | Upstream reasons for late readiness; downstream shipment effects; the same batch event may be duplicated across jobs | “Only two batch events occurred, so batching cannot matter much.” |
| Tool | Holds, releases, recalibration, and shared use of `FIX-A`, `RACK-7`, `CAL-3` | Whether the tool itself or a policy on the tool caused the delay; missing explicit wait states can still obscure queueing | “Tool event counts are low, so tool contention is low-impact.” |
| ComponentLot | Quarantine/release states shared across components and jobs | Overrides can break the relationship; job-level flattening may omit the lot relation entirely; duplicated lot events can overstate frequency if naively joined | “Lot controls are irrelevant” or, in the opposite direction, “every linked job was blocked equally.” |
| Shipment | `Create Shipment -> Load -> Dispatch` on the shipment object | Early-ready job dwell inside the shipment; dependence on partner jobs; pack-to-dispatch wait at job level | “Shipments are quick, so logistics is not a source of delay.” |

Low machine utilization can coexist with long job waiting because jobs can be infeasible to process even when machines are idle. `J4` shows this at `CNC-2`: the machine is idle, but `FIX-A` is not free. `B2` shows it at the oven: `OVEN-1` is idle, but `RACK-7` is in cleaning/quarantine. `J1` shows it after production: the job is packed, but `S1` has not become feasible because `J4` is not ready. The scarce object is often a tool, lot state, or policy-defined group, not the machine.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence
**Narrative A: `J1` through `B1` and then `S1`**

- Temporal precedence: `J1` is ready for batch at 08:10. `J3` is the last `B1` member to become ready at 11:55. `B1` starts at 12:05 on `OVEN-1` with `RACK-7`. `J1` is packed at 13:30. `S1`, which also contains `J4`, dispatches only at 19:25.
- Resource feasibility: `J1` cannot enter the cure without the batch, the oven, and the rack being jointly available. After packing, `J1` cannot leave in `S1` until the shipment is loadable and dispatchable.
- Policy constraints: Batch formation and shipment consolidation both create group-level release rules. Neither is visible as a dedicated wait activity in the Job DFG.
- Causal speculation: It is plausible that most of `J1`’s elapsed time is driven by waiting on other instances, first `J3` via `B1`, then `J4` via `S1`, rather than by `J1`’s own processing content.

**Narrative B: `J3`’s fixture hold cascading into `J4`, then into `J1`**

- Temporal precedence: `J3` acquires `FIX-A` at 09:00 and releases it at 11:40. `J4` reaches `CNC-2` at 09:50. `CNC-2` is idle 10:05-10:47. `J4` begins `Machine Process` at 11:45 with `FIX-A`. `J4` is ready for `B2` at 13:10, but `B2` starts only at 14:50. `J4` is packed at 19:10, and `S1` dispatches at 19:25 with `J1`.
- Resource feasibility: `J4` needs both `CNC-2` and `FIX-A`. Later it needs `B2`, `OVEN-1`, and `RACK-7`. Finally, `S1` needs both `J1` and `J4`.
- Policy constraints: Tool sharing, batching, and shipment consolidation all convert one local delay into cross-instance propagation.
- Causal speculation: A fixture delay on `J4` may have cascaded into a later batch start and then into `J1`’s shipment wait. The chain is plausible but not proved because hidden operators or scheduling priorities may also intervene.

**Narrative C: `L7` governance on `J5`, then `CAL-3` governance on `J6`**

- Temporal precedence: `L7` is quarantined at 10:20 and released at 13:50. It contains `C2` for `J2` and `C5` for `J5`. `J2` enters `B1` before release because of a supervisor override in another system. `J5` does not proceed until after release. Later `J5` and `J6` are ready for final test at 16:00 and 16:05. `J5` tests from 16:10 to 16:35. `CAL-3` is recalibrated 16:35-17:20. `J6` tests 17:25-17:50.
- Resource feasibility: `J5` plausibly needs `L7` released to consume `C5`. `J6` needs both `TEST-1` and valid `CAL-3`.
- Policy constraints: Lot quarantine/release and calibration rules are administrative or quality controls, not raw machine capacity limits. Overrides can selectively break the lot-to-job dependency.
- Causal speculation: This narrative suggests two different control objects, `L7` and `CAL-3`, serially shaping flow. A flat machine view would miss both the asymmetric override on `J2` and the tester idleness caused by the tool rather than the machine.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers
| Intervention | Target mechanism | Expected benefit | Helped objects | Potentially harmed objects | New cost or risk | Perverse metric | Object-centric evaluation design |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Max-wait rule for oven batches or allow smaller `B1/B2` launches | Batch synchronization | Cuts the `235/220 min` style early-ready waiting on `B1` | `J1,J2`, and any early-ready batch members | `OVEN-1`, energy use, downstream pack/load labor, possibly `J3/J5` if more setups disrupt them | More partial loads, lower thermal efficiency, more handling | Maximizing batch fill rate could resist a change that improves lead time | Compare ready-to-batch-start wait by batch size and fill ratio before/after; stratify by `RACK-7` status and lot status |
| Add a second `FIX-A`, or enforce explicit fixture reservation/release SLA | Shared-tool contention at `CNC-2` | Reduces machine-idle-plus-job-wait situations like `J4` | `J4`-like jobs, `CNC-2` throughput | Jobs currently favored by long uninterrupted fixture holds, e.g. `J3` | Capital cost, more setup/changeover, possible fixture mismatch errors | Tool utilization alone might worsen even as job delay improves | Measure time where a job has reached a machine but cannot start because required tool is held elsewhere; compare by tool ID |
| Add a second `RACK-7` or split cleaning/quarantine from rack availability | Rack-mediated batch gating | Reduces the `85 min` non-machine delay seen before `B2` | `J4,J5`, any oven-bound jobs | Cleaning staff, QA workload, rack inventory cost | More WIP, more cleaning complexity, contamination risk if quarantine is relaxed improperly | Minimizing oven idle time could encourage unsafe rack reuse | Track `Batch` start delay decomposed into `job-ready`, `rack-ready`, and `oven-ready` components, not just oven utilization |
| Max packed-dwell rule or optional partial dispatch for consolidated shipments | Shipment consolidation | Reduces `J1`-style `355 min` packed-to-dispatch dwell | Early-packed jobs such as `J1` | Freight cost center, customers expecting one combined delivery, warehouse staging | More shipments, higher freight cost, lower cube utilization | Measuring only on-time shipment count may reward excessive splitting | Evaluate by `Job` packed-to-dispatch time, `Shipment` size, and customer service outcomes; compare split vs non-split consignments |
| Spare calibrated `CAL-3` or schedule recalibration into known slack windows | Test-tool requalification | Reduces `J6`’s plausible `45-50 min` tool-induced wait | `J6`-like trailing test jobs and `TEST-1` throughput | Metrology budget, calibration team, quality assurance if calibration is rushed | Higher tooling cost or calibration complexity | Throughput-only metrics may incentivize skipped calibration | Compare ready-to-test-start waiting conditional on prior activity being `Final Test` vs `Recalibrate`; separate machine busy time from tool invalid time |
| Logging/integration fix: direct Job links for lot events, explicit wait states, explicit override events | Data-integration artifact and observability failure | Better diagnosis; fewer false “normal” flattenings; more reliable attribution | Analysts, planners, all jobs indirectly | Data entry burden, event noise, integration effort | Over-logging can create noisy or gamed wait categories | “More events logged” is not improvement by itself | Re-run object-centric discovery before/after on the same period; compare whether inferred waits become explicit and whether alternative pipelines agree on `L7`-related dependencies |
| Standardize lot-override policy and record reason codes | Component-lot coupling with asymmetry | Distinguishes physical shortage from governance exceptions; avoids arbitrary coupling | `J2,J5` and future lot-linked jobs | Supervisors who rely on ad hoc overrides; compliance if rules are too loose | Slower exception handling or, conversely, quality escape risk | Minimizing override count alone may just push exceptions off-system | Compare cycle time and defect/rework by lot status, override use, and downstream quality results |

The spillover pattern matters. Reducing one synchronization barrier can shift work onto packing, shipping, or QA. That is why the evaluation should stay object-centric instead of machine-centric.

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline
```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# Core OCEL summaries
interaction_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)
objects_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(ocel)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)

# Traditional flattenings for comparison only
job_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Job")
machine_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Machine")
shipment_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Shipment")

job_perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(job_log)
machine_perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(machine_log)
shipment_perf_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(shipment_log)

# Object-centric features for downstream modeling / ranking
job_features = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(
    ocel,
    obj_type="Job",
    include_obj_graph_features=True,
    include_object_lifecycle_paths=True
)

# Optional graph conversion for custom motifs
nx_graph = pm4py.convert_ocel_to_networkx(ocel)

events = ocel.events.copy()
relations = ocel.relations.copy()

def normalize_edges(graph_like):
    if isinstance(graph_like, pd.DataFrame):
        return graph_like.copy()
    if isinstance(graph_like, dict) and "edges" in graph_like:
        return pd.DataFrame(graph_like["edges"])
    return pd.DataFrame(graph_like)

def rel_type(df, obj_type, oid_col):
    out = df[df["ocel:type"] == obj_type][["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid"]].copy()
    return out.rename(columns={"ocel:oid": oid_col})

# Compare two object-centric frequency lenses rather than one flattening only:
# 1) co-occurrence interactions summary
# 2) typed graph edge frequencies from the OTG
if isinstance(interaction_summary, pd.DataFrame):
    pair_freq_1 = (
        interaction_summary.groupby(["object_type_1", "object_type_2"])["count"]
        .sum()
        .rename("coevent_interactions")
    )
else:
    pair_freq_1 = pd.Series(dtype="float64", name="coevent_interactions")

otg_edges = normalize_edges(otg)
if not otg_edges.empty and {"source_type", "target_type", "weight"}.issubset(otg_edges.columns):
    pair_freq_2 = (
        otg_edges.groupby(["source_type", "target_type"])["weight"]
        .sum()
        .rename("otg_edge_weight")
    )
else:
    pair_freq_2 = pd.Series(dtype="float64", name="otg_edge_weight")

pair_comparison = pd.concat([pair_freq_1, pair_freq_2], axis=1).fillna(0)
print(pair_comparison.sort_values(list(pair_comparison.columns), ascending=False).head(15))

# Use ETOT to find activities with unusually broad object-type participation
etot_df = normalize_edges(etot)
if not etot_df.empty and {"activity", "object_type"}.issubset(etot_df.columns):
    multi_type_acts = etot_df.groupby("activity")["object_type"].nunique().sort_values(ascending=False)
    print(multi_type_acts.head(10))

# Relation-based waiting calculations:
# Do NOT infer waiting only from machine gaps.
# Instead, connect readiness, batch starts, shipment dispatches, and tool states through shared objects.

ready_batch = events[events["ocel:activity"] == "Ready for Batch"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:timestamp"]].copy()
batch_start = events[events["ocel:activity"].isin(["Load Batch", "Batch Cure"])][["ocel:eid", "ocel:timestamp"]].copy()

ready_jobs = rel_type(relations.merge(ready_batch, on="ocel:eid"), "Job", "job_id")
ready_jobs = ready_jobs.rename(columns={"ocel:timestamp": "ready_ts"})[["job_id", "ready_ts"]]

start_jobs = rel_type(relations.merge(batch_start, on="ocel:eid"), "Job", "job_id")
start_batches = rel_type(relations.merge(batch_start, on="ocel:eid"), "Batch", "batch_id")
start_ts = batch_start.rename(columns={"ocel:timestamp": "batch_start_ts"})

job_batch_wait = (
    start_jobs
    .merge(start_batches, on="ocel:eid")
    .merge(start_ts, on="ocel:eid")
    .merge(ready_jobs, on="job_id", how="inner")
)
job_batch_wait["wait_min"] = (
    job_batch_wait["batch_start_ts"] - job_batch_wait["ready_ts"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
print(job_batch_wait.groupby("batch_id")["wait_min"].describe())

packed = events[events["ocel:activity"] == "Pack"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:timestamp"]].copy()
dispatch = events[events["ocel:activity"] == "Dispatch"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:timestamp"]].copy()

pack_jobs = rel_type(relations.merge(packed, on="ocel:eid"), "Job", "job_id")
pack_jobs = pack_jobs.rename(columns={"ocel:timestamp": "pack_ts"})[["job_id", "pack_ts"]]

dispatch_ship = rel_type(relations.merge(dispatch, on="ocel:eid"), "Shipment", "shipment_id")
dispatch_ship = dispatch_ship.rename(columns={"ocel:timestamp": "dispatch_ts"})[["shipment_id", "dispatch_ts"]]

job_ship_links = (
    rel_type(relations, "Job", "job_id")
    .merge(rel_type(relations, "Shipment", "shipment_id"), on="ocel:eid")
    [["job_id", "shipment_id"]]
    .drop_duplicates()
)

job_ship_wait = (
    job_ship_links
    .merge(pack_jobs, on="job_id", how="inner")
    .merge(dispatch_ship, on="shipment_id", how="inner")
)
job_ship_wait["packed_to_dispatch_min"] = (
    job_ship_wait["dispatch_ts"] - job_ship_wait["pack_ts"]
).dt.total_seconds() / 60.0
print(job_ship_wait.groupby("shipment_id")["packed_to_dispatch_min"].describe())

# Tool-focused relation checks
tool_events = relations[relations["ocel:type"] == "Tool"][["ocel:eid", "ocel:oid"]].rename(columns={"ocel:oid": "tool_id"})
tool_timeline = tool_events.merge(events[["ocel:eid", "ocel:activity", "ocel:timestamp"]], on="ocel:eid")
print(tool_timeline.sort_values(["tool_id", "ocel:timestamp"]).head(30))

# OCDFG can be compared against flat DFGs to see which edges disappear under flattening
```

## 8. Falsification and additional data
Observations that would weaken the top-ranked batch-synchronization explanation:

- Evidence that `OVEN-1` was actually unavailable for preheat, maintenance, or another unlogged batch until just before 12:05 and 14:50.
- Audit proof that the “ready for batch” timestamps for `J1`, `J2`, or `J4` were backfilled early and did not represent true physical readiness.
- Operator-allocation records showing that an oven operator, not `J3` or `RACK-7`, was the limiting factor for `B1` or `B2`.
- Repeated cases where underfilled batches start immediately even when waiting partners exist, implying no strong fill policy.
- Quality rules showing that `B1` could not legally start before a common QA release unrelated to the jobs’ readiness times.
- Strong evidence that `RACK-7` quarantine was only clerical and another equivalent rack was feasible but not chosen for unrelated planning reasons.
- A broader sample showing that long waits occur equally on non-batched jobs, which would reduce the explanatory power of batch synchronization.

Additional fields or event types that would discriminate among hypotheses:

- Explicit `Wait for Batch`, `Wait for Tool`, `Wait for Lot Release`, and `Wait for Shipment Partner` start/end events with reason codes.
- Source system, author, and confidence flag for each readiness timestamp such as `Ready for Batch` or `Ready for Final Test`.
- Tool validity intervals and release/acquire events for all tools, including whether the hold is physical use, cleaning, quarantine, or administrative reservation.
- `RACK-7` cleaning/quarantine start and end events with contamination reason and whether a substitute rack existed.
- Lot-override events in the primary log, including approver, affected job, affected component, and expiration time.
- Shipment policy attributes: customer consolidation flag, carrier cutoff, promised ship date, and whether split shipment was allowed.
- Machine queue entry/exit events to distinguish “job waiting near machine” from “job not yet machine-eligible.”
- Operator assignment and labor availability events for `OVEN-1`, `CNC-2`, and `TEST-1`.
- Quality hold/release events tied directly to both jobs and lots.

The main falsification principle is simple: if earlier readiness is unreliable, or if hidden availability constraints explain the same gaps without shared-object synchronization, the ranking should change. But with the evidence given, the strongest explanation is cross-instance coupling through batches, tools, lot governance, and shipment consolidation rather than raw machine saturation.
