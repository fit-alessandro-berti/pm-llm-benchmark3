# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

Process mining models cannot be evaluated in a vacuum; model quality is strictly conditional on operational intent. For custom-order dossier processing, we distinguish four operational use cases:

1. **Exploration and Process Discovery**: Aimed at process analysts seeking to understand actual process execution, discover common routing paths, identify bottlenecks, and observe informal workarounds. In this context, high fitness on the observed log is prioritized to reflect reality, and moderate precision is tolerated to avoid discarding emergent or incomplete traces.
2. **Conformance Enforcement and Compliance Auditing**: Aimed at legal, risk, internal audit, and regulatory compliance. The model acts as an authoritative normative specification or automated gatekeeper (e.g., in a workflow engine or run-time compliance monitor). Here, precision against established business policy (preventing unauthorized shortcuts, fraud, or unreviewed order releases) is non-negotiable. An over-permissive model that admits illegal behavior is a critical liability.
3. **Discrete-Event Simulation and Digital Twins**: Aimed at capacity planning, queue management, and scenario testing. The model must accurately capture branching ratios and concurrency without generating phantom execution paths (which distort simulated cycle times) or getting stuck in artificial deadlocks.
4. **Stakeholder Communication and Training**: Aimed at business line managers, case workers, and IT architects as a shared mental model and contract. This demands low cognitive complexity, modularity, intuitive control flow, and clear responsibility hand-offs.

### The Fitness/Precision Trade-off Across Use Cases

The acceptable balance between alignment fitness and precision shifts dramatically across these domains:
- In **Exploration**, a discovery team may intentionally accept lower precision (e.g., 0.60) in exchange for structural simplicity (0.90+) and high fitness (0.98+), using the model as an aggregated cognitive map.
- In **Conformance Enforcement**, high fitness achieved by making mandatory checks optional is fatal. A false negative in compliance detection (failing to flag an unverified order release) exposes the firm to financial, operational, and regulatory penalties. Precision must be measured not merely against historical positive logs, but against a curated suite of negative/forbidden policy behaviors.
- In **Simulation**, extreme overfitting (like Model C) fails because it cannot generate plausible unobserved combinations, while extreme underfitting (like Model S) simulates orders released without credit checks.

Custom-order processing carries direct financial credit risk and technical production risk. Therefore, our primary governance evaluation assumes **Conformance Enforcement and IT System Governance**, while evaluating suitability for exploration and stakeholder alignment.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

### Strongest Case For Model S
Model S is structurally compact, provably sound as a workflow net, highly intelligible to business users (comprehension score 4.4), and computationally efficient (18 ms per case). It captures the high-level macro-milestones of the order lifecycle: an order starts with `Register Dossier`, proceeds through pre-approval evaluation, requires a single approval gate (`Manager Approval` XOR `Auto Approval`), and concludes with `Release Order`. In an exploratory discovery setting where logs are noisy, asynchronous logging delays occur, or intermediate tasks are frequently unrecorded, Model S provides a clean, resilient executive overview without crashing or flagging false-positive bottlenecks.

### Strongest Case Against Model S
Model S is legally, financially, and operationally dangerous. In process mining terms, it is an under-constrained, flower-like model over the verification phase. By nesting all four review activities—`Credit Check`, `Technical Review`, `Supplier Review`, and `Expedite Review`—inside an AND construct (`+`) where every single child is wrapped in an optional choice `X(activity, tau)`, Model S permits any arbitrary interleaving of any subset of these activities, including the empty set. 

This directly destroys the core governance policies of the organization:
- It permits an order to be approved and released without either a credit check or a technical review (violating P2).
- It permits an order to be expedited before mandatory diligence is completed (violating P4).
- It completely omits `Correct Dossier` and rework cycles (violating P6 and P7).

### Five Concrete Policy Violations Admitted by Model S

1. **Complete Bypass of Mandatory Diligence (Probe B3)**:
   $$\langle \text{Register Dossier}, \text{Manager Approval}, \text{Release Order} \rangle$$
   *Analysis*: Skips all four branches of the parallel construct via silent transitions ($\tau$). Bypasses mandatory `Credit Check` and `Technical Review` (violating P2). An order with catastrophic insolvency or impossible technical specifications can be immediately approved and released.

2. **Expedite Inversion and Priority Bypass (Probe B4)**:
   $$\langle \text{Register Dossier}, \text{Expedite Review}, \text{Credit Check}, \text{Technical Review}, \text{Manager Approval}, \text{Release Order} \rangle$$
   *Analysis*: Because `Expedite Review` is in parallel with the checks, it can fire first. Violates P4, which dictates that `Expedite Review` can only occur *after* mandatory credit and technical validation has been successfully established.

3. **Expedite Without Verification Followed by Auto Approval**:
   $$\langle \text{Register Dossier}, \text{Expedite Review}, \text{Auto Approval}, \text{Release Order} \rangle$$
   *Analysis*: Simultaneously violates P2 and P4. The system executes an expedite check, skips both mandatory checks via $\tau$, auto-approves the dossier, and releases the order. This is a severe audit failure.

4. **Single-Check Partial Diligence**:
   $$\langle \text{Register Dossier}, \text{Credit Check}, \text{Manager Approval}, \text{Release Order} \rangle$$
   *Analysis*: Executes `Credit Check` while skipping `Technical Review` via $\tau$. Violates P2. Allows an order with unverified engineering feasibility to enter production.

5. **Disordered Supplier Sourcing and Check Bypass**:
   $$\langle \text{Register Dossier}, \text{Supplier Review}, \text{Credit Check}, \text{Auto Approval}, \text{Release Order} \rangle$$
   *Analysis*: Executes `Supplier Review` before or without `Technical Review`. If the order is internal, it violates P3; if external, it still violates P2 by omitting `Technical Review`.

### Intentional Noise Tolerance vs. Accidental Semantic Flaws
The discovery team claimed Model S is "robust to missing events and easy for business users." However, there is a fundamental distinction between intentional noise tolerance and accidental semantic flaws:
- **Intentional noise tolerance** relaxes strict sequencing when logging pipelines are known to drop asynchronous telemetry (e.g., allowing `Supplier Review` to log slightly out of sync).
- **Accidental semantic flaw**: The modelers conflated *concurrency* ("Credit Check and Technical Review may complete in either order") with *optionality and unconstrained independence* ("any check can be skipped and any check can interleave with expedite"). Using `+(X(A, tau), X(B, tau), ...)` is an elementary modeling error that generates the power set of all permutations of the sub-activities. It was an accidental semantic failure, not a principled engineering choice.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

### Strongest Case For Model C
Model C is a strict, conservative model designed to prevent unauthorized behavior. It achieves a high training precision (0.941) and guards the core compliance invariants: it never allows mandatory checks to be bypassed, it never allows `Expedite Review` to precede mandatory checks, and it rejects uncorrected repeated checks. For an audit team that demands an explicit whitelist of historically verified and legally approved execution sequences, Model C guarantees that no trace outside of the eight pre-cleared paths will pass conformance validation without human escalation.

### Strongest Case Against Model C
Model C is not a process model; it is an eight-leaf decision tree that verbatim memorizes eight specific training traces. It suffers from severe combinatorial brittleness, extreme out-of-sample degradation, catastrophic maintenance overhead, and metric distortion.

### Key Pathologies of Model C

1. **Branch Enumeration and Combinatorial Explosion**:
   Business processes are inherently compositional: check order (2 options: Credit then Technical, or Technical then Credit), supplier involvement (2 options: present or absent), expedite review (2 options: present or absent), and approval type (2 options: Manager or Auto) yield $2 \times 2 \times 2 \times 2 = 16$ basic valid paths even before considering rework loops. Model C attempts to capture this through top-level exclusive choice `X(...)`. Enumerating 8 specific paths while omitting the other 8 valid combinations guarantees failure on legitimate variations.

2. **Severe Duplicate Labels**:
   Model C contains 33 duplicate labels. When converted to a Petri net, it generates 50 transitions for just 9 unique business activities. Each activity (such as `Register Dossier`, `Manager Approval`, and `Release Order`) is duplicated across nearly every branch. This destroys process mining diagnostics:
   - Token replay and alignments cannot deterministically map an observed event to a unique transition without backtracking.
   - Bottleneck analysis and performance mining cannot determine which transition represents the true operational station.
   - IT architecture and ownership mapping (F3) become impossible.

3. **Rejection of Unseen Legitimate Combinations (Probes B2 and B5)**:
   Model C exhibits disastrous holdout degradation (dropping from 0.973 to 0.744 fitness). This is directly explained by its failure on B2 and B5:
   - In B2 (`Register Dossier -> Technical Review -> Credit Check -> Auto Approval -> Release Order`), the alternate check order is valid (P2) and `Auto Approval` is valid (P5). But Model C only memorized `Auto Approval` with the *Credit Check first* order (Branch 8), and only memorized *Technical Review first* with `Manager Approval` (Branch 2). It rejects B2 with an alignment fitness of 0.80.
   - In B5 (combining reverse check order, `Supplier Review`, `Expedite Review`, and `Auto Approval`), Model C fails completely (fitness 0.75) because this multi-feature combination was absent from the training set.

4. **Rigid and Pathological Correction Handling**:
   Branch 7 encodes rework as:
   $$\langle \text{Register Dossier}, \text{Credit Check}, \text{Technical Review}, \text{Correct Dossier}, \text{Credit Check}, \text{Technical Review}, \text{Manager Approval}, \text{Release Order} \rangle$$
   This representation has three fatal flaws:
   - It is strictly bounded to exactly one correction cycle (no looping). A second correction is rejected.
   - It forces *both* `Credit Check` and `Technical Review` to repeat, violating business reality (F6) where corrections typically affect only credit terms or only technical specifications. Probe B6 is rejected (fitness 0.92).
   - It only permits correction after the `Credit Check -> Technical Review` initial order, rejecting corrections that follow `Technical Review -> Credit Check`.

5. **Maintainability and Technical Debt**:
   If the organization introduces a new pre-release notification or modifies approval rules, developers must update every branch of Model C independently. Inevitable discrepancies across branches will introduce latent bugs and audit contradictions.

6. **High Precision as an Artifact of Memorization**:
   The 0.941 training precision is not evidence of a well-defined process abstraction. It is an artifact of overfitting to the positive training log. Because Model C defines its language as exactly the observed variants, its precision against that log is near-perfect, but its utility for true process governance is near zero.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Metric-driven governance is vulnerable to severe distortions when metrics are uncoupled from underlying process semantics. Below is a rigorous analysis of the ten observed pathologies:

1. **Train/Holdout Leakage or Split Sensitivity**:
   The teams used an unseeded, non-stratified 80/20 random split. In business processes with power-law variant distributions, a random split often places frequent variants entirely in training and complex, multi-feature edge cases (like B2 and B5) in holdout. This exaggerates Model C's out-of-sample failure while allowing Model S's vacuous permissiveness to masquerade as "generalization."
2. **Missing-Event Bias**:
   As noted in F4, `Supplier Review` events may be inconsistently logged. If an event is intermittently missing from the log, alignment algorithms penalize models that strictly require it. Model S is artificially rewarded because its silent transition $\tau$ absorbs missing events without penalty, hiding telemetry infrastructure failures.
3. **Positive-Log-Only Precision**:
   Precision in PM4Py is evaluated by measuring how often the model allows transitions that were not observed in the positive log. Because no curated negative trace suite was used (F5), precision metrics only penalize deviations from historical behavior, completely failing to detect whether the model admits legally catastrophic paths (like B3 or B4).
4. **Duplicated-Label Effects**:
   Model C instantiates 33 duplicate labels across 8 branches. During alignment and precision calculations, duplicate labels create non-deterministic replay paths. The $A^*$ search cannot resolve which transition to fire without exploring all duplicate paths, leading to state-space explosion and distorting prefix-based precision estimators.
5. **Silent-Transition ($\tau$) Effects**:
   Model S relies heavily on silent transitions in `+(X('Credit Check', tau), ...)`. Silent transitions provide "free" bypasses in standard alignment cost functions. This artificially deflates alignment costs, boosting fitness scores while gutting the model’s behavioral precision.
6. **Alignment-Cost Sensitivity**:
   Standard alignment configurations assign equal unit weight (1.0) to all moves on log and model. In custom-order processing, skipping a mandatory `Credit Check` (a severe fraud/regulatory violation) incurs the exact same cost penalty (1.0) as an unlogged optional `Supplier Review`. This makes fitness scores blind to critical business risk.
7. **Fitness Rewarded by Optionalizing Mandatory Behavior**:
   A modeler can trivially achieve $\ge 0.99$ fitness on any process log by wrapping all activities in optional bypasses (`X(A, tau)`). Model S exploits this mathematical loophole: its 0.992 training fitness is a metric artifact of permissiveness, not process fidelity.
8. **Generalization Rewarded by Excessive Permissiveness**:
   Token-based generalization (`generalization_tbr`) measures the probability that a newly observed trace can be replayed without creating missing or remaining tokens, evaluating state visitation frequencies. Highly permissive models with parallel silent loops provide near-infinite token availability, perversely earning high generalization scores (0.907 for S vs. 0.412 for C).
9. **Stakeholder-Score Methodology**:
   The reported 4.4 vs. 1.9 stakeholder scores reflect informal, uncontrolled subjective sentiment. Stakeholders rate visual cleanliness and low diagrammatic complexity, mistaking visual simplicity for functional correctness. Without task-based testing (e.g., asking users to identify if an illegal trace is allowed), stakeholder polling measures aesthetic appeal rather than semantic validity.
10. **Runtime Confounding by Model Size and Duplicates**:
    Model C’s 0.440 s alignment runtime (24x slower than S) is not due to process complexity, but to poor model engineering. The combination of top-level exclusive choice and 33 duplicate labels forces the $A^*$ search queue to evaluate dozens of identical-looking search paths.

### Ranking of Pathologies by Likely Decision Impact

1. **Fitness Rewarded by Optionalizing Mandatory Behavior (Rank 1 - Highest Impact)**: Directly led the discovery team to declare Model S "robust," when in reality it legalized complete compliance bypasses.
2. **Positive-Log-Only Precision (Rank 2)**: Masked Model S’s dangerous permissiveness and created an illusion that Model C was "audit-ready."
3. **Train/Holdout Split Sensitivity (Rank 3)**: Obscured whether Model C’s 0.744 holdout fitness was caused by bad process discovery or unstratified sampling of orthogonal feature combinations.
4. **Silent-Transition Effects (Rank 4)**: Mechanically enabled the fitness inflation in Model S without exposing the loss of control flow.
5. **Duplicated-Label Effects (Rank 5)**: Crippled Model C's runtime performance, maintainability, and alignment diagnostics.
6. **Alignment-Cost Sensitivity (Rank 6)**: Equated minor logging omissions with major regulatory non-compliance.
7. **Generalization Rewarded by Permissiveness (Rank 7)**: Provided false statistical legitimacy to Model S's underfitting.
8. **Missing-Event Bias (Rank 8)**: Confounded logging pipeline reliability with business process routing.
9. **Runtime Confounding by Model Size (Rank 9)**: Created a false impression that precise models are inherently non-viable in real-time environments.
10. **Stakeholder-Score Methodology (Rank 10 - Lowest Impact on Board Decision)**: While operationally relevant, informal user preference must never override formal audit verification.

# 7. Third Alternative

To resolve the fatal permissiveness of Model S and the brittle memorization of Model C, we design a balanced hybrid architecture: a **Partially Ordered Workflow Language (POWL)** procedural backbone that strictly enforces all control-flow invariants (P1, P2, P4, P5, P7), augmented with an **explicit declarative / data-policy layer** to address P3 (external sourcing) and P6 (selective correction rechecks) without resorting to blanket optionality.

### 1. Compact Formal Description

The procedural control-flow backbone is structured as a strict partial order of modular phases:
$$\text{model\_third} = \text{PO}(\text{Nodes}, \prec)$$

$$\text{Nodes} = \{ T_{\text{reg}}, \text{PO}_{\text{init}}, \text{Loop}_{\text{corr}}, \text{PO}_{\text{rev}}, \text{XOR}_{\text{app}}, T_{\text{rel}} \}$$

$$\text{Order} \prec = \{ (T_{\text{reg}}, \text{PO}_{\text{init}}), (\text{PO}_{\text{init}}, \text{Loop}_{\text{corr}}), (\text{Loop}_{\text{corr}}, \text{PO}_{\text{rev}}), (\text{PO}_{\text{rev}}, \text{XOR}_{\text{app}}), (\text{XOR}_{\text{app}}, T_{\text{rel}}) \}$$

- **Phase 1 (Registration)**: $T_{\text{reg}} = \text{Transition}(\text{'Register Dossier'})$.
- **Phase 2 (Mandatory Verification)**: $\text{PO}_{\text{init}} = \text{PO}(\{ T_{\text{cc}}, T_{\text{tr}} \}, \emptyset)$. Both `Credit Check` and `Technical Review` are strictly mandatory; the empty edge set permits concurrent execution and either completion order (P2).
- **Phase 3 (Controlled Correction Loop)**: $\text{Loop}_{\text{corr}} = \text{LOOP}(\tau, \text{PO}_{\text{body}})$, where:
  $$\text{PO}_{\text{body}} = \text{PO}(\{ T_{\text{cd}}, \text{Choice}_{\text{recheck}} \}, \{ (T_{\text{cd}}, \text{Choice}_{\text{recheck}}) \})$$
  $$\text{Choice}_{\text{recheck}} = \text{XOR}(T_{\text{cc\_re}}, T_{\text{tr\_re}}, \text{PO}(\{ T_{\text{cc\_re2}}, T_{\text{tr\_re2}} \}, \emptyset))$$
  Semantics: The initial iteration exits immediately via $\tau$ if no correction is needed. If `Correct Dossier` fires, it *must* be followed by repeating only Credit Check, only Technical Review, or both in either order. No repeated check can occur without `Correct Dossier` (P6, P7).
- **Phase 4 (Extended Reviews)**: $\text{PO}_{\text{rev}} = \text{PO}(\{ \text{Opt}_{\text{sr}}, \text{Opt}_{\text{er}} \}, \emptyset)$, where $\text{Opt}_{x} = \text{XOR}(T_x, \tau)$. Both reviews must strictly follow mandatory verification and rework, and must precede approval. When both occur, they can execute in either order (P4).
- **Phase 5 (Approval Gate)**: $\text{XOR}_{\text{app}} = \text{XOR}(T_{\text{ma}}, T_{\text{aa}})$. Exactly one approval fires (P5).
- **Phase 6 (Release)**: $T_{\text{rel}} = \text{Transition}(\text{'Release Order'})$ (P1).

### 2. Self-Contained PM4Py Code Assigning `model_third`

```python
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.powl.obj import Transition, SilentTransition, OperatorPOWL, StrictPartialOrder, Operator

    # Phase 1: Registration
    t_reg = Transition(label='Register Dossier')

    # Phase 2: Mandatory initial diligence (Credit Check and Technical Review in parallel)
    t_cc_init = Transition(label='Credit Check')
    t_tr_init = Transition(label='Technical Review')
    mandatory_checks = StrictPartialOrder(nodes=[t_cc_init, t_tr_init])
    # No edge between t_cc_init and t_tr_init enforces that both must occur, in either order

    # Phase 3: Controlled rework loop
    t_cd = Transition(label='Correct Dossier')
    t_cc_re = Transition(label='Credit Check')
    t_tr_re = Transition(label='Technical Review')
    t_cc_re2 = Transition(label='Credit Check')
    t_tr_re2 = Transition(label='Technical Review')
    recheck_both = StrictPartialOrder(nodes=[t_cc_re2, t_tr_re2])

    recheck_choice = OperatorPOWL(Operator.XOR, [t_cc_re, t_tr_re, recheck_both])
    correction_body = StrictPartialOrder(nodes=[t_cd, recheck_choice])
    correction_body.order.add_edge(t_cd, recheck_choice)
    correction_loop = OperatorPOWL(Operator.LOOP, [SilentTransition(), correction_body])

    # Phase 4: Extended reviews (Supplier Review and Expedite Review)
    t_sr = Transition(label='Supplier Review')
    opt_sr = OperatorPOWL(Operator.XOR, [t_sr, SilentTransition()])

    t_er = Transition(label='Expedite Review')
    opt_er = OperatorPOWL(Operator.XOR, [t_er, SilentTransition()])

    extended_reviews = StrictPartialOrder(nodes=[opt_sr, opt_er])

    # Phase 5: Mutually exclusive approval
    t_ma = Transition(label='Manager Approval')
    t_aa = Transition(label='Auto Approval')
    approval_choice = OperatorPOWL(Operator.XOR, [t_ma, t_aa])

    # Phase 6: Order release
    t_rel = Transition(label='Release Order')

    # Assemble complete POWL model
    model_third = StrictPartialOrder(
        nodes=[t_reg, mandatory_checks, correction_loop, extended_reviews, approval_choice, t_rel]
    )
    model_third.order.add_edge(t_reg, mandatory_checks)
    model_third.order.add_edge(mandatory_checks, correction_loop)
    model_third.order.add_edge(correction_loop, extended_reviews)
    model_third.order.add_edge(extended_reviews, approval_choice)
    model_third.order.add_edge(approval_choice, t_rel)
```

### 3. Declarative Dictionary and External Post-Hoc Checks

Because the control-flow log lacks `is_external` and `affected_check` attributes, procedural modeling alone cannot enforce data-dependent policies. The procedural backbone above is therefore paired with an external declarative rule engine:

```python
declarative_policy_checks = {
    # P3: Supplier Review is mandatory for external orders, strictly forbidden for internal orders
    "P3_SUPPLIER_REVIEW_POLICY": {
        "rule_type": "attribute_guard",
        "condition": lambda trace, attrs: (
            ("Supplier Review" in [e["concept:name"] for e in trace]) if attrs.get("order_type") == "external"
            else ("Supplier Review" not in [e["concept:name"] for e in trace])
        ),
        "violation_severity": "CRITICAL"
    },
    # P6: Recheck must match the specific department affected by the correction
    "P6_AFFECTED_RECHECK_POLICY": {
        "rule_type": "correction_scope_guard",
        "condition": lambda trace, attrs: (
            all(
                (attrs.get("correction_target") == "credit" and "Credit Check" in trace[i+1:i+3]) or
                (attrs.get("correction_target") == "technical" and "Technical Review" in trace[i+1:i+3]) or
                (attrs.get("correction_target") == "both")
                for i, e in enumerate(trace) if e["concept:name"] == "Correct Dossier"
            ) if "Correct Dossier" in [e["concept:name"] for e in trace] else True
        ),
        "violation_severity": "HIGH"
    },
    # P7: Anti-tamper check: No repeated mandatory checks without immediately preceding Correct Dossier
    "P7_UNAUTHORIZED_RECHECK_POLICY": {
        "rule_type": "sequence_guard",
        "condition": lambda trace: (
            all(
                trace[i-1]["concept:name"] == "Correct Dossier"
                for i in range(1, len(trace))
                if trace[i]["concept:name"] in {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}
                and trace[:i].count(trace[i]["concept:name"]) >= 1
            )
        ),
        "violation_severity": "CRITICAL"
    }
}
```

### 4. Explicit Semantics of Policy Enforcement
- **Mandatory Checks (P2)**: `mandatory_checks` contains no silent transitions. Both `Credit Check` and `Technical Review` must fire before the token can advance to subsequent phases. Concurrency allows any completion order.
- **Optional Supplier Review (P3)**: Modeled as optional in control flow to absorb the missing data attribute in the discovery log, but strictly regulated at runtime by the `P3_SUPPLIER_REVIEW_POLICY` data guard.
- **Expedite Review Ordering (P4)**: Positioned in `extended_reviews` downstream of `mandatory_checks` and `correction_loop`, and upstream of `approval_choice`. It cannot fire prior to mandatory diligence, but can fire before or after `Supplier Review`.
- **Exactly-One Approval (P5)**: Modeled as a strict 1-of-2 XOR operator between `Manager Approval` and `Auto Approval`.
- **Correction and Recheck (P6, P7)**: Modeled in `correction_loop`. Initial pass allows 0 corrections (via $\tau$). If `Correct Dossier` executes, the body mandates firing at least one recheck (`Credit Check`, `Technical Review`, or both). Firing repeated checks outside this construct is mathematically impossible.

### 5. Limitations Caused by Missing Data Attributes
Without `order_type` (`external` vs `internal`) and `affected_check` in the event log:
1. **Control-flow ambiguity**: A pure control-flow validator cannot detect an unauthorized `Supplier Review` executed on an internal order, nor can it detect an omitted `Supplier Review` on an external order.
2. **Audit imprecision**: A pure control-flow validator cannot verify whether re-running only `Technical Review` was appropriate when the underlying defect was actually in the credit dossier.
Treating these activities as silently optional everywhere (as Model S did) completely destroys governance. Our hybrid architecture confines control-flow variability strictly to valid policy bounds and flags data-dependent constraints for external attribute enrichment.

# 9. Validation Experiments

To replace anecdotal model claims with rigorous empirical evidence, we design an end-to-end reproducible validation program.

### Concrete Evaluation Program Protocol
1. **Reproducible Case Splits**: Implement stratified 5-fold cross-validation with fixed pseudo-random seeds (`seed=42`). Preprocessing, activity filtering, and case splits must be serialized to JSON/Parquet scripts.
2. **Attribute-Stratified Holdout Testing**: Stratify holdout splits by trace length, approval type, and presence of `Supplier Review` to guarantee that out-of-sample tests evaluate genuine generalization rather than variant omission.
3. **Curated Negative Trace Suite**: Formulate an authoritative test suite of synthetic non-compliant traces (e.g., probes B3, B4, Adv3, and double-approval traces) to test true discriminative power.
4. **Perturbation Testing**: Inject synthetic logging noise:
   - Randomly drop 5% of non-mandatory events (`Supplier Review`, `Expedite Review`) to evaluate missing-event robustness.
   - Inject uncorrected duplicate events to verify anti-tamper detection (P7).
5. **Unseen-Combination Testing**: Systematically construct all 16 orthogonal Cartesian product combinations of valid features and evaluate alignment fitness across models.
6. **Alignment-Cost Sensitivity Sweep**: Re-evaluate alignments across a grid of cost settings:
   - Standard: $\text{cost}(\text{log}) = 1.0, \text{cost}(\text{model}) = 1.0, \text{cost}(\tau) = 0.0$.
   - Compliance-Weighted: $\text{cost}(\text{model}_{\text{mandatory}}) = 5.0, \text{cost}(\text{model}_{\text{optional}}) = 1.0$.
7. **Soundness and Reachability**: Execute automated structural checks for workflow net soundness, absence of dead transitions, and 1-boundedness.
8. **Disaggregated Reporting**: Fitness, precision, generalization, and simplicity must be reported independently; no composite aggregate scores are permitted.
9. **Documented Stakeholder Protocol**: Measure comprehension through a timed, task-based objective test with 20 cross-functional participants (5 auditors, 5 case workers, 5 managers, 5 developers). Participants must evaluate whether 10 sample traces are compliant. Score = accuracy (%) $\times$ speed (cases/min).
10. **Hard Governance Decision Rule**: Any model that achieves an alignment fitness $> 0.90$ on any curated *negative* trace (such as B3 or B4) is **instantly disqualified**, regardless of its aggregate fitness, simplicity, or user preference score.

### PM4Py Validation Code Skeleton

```python
import pm4py
from pm4py.objects.log.obj import EventLog, Trace, Event

def run_model_validation_suite(model_obj, train_log, holdout_log, negative_log):
    """
    Executes an empirical validation program on a candidate process model.
    Accepts POWL models or Process Trees, converting to Petri Nets.
    """
    # 1. Structural Soundness and Reachability Check
    net, im, fm = pm4py.convert_to_petri_net(model_obj)
    is_sound, diagnostics = pm4py.check_soundness(net, im, fm)
    if not is_sound:
        raise ValueError(f"Governance Rejection: Model is not sound. Diagnostics: {diagnostics}")

    # 2. Baseline Training Metrics (Reported Disaggregated)
    train_fitness_dict = pm4py.fitness_alignments(train_log, net, im, fm)
    train_precision = pm4py.precision_alignments(train_log, net, im, fm)
    train_generalization = pm4py.generalization_tbr(train_log, net, im, fm)
    simplicity_arc_degree = pm4py.simplicity_petri_net(net, im, fm, variant="arc_degree")

    # 3. Holdout Generalization Test (Out-of-sample Fitness)
    holdout_fitness_dict = pm4py.fitness_alignments(holdout_log, net, im, fm)

    # 4. Curated Negative Trace Suite (Policy Discriminative Test)
    negative_diagnostics = pm4py.conformance_diagnostics_alignments(negative_log, net, im, fm)
    
    # 5. Apply Hard Governance Decision Rule
    # Immediate disqualification if any forbidden trace achieves alignment fitness >= 0.90
    max_negative_fitness = max(res["fitness"] for res in negative_diagnostics)
    governance_passed = max_negative_fitness < 0.90

    results = {
        "soundness": is_sound,
        "simplicity_arc_degree": simplicity_arc_degree,
        "train_log_fitness": train_fitness_dict["log_fitness"],
        "train_precision": train_precision,
        "train_generalization": train_generalization,
        "holdout_log_fitness": holdout_fitness_dict["log_fitness"],
        "max_negative_trace_fitness": max_negative_fitness,
        "governance_status": "APPROVED" if governance_passed else "DISQUALIFIED_POLICY_BREACH"
    }
    
    return results
```

# 11. Dissenting View

### Rebuttal to Governance Recommendation

The recommendation to retire both models and deploy an unproven hybrid architecture underestimates enterprise reality and dismisses legitimate stakeholder needs. Model C's advocate correctly argues that in heavily regulated, high-value custom manufacturing, the primary mandate is risk containment, not elegant process generalization. A model that strictly whitelists eight verified, legally audited execution variants guarantees zero compliance false negatives for those paths. In an audit enforcement context, rejecting an unseen combination (like B2 or B5) is not a defect; it is a vital fail-safe that forces human compliance intervention before an untested variant enters production. 

Conversely, the recommendation dismisses Model S’s 4.4 stakeholder rating too readily. Line workers and operational managers will not adopt a complex POWL model coupled to external Python declarative dictionaries. Introducing a dual-layer architecture—partly Petri net, partly runtime rule engine—creates split ownership between process analysts and software engineers, multiplying integration failure points. Model C provides an immutable, transparent whitelist, while Model S provides an intuitive operational summary. Abandoning two working models for an abstract hybrid construct introduces unnecessary implementation risk, project delay, and organizational friction without proving that the added complexity will be maintained once the consultants depart.

### Additional Evidence Required to Overturn Decision

We would reverse our recommendation and maintain the existing models under the following empirical findings:
1. If an historical audit reveals that 99.9% of all historical business volume is strictly covered by Model C's 8 branches, and that unseen variants in holdout logs represent rogue employee non-compliance rather than legitimate business needs. In that case, Model C's out-of-sample rejection would be an asset rather than an overfitting defect.
2. If IT performance benchmarks prove that running external declarative guards alongside POWL alignment engines introduces higher latency and transaction failure rates than Model C's 440 ms duplicate-label alignments.
3. If stakeholder comprehension testing reveals that operational teams misinterpret the selective rework loop of the Third Model, leading to higher manual logging error rates than the visual simplicity of Model S.
4. If enterprise logging infrastructure cannot be updated to capture `order_type` or `correction_target`, rendering data-dependent declarative constraints impossible to execute. In that event, a constrained procedural variant whitelist would be the only enforceable fallback.
