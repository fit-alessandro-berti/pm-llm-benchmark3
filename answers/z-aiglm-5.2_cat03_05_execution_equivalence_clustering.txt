## 1. Equivalence design space

To analyze the eight object-centric executions, we define three distinct equivalence relations, ranging from syntactic to semantic. 

### 1.1 Activity-sequence equivalence
**Definition:** Two executions are equivalent if their sequences of activity labels are identical. 
**Invariants preserved:** The exact ordered sequence of activity labels (e.g., `[Create Order, Add Item, Add Item, Pack Package, Dispatch, ...]`).
**Information discarded:** Object identifiers, object types, object multiplicities, external object flags, O2O relations, and O2O qualifiers.
**Properties:** Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
**Concurrency handling:** If two events share the exact same timestamp, they are considered concurrent. Their relative order is discarded, and they are treated as a set (or sorted alphabetically to form a deterministic sequence).

### 1.2 Typed structural equivalence under identifier renaming
**Definition:** Two executions are equivalent if there exists an isomorphism between their object-centric event graphs that maps objects to objects preserving object types, preserves the `EXT` external flag, preserves the exact E2O incidence, preserves event order (as a partial order for concurrent events), and preserves O2O relations and qualifiers.
**Invariants preserved:** Event order, E2O incidence, O2O topology and qualifiers, object types, and external object presence.
**Information discarded:** Raw local object identifiers (e.g., `I11` vs `I21`).
**Properties:** Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
**Concurrency handling:** Concurrent events are treated as an unordered set; only the partial order induced by timestamps is preserved.

### 1.3 Business-semantic equivalence
**Definition:** Two executions are equivalent if they share the same "business motif" defined by: structural multiplicities (number of packages, deliveries, payments), the presence of external shared objects, the presence of lifecycle qualifiers, and the presence of return/replace events.
**Invariants preserved:** The business shape of the execution (e.g., "single package, shared delivery", "cross-order invoice sharing", "hidden replacement").
**Information discarded:** Exact object counts if they do not alter the motif (e.g., 2 items vs 3 items in a single package are equivalent), exact interleaving of events if the motif remains intact, and local identifiers.
**Properties:** Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
**Concurrency handling:** Handled via partial orders, where only the high-level motif (e.g., "interleaved packing and dispatch") is retained.

**Operational risks/opportunities:** Risk of billing the customer for the wrong item. Opportunity to silently fulfill orders despite stockouts.
**Traditional flattening issue:** Traditional flattening loses the O2O qualifiers entirely, making the replacement invisible and treating it as a standard order.
**Stability under data repair:** Fragile. If qualifiers are ignored, this collapses into the Baseline cluster.

### SEM-6: Return & Repack (X8)
**Concise signature:** `(2, 2, 1, F, F, T)` — Two packages, two deliveries, one payment, with return events.
**Business behavior:** A package is dispatched, but an item is returned, inspected, repacked, and redispatched. 
**Operational risks/opportunities:** High logistical cost and risk of inventory shrinkage. Opportunity to recover value from returned goods.
**Traditional flattening issue:** Traditional flattening would create a confusing loop of `Dispatch -> Return -> Pack -> Dispatch` without distinguishing that different items and packages are involved.
**Stability under data repair:** Highly stable due to the explicit distinct activity labels (`Return Item`, `Inspect Return`).

## 5. Borderline cases

### X1 versus X2
**Disagreement:** These two executions do not disagree under any of the defined equivalences. Under activity-sequence equivalence, they are identical. Under typed structural equivalence, the isomorphism maps `I11` to `I21` and `I12` to `I22` (note that the reversed order of `Add Item` does not matter if we assume they are concurrent or if we only preserve the partial order). Under business-semantic equivalence, they share the baseline motif. They serve as the proof of concept that local identifier renaming should be ignored.

### X1 versus X5
**Disagreement:** Under activity-sequence equivalence, X1 and X5 are identical (both follow `Create, Add, Add, Pack, Dispatch, Issue, Record`). However, typed structural equivalence strongly disagrees: X5 includes `EXT-OA` and `EXT-IA`, meaning any isomorphism must map local objects to local objects and external objects to external objects, which is impossible. Business-semantic equivalence also disagrees, grouping X5 with X6 due to the `has_ext` flag.

### X1 versus X6
**Disagreement:** Similar to X5, activity-sequence equivalence groups X6 with X1. Typed structural equivalence separates them because X6's `Issue Invoice` and `Record Payment` events include `EXT-OB`, breaking isomorphism with X1. Business-semantic equivalence groups X6 with X5, acknowledging that both represent cross-order sharing, just in different domains (logistics vs. finance).

### X1 versus X7
**Disagreement:** Activity-sequence equivalence treats them as identical. Typed structural equivalence separates them because X7 contains the `removed_before_dispatch` qualifier on the O2O relation between `P71` and `I71`, an edge that does not exist in X1. Business-semantic equivalence separates them because the `has_qualifier` flag is true for X7, indicating a lifecycle anomaly (hidden replacement).

### X3 versus X4
**Disagreement:** Activity-sequence equivalence separates them (X3 has `Pack, Pack, Dispatch`; X4 has `Pack, Dispatch, Pack, Dispatch`). Typed structural equivalence separates them due to different multiplicities of deliveries and payments. However, one could define a "Coarse Logistics" equivalence that only looks at whether `num_packages > 1`. Under that coarse equivalence, X3 and X4 would be grouped together, ignoring the delivery split and partial payments.

### X4 versus X8
**Disagreement:** Activity-sequence equivalence separates them (X8 has explicit `Return Item` and `Inspect Return` events). Typed structural equivalence separates them due to the different activity sequences and E2O incidence. However, if we define a "Multi-Package" equivalence that only checks `num_packages == 2`, X4 and X8 would be grouped, collapsing the distinction between a clean interleaved fulfillment and a reverse-logistics return process.

## 6. Relation to `pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel`

**What PM4Py key structure preserves:** The `pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel` function typically hashes the exact structure of the object-centric event log for a given lead object. This includes the raw object identifiers, the exact E2O incidence matrix, and the sequence of activities. 

**Why raw object identifiers make exact clusters too fine:** Because every new order creates new, unique identifiers (e.g., `O4`, `I41`, `P42`), no two orders will ever share the same raw graph structure. This results in a clustering where every order is in its own singleton cluster, defeating the purpose of segmentation.

**Canonicalization or post-processing:** To make PM4Py clusters useful, we must canonicalize the object identifiers before clustering. We map local objects to type-relative labels (e.g., `Package_1`, `Package_2`) while preserving the `EXT` prefix for external objects. We also extract semantic features (like the motif signature defined in Section 2) and cluster on those features rather than the raw graph hash.

**When exact equivalence is preferable:** Exact equivalence is preferable when debugging a specific data quality issue or investigating a known anomalous high-value order where the precise identity of objects and their exact timestamp ordering are legally or operationally required.

**Impact of `max_objs`:** `max_objs` limits the size of the connected component extracted around the lead object. If an execution involves a large cross-order network (like a massive shared invoice), `max_objs` might truncate the graph, accidentally dropping `EXT` objects. This would cause a cross-order sharing execution (SEM-4) to be falsely clustered as a baseline isolated execution (SEM-1).

```python
import pm4py
from collections import Counter

def cluster_orders_semantically(ocel, lead_type="Order"):
    # 1. Extract equivalent clusters using PM4Py's built-in function
    # This groups orders that are exactly identical in structure
    raw_clusters = pm4py.cluster_equivalent_ocel(ocel, lead_object_type=lead_type)
    
    semantic_clusters = {}
    
    for cluster_idx, cluster_df in raw_clusters.items():
        for _, row in cluster_df.iterrows():
            order_id = row["object_id"] # Assuming this maps to the lead object
            
            # 2. Extract events and O2O relations for this specific order
            events = get_events_for_order(ocel, order_id)
            o2o_notes = get_o2o_for_order(ocel, order_id)
            
            # 3. Apply custom canonicalization and semantic signature
            # This ignores local IDs and extracts the business motif
            signature = get_business_semantic_signature(events, o2o_notes)
            
            # 4. Re-cluster based on the semantic signature
            if signature not in semantic_clusters:
                semantic_clusters[signature] = []
            semantic_clusters[signature].append(order_id)
            
    return semantic_clusters

# Note: get_events_for_order and get_o2o_for_order are helper functions 
# assumed to exist, querying the ocel object for the subgraph of a specific lead object.
```

## 7. Robustness analysis

We analyze how cluster assignments change under four perturbations:

1. **Ignore all O2O qualifiers:** 
   - **Change:** X7 loses its `removed_before_dispatch` qualifier. Its signature becomes `(1, 1, 1, F, F, F)`.
   - **Impact:** X7 collapses from SEM-5 (Hidden Replacement) into SEM-1 (Baseline Isolated). The system loses visibility of item substitutions, treating them as standard orders.

2. **Ignore external objects:**
   - **Change:** X5 and X6 lose their `EXT` flags. Their signatures become `(1, 1, 1, F, F, F)`.
   - **Impact:** Both collapse into SEM-1 (Baseline Isolated). The system completely misses cross-order sharing, leading to incorrect revenue and capacity calculations.

3. **Collapse repeated same-label events:**
   - **Change:** X4's two `Record Payment` events are collapsed into one. X4's signature changes from `(2, 2, 2, ...)` to `(2, 2, 1, ...)`.
   - **Impact:** X4 moves from SEM-3 (Interleaved & Partial Pay) to a new intermediate cluster. It hides the fact that the invoice was settled in multiple transactions, which is critical for financial compliance.

4. **Treat Pack and Dispatch event order as a partial order rather than a total order:**
   - **Change:** The strict interleaving `Pack -> Dispatch -> Pack -> Dispatch` in X4 is treated as concurrent sets. 
   - **Impact:** Under typed structural equivalence, X4 becomes isomorphic to an execution with `Pack -> Pack -> Dispatch -> Dispatch`. However, its business-semantic signature `(2, 2, 2, F, F, F)` remains unchanged because the multiplicities are preserved. The cluster assignment is stable.

5. *(Optional)* **Treat payment multiplicity as an amount attribute rather than structure:**
   - **Change:** The two `Record Payment` events in X4 are replaced by a single event with an attribute `amount=2`.
   - **Impact:** Similar to collapsing repeated events, X4's signature becomes `(2, 2, 1, ...)`. While this simplifies the graph, it loses the temporal aspect of partial settlements (e.g., customer paid a deposit, then the remainder).

## 8. Limitations and recommendation

**Recommendations by purpose:**

1. **Compliance monitoring:** Use **Typed structural equivalence**. Compliance requires exactness. If an item was replaced (X7) or an invoice was shared (X6), the exact O2O qualifiers and external objects must be preserved to ensure legal and financial regulations are met. Coarse semantic groupings might hide compliance violations.

2. **Operational segmentation:** Use **Business-semantic equivalence**. For routing orders to different operational teams (e.g., a team specialized in returns vs. a team handling standard fulfillment), the high-level business motif is the most useful grouping. It abstracts away noise (like local IDs) while highlighting the process shape.

3. **Root-cause analysis:** Use a combination of **Activity-sequence and Business-semantic equivalence**. Root-cause analysis requires identifying deviating paths. Activity-sequence equivalence is too coarse (grouping X1 and X7 together), but business-semantic equivalence correctly isolates the hidden replacement motif (SEM-5) allowing analysts to investigate why replacements are happening.

4. **Descriptive reporting:** Use **Business-semantic equivalence**. For management dashboards reporting on the percentage of standard vs. split vs. shared orders, the semantic clusters provide intuitive, high-level categories that are not cluttered by identifier noise or minor event interleaving.

**Why no single equivalence is best:**
No single equivalence notion is best for all four purposes because they operate on different axes of information. Strict structural equivalence preserves too much noise (local IDs), making it useless for segmentation but perfect for compliance. Activity-sequence equivalence is too blind to object structures, making it dangerous for root-cause analysis. Business-semantic equivalence balances these by discarding noise while preserving shape, but it deliberately discards exact qualifiers that compliance might need. The optimal choice depends on the specific trade-off between syntactic precision and semantic generalization required by the task.
