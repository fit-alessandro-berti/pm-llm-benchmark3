# Robust Redesign of the Diagnostic-Lab Sample Process

## 1. Stress scenarios

**S1 — Winter mix shift (demand composition shock).**
Volume rises ~20–35% versus normal weekly rate, but the damaging feature is composition: Urgent share grows from 18% to ~29% and Complex-panel from 13% to ~20% (as in the observed peak log). This shifts load away from batchable Routine work toward validation-heavy work: in the peak DFG, Technical validation → Medical validation rises from 24.5% of technically validated cases (3,900/15,890) to 47% (3,420/7,240), and Hold for specialist review doubles as a share (290 → 610 on roughly half the volume).

**S2 — Assay-machine degradation or outage.**
M2 develops an elevated failure rate (its known weakness) or M1 goes down for 24–72h. The combined pool already runs at 98% utilization in peak with an 18h median queue, so losing even 30% of effective capacity is not absorbable. Repeat-after-failure work is clustered after night shifts and M2 usage, so degradation is self-amplifying: each failure consumes assay capacity twice (peak repeat rate is already 1,260/8,500 ≈ 14.8% of assay exits versus 6.7% in normal periods).

**S3 — Medical validator / specialist absence.**
One or more medical validators absent during a peak week, compounded by the structural absence of one specialist every second Friday. Medical validators sit at 93% peak utilization with a 22h median queue and specialist review at 87% with a 36h queue; the Hold → Medical validation edge already degrades from 18h to 48h in peak. Absence during a mix shift (S1) is the worst realistic combination because exactly the cases that grow (Urgent, Complex-panel) require these resources.

**S4 — Courier arrival compression or delay.**
Weather or route consolidation compresses arrivals further into the 10:00 and 17:00 spikes, or delays the morning wave by 3–5h. Courier intake runs at 88% in peak with a 7.5h median queue; compression converts a capacity problem into a synchronization problem: pre-analytical check (96% peak) receives a wall of samples, and the Receive sample → Pre-analytical check edge (2h normal, 9h peak) blows out further. Delay also silently eats into the 18h Urgent SLA before the lab has even touched the sample.

**S5 — Compound scenario (mix shift + M2 degradation + Friday specialist gap).**
Used as the acid test for the redesign: winter wave with elevated repeats and reduced specialist coverage. Any design that only survives S1–S4 individually but not a plausible pairing is not robust.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

**S1 (mix shift).** The first visible constraint is the assay pool (98%), but the *binding* constraint migrates downstream: Technical validators exceed capacity (101% peak utilization — an unstable queue that grows without bound), and because the mix shift routes ~47% of technically validated cases onward to Medical validation, the queue then migrates to Medical validators (58% → 93%, queue 2h → 22h) and Specialist review (43% → 87%, queue 5h → 36h). Relieving assay throughput alone (e.g., faster runs on M2) *worsens* this: it pumps more work per hour into a validation chain that is already the true constraint, and M2's higher failure rate feeds the Repeat assay loop (Repeat assay → Assay run round trip is 26h median in peak), consuming the very assay capacity the fix was meant to free. Upstream, Urgent bypass of Batch wait drops from 74% to 58% in peak — meaning priority discipline erodes exactly when it matters — while Batch wait entries rise from 4.5% to 12.7% of preparation output with a 22h wait to assay.

**S2 (machine degradation).** Bottleneck starts at the assay pool, but the failure loop moves it in two directions at once. Downstream: fewer completed runs per hour starves technical validation briefly, then the repeat surge arrives and overloads it. Upstream: Test preparation (89%) accumulates WIP because dispatch stalls; managers respond by enlarging batches (I2 instinct), which lengthens Batch wait → Assay run further and pushes Urgent cases past the 18h SLA. If I3 (dedicate M1 to Urgent/Complex, Routine to M2) is active during M2 degradation, all Routine volume runs on the failing machine — Repeat-after-failure cycle time (already 145h median / 240h p90 in peak) explodes, and repeats then compete for M1 slots, breaking the dedication scheme itself.

**S3 (validator/specialist absence).** The bottleneck is immediately Medical validation / Specialist review, but it migrates upstream as blocked WIP: cases pile up post-technical-validation, technical validators lose bench space and start context-switching to status inquiries, and Urgent/Complex cases age past SLA while physically finished. If the countermeasure is I4 (pull technical validators into accession correction) at the same time, technical validation — already at 101% — collapses, and the queue re-forms one station earlier. The correct migration reading: this scenario cannot be fixed by any upstream intervention; only validation-side capacity or diversion (I5, I10) addresses it, and I10 shifts the constraint to transport and traceability (12h delay makes it useless for Urgent).

**S4 (courier compression).** The bottleneck begins at Courier intake and Pre-analytical check simultaneously. Pre-analytical at 96% cannot absorb the spike, so defect detection is delayed; delayed detection lengthens the recollection loop (Request recollection → Receive sample: 42h normal, 60h peak) and raises abandonment (Request recollection → Cancel order rises from 290/570 splits in normal to 370 in peak on half the volume). Accession correction (2 specialists, 91% utilization, 14h queue) becomes a hidden serial bottleneck: each stuck correction blocks a case from entering preparation at all. Relieving registration via I8 automation without fixing arrival smoothing simply delivers the wall of samples to pre-analytical faster — the conflict hint is correct that I8 exposes pre-analytical and assay bottlenecks.

**S5 (compound).** The system fails in a chain: intake spike → delayed defect detection → recollection loop consumes clerk time (per variant notes, clerks explaining errors to physicians reduces intake capacity — a feedback loop) → preparation batches enlarge → assay pool saturated with repeats → technical validation over 100% → medical validation and specialist review queues exceed 24–48h → Urgent penalties and Repeat-after-failure p90 beyond 240h. Every station is coupled; the design goal is to break the feedback loops (repeats, recollection) rather than to add capacity at whichever station screams loudest.

## 3. Robust redesign package

These interventions help in most scenarios and do not depend on knowing which scenario will occur.

**R1 — Early pre-analytical defect triage (I6).**
Reject or flag poor samples within 2h of receipt. Robust because the recollection loop is a pure cycle-time and capacity parasite in every scenario: it costs a 42–60h round trip, consumes clerk time on physician calls, and drives Repeat-after-failure tails (132h → 240h p90). Cutting detection latency shortens the loop and raises the chance a same-day recollection succeeds instead of ending in Cancel order. *Assumption:* triage staffing comes from restructured pre-analytical work, not new heads, and triage quality is good — false rejects would create recollection load rather than remove it. *Failure mode:* under S4 compression, the 2h target is unreachable at spike hours unless combined with R2.

**R2 — Courier arrival smoothing (I7).**
Spread pickup slots to flatten the 10:00/17:00 walls. Robust because it attacks synchronization, not capacity: pre-analytical at 96% peak utilization can only survive if arrivals are level-loaded; the same intake capacity handles far more volume when it is not delivered in two spikes. It benefits every scenario and directly mitigates S4. *Assumption:* courier contracts and clinic draw schedules can actually shift. *Failure mode:* smoothing delays some morning samples' arrival; Urgent samples must keep a fast direct route or their 18h clock suffers.

**R3 — Registration automation for complete digital orders (I8), gated.**
Automate the clean majority to free clerks for exception handling and recollection communication (the observed clerk drain). Robust because clerk time is a shared resource across scenarios, and overtime beyond 9h shifts raises error rates that feed accession correction. *Explicit downstream guard:* per the conflict hint, automation exposes pre-analytical and assay constraints, so it ships only together with R1/R2 and the dispatch rules in R5 — automation increases arrival rate at pre-analytical, and without triage and smoothing it just moves the queue one station downstream. *Failure mode:* poor metadata quality routes more, not fewer, orders into Manual accession correction.

**R4 — Two-way cross-training pool for correction and technical validation (modified I4).**
Cross-train two technical validators for accession correction *and* cross-train two other staff (e.g., senior preparation techs) toward technical validation (4-week training per the resource notes). Robust because the two stations bottleneck in different scenarios: correction chokes in S4/S5 (14h queue, only 2 specialists, each stuck case blocks everything downstream), technical validation chokes in S1/S2 (101%). A one-way move (raw I4) fails exactly when technical validation is the constraint; a pooled, rule-governed float ("float to correction only when technical validation queue age < 6h") is robust. *Assumption:* skills are genuinely transferable and the float rule is enforced. *Failure mode:* both stations saturate simultaneously in S5; the pool then only chooses which queue grows.

**R5 — Dynamic dispatch with WIP caps and aging promotion (replaces raw I1/I2).**
At preparation and assay: Urgent goes first, but (a) Routine and Complex cases are promoted to Urgent-equivalent priority once their elapsed time crosses a fraction of target (e.g., Routine at 48h of its 72h target), and (b) batch size is a function of queue state — small batches (8–12) when any Urgent case waits, larger batches (up to 24) only when the Urgent queue is empty and Routine backlog exceeds a threshold. Robust because strict Urgent-first (I1) starves Routine/Complex and inflates WIP, while fixed large batches (I2) sacrifice Urgent SLA; a state-dependent rule degrades gracefully in all scenarios and directly counters the observed erosion of Urgent bypass from 74% to 58% in peak. *Assumption:* the LIMS can implement queue-state-dependent rules and staff follow them under pressure. *Failure mode:* if everything is aged/promoted (deep S5), the rule degenerates to FIFO — which is at least fair, not catastrophic.

**R6 — Attack the repeat loop at the source (M2 reliability + repeat-cause logging).**
Preventive maintenance and calibration on M2, plus mandatory cause codes on every Repeat assay (machine, sample quality, operator/night shift). Robust because repeats are the single largest hidden capacity drain in peak (≈15% of assay exits, each consuming a second machine slot and a 26h round trip) and they amplify every scenario, especially S2. This is capacity recovered without adding machines, honoring the "don't add capacity everywhere" constraint. *Assumption:* a meaningful share of repeats is machine/shift-attributable, which the clustering after night shifts and M2 usage strongly suggests. *Failure mode:* if repeats are dominated by sample quality, R1 is the fix and R6 yields little.

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

**P1 — Evening medical-validation shift (I5).**
*Activate when:* Medical validation queue age > 8h for two consecutive half-days, or (Urgent + Complex) share of daily arrivals > 40%, or a validator absence is announced during a declared peak. *Deactivate when:* queue age < 4h for three consecutive days. Targets S1/S3/S5. Downstream guard: releases from evening validation land on Release result, which is not a constraint (Medical validation → Release is 2–5h), so this is one of the few interventions with no meaningful downstream overload.

**P2 — Complex-panel overflow outsourcing (I10).**
*Activate when:* Specialist review queue age > 24h *and* the forecast (validator roster + Friday specialist gap) shows no recovery within 48h. Route only Routine-priority Complex panels whose remaining target comfortably exceeds the 12h transport delay; never Urgent. *Deactivate when:* specialist queue age < 12h. Targets S3/S5. Downstream guard: transport and traceability become the new risk (see Section 6); cap outsourced volume at the partner's committed capacity and reconcile results daily.

**P3 — Machine dedication (I3), only during degradation events.**
*Activate when:* M2 repeat rate exceeds 1.5× its trailing 4-week baseline, or one machine is down: reserve the healthy/faster-turnaround machine for Urgent and Complex, run Routine on the degraded machine *with reduced batch sizes* to limit repeat blast radius. *Deactivate* on repeat-rate normalization. As a standing policy, I3 is fragile (the conflict hint is right: all-Routine-on-M2 raises repeats in high load); as a temporary containment rule during S2, it protects SLA-critical work.

**P4 — Strict Urgent-first override (raw I1).**
*Activate only when:* Urgent p90 in-process age approaches 15h (3h of SLA buffer left) — a genuine penalty-avoidance emergency. *Time-box to 48h* and monitor Routine aging explicitly, because this policy deliberately sacrifices Routine flow. *Deactivate* when Urgent p90 age < 12h.

**P5 — Batch-report Routine release (I9), narrow use only.**
*Activate only when:* Release-step or validator clerical load is demonstrably constraining (it is not in the data — Medical validation → Release is 2h normal) and Routine cases have > 24h of target headroom. Default state: **off**. I9 adds pure waiting to Routine cases whose p90 already reaches 120h in peak; it is a cost-saving lever, not a robustness lever, and under S1/S5 it would push Routine p90 past the 72h target for a larger fraction of cases.

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

1. **Specialist review remains a single-point-of-failure.** With one specialist unavailable every second Friday and 87% peak utilization, no policy above adds specialist capacity in-house. P2 (outsourcing) is a partial diversion, but an unplanned absence coinciding with a peak Friday still produces 36h+ hold queues for Complex panels. Structural fix (recruiting or tele-review agreements) is outside the current lever set and should be raised to management.
2. **Redesign-induced: the float pool can be double-claimed.** R4 assumes correction and technical validation do not saturate simultaneously; in S5 they do. The float rule then merely selects which queue grows, and worse, a manager under pressure may hold floats at their home station, silently reverting to raw I4 behavior — recreating the exact technical-validation collapse the modification was designed to prevent. This is a fragility *created* by the redesign's reliance on rule discipline.
3. **Redesign-induced: outsourcing traceability.** P2 introduces a parallel results pathway with a 12h transport leg. Sample chain-of-custody errors or result-format mismatches would generate a new class of rework invisible to the current DFG (it would surface as unexplained Repeat-after-failure or manual reconciliation effort at Release).
4. **Triage calibration risk.** If R1's reject criteria are tuned too aggressively, recollection volume rises, clinics lose confidence, and Cancel-after-recollection (already 370 in the 3-week peak) grows — lost orders rather than slow orders.
5. **Compound tail cases.** Repeat-after-failure Complex panels during S5 pass through every mitigated-but-not-eliminated queue twice; their p90 will improve but remain the worst in the system. No average-based reporting should be allowed to hide this tail.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

Daily during declared peak periods; leading indicators are read at mid-shift so same-day action is possible.

**Leading indicators (predict tomorrow's failure):**
1. **Queue age (oldest waiting case), per station**, for Medical validation, Specialist review, Technical validation, Accession correction. Bands: green < 4h, amber 4–8h, red > 8h (Medical validation red triggers P1; Specialist review > 24h triggers P2 evaluation).
2. **Urgent in-process age distribution:** count of Urgent cases older than 12h not yet in validation. Amber ≥ 5, red ≥ 10 (red triggers P4 consideration).
3. **Repeat-assay rate per machine per shift:** amber > 10%, red > 1.5× trailing baseline (red triggers P3).
4. **Arrival concentration:** share of daily samples arriving within the two worst hours. Amber > 35%, red > 45% (red = escalate courier smoothing, pre-stage triage staff).
5. **Defect detection latency:** % of pre-analytical rejects issued within 2h of receipt. Green > 85%; falling values predict next week's Repeat-after-failure tail.
6. **Urgent batch-bypass rate:** amber < 70%, red < 60% (dispatch discipline eroding — the observed peak signature).
7. **Aging-promotion count (R5):** number of Routine/Complex cases promoted per day. A rising trend is the earliest signal that the whole system, not one station, is saturating (S5 onset).

**Lagging outcome metrics (confirm, weekly):** p90 cycle time by case type (not medians — the tails carry the penalties), Urgent SLA breach rate, Routine 72h attainment, overall rework %, Cancel-after-recollection count, and outsourced-case turnaround versus partner commitment. These validate the redesign but are explicitly *not* the steering signals: by the time p90 moves, the leading indicators above should already have fired.
