## 1. Analytical assumptions

A case is one object of the selected type. Its trace contains each event directly related to that object once, in timestamp order. I use **convergence** for the same event entering multiple cases of the selected type. I use **divergence** for distinct events from different related object lifecycles being serialized in one case, so repetition or adjacency can be mistaken for rework or a handoff. O1's two dispatches illustrate divergence. V2's two payments are genuine stages of one invoice lifecycle, even though two Payment objects are involved.

A shared event is evidence of participation in every linked case and duplication when those rows are used to count source events. For example, e16 is one dispatch, involves three orders and two packages, and contributes three Order rows or two Package rows. I keep source event IDs and report the counting unit with each frequency or average. A directly-follows graph (DFG) describes adjacency within a projection. Its edges do not establish causality.

The baseline uses direct E2O only. I would join qualified O2O links to explain relationships and test explicitly inherited event associations in a separate projection. For instance, invoice events can be inherited as billing context by an item via `Invoice —bills→ Order —contains→ Item`. I would retain the path and the direct or inherited status, deduplicate by event and target object, and avoid unrestricted traversal. Durations below are elapsed time between recorded timestamps.

## 3. Concrete distortion analysis

1. **Order flattening turns shared packing work into multiple rows.** e13 is one Pack Package event for P3, containing I21 from O2 and I31 from O3. Both order traces count it, so a workload tally finds six pack rows from four packing events. Two order touches are informative for exposure. Two packs attributed to e13 would inflate throughput.
2. **Order flattening creates a cross-package loop for O3.** e13 packs I31 into P3 and e14 packs I32 into P4, yielding Pack Package→Pack Package with an eight-minute gap in O3. P3 also serves O2 and P4 also serves O4. The gap describes the timing of two milestones for O3. Interpreting it as time spent repacking one package is harmful.
3. **Package flattening conceals mixed-order composition.** P3's e13→e16→e24 and P4's e14→e17→e27 both have the same activity variant as the single-order P1 and P2. The common variant is informative for package handling. It hides which orders would be affected if P3 or P4 were delayed.
4. **Delivery flattening compresses D2's reach into one trace.** e16→e24 is one transit observation for D2, two for packages P2/P3, and three for items I12/I21/I31. The Delivery, Package and Item means over the snapshot are respectively 10 h 13 min 20 s, 8 h 53 min 45 s and 10 h 32 min 30 s. Each mean describes a different population experiencing delay. Comparing the three as carrier performance would mix denominators.
5. **Order flattening omits Dispatch→Confirm as a direct edge.** Every order has billing activity between its last dispatch and its first confirmation. O1, for example, shows e16→e18→e21→e23, where e16 concerns D2 and e23 confirms D1. The missing edge is informative about the order's interleaved milestones. It cannot measure missing confirmations, and pairing the nearest timestamps would mismatch deliveries for O1.
6. **Order flattening inflates invoice volume under consolidated billing.** e19 issues V2 once, and both O2 and O3 contain it. The order DFG has four Issue Invoice→Record Payment occurrences, including two copies of e19→e22. The invoice DFG has three. The order frequency measures first-payment exposure across orders. Treating it as billing workload overcounts, and attributing the full payment to both orders would require allocation information absent here.
7. **Invoice flattening exposes the instalment interval but requires a settlement definition.** On V2, e19→e22 measures 35 min to a partial payment. e22→e25 measures another 20 h 20 min to completion, as indicated by the M2 and M3 settlement qualifiers. In Order traces O2 and O3, e24 separates e22 from e25, so there is no Record Payment→Record Payment edge. The invoice edge is useful for collection timing. A first-payment metric used as a settlement metric understates V2's time by 20 h 20 min.
8. **Payment flattening makes an unobserved lifecycle appear instantaneous.** M2 consists of e22 and M3 of e25. Four singleton traces produce four starts and four ends at Record Payment, with no transitions. This is informative about recording frequency. A zero first-to-last duration cannot establish zero payment processing time or recover V2's instalment schedule.
9. **Order flattening can suggest a payment-to-delivery dependency.** O3 contributes Record Payment→Confirm Delivery twice, via e22→e24 and e25→e27. The payment in each pair concerns V2, while the confirmations concern D2 and D3, respectively. Those edges are useful for reviewing O3's observed sequence. Their frequency alone gives no evidence that recording an instalment releases a delivery.

## 4. Traditional versus object-centric analysis portfolio

Create Order and Invoice logs for recurring order-close and collection reporting. On Order, measure creation to closure for O1–O4 and clearly name milestones such as *last* confirmation. On Invoice, distinguish first payment from completion using M2 and M3's O2O qualifiers. Create Delivery, Package and Item logs when the question is transit per delivery, time from packing to dispatch per package, or time from item addition to confirmation, respectively. The P2/P3 and I21/I31 examples show why those choices should be explicit. A payment-event ledger suffices for recording counts. A Payment case log is useful as a projection diagnostic here.

- Use an **OC-DFG** to compare adjacency by object type. It shows Order's interleaved fulfilment and finance, Delivery's three transit observations, and Invoice's e22→e25 instalment transition in one model. Use event IDs and qualified links to trace specific cases back from the aggregated model.
- Use the **OTG** to inspect routes among types, such as Delivery–Package–Item–Order and Order–Invoice–Payment. Then follow the qualified instance links to identify which orders D2 reaches and why. A type connection alone cannot give D2's instance counts.
- Use an **ET–OT graph** to check which object types participate in each activity, for example that Dispatch includes Delivery and Item while Record Payment includes Invoice and Payment. Inspect the underlying E2O sets to distinguish e16's one Delivery, two Packages and three Orders.
- Use an **object interaction summary** to find shared work, then confirm the role of each object with E2O and O2O. In particular, O2 and O3 share both P3 and V2, while O3 and O4 share P4. Distinguish physical consolidation from shared billing in the resulting exposure analysis.

Equivalent-execution clustering would address which recurring multi-object execution shapes occur in a larger log with meaningful execution units. Here, D2 links O1 to O2/O3, and P4 links O3 to O4. Connected-component grouping of the supplied graph yields one component, so it provides no basis for comparing execution clusters.

When findings differ, align their counting units and recover their supporting event IDs. For example, six Order dispatch rows and three Delivery dispatches reconcile to e15, e16 and e17 with order multiplicities of one, three and two. For elapsed time, reconcile endpoints using a shared object ID. Use D2 to pair e16 with e24 and use V2 and its qualifiers to pair e19 with e25 for settlement. Retain both results when they describe different populations or milestones.

## 5. Lead-object decision

**C. Reject a single lead object for this process.**

- The decision depends on whether a case preserves the boundaries of the work being counted, pairs duration endpoints on the right lifecycle, and retains the relationships needed to explain delays across functions.
- The strongest evidence is that the units for fulfilment and settlement overlap differently. D2 covers O1, O2 and O3, while V2 bills O2 and O3. O1 also uses D1 and O3 uses D3. An order trace gives a valid customer timeline, but neither a D2 transit observation nor a V2 settlement has a unique order owner.
- The strongest counterargument is that orders define clear accountability. O1–O4 each have creation and closure, and together their traces cover all 31 events. This makes Order useful for customer-facing service levels and for a timeline joining logistics and finance from the customer's perspective.
- The residual risk is inconsistent reporting across teams. For example, a dashboard may place a package-weighted transit average beside a delivery-level target or use e25 to infer the shares of V2 paid for O2 and O3. Metric definitions need explicit endpoints, units and any shared-event weighting.
- I would change to an Order-led analysis with secondary lifecycles if the scope became explicitly order-level SLA reporting and order milestone definitions were agreed. Shared delivery and settlement operations would still be measured on Delivery and Invoice or by qualified joins.

## 6. PM4Py analysis outline

The outline assumes a PM4Py release that exposes the named discovery functions. It uses the six projections for diagnosis and independently audits DFG support from source event IDs. Log skeletons describe the observed traces. For example, a shared activity pattern across P1–P4 supplies no rule about how many orders a package may hold.

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

print(ocel)

event_id = ocel.event_id_column
activity = ocel.event_activity
timestamp = ocel.event_timestamp
object_id = ocel.object_id_column
object_type = ocel.object_type_column
case_types = ("Order", "Item", "Package", "Delivery", "Invoice", "Payment")

events = ocel.events[[event_id, activity, timestamp]]
objects = ocel.objects[[object_id, object_type]]
activity_frequencies = events[activity].value_counts()
object_type_frequencies = objects[object_type].value_counts()

direct_memberships = (
    ocel.relations[[event_id, object_id]]
    .drop_duplicates()
    .merge(events, on=event_id, validate="many_to_one")
    .merge(objects, on=object_id, validate="many_to_one")
)
projection_counts = direct_memberships.groupby(object_type).agg(
    cases=(object_id, "nunique"),
    source_events=(event_id, "nunique"),
    case_event_rows=(event_id, "size"),
)
projection_counts["extra_rows"] = (
    projection_counts["case_event_rows"] - projection_counts["source_events"]
)
print(activity_frequencies)
print(object_type_frequencies)
print(projection_counts)

flattened_logs = {
    kind: pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, kind) for kind in case_types
}
frequency_dfgs = {
    kind: pm4py.discover_dfg(log) for kind, log in flattened_logs.items()
}
log_skeletons = {
    kind: pm4py.discover_log_skeleton(log) for kind, log in flattened_logs.items()
}
shipping_performance_dfgs = {
    kind: pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(flattened_logs[kind])
    for kind in ("Item", "Package", "Delivery")
}
for kind, log in flattened_logs.items():
    assert len(log) == projection_counts.loc[kind, "case_event_rows"], kind

object_centric_dfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)
object_type_graph = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)
event_type_object_type_graph = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)
object_interactions = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)

# Audit observed adjacency independently of the flattened log's event ID field.
# There are no same-object timestamp ties in this snapshot.
ordered_memberships = direct_memberships.sort_values(
    [object_type, object_id, timestamp, event_id]
).copy()
case_groups = ordered_memberships.groupby([object_type, object_id], sort=False)
ordered_memberships["next_event_id"] = case_groups[event_id].shift(-1)
ordered_memberships["next_activity"] = case_groups[activity].shift(-1)
edge_occurrences = ordered_memberships.dropna(subset=["next_event_id"])

edge_keys = [object_type, activity, "next_activity"]
edge_support = pd.concat(
    {
        "event_couples": edge_occurrences.drop_duplicates(
            edge_keys + [event_id, "next_event_id"]
        ).groupby(edge_keys).size(),
        "unique_objects": edge_occurrences.groupby(edge_keys)[object_id].nunique(),
        "total_objects": edge_occurrences.groupby(edge_keys).size(),
    },
    axis=1,
)
print(edge_support.loc[[
    ("Order", "Record Payment", "Confirm Delivery"),
    ("Item", "Dispatch", "Confirm Delivery"),
    ("Package", "Dispatch", "Confirm Delivery"),
    ("Delivery", "Dispatch", "Confirm Delivery"),
]])
```

## 7. Sensitivity and validation plan

1. **Reweight shared events without deleting their case memberships.** Within each projection, give an event appearing in `k` cases weight `1/k` per row. On Order, e16 and e24 then each sum to one source event, and Dispatch drops from six case incidences to three source events. For edge counts, deduplicate by event pair or weight by the inverse number of cases supporting that pair. On Package, e16→e24 then contributes once to operational frequency and twice to package exposure. Deleting arbitrary copies before computing adjacency can erase real participation and create new edges by bridging across the deletion.
2. **Compare direct with qualified inherited relations.** From direct Invoice associations, attach e18–e22, e25 and e26 to items using `bills` then `contains`. This adds 15 Item rows, raising the total from 24 to 39. For I21 and I31, e19 and e22 now intervene between e16 and e24, breaking the direct Dispatch→Confirm edge in those traces. Check how much of the DFG change is generated by inheritance. Keep the underlying D2 transit time fixed and interpret the new rows as invoice context without assuming an item-level payment allocation.
3. **Rotate the analytical anchor.** Compare O3's full order trace with D2 and D3 and with V2. Order shows two dispatches and an order closure at e30. Delivery gives two separate transit intervals and Invoice gives one final settlement milestone for V2. For like-for-like checks, explicitly match delivery and invoice IDs and verify that e16→e24 remains 4 h 55 min and e19→e25 remains 20 h 55 min wherever those same endpoints are used.
4. **Compare the OC-DFG support measures.** For edges, count distinct source-event pairs (`event_couples`), distinct objects supporting the activity edge (`unique_objects`), and event-pair/object incidences (`total_objects`). Dispatch→Confirm yields 3/6/6 for Item, 3/4/4 for Package and 3/3/3 for Delivery. On Order, Record Payment→Confirm Delivery yields 4/4/5. O2 and O3 share e22→e24, and O3 also contributes e25→e27. At the activity level, Order Dispatch similarly has three source events, four unique orders and six event-order incidences.
5. **Validate lifecycle endpoints and sample size.** Reconcile all 31 source IDs against the projection counts, verify D2 against its `carries` relations to P2/P3, and confirm V2's settlement endpoint against `completes_settlement`. Keep processing duration unavailable for each payment singleton. The tie between e29 and e30 is across orders and has no within-case ordering consequence. With four orders and three deliveries, report observed differences without treating them as stable process rates.
