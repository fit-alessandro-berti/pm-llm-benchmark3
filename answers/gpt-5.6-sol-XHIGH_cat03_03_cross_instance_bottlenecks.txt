## 1. Interaction-graph description

The appropriate representation is a typed, event-supported interaction graph rather than six independent Job traces. Important node types are **Job, Component, ComponentLot, Machine, Tool, Batch, and Shipment**. Events can be retained as hyperedge nodes because several critical events simultaneously relate more than two objects.

Use these evidence labels:

- **D:** directly recorded O2O relation or event–object relation.
- **P:** pairwise edge projected from a directly observed multi-object event.
- **I:** operational interpretation inferred from timing; not direct proof.

Compact edge/hyperedge representation:

```text
[D] J1..J6 --requires--> C1..C6
[D] L7 --contains--> {C2,C5}; L8 --contains--> {C1,C3}
[D] B1 --batches--> {J1,J2,J3}; B2 --batches--> {J4,J5}
[D] S1 --consolidates--> {J1,J4}
[D] S2 --consolidates--> {J2,J3}
[D] S3 --consolidates--> {J5,J6}

[D] Cure@12:05 --relates--> {B1,OVEN-1,RACK-7,J1,J2,J3,C1,C2,C3}
[D] Quarantine@10:20, Release@13:50
    --relate--> {L7,C2,C5,J2,J5}
[D] Acquire@09:00, Release@11:40 --relate--> {J3,FIX-A}
[D] MachineProcess@11:45 --relates--> {J4,C4,CNC-2,FIX-A}
[D] FinalTest@16:10 --relates--> {J5,C5,TEST-1,CAL-3}
[D] Recalibrate@16:35–17:20 --relates--> {CAL-3}
[D] FinalTest@17:25 --relates--> {J6,C6,TEST-1,CAL-3}

[P] J3 --competes-with--> J4 through FIX-A
[P] J5 --precedes-on--> J6 through TEST-1/CAL-3
[I] FIX-A unavailability plausibly blocks J4
[I] CAL-3 recalibration plausibly blocks J6
[I] late batch members hold early members at synchronization barriers
```

Important higher-order motifs include:

1. **Batch–Job–Component–Machine–Tool synchronization:** B1 joins three Jobs and Components in one event using OVEN-1 and RACK-7. The join, not merely oven capacity, determines feasibility.
2. **Job–Tool–Machine contention:** J3 holds FIX-A while J4 is at CNC-2. CNC-2 can be idle even though J4 cannot start because machine and fixture must be available simultaneously.
3. **ComponentLot–Component–Job–Batch policy bridge:** L7 couples C2/J2 in B1 with C5/J5 in B2. A single disposition can affect different batches, while the J2 override makes the effect asymmetric.
4. **Job–Machine–Tool–Shipment propagation:** J5 and J6 share TEST-1 and CAL-3 and are later consolidated in S3. A tool-state delay can become a shipment delay if the affected job is the last shipment member.
5. **Cross-process shipment join:** S1 joins J1 and J4 despite their different production paths. J1’s completion therefore depends on J4 after J1 itself is packed.

The event–object incidences are observations, but competition, blocking, and propagation are interpretations. In particular, an idle machine interval does not by itself identify the unavailable prerequisite.

## 4. What flattening misses

- **Job viewpoint:** Shows individual activity order, rework, and directly linked elapsed times. It hides joins with other Jobs and currently omits L7 events. Propagating shared events can instead duplicate one Batch Cure across J1, J2, and J3. A misleading conclusion is that normal total durations and a 1.1-hour median StageMachine edge imply little waiting.

- **Machine viewpoint:** Shows utilization, processing sequences, and machine-event gaps. It hides losing demand—J4 is not related to CNC-2 during an unrecorded tool wait—and complementary-resource states. Concluding that a 42-minute idle interval represents lack of work, or that the entire interval is avoidable machine downtime, would both be unsafe.

- **Batch viewpoint:** Shows membership, readiness dispersion, and the common cure event if readiness events are related to B1/B2. It may hide pre-batch lot constraints, post-batch shipment effects, and RACK-7 activity not explicitly linked to a Batch. “Only two batch-machine interactions, therefore low impact” is misleading.

- **Tool viewpoint:** Shows acquire/release, cleaning, quarantine, and calibration lifecycles. It usually hides jobs that requested a tool but never acquired it. Thus FIX-A utilization may look reasonable while J4’s lost opportunity is absent. Multi-object process events can also be repeated across tools.

- **ComponentLot viewpoint:** Shows L7’s quarantine/release and shared component membership. It can hide Job consequences if traversal stops at Components, or duplicate the same release into several Job traces if relations are propagated. It would be wrong either to say “no Jobs were affected” or “all L7 Jobs were blocked”: J2 used an override while J5 did not.

- **Shipment viewpoint:** Shows CreateLoadDispatch, which is fast after loading. It hides the interval from an early Job’s packing to the last partner’s readiness. Consequently S1 appears healthy even though J1 waits 355 minutes.

Low utilization coexists with long waiting because production requires the **intersection** of machine, tool, material disposition, batch membership, labor, and policy availability. CNC-2 or TEST-1 may be idle while FIX-A or CAL-3 is unavailable; OVEN-1 may be idle while B2 is incomplete. Machine utilization measures busy time, not the feasibility of starting a particular Job.

## 5. Object-lifecycle evidence

**Narrative A — B1 synchronization**

- **Temporal precedence:** J1/C1 is ready at 08:10, J2/C2 at 08:25, and J3/C3 at 11:55. B1 starts on OVEN-1 with RACK-7 at 12:05.
- **Resource feasibility:** The observed cure requires the Batch, three Jobs and Components, oven, and rack together.
- **Policy constraint:** B1 membership suggests a common launch barrier, although minimum batch size and recipe rules are not supplied.
- **Causal speculation:** J3’s later readiness may explain most of J1/J2’s wait. A fixed noon schedule or unavailable labor could instead have produced the same timestamps.

**Narrative B — FIX-A, J4, B2, and S1**

- **Temporal precedence:** J3 holds FIX-A from 09:00–11:40. J4/C4 reaches CNC-2 at 09:50 and starts with FIX-A at 11:45. J4 is ready for B2 at 13:10, packs at 19:10, and S1 with J1 dispatches at 19:25.
- **Resource feasibility:** J4’s process needs both CNC-2 and FIX-A; CNC availability alone is insufficient.
- **Policy constraint:** B2 waits for J5, and S1 waits for both J1 and J4.
- **Causal speculation:** FIX-A may have delayed J4, but removing that delay might not advance B2 because J5 is not ready until 14:40. Likewise, no evidence proves the morning fixture delay caused J4’s 19:10 packing time. Synchronization can absorb an upstream improvement—or transmit it if the improved Job remains the last member.

**Narrative C — L7 across two batches**

- **Temporal precedence:** L7/C2/C5/J2/J5 is quarantined at 10:20 and released at 13:50. J2 participates in B1 at 12:05 under a separately recorded override; J5 reaches B2 readiness at 14:40.
- **Resource feasibility:** The components exist, but policy disposition determines whether they may be consumed.
- **Policy constraint:** The override makes lot release non-uniform across Jobs.
- **Causal speculation:** L7 may have delayed J5 and therefore J4/B2, but the data lack J5’s otherwise-ready timestamp. The 13:50 release window is exposure, not a measured 210-minute Job delay.

**Narrative D — CAL-3 and S3**

- **Temporal precedence:** J5/C5 and J6/C6 are test-ready at 16:00/16:05. J5 uses TEST-1 and CAL-3 until 16:35; CAL-3 is recalibrated until 17:20; J6 starts at 17:25. S3 consolidates J5 and J6.
- **Resource feasibility:** TEST-1 is idle but cannot test without a valid CAL-3.
- **Policy constraint:** Calibration and shipment consolidation are separate barriers.
- **Causal speculation:** CAL-3 plausibly adds 50 minutes to J6’s start and could delay S3 if J6 is its final packed member. S3 packing and dispatch timestamps are needed to establish that propagation.

## 6. Intervention portfolio and spillovers

| Intervention | Target mechanism | Expected benefit; helped objects | Potentially harmed objects | New cost/risk and perverse metric | Object-centric evaluation design |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Maximum-wait rule or smaller dynamic batches | Batch synchronization | Reduces early-member waits for J1/J2 and possibly J4 | Later members such as J3; OVEN-1 and RACK-7 may face more setups | Energy, cleaning, quality variation. Minimizing batch wait could encourage inefficient underfilled runs | Compare matched Batch episodes; measure job-minutes waiting, fill, energy, rack occupancy, lateness, and downstream queues |
| Reserve FIX-A by executable readiness, or add a qualified second fixture | Tool contention | Helps J4-like Jobs and reduces CNC idle-with-demand | J3-like Jobs may lose reservations or be preempted | Capital, maintenance, duplicate-fixture quality risk. Maximizing CNC utilization could cause fixture hoarding | Reconstruct request–grant–release intervals and compare competing Job clusters before/after |
| Add spare CAL-3 or schedule calibration before demand peaks | Tool-state constraint | Helps J6 and future TEST-1 users | Calibration staff and low-priority tests may be displaced | Calibration cost and measurement risk. Throughput targets may encourage skipped calibration | Link every Final Test to CAL-3 validity state; compare wait, retest, escape, and calibration-compliance rates |
| Accelerate rack turnaround or add RACK-7 capacity | Batch/tool feasibility | Improves robustness for B2-like batches | More cleaning workload; extra racks may occupy space | Capital and sanitation risk. Rack-availability targets could promote premature release | Evaluate only episodes where rack availability exceeds latest-member readiness; B2 itself would not show a direct gain |
| Component-level disposition with integrated override workflow | Lot coupling | Makes J2/J5 eligibility explicit; may selectively release unaffected components | Quality organization and Jobs relying on conservative lot holds | Safety/compliance risk. “Release lead time” can incentivize unsafe overrides | Compare lot-member outcomes within L7-like lots, including defects, override reasons, batch delay, and audit results |
| Maximum shipment hold or economic split-dispatch rule | Shipment consolidation | Could remove up to 340 minutes of partner wait for J1 | Freight planning, carrier capacity, and consolidation economics | Higher cost/carbon. Packed-to-dispatch targets may produce many half-empty shipments | Randomize or phase policy by Shipment; measure per-Job wait, on-time delivery, shipment count, cost, and emissions |
| Log `Ready`, `Request Resource`, `Wait Start/End`, state changes, and relationship provenance | Data integration | Improves attribution for all objects without claiming operational improvement | Operators face recording burden | Gaming, timestamp noise, and false precision. Completeness targets may create dummy events | Audit event–object links against physical samples; track source lineage, clock consistency, orphan events, and qualified wait coverage |

Spillovers should be measured rather than assumed. Smaller batches create more rack cleaning; a spare fixture may merely move J4 to the B2 barrier; faster lot release may increase oven demand; faster testing may shift waiting to S3. Conversely, advancing J4 enough could benefit J1 through S1, demonstrating that an intervention can help Jobs that never use the targeted resource.

## 7. PM4Py analysis outline

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

# Assumption: `ocel` is an already loaded PM4Py OCEL.

# 1) Global interaction and type-level structure
interaction_summary = pm4py.ocel_objects_interactions_summary(ocel)
object_interaction_graph = pm4py.discover_objects_graph(
    ocel, graph_type="object_interaction"
)
otg = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)       # object-type graph
etot = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)     # event-type/object-type relations
ocdfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)

print("Interaction summary:")
print(interaction_summary)
print("OTG:", otg)
print("ETOT:", etot)

# 2) Compare two OCDFG edge-frequency semantics.
# Typical PM4Py OCDFGs store event_couples and unique_objects for each
# object type and directly-follows edge. They answer different questions:
# repeated transitions versus distinct objects experiencing a transition.
def cardinality(value):
    if isinstance(value, (set, list, tuple, dict)):
        return len(value)
    return value

metric_rows = []
for object_type, edge_map in ocdfg.get("edges", {}).items():
    for edge, payload in edge_map.items():
        metric_rows.append({
            "object_type": object_type,
            "source_activity": edge[0],
            "target_activity": edge[1],
            "event_couples": cardinality(payload.get("event_couples", 0)),
            "unique_objects": cardinality(payload.get("unique_objects", 0)),
            "total_objects": cardinality(payload.get("total_objects", 0)),
        })

oc_frequency_comparison = pd.DataFrame(metric_rows)
if not oc_frequency_comparison.empty:
    oc_frequency_comparison["couples_per_unique_object"] = (
        oc_frequency_comparison["event_couples"] /
        oc_frequency_comparison["unique_objects"].replace(0, pd.NA)
    )
    print(oc_frequency_comparison.sort_values(
        ["object_type", "event_couples"], ascending=[True, False]
    ))

# 3) Flatten multiple viewpoints, but keep their conclusions separate.
job_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Job")
machine_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Machine")
shipment_log = pm4py.ocel_flattening(ocel, "Shipment")

job_performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(job_log)
machine_performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(machine_log)
shipment_performance_dfg = pm4py.discover_performance_dfg(shipment_log)

# 4) Object-centric features can be used for clustering or before/after models.
job_features = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, "Job")
tool_features = pm4py.extract_ocel_features(ocel, "Tool")
print(job_features.head())
print(tool_features.head())

# 5) Inspect explicit readiness, request, state, and release evidence.
# Do NOT convert every interval between Machine events into Job waiting.
relations = ocel.relations.copy()
if "ocel:activity" not in relations.columns:
    relations = relations.merge(
        ocel.events[["ocel:eid", "ocel:activity", "ocel:timestamp"]],
        on="ocel:eid",
        how="left"
    )

evidence_pattern = (
    r"Ready|Request|Acquire|Release|Quarantine|Cleaning|"
    r"Calibrat|Load Batch|Batch Cure|Final Test|Pack|Dispatch"
)
explicit_evidence = relations[
    relations["ocel:activity"].astype(str).str.contains(
        evidence_pattern, case=False, regex=True, na=False
    )
].sort_values(["ocel:timestamp", "ocel:eid", "ocel:oid"])

print(explicit_evidence[
    [c for c in [
        "ocel:eid", "ocel:activity", "ocel:timestamp",
        "ocel:oid", "ocel:type", "ocel:qualifier"
    ] if c in explicit_evidence.columns]
])

# Candidate waiting intervals should be constructed only after verifying:
# (a) an explicit ready/request event,
# (b) all required object states,
# (c) the eventual acquire/start event, and
# (d) policy eligibility. Machine gaps are diagnostic context, not wait proof.
```

## 8. Falsification and additional data

Observations that would weaken batch synchronization as the top-ranked explanation include:

1. Audits show that J1’s 08:10 and J2’s 08:25 timestamps mean staging started, not all prerequisites ready.
2. OVEN-1, RACK-7, labor, or the cure recipe was unavailable until approximately 12:05 regardless of J3.
3. B1 had a fixed scheduled cure window at 12:05 that would not change if J3 were ready earlier.
4. Quality rules require exactly the B1 composition, making individual or smaller-batch counterfactuals infeasible.
5. A larger sample shows little association between within-batch readiness spread and early-member waiting or due-date performance.
6. Trials with smaller batches do not reduce completion time because added setups, cleaning, or downstream queues offset the gain.
7. Most severe waits occur in non-batched Jobs or after Pack, making shipment/tool mechanisms quantitatively dominant.
8. Early batch members perform productive, recorded work during the purported waiting intervals.

Additional discriminating data should include:

1. Explicit `Ready/Enabled`, `Resource Requested`, `Resource Granted`, `Wait Start`, and `Wait End` events with reason codes.
2. Planned versus actual batch start, minimum/maximum batch size, compatibility rules, recipes, and priority logic.
3. Tool location, reservation, condition, cleaning, calibration-validity, and capacity intervals.
4. Machine schedules, operator/crew availability, setup matrices, and queue snapshots.
5. Per-component lot disposition, override authorization, expiry, test result, and release scope.
6. Relationship provenance showing whether Job links are source-recorded, propagated through Components, or inferred.
7. Pack times for every Job, shipment cutoff times, carrier appointments, split-shipment eligibility, cost, and service promises.
8. Synchronized source-system clocks, event creation versus execution timestamps, missing-event indicators, and correction history.

These fields would permit competing explanations to be tested while preserving the multi-object structure instead of forcing every delay into a single Job or Machine trace.
