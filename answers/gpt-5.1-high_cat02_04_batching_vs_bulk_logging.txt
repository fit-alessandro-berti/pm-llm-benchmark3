# 3. Consequences for performance analysis

### G1 – RBT-7 PLC batch

- **Waiting time**: Queue-arrival times differ, but starts coincide at 09:00, so waiting estimates per item (queue_arrival → start) are reasonable. However, the decision to wait until four items are available means later arrivals (e.g. L04) see shorter waits than early ones (L01–L03); the log correctly reflects this batching policy.
- **Service time**: If we treat each case’s service time as start → complete (3 minutes), we effectively multiply the cell’s busy time by the batch size (4). Physically, RBT-7 executed one 3‑minute cycle, not 12 minutes of work, so per-item service times are fine but resource-level service sums are inflated.
- **Resource utilization**: Summing case-level service times for RBT-7 will overestimate utilization by roughly the average batch size; utilization models that assume one case per server at a time will misclassify this cell as overloaded.
- **Bottleneck detection**: At case level, throughput and waiting are realistic, but server-level analysis may wrongly conclude that RBT-7 is a bottleneck because it appears to be simultaneously handling four long tasks; treating the cell as a batch server with capacity four corrects this.

### G2 – Surface inspection on tablet (start batching)

- **Waiting time**: Using queue_arrival → start (10:00) as waiting underestimates true waits for later items, because they only begin receiving attention when the inspector switches focus to them. Items S02–S04 appear to have near-zero wait between “start” and actual work onset, which is false.
- **Service time**: Durations from 10:00 to individual completion mix true service and queueing within the batch. Later items inherit extra “service” time while idle, biasing service-time distributions upward and masking queuing before their true work began.
- **Resource utilization**: Utilization appears extremely high between 10:00 and 10:27 with four overlapping cases, even though the inspector likely processes them sequentially. This leads to overestimation of multitasking and underestimation of per-item processing efficiency.
- **Bottleneck detection**: The inspection step may be mis-identified as a highly parallel but slow operation, rather than a single-server activity with a group start and then sequential work. Upstream queues may be blamed less than they should be, especially for later items in the batch.

### G3 – Release review with folder-level completion

- **Waiting time**: Queue_arrival → start captures when each review begins and is probably reliable, so waiting estimates are acceptable. However, if analysts time their starts relative to folder availability rather than case readiness, some pre-start work may be unobserved.
- **Service time**: Start → complete is badly biased. E01 appears to run for 28 minutes and E04 for only 6, but T‑C reports that reviews happen throughout the half-hour and the form is often submitted only at the end. That means the earlier reviews include idle or already-finished time up to 11:30, while later ones have artificially short durations.
- **Resource utilization**: From 11:02 to 11:30 the log shows four overlapping cases, overstating concurrent work. True utilization may still be high but with fewer concurrent reviews than the log suggests; load is shifted artificially towards the end of the window.
- **Bottleneck detection**: This step may look like a late bottleneck that “holds” all cases until the end of the half-hour. In reality, the bottleneck might be elsewhere (e.g. upstream preparation), and this pattern is mainly an end-batching artifact. Queue analysis that trusts the common completion time will misplace delays.

### G4 – Sequential packing on scanner

- **Waiting time**: Queue_arrival → start (11:58–12:05, etc.) reflects a reasonable build-up followed by steady work. There is a small period where arrivals outpace service (11:58–12:02) creating a short queue, correctly observable in the log.
- **Service time**: Start → complete forms a chain of non-overlapping intervals of similar length. Service-time statistics are trustworthy, aside from minor rounding and scanning delays of a few seconds visible in `recorded_at`.
- **Resource utilization**: Utilization estimates based on service intervals are accurate; the scanner appears to be a single-server station processing one item at a time with little idle time between items.
- **Bottleneck detection**: Because the data show clean sequential work, this cluster is safe to use as a baseline for non-batched behavior. If other steps appear slower or more variable, that is more likely to be operational rather than logging-related.

### G5 – Root-cause analysis with shared VDI account

- **Waiting time**: Queue_arrival → start looks reasonable per case, and is less affected by the shared account. However, analysts may pre-read or stage work before hitting “start”, so some pre-start activity could be hidden.
- **Service time**: Overlapping intervals with out-of-order completions (e.g. A02 completing before A01) make it ambiguous whether a single analyst is multitasking or multiple analysts are each handling a subset. If we assume one analyst, service times and overlap are greatly exaggerated; if we assume three, individual service times are plausible but unassignable to specific people.
- **Resource utilization**: Treating `analyst_pool` as a single resource will show unrealistically high utilization and concurrency, possibly near saturation. Correctly, this is at least a 3-capacity resource pool; utilization per analyst is lower than the log suggests.
- **Bottleneck detection**: Queue and throughput analyses may incorrectly flag the analyst pool as a severe bottleneck due to an apparent overload of a single resource. Without de-anonymizing or modeling the pool capacity explicitly, we risk designing interventions (e.g. more analysts) based on already adequate staffing.

### G6 – Ticket closure via manual bulk form

- **Waiting time**: If we interpret the 17:00 start as the onset of closure work, then waits from queue_arrival to 17:00 are very long (1–3+ hours), suggesting a large queue. However, actual resolution work might have occurred earlier during the day and only been logged at the end, so this overstates waiting for the “Close ticket” step.
- **Service time**: Because start and complete are identical (17:00), service times are recorded as zero. This makes the closure step appear instantaneous even though operators may spend substantial time resolving tickets earlier (unlogged) or performing the closure action.
- **Why G6 can show inflated waiting and near-zero service**: If analysts resolve tickets throughout the afternoon but only bulk-update the status at 17:00, the log shows all cases arriving between 14:05 and 16:25 and then “waiting” until 17:00, at which point they are all closed in zero time. Queue analysis then attributes long queues to the “Close ticket” step (inflated waiting), while simultaneously suggesting the step is infinitely fast (near-zero service). This paradox arises because execution and logging are decoupled.
- **Resource utilization and bottlenecks**: Utilization calculated from start → complete is near zero, implying the closure step cannot be a bottleneck. Yet if analysts actually spend time resolving tickets earlier, the real bottleneck could be resolution efforts masked by the logging practice. Any serious performance analysis should either re-time the closure based on when underlying work occurred or treat G6 data as interval-censored and unsuitable for precise queue modeling.

### G7 – Manual measure in legacy MES with quarter-hour rounding

- **Waiting time**: With all starts at 14:00 and arrivals from 13:58–14:11, waits appear to range from −2 minutes (due to rounding) to 13 minutes, but these are dominated by the undocumented rounding rule. The real waits may be only a few minutes and in a different order entirely.
- **Service time**: All services last exactly 15 minutes in the log, but that is a rounding artifact. Actual measurement times could be quite short or vary significantly. Service-time distributions derived from G7 would therefore be dominated by the rounding grid rather than process behavior.
- **Resource utilization**: Because multiple cases appear to run from 14:00 to 14:15 on a single resource, utilization and concurrency are overstated; in reality, measurements might be sequential or lightly overlapping.
- **Bottleneck detection**: G7-like data are unreliable for fine-grained bottleneck detection. At best, we can say that the measurement work occurred sometime in the 14:00–14:15 window; any attempt to compare this station’s cycle time to millisecond-accurate stations will be misleading unless we aggregate all steps to comparable resolution.

### G8 – Repair triage with safety-related downtime

- **Waiting time**: Items arriving between 15:00 and 15:08 wait until 15:38 or later, because the resource is busy with a safety-check event until around 15:35. The log correctly shows a long pre-start gap, but without downtime context we might blame repair triage rather than the safety check or upstream constraints.
- **Service time**: Once work starts, start → complete intervals are sequential and of similar length, so service-time estimates are reliable for the catch-up phase.
- **Resource utilization**: Utilization appears low between 15:00 and 15:38 (no triage events) even though the resource is busy with safety checks. Then it spikes to near-continuous work from 15:38 to 16:13. If we only look at triage events, we misinterpret the early idle period as lack of demand rather than busy time on another activity.
- **Bottleneck detection**: Queue build-up during downtime may cause triage to look like a bottleneck at the moment capacity returns, but the root cause is capacity loss (safety check). A robust analysis should separate base triage performance (sequential work once active) from the externally imposed downtime window.

def run_batch_discovery(df_pm: pd.DataFrame):

def compute_pandas_diagnostics(df: pd.DataFrame) -> pd.DataFrame:
    # 5. Independent diagnostics in pandas
    df = df.copy()
    df["recording_lag_sec"] = (df["recorded_at"] - df["complete"]).dt.total_seconds()
    df["wait_sec"] = (df["start"] - df["queue_arrival"]).dt.total_seconds()
    df["service_sec"] = (df["complete"] - df["start"]).dt.total_seconds()

    def equality_ratio(group: pd.DataFrame, col: str) -> float:
        counts = group[col].value_counts()
        equal_cases = counts[counts > 1].sum()
        return float(equal_cases) / len(group) if len(group) > 0 else 0.0

    def overlap_rate(group: pd.DataFrame) -> float:
        # Simple sequential-overlap rate within a group (e.g. resource × cluster)
        group = group.sort_values("start")
        if len(group) <= 1:
            return 0.0
        overlaps = 0
        prev_end = None
        for _, row in group.iterrows():
            if prev_end is not None and row["start"] < prev_end:
                overlaps += 1
            prev_end = max(prev_end, row["complete"]) if prev_end is not None else row["complete"]
        return overlaps / (len(group) - 1)

    summaries = []
    for cluster, g in df.groupby("cluster"):
        equal_starts = equality_ratio(g, "start")
        equal_ends = equality_ratio(g, "complete")
        # Overlap per resource, averaged
        overlap_values = []
        for _, gr in g.groupby("resource"):
            overlap_values.append(overlap_rate(gr))
        overlap_avg = sum(overlap_values) / len(overlap_values) if overlap_values else 0.0

        # Timestamp granularity indicator: unique precisions and second component variance
        precisions = sorted(g["precision"].unique().tolist())
        seconds_spread = g["start"].dt.second.nunique() + g["complete"].dt.second.nunique()

        # Queue build-up: simple indicator comparing mean wait and mean inter-arrival time
        inter_arrival = g.sort_values("queue_arrival")["queue_arrival"].diff().dt.total_seconds()
        mean_inter_arrival = inter_arrival[1:].mean() if len(inter_arrival) > 1 else None
        mean_wait = g["wait_sec"].mean()
        queue_build_up_flag = (
            mean_inter_arrival is not None and mean_wait is not None and mean_wait > (mean_inter_arrival or 0) * 1.5
        )

        summaries.append({
            "cluster": cluster,
            "n_cases": len(g),
            "equal_start_ratio": round(equal_starts, 2),
            "equal_end_ratio": round(equal_ends, 2),
            "overlap_rate": round(overlap_avg, 2),
            "mean_recording_lag_sec": round(g["recording_lag_sec"].mean(), 1),
            "timestamp_precisions": ",".join(precisions),
            "seconds_spread_indicator": seconds_spread,
            "mean_wait_sec": round(mean_wait, 1) if mean_wait is not None else None,
            "mean_inter_arrival_sec": round(mean_inter_arrival, 1) if mean_inter_arrival is not None else None,
            "queue_build_up_flag": bool(queue_build_up_flag),
        })

    summary_df = pd.DataFrame(summaries).sort_values("cluster")
    return summary_df


def compare_with_pm4py(summary_df: pd.DataFrame, df_pm: pd.DataFrame, batch_results: dict):
    print("Cluster-level diagnostics (pandas):")
    print(summary_df.to_string(index=False))

    # 7. Compare pandas diagnostics with PM4Py batch categories
    for key, batches_df in batch_results.items():
        print(f"\nPM4Py batch detection summary for setting {key}:")
        # Assume discover_batches returns one row per event with a batch label; adapt by joining on case/resource if needed
        merged = df_pm.merge(
            batches_df,

            how="left",
            suffixes=("", "_batch"),
        )
        batch_col_candidates = [c for c in merged.columns if "batch" in c.lower() or "BATCH" in c]
        if not batch_col_candidates:
            print("  (No explicit batch label column found; showing counts by cluster instead.)")

        batch_col = batch_col_candidates[0]

            .reset_index(name="n_cases")
            .sort_values(["cluster", "n_cases"], ascending=[True, False])
        )
        print(cluster_batch_counts.to_string(index=False))

# 5. Robustness and alternative explanations

### 5.1 Resource-identity assumptions

- **Assuming resource IDs are fully trusted (per-person identifiers)**  
  - G1, G2, G3, G4, G6, G7, and G8 are largely unaffected: the cell IDs or terminals plausibly map to single stations or machines, so concurrency patterns mainly reflect batching or logging choices, not identity confusion.  
  - Under this assumption, G5 would suggest an implausibly high degree of multitasking and concurrency on a single analyst, implying severe overload and making the analyst a clear bottleneck.

- **Treating some resource IDs as shared accounts or pools**  
  - For G5, recognizing `analyst_pool` as a shared login transforms the story: overlap and out-of-order completions become consistent with multiple analysts handling different tickets in parallel. Concurrency per analyst is then bounded by pool size rather than the apparent concurrency of all four cases.  
  - If we also allowed for shared accounts in other clusters (e.g. tablet or desktop shared across shifts), some overlap attributed to multitasking could instead represent different workers using the same device at different times. However, the small size and sequencing in G1–G4 and G8 make heavy shared-account artifacts there less likely.
  - Robust queue analysis should therefore: (a) treat uniquely identified machines (RBT-7, T-D, T-H) as trusted resources, and (b) model `analyst_pool` explicitly as a multi-server resource with unknown but bounded capacity.

### 5.2 Timestamp precision and interval-censoring

- **Assuming timestamps are exact (within reported precision)**  
  - G1’s identical start and end times strongly support a genuine simultaneous batch on a capacity-4 fixture.  
  - G2’s equal starts plus staggered ends cleanly match start batching; we can safely separate common setup from sequential completions.  
  - G3’s shared completion at 11:30 is then interpreted as deliberate end batching or bulk form submission, since 1‑minute precision should be capable of distinguishing distinct completion moments.  
  - G6’s 17:00/17:00 start/complete for all cases points to bulk closure with zero recorded service, driving the “inflated waiting plus near-zero service” paradox.  
  - G7, however, is inconsistent under a “precise” interpretation: equal 14:00 and 14:15 times cannot be taken literally because we know the MES rounds to quarter-hours.

- **Treating timestamps as interval-censored by precision**  
  - For systems with 1‑sec precision (PLC, scanner), censoring intervals are narrow; G1, G4, and G8 can still be interpreted with high confidence as a real batch (G1) or sequential service (G4, G8).  
  - For 1‑min precision (tablet, desktop_form, manual_bulk_form, shared_VDI), each time should be treated as lying somewhere within a ±30‑second (or similar) window. This softens claims about exact ordering but does not eliminate patterns like “all ends at 11:30” (G3) or “all events at 17:00” (G6); these remain strong indicators of logging batches.  
  - For the 15‑min MES (G7), start at 14:00 and end at 14:15 only tell us that each measurement occurred sometime within those bounds. Any detailed queue or concurrency metric for G7 should therefore be based on interval reasoning (e.g. all work occurred within [14:00, 14:15]) or excluded from fine-grained analysis.

### 5.3 Multiple explanations for the same temporal pattern

1. **G1 – identical start and end times at machine level**  
   - Could indicate a genuine simultaneous batch on a 4-capacity fixture (our top interpretation).  
   - Could also reflect a PLC logging cycle boundaries that are applied uniformly to each in-fixture item, even if micro-sequencing occurred.  
   - Under coarser or misconfigured precision, it could even mimic a bulk logging event if starts/completes were backfilled from a single timestamp.

2. **G3 – shared completion time at 11:30**  
   - May indicate an end-of-folder submission pattern, where individual reviews are done earlier but all “completed” in the UI at once.  
   - Could also reflect a real coordination point where analysts deliberately finish reviews close to the half-hour boundary.  
   - In some organizations, assistants or batch scripts might update statuses at fixed times, producing the same equal-completion signature for automated bulk updates.

3. **G6 – all starts and completes at 17:00**  
   - Fits bulk closure via manual form at shift end, after work occurred earlier.  
   - Could also describe a genuine “close-out” activity where tickets are intentionally held open and resolved in a concentrated effort around 17:00.  
   - In extreme timestamp coarsening or misalignment scenarios, it might combine both early work and true 17:00 actions into one indistinguishable time point.

4. **G7 – quarter-hour-rounded starts and ends**  
   - Most consistent with timestamp coarsening in the legacy MES, which rounds to the nearest quarter-hour.  
   - But the same pattern could also arise from a real measurement round starting just after 14:00, logged once per round.  
   - Without independent evidence (e.g. raw sensor times), we cannot fully separate coarse logging from true operational grouping.

These overlaps in temporal signatures underscore why batch detection must be combined with source knowledge (fixtures, UIs, rounding rules, shared accounts) rather than treated as proof of specific physical execution semantics.


# 6. Data-collection redesign

Prioritized recommendations to make batching and bulk logging analyzable and distinguishable:

1. **Execution timestamps**  
   - Capture distinct lifecycle states (e.g. ready, setup/start, in-progress, done) per case, with explicit timestamps for each state.  
   - For true batches, record both batch-level execution times (start/end of the batch) and per-item entry/exit within the batch when feasible.

2. **Recording timestamps**  
   - Always store `recorded_at` (database insertion/update time) separately from execution timestamps.  
   - Retain both initial creation and last-update times so that late bulk submissions (as in G3 and G6) can be distinguished from real execution events.

3. **Source clocks and synchronization**  
   - Synchronize clocks across PLCs, tablets, desktops, MES, and safety systems using NTP or equivalent.  
   - Record timezone and clock source; for systems like the safety-check in G8, store clock-offset metadata so cross-system alignment is possible.

4. **Resource and badge identity**  
   - Replace shared logins such as `analyst_pool` with personal IDs, or at least log both a pool ID and a separate badge/swipe identifier.  
   - For pooled resources, record pool membership and effective capacity (e.g. number of analysts logged in) to correctly interpret concurrency.

5. **Explicit group/batch identifiers**  
   - Introduce batch IDs for operations like RBT-7 labeling, T-B inspection groups, and bulk closures. Each case should reference both its own case ID and the batch/group ID.  
   - For UIs with “begin group” or “bulk close” buttons, create explicit group-start and group-completion events linked by a shared identifier.

6. **Workstation capacity metadata**  
   - Maintain metadata about station capacity (e.g. RBT-7 can process 4 items simultaneously; triage is single-piece flow).  
   - Use this metadata during analysis to normalize utilization and avoid inflating resource load by counting each batched item as a full unit of time.

7. **Pause/resume and downtime events**  
   - Log pause/resume events for safety checks, maintenance, or other capacity losses with the same timeline as production events.  
   - Associate downtime reasons and responsible resources explicitly (e.g. “T-H unavailable due to safety check 15:00–15:35”) so queue accumulation can be attributed correctly.

8. **Timestamp precision metadata and raw times**  
   - Record both the stated precision (1 sec, 1 min, 15 min) and, where available, the underlying raw time before rounding.  
   - For legacy systems like the MES, document the rounding rule and store bounds (e.g. earliest and latest possible time within the quarter-hour) so interval-censored analysis is possible.

Implementing these changes would allow process mining tools to separate operational batching from logging batches, model batch servers explicitly, and perform queueing analysis that respects both true concurrency and measurement uncertainty.


# 7. Final adjudication

- **Clusters interpretable with high confidence for queue and performance analysis (with minor caveats)**  
  - **G1**: High-confidence physical batch on a capacity‑4 robot cell; suitable for queue analysis if modeled as a batch server and utilization is computed at the batch (not per-item) level.  
  - **G2**: Clear start batching with sequential completions; usable if we treat the common start as a setup event and focus on completion times for service, not on raw overlap counts.  
  - **G4**: Reliable single-piece sequential work; can be used as a clean reference for non-batched behavior.  
  - **G8**: Reliable sequential catch-up after a downtime window; suitable for analysis if downtime is modeled explicitly rather than treated as ordinary waiting.

- **Clusters that should be excluded or interval-censored in detailed queue analysis**  
  - **G3**: Use starts and queue-arrivals only; treat completions as at-best interval-censored around 11:30 and avoid point estimates of service times.  
  - **G6**: Do not use 17:00 start/complete as true service times; closure times should be treated as approximate markers, and this activity should be excluded from fine-grained queue modeling.  
  - **G7**: With 15‑minute rounding, both start and completion times are interval-censored; include only in coarse-grained analyses at quarter-hour or coarser resolution.

- **Clusters requiring operational validation before drawing strong performance conclusions**  
  - **G5**: Needs clarification of how many analysts share `analyst_pool` and how they use the system; without this, any inference about utilization, concurrency, or bottlenecks for root-cause analysis is speculative.  
  - **G3 and G6** may also merit targeted validation (e.g. workflow walkthroughs) if they play a central role in decision-making, to confirm whether the observed end batching reflects policy or purely logging convenience.
