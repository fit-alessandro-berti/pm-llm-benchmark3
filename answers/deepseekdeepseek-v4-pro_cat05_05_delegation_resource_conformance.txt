## 2. Rule Interpretation

**RR1 – Drafter/approver separation**  
The human who executes `Draft_Change_Request` must differ from the human who executes `Approve_Change` for the same case. Based on eventtime identities after alias normalisation. A change approved by the same human violates the rule regardless of risk or delegation status.

**RR2 – Implementer/verifier separation**  
The human executing `Implement_Change` must differ from the human executing `Verify_Implementation`. No role requirements beyond different human identity.

**RR3 – Highrisk approval authority**  
For HIGHrisk cases, `Approve_Change` must be carried out by:
- The principal QM (Asha), or
- A valid QM delegate under a delegation that satisfies all of:
  - effective interval covering the approval timestamp,
  - scope matching the change,
  - principal actually absent or formally unavailable at that time,
  - no prohibited subdelegation.

The principal may act even when on leave; leave does not strip authority. A delegate uses the delegation only when the principal is absent.

**RR4 – Emergency substitution**  
HIGHrisk emergency changes may be implemented after approval by the Incident Commander (Diego) or a valid IC delegate.  
A `Retrospective_QM_Review` must then be performed by the QM (Asha) or a valid QM delegate **within two business days** of the `Implement_Change` timestamp.

*Businessday counting* – business days are Monday–Friday; site calendar has no holidays. The deadline is 23:59 of the **second business day after the `Implement_Change` event**. Example: implemented on Tuesday  deadline Thursday 23:59; implemented on Friday  deadline Tuesday 23:59. A review after the deadline is noncompliant.

The QM delegate performing the retrospective review must be authorised at that time for QM functions on the change’s scope.

**RR5 – Identity evidence**  
A service account (`svc_qms`) is not a human. Conformance is possible only if reliable session evidence maps the action to an authorised human. Aliases belonging to the same human are normalised before SoD checks.

**RR6 – Suspicion versus proof**  
Repeated resource pairs, reciprocal approvals, shared workstations, and concentrated handovers are indicators. They are not proof of collusion without supporting evidence.

**Other principles**

- An action on leave by the principal is accepted at face value unless contraevidence exists, but the possibility of credential sharing must be acknowledged.
- A delegation recorded later in a database (e.g., during migration) is still valid if the signed, timestamped document (PDF) preexists the interval. We accept DQM01 despite the later DB entry.
- A delegation condition “principal must be absent” is evaluated at the timestamp of the delegated act.

## 4. Collusion and AlternativeExplanation Analysis

**Indicators considered** (from RR6 and pattern evidence):

### 4.1 Repeated drafter–approver pair (Clara – Ben)
- **Observation:** In the 60 days before the log, Clara drafted and Ben approved 18 of 23 HIGHrisk production changes. They belong to the same production cell; no formal rotation policy.
- **Benign explanation:** The production cell has a natural workflow where Clara prepares changes and Ben, as local QM delegate, provides timely approval. No rule prohibits such a steady pairing; it may be efficient.
- **Adverse explanation:** The pair may be colluding to bypass a more rigorous independent review, possibly because Ben’s approvals are less scrutinised or because they are coordinating to hide deviations.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Review the content of the approved changes for unusual patterns (e.g., all approved with minimal comments). Check whether changes approved by Asha (3 cases) show longer cycle times or rejections – if Ben’s approvals are consistently faster, that would raise suspicion. Network analysis of email/chat during approvals could reveal collusion signals.

### 4.2 Reciprocal approvals (Ben drafts training changes, Clara approves)
- **Observation:** Ben drafted four LOWrisk training changes that Clara approved; no direct financial benefit known.
- **Benign explanation:** Lowrisk training documents can be approved by any competent person; Clara’s approval is well within her capabilities and no SoD rule is violated (LOW risk, no QM required). A simple “you approve mine, I approve yours” arrangement may reflect administrative convenience, not malice.
- **Adverse explanation:** The reciprocity could be a quidproquo that foreshadows similar exchanges on higherrisk changes, or a deliberate attempt to normalise mutual approvals.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Examine whether these reciprocal approvals involved any substantive changes or if they were automatic. If Ben started drafting more highrisk changes and Clara approving them (which would require a valid delegation), that would be alarming.

### 4.3 Shared workstation (svc_qms on WSDM04)
- **Observation:** CHG503 verification was executed by `svc_qms` from Diego’s normal workstation while Diego was present. No individual reauthentication.
- **Benign explanation:** Diego could have stepped away and a colleague used the terminal without logging in separately; the verification might still have been performed correctly by that colleague.
- **Adverse explanation:** Diego himself used the service account to selfverify, circumventing RR2, or another person colluded with him to pretend independence.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Badgein/out logs for the room around the event, userbehaviour analytics (keystroke patterns), and testimony from other personnel present.

### 4.4 Account usage during Ben’s sick leave (CHG506 approval)
- **Observation:** `u_bk` approved CHG506 at 13:00 on 13Aug, one hour after Ben’s certified sick leave began, from an office workstation, not his home IP. Ben denies performing it.
- **Benign explanation:** Ben may have come to the office briefly despite illness, or the sick leave start time may have been recorded with a slight delay; he could still be physically present and legitimate.
- **Adverse explanation:** Someone else (possibly Clara, given their pairing) used Ben’s credentials to push through the approval, indicating credential sharing or unauthorised access.
- **Discriminating evidence:** CCTV from the office area, authentication logs (if MFA was used), badgein records of who entered the building near that time, and digital forensic examination of the workstation session.

### 4.5 Attempted subdelegation chain (CHG510)
- **Observation:** Clara relied on DIC04, a prohibited subdelegation originating from Farid (who himself had IC authority only for CI44).
- **Benign explanation:** Clara may have misunderstood the delegation rules and acted in good faith, assuming the email from Farid was sufficient. There was an urgent cooling emergency; a quick decision was needed.
- **Adverse explanation:** The subdelegation could be a deliberate endrun around authority rules, possibly with Farid’s informal permission, indicating a culture of bypassing controls.
- **Discriminating evidence:** Interview Farid and Clara; check whether the emergency change was later justified and whether the subdelegation was discussed before or after the event. Examine if similar emergency workarounds have occurred in other cases.

**Overall:** The collection of indicators – concentrated pairing, reciprocity, a sharedworkstation verification, an impossible approval during sickness, and an invalid delegation – creates a **heightened suspicion environment**, but individually each could have a benign explanation. No single piece of evidence proves collusion. The most critical gap is definitive identity proof for the CHG506 approval and the CHG503 verification.

## 6. Control Improvements

1. **Delegation creation and recording** – Require all delegations to be created, approved, and stored in a controlled system **before** the effective interval begins. A postdated database entry during migration is acceptable only if the signed, timestamped source document is verified and retained.  
2. **Effectivetime and scope fidelity** – Delegation records must explicitly list the activities they cover (e.g., “Approve_Change”, “Retrospective_QM_Review”) and, for incidentspecific delegations, the exact case identifiers.  
3. **Principal availability monitoring** – Integrate HR absence records directly into the delegation evaluation engine. A delegation that requires principal absence should become inactive automatically as soon as the principal is shown to be present (e.g., VPN session, badgein).  
4. **Alias management** – Maintain a single, authoritative identity master and enforce that every account used in the process is mapped to that master. Run periodic reconciliation to detect unreported aliases.  
5. **Individual authentication for privileged actions** – Mandate stepup authentication (e.g., FIDO token, biometric) when a service account is used for verification or approval, and log the authenticated human. Prohibit use of shared terminals for such actions unless this is enforced.  
6. **Emergency review procedures** – Tie emergency change cases explicitly to an incident record. Automatically calculate the retroreview deadline and escalate if missed. Ensure that QM delegations explicitly state whether they cover retrospective reviews.  
7. **Pair rotation and independence monitoring** – Implement a policy to rotate drafter–approver and implementer–verifier pairs periodically. Use the handover network to detect concentration and trigger mandatory rotation or additional independent review.  
8. **Network and collaboration monitoring** – Continuously monitor workingtogether metrics and reciprocal approval patterns as risk indicators; when thresholds are exceeded, trigger a mandatory management review, but do not treat them as violations without further evidence.  
9. **Evidential retention** – Keep immutable logs of all session attributes (IP, workstation, badge/FIDO, reauthentication), and retain them for at least the record retention period of the change to allow retrospective identity resolution.
