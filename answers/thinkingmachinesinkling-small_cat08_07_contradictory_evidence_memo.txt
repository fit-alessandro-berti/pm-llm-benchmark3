3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness, low precision.** Fitness measures how well the discovered model explains observed traces; because Redesign R creates a dominant simplified path (coordinator owns case after validation, merged scheduling, direct to installation), most pilot cases align closely to this path, raising fitness. Precision measures whether the model allows behavior not observed; by removing survey, permit-check, and pre-billing activation controls, the redesign permits many unobserved exception combinations (skipped survey + manual activation + second visit + billing before activation). The result is a model that describes what happened well but fails to constrain what could happen—exactly the profile of a process that is efficient for standard cases but uncontrolled for exceptions.

**Improved median, worse tail.** Cycle-time distributions are heterogeneous. The median is pulled down by the large simple-house cohort (34% of pilot) that benefits from faster scheduling and fewer handovers. The tail is driven by the remaining 66%—especially apartments and old buildings—where predictive survey skipping and missing permits generate failures, second visits, and manual activation. Because resource bottlenecks (coordinator 93%, second-visit team 92%) compound these failures, delay accumulates non-linearly in the tail. The temporal profile confirms this: Address validation  Appointment scheduling improved for simple houses, but Installation visit  Activation test worsened for apartments, and Billing start  Complaint escalation became a new long-tail relation.

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience.** The handover-of-work network simplified, reducing transfer delays. But the working-together network centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. Resilience in process networks depends on redundancy—when context is shared across roles, absence of one actor does not stall the case. By concentrating ownership, the redesign reduced redundancy; when coordinators are absent, cases stall because context is not distributed. This explains why lower handover counts co-occur with worse operational recovery.

**Lower visible cost, higher rework/downstream cost.** The redesigned path cuts visible cost by skipping surveys and accelerating billing. However, the cost measure excludes downstream complaint-handling after week 4, and first-time-right dropped (82%  76%) with complaints rising (6.1%  9.8%). The process artifacts reveal the mechanism: billing starts before activation is confirmed (move-on-log), creating billing disputes; second visits rise (11%  18%); manual activation doubles (7%  15%). These generate hidden costs—rework labor, customer service, complaints, and potential regulatory/reputational exposure—not fully captured in the visible cost line.

**Simple houses improve; apartments/old buildings deteriorate.** The redesign applies a “one-size-fits-all” simplification (merged scheduling, survey skip, coordinator ownership). For simple, low-risk houses, simplification removes needless steps. For medium-risk apartments and permit-sensitive old buildings, the same simplification removes necessary controls (survey to confirm access/risks, permit check to avoid legal/technical failure). The predictive rule for survey skipping is likely miscalibrated for these segments; the absence of permit checks is directly visible in alignment (move-on-model). Thus the same redesign produces opposite effects by segment.

| Alternative | Core idea | Advantages | Key risks / limits | When it would be reasonable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Scale R nationally** | Deploy redesign to all regions and segments | Captures median speed; lowers handover overhead; standardizes accountability | Exposes 66% of cases to tail blow-up (apartment/old building), compliance risk (billing before activation), hidden cost reversal, and resilience loss; pilot is not representative | Only if M4 shows full-cost neutrality/recovery, M7 shows zero conformance violations, and M1/M6 prove survey skip is safe after refinement |
| **B. | **C. Scale coordinator-ownership only; suspend survey skip and post-billing activation** | Keep one beneficial element (accountability/handover reduction), remove two dangerous changes | Retains speed/quality for cases where coordinator helps; removes worst failure modes | Still applies coordinator ownership to complex segments without filters; does not address missing permits or resource imbalance (93% coord, 92% second-visit); may still over-centralize | As an intermediate step **only if combined with segment filters** and utilization caps; better than A but insufficient alone |
| **D. Controlled regional experiment with better measurement** | Continue pilot-style deployment in selected regions with improved data (start times, risk attributes, 60-day cost, reason codes) | Generates causal evidence; limits national harm; improves measurement quality | Delays benefits; requires discipline to not let harmful practices (skip, billing activation) continue uncontrolled during the experiment | When management demands causal proof before committing; best paired with immediate suspension of survey skip and post-billing activation during the experiment |

6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: Alternative E — Segmented Hybrid with immediate guardrails.**

**Immediate actions (next 2 weeks):**
- **Suspend predictive field-survey skipping** for all medium/high building-risk addresses; restore full survey for apartments, old buildings, permitsensitive cases.
- **Suspend post-billing activation**; require successful activation test (or documented exception with customer agreement) before billing start for all cases; enforce via M7 conformance monitoring.
- **Deploy coordinator-ownership + merged scheduling exclusively for simple, low-building-risk houses** that match the successful pilot segment (low risk, single-family/low-complexity). Apply strict routing rules.
- **Restore baseline controls** (address validation  survey/permit  scheduling with checks  installation  activation  billing) for apartment, old-building, business, rural, and any case with incomplete risk data.
- **Initiate M4** (60-day cost tracking), **M3** (reason codes), and **M7** (conformance alerts) immediately.

**What to pause:**
- National scaling of Redesign R in any form.
- Predictive survey-skipping rules until M1/M6 validate by stratum.
- Any activation-check bypass or billing-start inversion.

**What to scale now:**
- Coordinator ownership and merged validation/scheduling **only within simple low-risk houses** (~34% of volume, with verified data).
- Use freed survey-team capacity (down to 52%) to support complex segments and second-visit reduction.

**What to measure next:**
- **M7 + M3:** Real-time conformance and reason-code capture for all activation/billing/permit exceptions.
- **M4:** 60-day full-cost tracking on both cohorts.
- **M1 / M6:** Stratified experiment to refine (not just abandon) survey rules.
- **M5:** Resilience test under coordinator absence with utilization caps (target <85%).

**Thresholds justifying broader rollout (to be reviewed at 10 weeks):**
- Simple-house cohort maintains median  10 days, p90  baseline (31 days) or lower, first-time-right  83%, complaints  5%, and 60-day cost  EUR 180.
- Complex-case cohorts (apartment, old building, business, rural) on restored controls recover to **baseline first-time-right (82%) and p90 ( baseline)**.
- Zero conformance violations (billing before activation; missing permits) in monitored cases over 4 consecutive weeks.
- Full 60-day cost for all-method mix  EUR 190.

**Defensibility check:** This recommendation does not pretend a single metric settles the decision. It separates the redesign’s real benefit (simple-case efficiency, accountability, fewer handovers) from its dangerous side effects (tail blow-up, activation/compliance risk, hidden cost, centralized fragility). It does not roll back beneficial elements solely because other metrics worsened, and it does not scale nationally without addressing the worsened tail, rework, and process-model imprecision. It uses the process-mining artifacts—fitness/precision divergence, alignment diagnostics, DFG shifts, social-network centralization, and temporal-profile deviations—to justify targeted controls rather than universal adoption.
