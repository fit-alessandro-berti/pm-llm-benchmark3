# 1. Review Scope and Assumptions

I distinguish four use cases:

| Use case | Primary objective | Acceptable trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Exploration | Discover behavior, tolerate noise, avoid excluding interesting variants | Low precision is acceptable; recall and human readability are more important |
| Conformance enforcement | Reject forbidden behavior, allow valid behavior, including unseen combinations of known steps | Precision and semantic compliance dominate; permissiveness is dangerous |
| Simulation | Generate behavior that is both structurally valid and representative | Soundness and well-defined probability distributions matter |
| Stakeholder communication | Explain what the process is and why | Simplicity helps, but misleading simplicity is worse than complexity |

The reported metrics are evidence, not ground truth. Because seeds, preprocessing, alignment settings, and holdout definition are absent, I treat the numbers as indicative only. For behavior probes, I interpret “accepts” as language membership under PM4Py process-tree semantics; conformance alignment may assign non-zero fitness to some non-members, but that is a scoring artifact, not acceptance.

# 3. Adversarial Review of Model S

## Strongest case for Model S

Model S is compact, readable, fast, and tolerant of missing events. Its use of silent `tau` alternatives and a parallel block means it can replay many plausible variants without needing a new branch per variant. If the goal is exploratory mining, high recall is useful. Its strong holdout fitness and high stakeholder comprehension make it attractive for workshops where a process analyst wants a rough control-flow backbone.

## Strongest case against Model S

Model S is not a safe compliance model. Its permissiveness is not merely “noise tolerance”; it accidentally turns mandatory checks into optional checks and destroys ordering constraints.

Concrete undesirable or policy-ambiguous traces Model S admits:

1. `Register Dossier, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Skips both mandatory checks and all reviews; directly violates P2.

2. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Expedite occurs before both mandatory checks; directly violates P4.

3. `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Skips Technical Review and places Expedite before a full mandatory-check set.

4. `Register Dossier, Supplier Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Supplier Review occurs without Credit Check or Technical Review; even if external, mandatory checks are absent.

5. `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Skips Credit Check; violates P2.

6. `Register Dossier, Expedite Review, Manager Approval, Release Order`  
   Expedite without any mandatory check.

Some of this permissiveness is intentional noise tolerance: the `tau` alternatives make events skippable. But that intentional design accidentally makes mandatory controls optional. The parallel block intentionally allows check reordering, but it also removes the precedence rule that Expedite Review must follow both mandatory checks. Thus Model S conflates “missing event tolerance” with “managerially optional activity,” which is exactly the wrong conflation for compliance.

Model S also cannot represent `Correct Dossier` at all. It rejects correction traces because it has no `Correct Dossier` leaf. Its treatment of duplicate mandatory checks is therefore accidental rather than diagnostic: it rejects duplicates because each leaf can fire once, not because it understands the suspicious-repetition rule.

# 4. Adversarial Review of Model C

## Strongest case for Model C

Model C encodes frequent observed variants, has no silent-skip mechanism, and rejects some obviously forbidden traces such as B3 and B4. Its high precision on the training log is appealing for an audit team that wants a model closely mirroring what was seen. If the goal is to classify only the most frequent variants, Model C is a readable catalog of those variants.

## Strongest case against Model C

Model C is a branch enumeration, not a general process model. It memorizes frequent sequences and fails on unseen but valid combinations of known steps.

Concrete consequences:

- It rejects B2: `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Credit Check, Auto Approval, Release Order` because it lacks a `Technical Review, Credit Check, Auto Approval` branch.
- It rejects B5: `Register Dossier, Technical Review, Credit Check, Supplier Review, Expedite Review, Auto Approval, Release Order` because it lacks that combination.
- It rejects valid Supplier + Auto Approval variants such as `Register Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Supplier Review, Auto Approval, Release Order`.
- For correction, it has only one branch: `Credit Check, Technical Review, Correct Dossier, Credit Check, Technical Review, Manager Approval`. It therefore rejects valid affected-check-only corrections such as B6.
- It has no correction branch with Auto Approval, Supplier Review, or Expedite Review.

The duplicate-label count of 33 is a symptom of branch reuse across exclusive choices. This hurts maintainability: adding a new allowed combination requires adding a whole new branch. It also complicates impact analysis and may cause ambiguous alignments depending on PM4Py settings.

High training precision may be an artifact of memorization. If the training log contains exactly the branches listed, then precision on those branches is high by construction. This does not mean the model is precise about the business policy; it means it is precise about the training sample. The low holdout fitness confirms this.

# 6. Likely Metric Pathologies

Ranked by likely decision impact:

1. **Train/holdout leakage or split sensitivity**  
   Without seeds, case-split code, or held-out case IDs, the holdout may contain training variants or be drawn differently. This undermines the strongest apparent evidence for S and against C.

2. **Fitness rewarded by optionalizing mandatory behavior**  
   Model S achieves high fitness by adding `tau` alternatives to mandatory checks. High fitness then measures tolerance of missing events, not compliance with mandatory controls.

3. **Positive-log-only precision**  
   Precision was computed against observed positive traces only. This makes C look precise because its branches match observations. It does not test whether the model rejects curated negative traces.

4. **Missing-event bias**  
   Supplier Review may be missing in external cases, and the external/internal attribute is absent. This biases fitness and generalization measurements and makes P3 impossible to enforce from control flow alone.

5. **Generalization rewarded by excessive permissiveness**  
   S scores very high token-based generalization because parallel/tau allows almost any next token. That is not semantic generalization; it is over-approximation.

6. **Alignment-cost sensitivity**  
   Alignment costs were not disclosed. High model-move or log-move penalties can make unfit traces look better or worse. This affects both fitness and precision comparability.

7. **Silent-transition effects**  
   Model S uses silent transitions to skip checks. Silent transitions can create multiple alignments, reduce precision, and hide semantic violations.

8. **Duplicated-label effects**  
   Model C has many duplicated labels. Depending on PM4Py conversion and alignment heuristics, duplicate labels can make conformance diagnostics ambiguous and complicate precision measurement.

9. **Stakeholder-score methodology**  
   The informal 1–5 score has no protocol, no anchored rubric, no randomization, and no separation of model readability from model correctness. It may overstate S’s advantage.

10. **Runtime confounding by model size**  
   Model C is slower, but this is confounded by its much larger size. A smaller semantically correct model would likely be faster.

# 7. Third Alternative

I propose a compact procedural backbone with a localized correction-loop block and post-hoc policy checks. This is not a POWL-only design and not a full DECLARE model; it is a procedural model with explicit uncertainty management.

## 7.1 Compact formal description

```text
->(
  'Register Dossier',
  +('Credit Check', 'Technical Review'),
  *(
    tau,
    ->(
      'Correct Dossier',
      X(
        'Credit Check',
        'Technical Review',
        +('Credit Check','Technical Review')
      )
    )
  ),
  +(
    X('Supplier Review', tau),
    X('Expedite Review', tau)
  ),
  X('Manager Approval', 'Auto Approval'),
  'Release Order'
)
```

Interpretation:

- Registration is first, release is last.
- The initial `+('Credit Check', 'Technical Review')` makes both checks mandatory and permits either order.
- The loop `*(tau, ->('Correct Dossier', X(...)))` allows zero or more correction-recheck cycles.
  - Each correction is followed by a non-empty affected-check set: either Credit Check, Technical Review, or both.
  - This represents the missing “affected check” attribute as a nondeterministic choice; it does not claim to know which checks were truly affected.
- The optional block `+(X('Supplier Review', tau), X('Expedite Review', tau))`:
  - makes Supplier Review optional in the backbone, because the external/internal attribute is absent;
  - makes Expedite Review optional;
  - places both after the mandatory checks and after correction-recheck cycles;
  - allows Expedite Review to occur before or after Supplier Review via the parallel operator.
- Exactly one of Manager Approval and Auto Approval occurs.
- Repeated mandatory checks without a preceding `Correct Dossier` are not generated by this model: the only duplicate check occurrences are inside the correction loop, immediately after `Correct Dossier`.

## 7.3 External checks

```python
MANDATORY = {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}
APPROVALS = {"Manager Approval", "Auto Approval"}

def external_policy_eval(trace, attrs=None):
    issues = []

    if not trace or trace[0] != "Register Dossier":
        issues.append("first_activity")
    if trace and trace[-1] != "Release Order":
        issues.append("last_activity")

    if sum(event in APPROVALS for event in trace) != 1:
        issues.append("exactly_one_approval")

    if attrs and attrs.get("external") is True and "Supplier Review" not in trace:
        issues.append("external_case_missing_supplier_review")

    # Suspicious duplicate mandatory check without preceding Correct Dossier.
    for i, event in enumerate(trace):
        if event in MANDATORY:
            prior_mandatory = any(ev in MANDATORY for ev in trace[:i])
            if prior_mandatory:
                correction_since_last_mandatory = False
                for ev in reversed(trace[:i]):
                    if ev == "Correct Dossier":
                        correction_since_last_mandatory = True
                        break
                    if ev in MANDATORY:
                        break
                if not correction_since_last_mandatory:
                    issues.append("duplicate_check_without_correction")

    return issues or True
```

This external checker is a skeleton, not a complete audit oracle.

## 7.4 Explicit semantics for the requirements

- **Mandatory checks:** Both Credit Check and Technical Review must occur before approval. They may occur in either order.
- **Optional Supplier Review:** Supplier Review is optional in the control-flow model only because the external/internal attribute is missing. If an external/internal attribute is available, the external checker enforces external orders to contain Supplier Review.
- **Expedite Review ordering:** Expedite Review is optional, occurs after mandatory checks, and occurs before approval. It may occur before or after Supplier Review.
- **Exactly-one approval:** The model chooses exactly one of Manager Approval or Auto Approval.
- **Correction/recheck behavior:** Correct Dossier may repeat. Each correction is followed by a non-empty subset of mandatory checks. Without the affected-check attribute, the model cannot verify which subset is correct; it only constrains that at least one check repeats.
- **Suspicious duplicate checks:** Duplicate mandatory checks are not allowed unless immediately preceded by Correct Dossier.

## 7.5 Limitations

- **Missing external/internal attribute:** The third model intentionally does not enforce “external implies Supplier Review” in the control-flow model. Doing so would either force Supplier Review for all orders or reject valid external cases with missing Supplier Review. The external checker handles this only when the attribute is present.
- **Missing affected-check attribute:** The correction loop allows any non-empty recheck subset. This is a deliberate under-constraint to avoid rejecting valid affected-check-only corrections. It cannot be used to prove that the correct subset was repeated.
- **Correction timing:** I place correction before Supplier Review and Expedite Review. This is a conservative interpretation. If the business confirms that corrections may occur after Supplier Review or Expedite Review, the model should be relaxed, but that relaxation should be a conscious policy decision.

MANDATORY = {"Credit Check", "Technical Review"}

def missing_event_variants(trace):
    for i, event in enumerate(trace):
        if event in MANDATORY:
            yield trace[:i] + trace[i+1:]

def duplicate_without_correction_variants(trace, event):
    for i in range(len(trace)):
        if trace[i] == event:
            yield trace[:i] + [event] + trace[i:]

## 9.9 Stakeholder comprehension protocol

Use a documented protocol:

1. Create model diagrams with identical visual notation.
2. Randomize model order across participants.
3. Give participants a fixed set of traces: B1–B8 plus negative and unseen-combination traces.
4. Ask participants to mark each trace as valid or invalid under the model.
5. Record time, confidence, and accuracy.
6. Use a 1–5 scale with anchored descriptions:  
   5 = can explain and correctly predict behavior;  
   1 = cannot interpret even with help.

## 9.10 Decision rule

Reject a model for enforcement despite good aggregate metrics if:

- any forbidden trace has fitness  0.90;
- a duplicate mandatory check without `Correct Dossier` has fitness  0.90;
- a valid unseen combination has fitness < 0.80 without a documented missing-event cause;
- soundness fails;
- stakeholder comprehension is below 3.0 for the enforcement audience;
- precision measured against a curated negative suite is worse than an agreed threshold.

# 10. Governance Recommendation

**Model S** may be used only for exploratory workshops and rough process discovery. It should not be used for conformance, audit, or enforcement because it admits forbidden traces that skip mandatory controls. Its high fitness and generalization are misleading for compliance.

**Model C** may be retained as a read-only catalog of frequent historical variants. It should not be used as a general conformance reference because it rejects valid unseen combinations and is expensive to maintain.

**Third alternative** should be piloted as the enforcement-oriented control-flow model. It is safer than Model S because mandatory checks and Expedite ordering are explicit. It is more generalizable and maintainable than Model C because it composes small blocks rather than enumerating branches.

Before deployment, the third alternative must meet these conditions:

- passes PM4Py soundness checks;
- rejects the curated negative suite;
- accepts the full unseen-combination suite;
- publishes reproducibility artifacts: seed, preprocessing, alignment settings, and holdout case IDs;
- collects external/internal and affected-check attributes for real enforcement;
- achieves stakeholder comprehension of at least 3.0 under a documented protocol;
- documents the conservative correction-timing assumption and obtains process-owner sign-off.

# 11. Dissenting View

My third alternative is still a compromise, not a proven compliant model. It uses a process-tree loop with a silent body, which is compact but may be less transparent to business users than a simple diagram. It preserves missing attributes as external checks, which means the deployed model is only as good as the attribute-quality effort; without external/internal and affected-check data, it still cannot enforce P3 or P6 precisely. Its conservative placement of Correction before Supplier and Expedite Review may reject real cases where correction occurs later in the dossier lifecycle. The proposed validation thresholds are also arbitrary: a fitness cut-off of 0.90 or a comprehension score of 3.0 may not match the organization’s actual risk tolerance. One could argue that the board should not pilot a new model until the missing attributes are collected and a proper negative suite is curated. I would change my recommendation if original-model reproducibility artifacts showed that Model C’s holdout degradation was caused by bad splitting rather than true overfitting, or if stakeholder interviews revealed that business users prefer a stricter enumerated model despite its complexity. Additional evidence from external/internal and affected-check logs would also sharpen the third model and reduce its explicit uncertainty.
