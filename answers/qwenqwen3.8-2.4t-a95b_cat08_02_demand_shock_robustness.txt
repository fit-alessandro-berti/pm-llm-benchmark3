# 1. Stress scenarios

The normal-period average hides fragility. Peak demand is only about 43% higher on a weekly basis (9,900 cases over 3 weeks versus 18,400 over 8 weeks), but the mix changes sharply: Urgent + Complex-panel rises from 31% of cases in normal periods to 49% in peak. That mix shift, not just volume, explains why p90 cycle times and long queues deteriorate so badly.

For redesign, I use the following design guardrails rather than “improve the average”:

- Urgent: protect the 18-hour contractual boundary; aim for 90–95% within 18h in normal conditions and 80–90% even in stressed conditions.
- Routine: protect the 72-hour target; aim for median 60–70h and p90 96–120h under peak stress.
- Complex-panel: no formal SLA is given, but aim to keep p90 below 180h and prevent specialist-hold aging beyond 24–48h.
- Repeat-after-failure: reduce p90 from 240h in peak toward 180h by reducing repeat loops and machine-related rework.
- System stability: keep overall rework below ~10% and keep the share of cases with any queue >12h below ~35% in peak scenarios.

At minimum, the redesign must survive these four plausible stress scenarios.

| Scenario label | Scenario description | Plausible mix / disruption | Primary stress points shown by logs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S1: Winter infection-wave mix shift** | Volume rises and mix shifts heavily toward Urgent and Complex-panel. | Routine 30–35%, Urgent 30–35%, Complex-panel 22–25%, Repeat 10%; specialist may be absent on second Friday; pre-analytical defects rise. | Pre-analytical check, accession correction, assay scheduling, medical validation, specialist review. |
| **S2: Assay-machine degradation or outage** | M2 fails, slows, or produces elevated repeats; M1 may also run below design capacity. | Peak or near-peak mix; M2 unavailable for 8–24h or both machines at 70–80% capacity; repeat rate rises. | Assay run, batch wait, repeat assay loop, preparation WIP, technical validation bursts. |
| **S3: Medical validator or specialist-review absence** | One medical validator is absent, or specialist review capacity is unavailable. | Peak mix with high Urgent/Complex-panel; specialist unavailable every second Friday or medical validation capacity down 20–30%. | Medical validation, specialist review, hold-for-specialist-review loop, release result. |
| **S4: Courier arrival compression or delay** | Couriers arrive in larger-than-planned spikes or routes are delayed by winter weather/traffic. | Peak volume; 60–70% of samples arrive in two large waves or routes delayed 2–4h; urgent orders arrive late in the day. | Registration, courier intake, receive sample, pre-analytical check, night-shift assay and validation capacity. |

These scenarios can overlap. A robust redesign must therefore control priority, WIP, quality, and downstream capacity rather than simply adding resources at one station.

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

## S1: Winter infection-wave mix shift

Likely bottleneck chain:

1. **Registration and accession pressure rises first.**  
   Peak registration utilization already reaches 85%, and clerks’ error rate rises after 9h shifts. Manual accession correction is handled by only two specialists, and peak accession queue reaches 14h. In peak, manual correction appears in 420 of 9,730 received cases (~4.3%), compared with 500 of 18,120 (~2.8%) in normal periods.

2. **Pre-analytical check becomes unstable.**  
   Peak pre-analytical utilization is 96%, with median queue rising from 1.8h to 9.5h. Recollection requests rise from 760/18,120 (~4.2%) to 780/9,730 (~8.0%). The recollection loop is extremely expensive: `Request recollection -> Receive sample` rises from 42h to 60h median. This loop consumes clerk and intake capacity because staff must explain errors to physicians.

3. **Urgent priority fragments preparation and assay scheduling.**  
   Urgent cases bypass batch wait in 74% of normal cases but only 58% of peak cases. That drop is a warning signal: urgent work is being trapped behind batching or scheduling congestion. A strict Urgent-first rule may restore bypass for Urgent cases, but it fragments machine schedules and reduces effective assay throughput. Peak assay utilization is already 98%, with assay queue rising from 4h to 18h and `Batch wait -> Assay run` rising from 9h to 22h.

4. **Complex-panel cases overload medical validation and specialist review.**  
   Complex-panel cases are 3.1x more likely to need medical validation and 2.4x more likely to enter specialist review. Peak medical validation queue rises from 2h to 22h, and specialist review queue rises from 5h to 36h. `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation` rises from 18h to 48h. Technical validation also becomes congested, rising from 76% to 101% utilization and queue from 3.5h to 16h.

5. **Routine cases become the shock absorber.**  
   If Urgent and Complex-panel are protected by brute-force priority, Routine cases wait longer. Routine p90 already rises from 68h to 120h in peak. Strict Urgent-first dispatch can therefore reduce Urgent median time while increasing total WIP and Routine p90.

Why local fixes migrate the bottleneck:

- Automating registration (**I8**) can push more orders into receive/pre-analytical check, exposing the 96% pre-analytical utilization.
- Early defect triage (**I6**) can shorten the time to identify bad samples, but it may increase immediate recollection calls and burden registration clerks unless physician notification is automated.
- Adding evening medical validation (**I5**) can reduce medical validation queue, but if technical validation cannot supply clean cases, the medical shift will be starved or will create pressure to validate incomplete work.
- Outsourcing Complex-panel overflow (**I10**) relieves specialist review but moves the bottleneck to transport, partner-lab capacity, and traceability.

## S2: Assay-machine degradation or outage

Likely bottleneck chain:

1. **Assay capacity drops immediately.**  
   M2 has higher failure rate but faster runtime. If M2 degrades or fails, assay queue can rise sharply from the peak baseline of 18h. Because peak assay utilization is already 98%, even a modest capacity loss creates a nonlinear queue increase.

2. **Preparation and batch wait fill with WIP.**  
   `Test preparation -> Batch wait` increases from 760 to 1,180 frequency in peak, and `Batch wait -> Assay run` rises from 9h to 22h. If preparation continues to release batches without a downstream pull, WIP accumulates before assay.

3. **Repeat-after-failure cases consume scarce assay capacity twice.**  
   Repeat cases cluster after night shifts and M2 usage. In peak, the repeat-assay branch is more prominent: 1,260 repeat-assay events versus 1,140 in normal, despite lower total case volume. `Repeat assay -> Assay run` rises from 10h to 26h. Repeats are not just rework; they are capacity destruction.

4. **Technical validation sees alternating starvation and surges.**  
   When assay is blocked, technical validators may be underloaded. When delayed batches finally complete, technical validation receives a surge. Technical validators are already at 101% peak utilization, so this burst behavior is harmful.

5. **Urgent and Complex-panel protection becomes harder.**  
   If M1 is rigidly dedicated to Urgent/Complex-panel and M2 handles Routine, an M2 outage either stops Routine work or forces Routine onto M1, crowding out critical cases. If Urgent-first dispatch is used aggressively, machine fragmentation worsens, reducing total throughput.

Why local fixes migrate the bottleneck:

- Increasing batch size (**I2**) may improve preparation efficiency, but if assay capacity is impaired it simply creates larger batch queues and longer `Batch wait -> Assay run` delays.
- Dedicating M1 to Urgent/Complex-panel (**I3**) helps critical cases only while M2 is healthy. During M2 degradation, the policy can either strand Routine volume or overload M1.
- Pushing more work into assay to “keep machines busy” can overload technical validation later.
- Adding QC or maintenance reduces short-term assay capacity, so it must be paired with WIP caps and priority rules.

## S3: Medical validator or specialist-review absence

Likely bottleneck chain:

1. **Medical validation becomes the constraint for critical work.**  
   Medical validation utilization rises from 58% to 93% in peak, and queue rises from 2h to 22h. This is not just volume: peak case mix contains more Urgent and Complex-panel cases, which require medical validation more often.

2. **Specialist review becomes a long parking lot.**  
   Specialist review utilization rises from 43% to 87%, and queue rises from 5h to 36h. The specialist is unavailable every second Friday, which is a predictable but dangerous disruption. `Hold for specialist review -> Medical validation` rises from 18h to 48h in peak.

3. **Technical validation accumulates “validated but unreleased” WIP.**  
   Technical validation can release many Routine cases directly: in normal, 11,700 of 15,890 technical-validation exits go directly to release (~74%); in peak, this falls to 4,050 of 7,240 (~56%). That means peak mix pushes more cases into medical validation. If medical validation or specialist review is absent, technical validation may keep producing cases that cannot be released, increasing risk of corrections and rework.

4. **Release result delays become visible to clinicians.**  
   Medical validation to release rises from 2h to 5h median, but the larger issue is upstream queueing. Urgent cases can miss the 18h target even if the final release step is fast.

Why local fixes migrate the bottleneck:

- Adding an evening medical-validation shift (**I5**) helps only if there are enough technically validated cases and if specialist-review holds are not the true blocker.
- If specialist review is absent, medical validators may not be able to clear held Complex-panel cases.
- Cross-training technical validators to help accession correction (**I4**) during a medical-validation absence can worsen technical validation availability and deepen the release bottleneck.
- Holding Routine releases until batch reports are ready (**I9**) can reduce release workload, but it can push Routine p90 beyond target if applied indiscriminately.
- Outsourcing Complex-panel work (**I10**) relieves specialist review but adds 12h transport delay and traceability risk.

## S4: Courier arrival compression or delay

Likely bottleneck chain:

1. **Courier intake and registration receive a lumpiness shock.**  
   Courier intake queue rises from 1h to 7.5h in peak, and arrivals spike at 10:00 and 17:00. If routes are delayed or compressed, receive sample and registration experience bursts rather than stable flow.

2. **Registration errors increase if overtime is used.**  
   Registration clerks can work overtime, but error rate rises after 9h shifts. More errors feed manual accession correction and recollection loops.

3. **Pre-analytical check absorbs the shock next.**  
   `Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check` rises from 2h to 9h median. Pre-analytical utilization reaches 96% in peak. If samples arrive late, urgent cases lose slack before processing even begins.

4. **Late urgent cases hit night-shift capacity.**  
   Repeat-after-failure cases cluster after night shifts and M2 usage. If courier delays push urgent work into the evening, the lab may use the more failure-prone machine under less stable staffing conditions.

5. **Downstream batching and validation miss planned cutoffs.**  
   If pre-analytical check is backlogged, preparation batches start late, assay runs shift, and validation queues become uneven.

Why local fixes migrate the bottleneck:

- Smoothing courier slots (**I7**) helps only if couriers comply. If they do not, the bottleneck remains at intake.
- If courier smoothing succeeds, the bottleneck can move to pre-analytical check, which is already near saturation in peak.
- Automating registration (**I8**) can reduce clerk workload, but incomplete or bad metadata may pass through and fail later in pre-analytical check or assay.
- Early defect triage (**I6**) can reduce late failures, but it can create a surge of recollection requests and physician calls unless the notification process is automated and well designed.

# 3. Robust redesign package

I do not recommend adding capacity everywhere. The redesign should be a bundle of controls that works across scenarios. The core idea is: **segment flow, protect critical cases, control WIP, improve intake quality, and use flexible capacity only where the bottleneck actually appears.**

## 3.1 Replace strict Urgent-first with class-based lanes and WIP caps

Modified version of **I1**.

Create four operating lanes:

1. **Urgent STAT lane**  
   - No Routine batch wait longer than 2h.
   - Protected assay slots and protected validation slots.
   - Escalation alert when remaining slack is below 4h.

2. **Complex-panel scheduled lane**  
   - Scheduled assay blocks because Complex-panel cases require multiple assays.
   - Reserved medical-validation and specialist-review appointments.
   - Trigger for outsourcing when specialist-hold aging becomes excessive.

3. **Routine due-date batch lane**  
   - Batched for efficiency, but batch size and release time are controlled by downstream capacity.
   - Not allowed to crowd out Urgent or Complex-panel work.

4. **Repeat/rework lane**  
   - Separate handling to avoid repeats consuming random assay capacity.
   - Root-cause review, especially for night-shift and M2-associated repeats.

Add CONWIP / pull logic:

- Do not start new Routine batches if assay queue is already high.
- Do not push more technical validation into medical validation if medical validation or specialist review is blocked.
- Limit new Complex-panel starts if specialist-hold queue is aging beyond threshold, unless outsourcing is activated.

Why robust:

- It protects Urgent cases without letting them starve Routine and Complex-panel cases.
- It recognizes that not all case types can be prioritized simultaneously.
- It prevents local optimization from creating system-wide WIP growth.

Assumptions:

- The LIS can identify case class, age, and remaining SLA slack.
- Dispatchers can enforce lane rules.
- Assay and validation schedules can accept protected slots.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- If WIP caps are too tight, queue builds at registration or pre-analytical check.
- If WIP caps are too loose, assay, technical validation, or medical validation become overloaded.
- If case classification is wrong, Urgent or Complex-panel cases may be misrouted.

## 3.2 Use adaptive batching, not a blanket increase from 12 to 24

Modified version of **I2**.

Policy:

- Routine batch size normally 8–12.
- During peak, allow 14–16 only if:
  - assay queue is below threshold,
  - no Urgent backlog exists,
  - Routine cases have enough SLA slack,
  - technical validation queue is stable.
- Do not use 24-sample batches as default because peak `Batch wait -> Assay run` already reaches 22h.
- Urgent and Complex-panel cases should have explicit batch exceptions.

Why robust:

- Preserves setup efficiency without recreating the peak problem where Urgent bypass falls to 58%.
- Prevents large Routine batches from delaying time-critical work.
- Allows batching to expand only when downstream capacity is available.

Assumptions:

- Preparation benches can change batch sizes quickly.
- Machine setup cost is understood by assay type.
- Batch exceptions do not become so common that batching efficiency disappears.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- If batch size expands during assay congestion, batch wait and assay queue worsen.
- If too many Urgent exceptions fragment the schedule, machine throughput falls.
- If batches are released in waves, technical validation may receive surge loads.

## 3.3 Dynamic M1/M2 routing with repeat prevention

Modified version of **I3**.

Policy:

- M1 is protected for Urgent, Complex-panel, and repeat-sensitive assays.
- M2 handles Routine volume only when:
  - M2 availability is healthy,
  - repeat rate is below threshold,
  - night-shift QC controls are in place.
- If M2 degrades:
  - reduce Routine batch size,
  - split Routine volume onto M1 within protected capacity,
  - prioritize Urgent and Complex-panel,
  - defer low-priority Routine work if needed.
- Add targeted QC after night shifts and on M2 when repeat rate rises.

Why robust:

- Avoids the fragility of a rigid “M1 for critical, M2 for Routine” design when M2 fails.
- Directly addresses the observation that repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 usage.
- Preserves critical-case capacity while allowing dynamic rerouting.

Assumptions:

- Both machines can run a sufficient subset of assays.
- Changeover time is manageable.
- QC staff can respond without creating a new bottleneck.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- If too much Routine volume is moved to M1, Urgent and Complex-panel slots are crowded out.
- Extra QC reduces short-term assay capacity.
- If both machines are degraded, routing rules cannot create missing capacity.

## 3.4 Pre-analytical quality gate: early defect triage plus digital registration

Combines **I6** and part of **I8**.

Policy:

- Perform early pre-analytical defect triage within 2h of sample receipt.
- Use standardized defect codes.
- Automatically notify the ordering physician when recollection is required.
- Aim for recollection decision within 4h, not after 42–60h.
- Auto-register only orders with complete digital metadata.
- Route incomplete or suspicious orders to an exception clerk, not to silent manual rework.

Why robust:

- Recollection loops are one of the largest hidden delays: `Request recollection -> Receive sample` is 42h normal and 60h peak.
- Reducing late failures protects assay and validation capacity.
- Better registration data reduces accession correction load, which is handled by only two specialists.

Assumptions:

- Physicians respond to automated recollection requests.
- Couriers can perform recollections without excessive delay.
- Metadata standards are enforceable.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- Early rejection can increase immediate call volume for registration clerks.
- If physicians delay recollection, samples sit in a new “awaiting physician” queue.
- If automation accepts bad metadata, errors move from registration to pre-analytical check or assay.

## 3.5 Smooth courier arrivals and create an urgent intake fast lane

Uses **I7**, supported by **I8**.

Policy:

- Assign courier arrival slots by hour.
- Create a fast-lane intake for Urgent samples.
- Pre-alert the lab when Urgent or Complex-panel loads are expected.
- Extend or shift pre-analytical coverage during known arrival peaks.
- Use registration automation for clean digital orders, with exception handling for poor metadata.

Why robust:

- Courier intake spikes at 10:00 and 17:00 create avoidable lumpiness.
- Smoothing arrivals stabilizes registration, receive, and pre-analytical check.
- Urgent fast lane protects the 18h contractual clock from intake delays.

Assumptions:

- Couriers can comply with slots.
- The lab can enforce or incentivize slot adherence.
- Urgent orders are identifiable before or at arrival.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- If couriers ignore slots, intake remains spiky.
- If intake is smoothed but pre-analytical capacity is unchanged, the queue simply moves to pre-analytical check.
- If automated registration accepts poor metadata, pre-analytical defect rate may rise.

## 3.6 Guardrailed cross-training and targeted temporary shifts

Modified use of **I4** and **I5**.

Policy:

- Cross-train two technical validators for accession correction, but only deploy them there when:
  - technical validation queue is below threshold,
  - predicted technical validation load is below high-risk level,
  - assay flow is not about to surge.
- Cross-train preparation staff for basic technical-validation support where safe.
- Use temporary evening medical-validation shift only when:
  - medical validation queue is high,
  - specialist review is available or holds can be cleared,
  - technical validation can supply cases.
- For known specialist absence on second Friday, pre-schedule backup review or outsourcing.

Why robust:

- Provides flexibility without permanent capacity everywhere.
- Targets transient bottlenecks rather than average demand.
- Prevents blind cross-training from creating a new bottleneck.

Assumptions:

- Cross-trained staff maintain acceptable error rates.
- Medical validators are available for evening shifts.
- Supervisors can switch staffing rules in near real time.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- Pulling technical validators to accession correction can worsen technical validation, which already reaches 101% peak utilization.
- Evening medical validation can cause fatigue or reduce next-day availability.
- If specialist review is absent, extra medical validation may not clear Complex-panel holds.

## 3.7 Exception-based validation and controlled Routine release holds

Modified use of **I9**.

Policy:

- Auto-release Routine, low-risk, normal-result cases after technical validation where clinically safe.
- Medical validation by exception for abnormal, urgent, complex, or high-risk cases.
- Hold Routine releases until batch report is ready only when:
  - release workload is overloaded,
  - Routine cases have enough SLA slack,
  - Urgent and Complex-panel releases are not affected.
- Never hold Urgent or Complex-panel releases for batch convenience.

Why robust:

- Protects scarce medical-validation and release capacity for cases that truly need it.
- Reduces unnecessary release work during peak.
- Allows Routine work to absorb some variability without harming critical cases.

Assumptions:

- Auto-release rules are clinically safe and auditable.
- Routine cases can be held briefly without harming patient care.
- Batch reporting does not become a permanent delay mechanism.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- Routine p90 can deteriorate if holds are applied too broadly.
- Clinician dissatisfaction may rise if batch reports arrive late.
- If auto-release rules are too permissive, abnormal results may escape review.

## 3.8 Complex-panel overflow outsourcing with strict gatekeeping

Uses **I10**.

Policy:

- Outsource selected Complex-panel cases when:
  - internal specialist-review queue exceeds threshold,
  - predicted internal delay exceeds partner-lab delay plus transport,
  - the case is clinically suitable for external handling.
- Maintain LIS traceability and chain-of-custody controls.
- Exclude unstable Urgent Complex-panel cases unless internal delay is clearly worse than partner delay.

Why robust:

- Converts an internal specialist-review bottleneck into external capacity.
- Useful in winter waves and specialist-absence scenarios.
- Avoids forcing all Complex-panel demand through one scarce internal resource.

Assumptions:

- Partner lab quality is reliable.
- Transport delay is stable around 12h.
- LIS can track outsourced orders end-to-end.

Where it may fail / downstream risk:

- Partner lab may become overloaded during regional waves.
- Transport delay and traceability complexity increase.
- If outsourcing is used for urgent cases without care, the 12h transport delay can cause breaches.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

These policies should activate only under defined conditions. They should also switch off when the stress condition disappears.

| Scenario / policy | ON trigger | Actions | OFF trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S1 Surge mix-shift protection** | Rolling 24h Urgent + Complex-panel share >35%, or medical validation queue >8h, or specialist hold queue >12h. | Activate protected Urgent and Complex-panel assay slots; cap Routine batch size at 12; suspend non-critical Routine release holds unless SLA slack >24h; evaluate Complex-panel outsourcing if specialist hold >24h; freeze diversions from technical validation. | Urgent + Complex-panel share <25%, medical validation queue <4h, specialist hold queue <8h for 24h. |
| **S2 Machine degradation mode** | Any assay machine availability <85% for >4h, or assay queue median >12h, or repeat rate >10% in a shift. | Dynamic M1/M2 routing; reduce Routine batch size to 8; prioritize Urgent and Complex-panel; defer low-priority Routine; extra QC on affected machine; repeat-review huddle; cap new preparation releases if assay queue remains high. | Machine availability >95%, assay queue <6h, repeat rate <7% for 24h. |
| **S3 Validation / specialist absence mode** | Planned specialist absence, or medical validation capacity <80%, plus Urgent + Complex-panel share >25%, or medical validation queue >8h, or hold-to-medical predicted >24h. | Activate evening medical validation if supply exists; reassign holds to available reviewer or tele-review; outsource suitable Complex-panel overflow; auto-release Routine low-risk cases; allow Routine release holds only with SLA slack >24h; do not pull technical validators to accession correction. | Medical validation queue <4h, specialist hold queue <8h, medical staffing normal for 24h. |
| **S4 Courier compression / delay mode** | Expected intake >130% of plan in any 2h window, or courier delay >2h, or courier intake queue >4h. | Enforce courier slots; open Urgent fast lane; pre-alert lab; extend pre-analytical coverage if possible; activate early defect triage; delay Routine batch starts if pre-analytical queue >8h; adjust batch cutoffs to protect Urgent. | Arrival variance <10% of plan, courier intake queue <2h for the shift. |
| **Pre-analytical / accession defect surge sub-policy** | Recollection rate >6%, accession correction queue >8h, or manual accession correction rate >5%. | Activate early defect triage; automated physician notification; temporary exception clerk for registration; deploy one cross-trained technical validator to accession correction only if technical validation queue <6h; pause auto-registration for incomplete metadata. | Recollection rate <4%, accession correction queue <4h for 24h. |

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

Even after the redesign, several fragilities remain.

1. **Dependence on partner lab for Complex-panel overflow — redesign-induced.**  
   Outsourcing helps, but it creates a new external dependency. If the partner lab is also hit by a winter wave, or if transport is delayed, Complex-panel cases may have no good internal fallback. Traceability and cold-chain control become more complex.

2. **Dynamic lane and WIP-cap misconfiguration — redesign-induced.**  
   The protected-lane system is more robust than strict Urgent-first dispatch, but it requires accurate real-time data and good rules. If caps are too tight, Routine cases may be starved or queues may build at registration/pre-analytical check. If caps are too loose, the system can still overload assay or validation.

3. **Evening medical-validation fatigue — redesign-induced.**  
   Evening shifts can relieve peak medical validation, but they may increase validation errors or reduce next-day availability. This is especially risky if medical validators are already scarce.

4. **Cross-training technical validators can create a validation hole.**  
   Using technical validators for accession correction helps pre-analytical bottlenecks, but technical validation is already at 101% peak utilization. If both pre-analytical and technical validation surge together, the cross-training policy can worsen the more critical downstream constraint.

5. **Early defect triage may increase physician and clerk burden.**  
   Rejecting poor samples within 2h is operationally useful, but it can create a surge of recollection requests and physician calls. If physician response is slow, the delay simply moves from the lab to the ordering clinician.

6. **Courier smoothing depends on external compliance.**  
   Slotting arrivals is valuable, but winter weather, traffic, and courier behavior can break the plan. The lab still needs a surge response if slots fail.

7. **M2 repeat risk remains a structural issue.**  
   M2 is faster but has higher failure risk, and repeats cluster after M2 usage and night shifts. Unless the root cause is solved, machine degradation scenarios will continue to consume assay capacity through repeats.

8. **Automated registration can pass bad metadata downstream.**  
   Automation reduces registration workload, but if metadata standards are weak, errors may appear later in pre-analytical check, accession correction, or assay preparation, where they are more expensive.
