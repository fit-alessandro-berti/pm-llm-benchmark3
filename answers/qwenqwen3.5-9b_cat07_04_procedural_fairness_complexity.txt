# 1. Fairness dimensions

To assess procedural fairness in this public-benefits context, we must evaluate multiple dimensions beyond simple approval rates. The evidence suggests that while some disparities are likely driven by legitimate case complexity, others may indicate structural inequities.

*   **Outcome Parity:** Raw approval rates differ significantly (Group A 78% vs. Group D 58%). However, raw parity is an insufficient metric because Groups B, C, and D have higher documented complexity (risk scores, incomplete documentation).
*   **Adjusted Outcome Disparity:** Even after controlling for risk, documentation, and channel, Group B shows a -5.5 percentage point drop in approval probability compared to Group A. This residual disparity suggests potential procedural bias or unobserved barriers.
*   **Procedural Burden:** Procedural fairness requires that rights-preserving steps (e.g., Language Support, Accommodation Review) do not impose excessive burdens. Groups B and C experience significantly longer cycle times (13.7d vs. 8.1d) and higher rework rates (22% vs. 11%), which may indicate excessive friction in the process for those exercising rights.
*   **Timeliness:** Median cycle times for Group B and C are substantially longer. Specifically, waiting times for Language Support Contact (3.8 days) and Accommodation Review (4.7 days) are not covered by service-level targets, creating uncertainty and potential hardship.
*   **Consistency of Routing:** Legitimate routing (e.g., Fraud Review for high risk) should be consistent. Group D is routed to Fraud Review 28% of the time (V3), consistent with their high risk score. However, Groups B and C are routed to support steps (V4, V5) which are not strictly risk-based but rights-based. If the routing to these steps creates bottlenecks without proportional benefit, it may be inconsistent with efficiency fairness.
*   **Quality/Accessibility of Support:** Group B cases are assigned to 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. If the capacity for support is insufficient relative to demand, the "quality" of the support (e.g., turnaround time) is compromised, affecting the applicant's ability to navigate the process.
*   **Appeal/Rework Burden:** Group B and D have significantly higher appeal rates (13% and 16% vs. 6%) and reopen rates. High appeal rates can be a signal of poor quality decisions or, alternatively, higher complexity. If Group B appeals more often despite adjusted controls for complexity, it suggests the initial decision process may be less accessible or clear for them.
*   **Resource-Allocation Fairness:** Resource assignment appears skewed. Group B relies on 7 bilingual staff, while Group A uses 52 general staff. This concentration creates a resource bottleneck for Group B specifically, whereas Group D is assigned to a dedicated fraud queue. This raises questions about whether resources are allocated proportionally to need or based on historical patterns.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

To distinguish between disparity, discrimination, legitimate routing, and uncertainty, the analysis must move beyond simple regression and incorporate process mining techniques.

*   **Matching or Stratification Variables:** Use propensity score matching or coarsened exact matching (CEM) on the adjusted covariates (risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month). This creates comparable cohorts within each group to isolate the effect of the applicant group itself.
*   **Path/Variant Comparison:** Compare the distribution of variants (V1–V6) across groups. Group B has a significantly higher share of V2 (Request Missing Documents) and V4 (Language Support). We must determine if V4 is necessary for Group B or if it is triggered by incomplete application data that could be resolved earlier. Compare median cycle times within specific variants (e.g., V4 for Group B vs. V4 for Group A, though Group A rarely uses V4).
*   **Resource-Assignment Comparison:** Filter event logs by `org:resource` to identify specific caseworkers handling Group B vs. Group A cases. Calculate workload variance. If Group B caseworkers have significantly higher case loads or higher overtime rates than Group A counterparts, this indicates resource imbalance.
*   **Waiting-Time Decomposition:** Decompose total cycle time into step-specific durations (e.g., `time:timestamp` difference between "Submit Application" and "Identity Verification"). Isolate the waiting time for specific support steps (Language Support Contact, Accommodation Review). Compare these against the statistical variance of general processing steps to see if support steps are outliers in delay.
*   **Sensitivity Analysis for Possibly Biased Covariates:** Treat `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` as potentially biased inputs (e.g., risk scores may reflect historical enforcement patterns). Run analyses excluding these variables or treating them as endogenous. If adjusted disparities persist without these controls, the risk of false attribution of fairness is lower.
*   **Subgroup Checks by Region and Channel:** Stratify the analysis by `region` and `channel`. The notes indicate "backlog in two regions" and "in-person applications wait longer." Check if Group B disparities are concentrated in specific regions or if the in-person channel creates a compounding disadvantage for Group B (who use it 38% of the time).

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Four mechanisms can explain the observed patterns, ranging from legitimate policy design to structural inequity.

1.  **Resource Capacity Constraints (Legitimate but Poorly Managed):** The 3.8-day wait for Language Support is likely due to a fixed resource pool (7 bilingual staff) against high demand. While the step is a right, the service level is insufficient, causing delay for Group B.
2.  **Historical Bias in Risk Scoring (Concerning):** If `risk_score_band` encodes historical enforcement patterns (e.g., higher scrutiny of minority or lower-income areas), Group D or Group B may be routed to more complex paths (Fraud Review, Supervisor Review) based on biased data, leading to longer times and lower approval rates that are not due to actual fraud risk.
3.  **Documentation Friction (Concerning):** The high share of incomplete documentation for Group B (46%) and the high rate of "Request Missing Documents" suggest that the Completeness Check process may be less effective or more stringent for in-person/minority language applicants. This creates a "churn" effect where cases are delayed while documents are requested and resubmitted.
4.  **Specialization Bottlenecks (Mixed):** Group C cases are assigned to 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also do supervisor review. This dual role likely inflates the Supervisor Review probability for Group C and causes the 4.7-day accommodation wait. This is a structural design flaw where specialized expertise is overburdened, affecting timeliness.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available event log provides a partial view of the fairness landscape. Several attributes are missing or potentially biased.

*   **Measurement Bias:** `documentation_completeness` is a binary or categorical metric that may not capture the nuance of language barriers. An application may be "complete" by policy but impossible for a non-English speaker to fill out accurately, leading to unnecessary rework.
*   **Missing Applicant Needs:** The log does not capture the *subjective* burden felt by the applicant (e.g., stress, confusion). A 4-day wait for accommodation review may be manageable for some but catastrophic for others.
*   **Unobserved Resource Workload:** Resource assignment is observed at daily granularity, but workload intensity (e.g., overtime, case complexity per worker) is not captured. We know Group B workers are "overloaded" but not the degree of this overload relative to Group A.
*   **Appeal Quality:** The `appeal_flag` is binary. It does not capture whether the appeal was successful or the quality of the explanation provided. A high appeal rate could mean the process is unclear (unfair) or that the applicant is highly motivated (neutral).
*   **Historical Bias in Risk Scores:** `risk_score_band` is likely generated by a model or rule set. If that model relies on historical claims data, it may perpetuate past discrimination. Treating it as a neutral control variable in fairness analysis risks masking the source of the disparity.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

Targeted interventions can improve fairness without removing rights-preserving steps.

*   **Adding Capacity for Language Support or Accommodation Review:**
    *   *Option:* Hire additional bilingual caseworkers or accommodation specialists to reduce the 3.8d and 4.7d waits.
    *   *Side Effect:* Increased operational costs. May require renegotiating SLAs with agencies.
*   **Service-Level Targets for Support Steps:**
    *   *Option:* Establish timeliness thresholds specifically for Language Support and Accommodation Review (e.g., 48 hours).
    *   *Side Effect:* May increase pressure on staff, potentially reducing quality if not accompanied by capacity. Risk of "gaming" the system to meet targets (e.g., pre-approving without full review).
*   **Routing Redesign to Reduce Handovers:**
    *   *Option:* Allow bilingual caseworkers to handle both Identity Verification and Language Support, reducing the general  bilingual  general handover chain.
    *   *Side Effect:* Requires training general staff on language protocols or hiring bilingual staff with broader skills. May increase initial training costs.
*   **Decision-Explanation Improvements:**
    *   *Option:* Implement standardized, plain-language explanations for denials and "Request Missing Documents" decisions, specifically tailored for minority languages.
    *   *Side Effect:* Requires investment in translation and UX design. May not reduce appeal rates if the underlying decision logic remains unclear.
*   **Monitoring Adjusted Disparities:**
    *   *Option:* Create a dashboard tracking approval rates and cycle times by group, stratified by the adjusted model.
    *   *Side Effect:* Monitoring alone does not fix disparities. Requires leadership buy-in to act on the data.
*   **Auditing Risk Score Band and Documentation Completeness:**
    *   *Option:* Periodically audit the risk scoring model for bias and review the "completeness" checks to ensure they are not disproportionately flagging minority-language applications.
    *   *Side Effect:* May reveal systemic issues that require policy changes, potentially leading to short-term disruption in routing logic.
