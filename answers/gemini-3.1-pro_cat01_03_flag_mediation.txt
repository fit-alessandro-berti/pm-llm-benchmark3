## 1. Intervention definitions and estimands

| estimand ID | intervention contrast | unit and population | outcome | total/direct/indirect or interventional analogue | well-defined? | main identification obstacle |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| EST-1 | P0 vs P1 | All incoming cases | Total cycle time | Total policy effect | Yes | Interference / queue spillover |
| EST-2 | P0 vs P1 | All incoming cases | Triage completion time | Direct effect analogue | Yes | Interference / queue spillover |
| EST-3 | P0 vs P2 | All incoming cases | Total cycle time | Separable indirect effect (occurrence) | Yes | Unobserved risk confounding |
| EST-4 | P0 vs P3 | All incoming cases | Total cycle time | Separable indirect effect (waiting) | Yes | Unobserved risk confounding |
| EST-5 | P0 vs P4 | Cases reaching review | Total cycle time | Separable indirect effect (escalation) | Yes | Collider bias at review entry |
| EST-6 | P0 vs P1 | All incoming cases | Cycle time | Natural direct effect | No | Cross-world independence |
| EST-7 | P0 vs P1 | All incoming cases | Final decision outcome | Total policy effect | Yes | Unobserved decision quality |

I use the **separable effects** framework rather than natural direct/indirect effects. Natural direct and indirect effects require cross-world independence assumptions (e.g., assuming a case's counterfactual triage wait is completely independent of its counterfactual manual review wait), which are structurally violated in queuing systems because altering treatment shifts shared resource availability. Separable effects map to distinct, manipulable policy components (e.g., removing the flag display vs. dropping the manual review mandate) without demanding untestable cross-world counterfactuals.

Interference heavily influences this system because triage, manual review, and escalation utilize shared resource queues. Prioritizing flagged cases directly reduces their `triage_wait` but inherently inflates `triage_wait` for unflagged cases. Additionally, mandating manual review for flagged cases drastically increases `manual_queue_wait` for everyone entering the queue. Exposure-induced confounding occurs because the flag (treatment) affects both `triage_wait` and `manual_review` occurrence, which sequentially alter the overall queue state—a critical confounder for downstream wait times. Treatment-confounder feedback is structurally present because the system's queue length at any given moment is determined by past cases' treatments and confounds future cases' downstream wait times.

For cases where a stage does not occur (e.g., no manual review), wait and service times are treated as structurally absent (`NA`) and excluded from median stage calculations. Residual-to-close measures the time from the completion of the last recorded event to case closure.

The observed contrasts reveal that flagged cases experience substantially shorter `arrival-to-triage-start` delays (median 12 vs 150 minutes) but dramatically longer `triage-done-to-manual-start` delays (405 vs 105 minutes) and total cycle times (987 vs 345 minutes). While these contrasts are consistent with acceleration at triage followed by severe downstream delay, they are not automatically causal. Flagged cases have inherently higher underlying risk and complexity, naturally requiring more scrutiny and prolonging review regardless of the flag. Furthermore, the priority lane forces flagged cases into the manual review queue almost simultaneously, inducing massive shared-queue congestion that inflates waiting times. Without adjusting for this shared-queue interference and unobserved risk confounding, naive subgroup differences overstate the causal delay directly attributable to the flag policy.

## 5. Identification strategy and falsification

| question | proposed design or analysis | adjustment or stratification set | assumptions | diagnostic or falsification test | result that would change your conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Effect of flag on triage | within-risk-score or local-threshold design | Cases narrowly around the flag threshold | Continuity of unobserved risk at threshold | Density of risk score at threshold | Severe discontinuity in risk score density |
| Effect of system load | queue-state-aware analysis | arrival_load, time of day | Exogenous variation in overall load | Placebo test on past, already-completed cases | No effect of load on historical wait times |
| Overall policy impact | policy-change or randomized encouragement design | Pre/post policy period | Parallel trends in counterfactual outcomes | Trends of events pre-intervention | Divergent pre-trends in the pre-policy era |
| Pathway isolation | mediation-oriented analysis (separable effects) | complexity, risk_score | Sequential ignorability | Robustness to unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding | High sensitivity to unmeasured confounders |
| Spurious correlations | negative-control outcome or exposure | Case demographic variables | Demographics don't alter underlying process | Association test with demographics | Demographics strongly predict wait times |
| Hidden risk impact | sensitivity analysis for unobserved risk | risk_score, complexity | Bounded influence of U_unobserved_risk | Calculate E-value for unmeasured confounding | A very small E-value easily overturning results |
| Queue spillover | interference-aware design | Concurrent case mix in shared queue | Stable unit treatment value | Variance in individual outcome by queue mix | Wait outcomes perfectly invariant to queue mix |

## 6. Policy comparison

| policy | expected triage effect | expected downstream queue effect | expected cycle-time effect | quality/compliance risk | capacity spillover | evidence strength | recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P0 | Delay for Flag 0 | Severe congestion | Extremely long for Flag 1 | Low | High | Strong | Do not recommend |
| P1 | Equalized across cases | Marginally reduced | Modestly reduced | High | Low | Moderate | Do not recommend |
| P2 | Fast for Flag 1 | Vastly reduced congestion | Substantially reduced | Moderate | Low | Moderate | Recommended for pilot |
| P3 | Fast for Flag 1 | Modestly reduced | Moderately reduced | Low | Medium | Weak | Too resource-intensive |
| P4 | Fast for Flag 1 | High manual, low escalation | Slightly reduced | High | High | Weak | Not recommended |

We recommend piloting **P2** (retaining priority triage but removing automatic mandatory manual review).

**Eligibility:** All new cases entering the system during the designated pilot window.  
**Randomization or assignment rule:** Due to intense shared-queue interference, we will use a cluster-randomized crossover design (e.g., randomizing operational days) rather than case-level randomization. Half the days will run the current baseline (P0), and half will run the pilot policy (P2), preserving realistic queue dynamics.  
**Queue-capacity guardrail:** If the backlog of unprocessed flagged cases exceeds a safety threshold of 50 active cases, the pilot temporarily suspends to clear the bottleneck using priority resources.  
**Primary and secondary outcomes:** The primary outcome is overall total cycle time. Secondary outcomes include decision quality (tracked via rejection rates and downstream compliance audits) and `triage-done-to-manual-start` wait times.  
**Minimum process data needed:** Case ID, arrival timestamp, triage start/end, manual review start/end, and final decision outcome.  
**Stopping conditions:** The pilot will immediately terminate if the overall rejection rate drops by more than 15% (indicating unacceptably high risk leakage) or if cycle times inexplicably lengthen beyond P0 baselines.

## 8. Reversal conditions

1. **Hidden risk**: If high `risk_score` cases universally contain completely invisible risk factors requiring extensive, unstructured deliberation, the manual-review bottleneck is a natural consequence of genuine case difficulty, directly reversing the recommendation to ease manual review.
2. **Queue interference**: If staff dynamically abandon unflagged cases mid-process to preemptively address newly arriving flagged cases without accurately recording preemption in the event log, the observed shared-queue interference is systematically undercounted, reversing our interpretation of triage efficiency.
3. **Decision quality**: If the mandatory manual review reliably catches and prevents severe downstream compliance failures that automated triage inherently misses, the massive cycle time delay is a completely justified trade-off, reversing any recommendation to remove the mandatory review rule.
4. **Timestamp semantics**: If the `triage_start` timestamp merely records the exact moment an automated script assigns the case to an active human queue rather than true human cognitive engagement, the priority lane's remarkable speed is a semantic artifact, reversing the conclusion that triage is meaningfully accelerated.
5. **Positivity or lack of overlap**: If there are structurally zero historical cases with `risk_score > 0.8` that managed to bypass manual review, estimating counterfactual wait times under P2 becomes impossible due to lack of overlap, completely invalidating our cycle-time improvement projections.
6. **Behavior change by reviewers**: If reviewers subconsciously handle unflagged cases with far less diligence simply because they inherently trust the new flag triage mechanism (automation bias), the observed cycle-time contrast is driven by gradual reviewer behavioral adaptation rather than the actual explicit policy rule, breaking the presumed mechanism structure.

## 9. Final synthesis

Directly observed: Flagged cases experience highly accelerated triage start times (a median of 12 minutes compared to 150 minutes for unflagged cases) but paradoxically exhibit much longer total cycle times (987 minutes versus 345 minutes) and massive wait times bridging between triage and manual review.

Plausible mechanism: The flag places cases into a designated priority lane, successfully speeding up their first touch. However, the accompanying operational policy mandates comprehensive manual review for virtually all flagged cases. This heavily floods the sharply limited manual review queue. The apparent overall delay for flagged cases is primarily a mediated effect of bottlenecking at the manual review stage and shared-queue interference, rather than a direct negative effect of the flag parameter itself.

Remains unidentified: We cannot definitively untangle exactly how much of the measured delay is purely due to the mandatory review policy versus the underlying case complexity (hidden risk confounding) and resource-allocation efficiency, given the clear violation of stable unit treatment values inherent in shared queues.

Next evidence: Intervening with a cluster-randomized pilot of policy P2 (retaining priority triage but proactively removing the automatic mandate for manual review) provides the absolute most useful evidence. It severs the structural link between the flag and the manual queue, directly testing if we can retain the triage speed benefit without paying the downstream congestion penalty, all while closely monitoring vital decision quality constraints.
