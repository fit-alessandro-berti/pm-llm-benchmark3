# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

- Intake automation and entitlement precheck: Nora's core is only A1 and A2, but at extreme volume. That should be read as an automation boundary role, not as evidence that the bot is the largest operational service team. The lack of peripheral work is itself important: Nora does not diagnose, negotiate, approve, or close, so the role has throughput but no discretionary authority.
- Permanent front-office service agents: Amira and Ben are defined by high-frequency entitlement validation, standard response, and closure. Their peripheral work matters because it shows bounded discretion: they can absorb some diagnosis, negotiation, and even occasional implementation, but the complexity profile stays mostly low/medium and formal authority-bearing work remains scarce. Amira's slightly higher complexity mix suggests a more experienced generalist tier inside the same formal role.
- Temporary front-office surge agents: Kai and Lina perform the same visible routine steps as front-office staff, but only on very simple cases and with almost no adjacent diagnostic or escalation work. The reopened cases linked to incomplete validation notes show that temporary substitution expanded capacity but did not replicate the tacit quality and documentation discipline of permanent agents. Their activity similarity to the front office is therefore operational, not capability-equivalent.
- Senior front-office agent with commercial shadow coverage: Giorgio's core remains front-office service handling, but his repeated pricing and negotiation events are too frequent to call random noise. The substitution episode is decisive: he can cover commercial work, yet slower cycle time shows that temporary coverage is not the same as owning the specialty. His authority is intermediate; he can carry complex customer-facing work and shadow pricing, but the evidence does not prove that he should be treated as the primary commercial approver.
- Technical resolution analysts: Chen and Dina are clearly anchored in diagnose-configure-implement work. Their peripheral A11/A14/A13 touches fit an end-to-end resolution role rather than a narrow engineering silo. Complexity separates them: Chen's mix is materially more high/critical and more security-adjacent, so the same formal role likely contains a senior-subspecialist layer that a pure role-mining algorithm would flatten.
- Security specialist: Erik's core is unmistakably A6 Security Review. His occasional diagnosis, implementation, and reopen work should be interpreted as specialist intervention inside critical cases, not as proof that he is a general technical analyst. Frequency alone understates the role because the cases are rarer but materially riskier, and the weeks 7-8 wait times show the cost of assuming referral filtering can substitute for scarce specialist capacity.
- Commercial exceptions specialist: Fatima's core is pricing judgment and negotiation. Her peripheral validation, closure, and approval-related events look like exception-case management around that nucleus. Complexity is well above routine front-office work, and the week 3-4 absence shows that the organization depends on this specialty enough to activate shadow coverage.
- Supervisor and formal approval authority: Hana's core is A9. Her peripheral participation in negotiation, reopen handling, and some quality or diagnostic touchpoints should be read as governance around difficult cases, not as ordinary execution. The recheck of Mateo's preliminary approvals shows that similar activities do not imply equal authority: Hana's role includes final accountability that brokerage roles do not have.
- Quality assurance and knowledge stewardship: Ivan and Julia are defined by high-frequency quality checks and knowledge-base updates, with peripheral technical and closure activity that supports a gatekeeping role. Their case complexity is moderate rather than trivial, which fits a control function positioned after technical work. The authority is process-quality authority rather than customer or commercial authority.
- Incident coordination and escalation brokerage: Mateo's core is not a single activity code but a pattern of routing, follow-up, and cross-team orchestration, especially around reopens and escalations. His peripheral touches across many activities reflect brokerage, not lack of specialization. Temporary substitution into preliminary approvals shows resilience value, but the later Hana rechecks show that coordination authority and formal approval authority must be separated.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

- R07_Giorgio as commercial shadow role: Giorgio looks like a deliberate resilience mechanism. He bridges front office and commercial exceptions, especially on complex customer-facing cases, and he can keep work moving when Fatima is absent. The benefit is continuity and reduced single-point dependency. The risk is that observed substitution can be mistaken for full equivalence; the sharp increase in pricing exception duration shows that shadow coverage is real but partial.
- R13_Mateo as cross-functional escalation broker: Mateo is the clearest bridging role in the log. He connects reopens, security referrals, specialist routing, and preliminary approval coordination across multiple teams. The benefit is lower false escalation noise and better orchestration of complex cases. The risk is organizational ambiguity: because he touches many activities, mining can overstate his formal authority or incorrectly position him as a supervisor rather than a broker.
- R05_Erik as rare but critical specialist: Erik is not a bridge because he works broadly; he is a bridge because certain cases cannot safely pass from technical or coordination roles to closure without his intervention. The benefit is concentrated expertise and risk control on the hardest security cases. The risk is that low event volume may tempt management to under-resource the role even though delay or absence has disproportionate impact.
- R14_Nora as an automation boundary role: Nora bridges the customer/intake boundary and the human organization. The benefit is high-volume standardization of registration and initial entitlement precheck. The risk is analytical distortion: a discovery algorithm can assign the bot to a human intake role if analysts ignore that automated work has different competence, authority, and failure modes.
- R09_Ivan and R10_Julia as quality gatekeepers: These resources bridge technical delivery and organizational learning. They are not only inspectors; their A14 work makes them part of the feedback loop that converts recurring defects and reopen patterns into knowledge updates. The benefit is defect containment and process learning. The risk is that if they are treated as generic back-office capacity, the organization may weaken the control point that keeps technical speed from degrading service quality.
- R11_Kai and R12_Lina as temporary workforce boundary roles: The temps bridge demand spikes and routine front-office throughput. The benefit is load absorption on low-complexity work. The risk is that similar activity profiles can hide the fact that temporary coverage may not carry the same note quality, escalation judgment, or reopen prevention as the permanent front office.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

```python
import pandas as pd
import pm4py


def flatten_network(net, value_name):
    rows = []
    for src, targets in net.items():
        for dst, weight in targets.items():
            rows.append({"source": src, "target": dst, value_name: weight})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


def normalize_roles(discovered_roles):
    """
    PM4Py organizational-role output can vary by version.
    This helper accepts either:
    - iterable of (activities, resources)
    - iterable of dict-like objects with 'activities' and 'resources'/'members'
    """
    rows = []
    for idx, role in enumerate(discovered_roles, start=1):
        if isinstance(role, dict):
            activities = role.get("activities", [])
            members = role.get("resources", role.get("members", []))
        else:
            activities, members = role
        rows.append(
            {
                "role_id": f"role_{idx}",
                "activities": tuple(sorted(activities)),
                "members": tuple(sorted(members)),
            }
        )
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)


# 1. Load and format the event log dataframe
df = pd.read_csv("event_log.csv")
df["time:timestamp"] = pd.to_datetime(df["time:timestamp"], utc=True)

log_df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
)
log_df = log_df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp"]).reset_index(drop=True)


# 2. Optional timing enrichment to study substitution and escalation effects
# Useful for questions like: did Giorgio's shadow coverage slow pricing work?
# Or: did critical security cases wait longer when Erik's capacity was constrained?
log_df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(
    log_df,
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    activity_key="concept:name",
)

service_time_by_activity = pm4py.get_service_time(
    log_df,
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
)


# 3. Discover behavior-based organizational roles
org_roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(
    log_df,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    activity_key="concept:name",
)
roles_df = normalize_roles(org_roles)


# 4. Discover activity-profile similarity
activity_similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    log_df,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    activity_key="concept:name",
)

# Depending on PM4Py version this may already be a DataFrame-like object.
# If it is a dict keyed by (resource_1, resource_2), flatten it.
if isinstance(activity_similarity, dict):
    sim_rows = []
    for pair, score in activity_similarity.items():
        if isinstance(pair, tuple) and len(pair) == 2:
            r1, r2 = pair
            sim_rows.append(
                {"resource_1": r1, "resource_2": r2, "cosine_similarity": score}
            )
    similarity_df = pd.DataFrame(sim_rows)
else:
    similarity_df = pd.DataFrame(activity_similarity)


# 5. Inspect relation networks instead of relying only on activity counts
handover_net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(
    log_df,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
)
working_together_net = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    log_df,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
)
subcontracting_net = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(
    log_df,
    resource_key="org:resource",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    case_id_key="case:concept:name",
)

handover_df = flatten_network(handover_net, "handover_weight")
working_together_df = flatten_network(working_together_net, "working_together_weight")
subcontracting_df = flatten_network(subcontracting_net, "subcontracting_weight")


# 6. Join role-discovery output with complexity, escalation, and substitution context
resource_stats = (
    log_df.groupby("org:resource")
    .agg(
        total_events=("concept:name", "size"),
        distinct_cases=("case:concept:name", "nunique"),
        median_case_complexity=("case_complexity", "median"),
        low_complexity_share=("case_complexity", lambda s: (s < 0.33).mean()),
        high_complexity_share=("case_complexity", lambda s: (s >= 0.66).mean()),
        temp_substitution_share=(
            "temporary_substitution",
            lambda s: pd.Series(s).fillna(False).astype(bool).mean(),
        ),
        escalation_events=("escalation_level", lambda s: s.notna().sum()),
    )
    .reset_index()
)

# Authority-bearing activities should be separated from merely similar work.
authority_activities = {
    "Security Review",
    "Pricing Exception Review",
    "Supervisor Approval",
}

authority_profile = (
    log_df.assign(is_authority_activity=log_df["concept:name"].isin(authority_activities))
    .groupby("org:resource")
    .agg(
        authority_event_share=("is_authority_activity", "mean"),
        authority_event_count=("is_authority_activity", "sum"),
    )
    .reset_index()
)

roles_exploded = roles_df.explode("members").rename(columns={"members": "org:resource"})

resource_role_view = (
    resource_stats.merge(roles_exploded, on="org:resource", how="left")
    .merge(authority_profile, on="org:resource", how="left")
)


# 7. Targeted slices to test shadow-role hypotheses
temp_sub_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log_df,
    "temporary_substitution",
    [True],
    level="event",
)

enterprise_security_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log_df,
    "product_line",
    ["Enterprise Security"],
    level="case",
)

variants_all = pm4py.get_variants(log_df)
variants_temp_sub = pm4py.get_variants(temp_sub_log)
variants_enterprise_security = pm4py.get_variants(enterprise_security_log)


# 8. Optional exploratory clustering: useful, but not authoritative
# clusters = pm4py.cluster_log(log_df, activity_key="concept:name")
# Do not equate a cluster with a formal role. Always review:
# - median case complexity
# - authority-bearing activity share
# - substitution indicators
# - handover and working-together position


# 9. Example guardrails against over-interpretation
shadow_candidates = similarity_df.merge(
    resource_role_view.add_prefix("r1_"),
    left_on="resource_1",
    right_on="r1_org:resource",
    how="left",
).merge(
    resource_role_view.add_prefix("r2_"),
    left_on="resource_2",
    right_on="r2_org:resource",
    how="left",
)

shadow_candidates = shadow_candidates[
    (shadow_candidates["cosine_similarity"] >= 0.70)
    & (
        (
            shadow_candidates["r1_median_case_complexity"]
            - shadow_candidates["r2_median_case_complexity"]
        ).abs()
        > 0.15
    )
    | (
        (
            shadow_candidates["r1_authority_event_share"]
            - shadow_candidates["r2_authority_event_share"]
        ).abs()
        > 0.05
    )
]

print(resource_role_view.sort_values(["role_id", "median_case_complexity"]))
print(shadow_candidates[["resource_1", "resource_2", "cosine_similarity"]].head(20))
print(handover_df.sort_values("handover_weight", ascending=False).head(20))

# Interpretation rule:
# If two resources look similar by activity frequency but differ materially in
# complexity handled, authority-bearing events, or only overlap during temporary
# substitution, classify them as potential shadow/bridge relationships rather
# than automatically assigning the same formal organizational role.
```

# 6. Operational interpretation

- Staffing: treat the front office as at least three layers, not one: automation intake, permanent human front-office agents, and temporary surge capacity. If management staffs from a single mined "front-office cluster," it will overestimate what temps and the bot can safely absorb.
- Training: formalize Giorgio's backup path for pricing exceptions and Dina's path toward higher-complexity technical work, but do so with explicit competence thresholds. The data supports cross-training, not automatic equivalence with Fatima or Chen.
- Risk control: protect Erik's capacity and define escalation filters carefully. Mateo's referral filtering reduced false escalations, which is good, but the critical waits show that efficiency gains can coexist with dangerous bottlenecks if scarce specialist time is not reserved for urgent cases.
- Governance: keep Hana's approval authority explicit even if Mateo continues to perform preliminary approval work. Similar event traces do not justify collapsing coordination and sign-off into one role without formal delegation and control redesign.
- Resilience: use Ivan and Julia as a learning loop, not just a checkpoint. The reopen pattern from temp-handled cases suggests that KB updates, QA feedback, and entitlement-note standards should be directly tied to staffing changes and onboarding.
- Caution 1: mined roles are descriptions of observed work allocation, not proof of formal authority, certification scope, or accountability boundaries. A resource can perform an activity during substitution and still not be the right permanent owner of that responsibility.
- Caution 2: low-frequency roles can be operationally decisive. If staffing decisions are based only on event volume or cluster size, security and supervisory control roles will be undervalued precisely because they intervene only when risk is high.
- Caution 3: high similarity does not imply interchangeable performance. Giorgio versus Fatima and permanent front office versus temps both show that similar activity vectors can hide different speed, quality, and risk outcomes.
