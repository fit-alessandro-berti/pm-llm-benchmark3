## 1. Executive memo

**To:** Executive Committee
**From:** Process Excellence Office
**Re:** Fiber-installation Redesign R — next-phase decision

**Recommendation:** Adopt a segmented hybrid (Alternative E). Scale Redesign R only for simple low-risk houses; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation for apartments and old buildings; add conformance gates; and run targeted measurement before any wider rollout. Do not scale nationally. Do not fully roll back.

The evidence is genuinely two-sided. Redesign R cut median cycle time from 14.2 to 10.8 days and reduced cases with 3+ handovers from 34% to 12%. For simple low-risk houses (34% of pilot volume), it is a clear win: median 6.0 days, p90 8.0 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints 1pp. Coordinator ownership and the merged validate/schedule step are valuable and should be retained where they work.

But the redesign also shifted work into rework and hidden cost. The tail worsened sharply (p90 31.038.5 days; p95 45.062.0). First-time-right fell 82%76%, complaints rose 6.1%9.8%, second visits 11%18%, manual activation 7%15%, and SLA breaches 13%16%. Visible cost fell (€184€161), but estimated full cost rose (€198€207) with downstream complaint handling excluded after week 4. Process mining confirms the mechanism: the pilot model has high fitness (0.94) but low precision (0.51), meaning the process is less standardized and permits many unobserved combinations. Alignments show billing start before successful activation, repeated installation without field survey, missing permit checks for older apartments, and complaints after billing start. Segment data pinpoints the harm: apartments/medium-risk (p90 +12 days, FTR 8pp, complaints +5pp) and old permit-sensitive buildings (p90 +25 days, FTR 11pp, complaints +7pp) deteriorated because the survey-skip rule is often wrong and permit checks are missing.

The trade-off is therefore not speed versus quality in general; it is speed for simple cases versus rework and compliance-like risk for complex cases. National scaling (A) would amplify known harms and overload coordinators (already 93% utilized in two regions). Full rollback (B) would discard proven benefits for simple houses and the handover reduction. Scaling only coordinator ownership (C) is directionally right but insufficient: it does not restore permit checks, enforce activation-before-billing, or address coordinator resilience. A pure experiment (D) is valuable but delays benefits and does not protect customers in the interim.

The hybrid (E) captures proven gains while containing harms. Immediately: route simple low-risk houses through R; require field survey and permit checks for apartments/old buildings; enforce activation-test success before billing start; deploy conformance monitoring for billing-before-activation and permit absence; add structured reason codes and start timestamps. Pause: survey skipping for non-simple segments and any post-billing activation. Measure: full downstream cost for 60 days (M4), a randomized survey-skip trial by building-risk stratum (M1), and validation of the building-risk rule (M6).

Broader rollout should be gated on: p90  31 days for apartments/old buildings; first-time-right  82% overall; complaints  6.1%; billing-before-activation < 1%; permit checks  99% for old buildings; full downstream cost  €198; coordinator utilization  85% with backup coverage. If these hold for 8 consecutive weeks, scale nationally. If full cost remains worse or segment harms persist despite controls, roll back the coordinator model for complex segments and retain only the merged validate/schedule step.

## 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

### 3.1 High fitness but low precision

Fitness measures how much of the observed behavior the model explains; precision measures how much behavior the model allows that was **not** observed. The pilot model has high fitness (0.94) because most pilot cases follow the simplified coordinator path, and the model captures those traces well. But precision is low (0.51) because the model also permits many combinations that were not actually observed—skipped surveys, manual activations, second visits, and billing starts in varying orders. The DFG corroborates this: new edges appear (Address validation  Combined validate/schedule at 38%) while survey edges shrink (Appointment scheduling  Field survey 31%18%), producing a simplified but more variable process. In process-mining terms, the pilot process is **underconstrained**: it fits the data but is not standardized. The baseline model (fitness 0.87, precision 0.79, generalization 0.70) was more precise and better generalizing. Low precision is a warning sign for scaling: a process that allows too many unobserved combinations is harder to control, audit, and predict, and the discovered model cannot reliably distinguish normal from deviant behavior.

### 3.2 Improved median but worse tail duration

The median is robust to outliers; p90/p95 capture the worst cases. Redesign R compressed the typical path (merged validate/schedule, coordinator ownership), so the median case finished faster. But it also created rework loops that hit a minority of cases very hard. The DFG shows the mechanism: Installation visit  Failed visit rose 13%19%, Failed visit  Second visit 10%17%, Installation visit  Manual activation 8%19%, and Billing start  Complaint escalation 3%8%. The temporal profile adds that Failed visit  Second visit delay increased in regions with high coordinator utilization, and Billing start  Complaint escalation became a new long-tail relation. The result is a more polarized distribution: many cases faster, a substantial minority much slower. The tail metrics capture the harm that the median hides. The 6-week pilot window may also right-censor some long cases, but the measured p90/p95 deterioration on completed cases is consistent with the rework-loop mechanism.

### 3.3 Fewer handovers but lower resilience

Handover count fell (34%12% with 3+ handovers) because a single coordinator owns each case. Structurally, the handover-of-work network became simpler. But the working-together network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. Context and case knowledge became concentrated in individuals rather than distributed across a team. When a coordinator was absent, cases stalled because context was not shared. So the reduction in handovers improved accountability and speed for the normal path but removed redundancy and slack—lower resilience. Coordinator utilization at 93% in two pilot regions confirms there is no spare capacity to absorb absence or surges. The metric conflict is thus structural: fewer handovers is a gain in efficiency and accountability, but the same centralization is a loss in organizational resilience.

### 3.4 Lower visible cost but higher rework/downstream cost

Visible cost (€184€161) captures only the direct process steps measured before complaint handling. The redesign shifted work downstream: more second visits (11%18%), manual activations (7%15%), and complaints (6.1%9.8%). These generate costs that are either excluded (complaint handling after week 4) or fall outside the measured cost boundary. The estimated full cost (€198€207) is worse but incomplete. Resource data corroborates the shift: field-survey team utilization fell to 52% (idle capacity) while second-visit team utilization rose to 92% (bottleneck). So the process looks cheaper on the visible ledger but moves cost into rework, downstream complaint handling, and team bottlenecks. The metric conflict is a boundary problem: the visible cost metric improves while the full system cost worsens because the redesign externalizes work into unmeasured or downstream activities.

### 3.5 Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings

The redesign's simplifications are well matched to simple, low-risk houses: merged validate/schedule works when the address is straightforward, and no field survey or permit check is needed. The temporal profile confirms Address validation  Appointment scheduling improved for simple houses. For apartments/medium-risk, the predictive skip rule is often wrong—the survey would have caught issues—so first-time-right falls (8pp) and complaints rise (+5pp). For old permit-sensitive buildings, permit checks are missing (a compliance-like gap), producing the worst tail (+25 days p90) and complaint increase (+7pp). The temporal profile also shows Installation visit  Activation test worsened for apartments. The aggregate metrics blend these opposing segment effects. The pilot regions also had more apartments/old buildings than baseline, so part of the aggregate deterioration is compositional, but the segment-level data confirms the effects are real within segments, not just mix shift.

## 4. Decision alternatives

### A. Scale Redesign R nationally

- **Advantages:** Captures median cycle-time gains (3.4 days) and handover reduction (34%12%) at scale; realizes visible cost savings (€23/case); benefits the simple-house segment (34% of volume) immediately; coordinator accountability model is simpler to manage.
- **Risks:** Scales known harms—tail duration (p90 +7.5 days, p95 +17 days), first-time-right (6pp), complaints (+3.7pp), second visits (+7pp), manual activation (+8pp), SLA breaches (+3pp); amplifies compliance-like issues (billing before activation, missing permit checks for old buildings); coordinator utilization already 93% in two regions—national scale would cause burnout, attrition, and case stalls; low model precision (0.51) means the process is not standardized enough to control at scale; pilot regions not representative (more apartments/old buildings); data gaps (cost, attributes, timestamps) unresolved.
- **Conditions under which reasonable:** Only after segment controls and conformance gates are in place, full downstream cost is confirmed favorable, coordinator capacity is expanded, and the pilot is replicated in representative regions. Not reasonable now.

### B. Roll back to the baseline process

- **Advantages:** Eliminates new harms—complaints, second visits, manual activation, billing-before-activation, missing permit checks; restores field-survey control and permit compliance; avoids coordinator burnout and resilience problems; returns to a more precise (0.79) and better-generalizing (0.70) process model.
- **Risks:** Discards proven benefits—median 3.4 days, handover reduction, simple-house improvements (median 6.0 days, FTR +3pp, complaints 1pp); loses visible cost savings; leaves field-survey team at 52% utilization (idle capacity) while second-visit team was already strained; may demoralize pilot teams and signal failure; the baseline itself had fitness 0.87 and generalization 0.70—not perfect.
- **Conditions under which reasonable:** If measurement shows full downstream cost is decisively worse and cannot be fixed by segment controls; if compliance risk (billing before activation, missing permits) is unacceptable to regulators or customers; if the coordinator model cannot be made resilient. Not justified solely by worsened metrics, given segment-specific benefits.

### C. Scale only the coordinator-ownership part; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation

- **Advantages:** Keeps the most structurally valuable change (coordinator ownership, handover reduction, accountability); removes the two most damaging changes (survey skip, post-billing activation); simpler to implement than a full hybrid; addresses the biggest segment harms; retains median gains for simple cases.
- **Risks:** Coordinator utilization already 93%—scaling ownership without adding capacity or resilience mechanisms may worsen stalls and burnout; does not by itself restore permit checks for old buildings (permit check is separate from survey skip—an explicit control is needed); activation specialists still report unclear ownership after billing start; does not resolve measurement gaps (cost, attributes, timestamps); may not capture full median gains if merged validate/schedule is also suspended.
- **Conditions under which reasonable:** If coordinator capacity can be expanded and backup/shared-context mechanisms added; if survey skip and post-billing activation are confirmed as the primary harm drivers; if permit checks are explicitly enforced for old buildings. A strong candidate but incomplete alone.

### D. Run a controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement

- **Advantages:** Resolves causal uncertainties (randomized survey skip by stratum, full downstream cost); avoids scaling on confounded data; enables segment-stratified inference; builds defensible evidence for a national decision; can test coordinator resilience (M5).
- **Risks:** Delays benefits for the simple-house segment by 8–12 weeks; a 6-week pilot may still not capture 60-day downstream cost; experimental regions may not represent the national mix; opportunity cost of not scaling proven parts; customers in complex segments remain exposed to harms during the experiment unless interim controls are added.
- **Conditions under which reasonable:** If the decision timeline allows 8–12 weeks; if leadership requires causal certainty before national commitment; if current data gaps are material. Should be combined with interim controls (hybrid), not run as a pure experiment without protecting customers.

### E. Hybrid: deploy R only for simple low-risk houses; add controls for apartments/old buildings; run measurement before wider scale

- **Advantages:** Captures proven benefits where the redesign works (simple houses: median 6.0, p90 8.0, FTR +3pp, complaints 1pp); contains harms by restricting risky changes (survey skip, post-billing activation) for complex segments; adds controls (permit checks, activation-before-billing gate, conformance monitoring); measurement before wider scale reduces risk; aligns with segment-level evidence and process-mining findings; preserves coordinator ownership where it is sustainable.
- **Risks:** Operational complexity of segment-dependent routing; requires reliable building-risk classification early (currently incomplete before week 3); coordinator utilization may remain high if simple houses dominate volume; may create scheduling complexity or perceived inequity; does not immediately resolve full cost uncertainty; a hybrid may be harder to govern than a single standard process.
- **Conditions under which reasonable:** If building-risk attributes can be completed at intake (before week 3); if segment routing is operationally feasible; if leadership accepts a staged rollout with gates. **This is the recommended option.**

## 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision:** Adopt **Alternative E (segmented hybrid)**, incorporating elements of C. Do not scale nationally (A). Do not fully roll back (B). Do not run a pure experiment without interim controls (D alone).

**Immediate action (next 2 weeks):**
- Route simple low-risk houses through Redesign R: coordinator ownership + merged address validation/appointment scheduling.
- Require field survey and permit checks for apartments/medium-risk and old permit-sensitive buildings. Suspend the predictive survey-skip rule for these segments.
- Enforce a hard gate: **no Billing start before successful Activation test**. Reverse the post-billing activation change.
- Deploy conformance monitoring (M7) for billing-before-activation and permit-check absence, with alerts.
- Add structured reason codes (M3) for Failed visit, Manual activation, Complaint escalation, and Second visit.
- Instrument start timestamps (M2) for Installation visit, Activation test, and Manual activation.

**What to pause:**
- Survey skipping for apartments/medium-risk and old buildings (until M1/M6 validate the rule).
- Any activation check moved after billing start.
- Any national scaling of Redesign R.
- Further expansion of coordinator caseloads beyond current levels until utilization  85% and backup coverage exists.

**What to scale:**
- Coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule for **simple low-risk houses only**.
- Conformance monitoring and reason-code capture across all regions (cheap, low-risk).

**What to measure next (in priority order):**
1. **M4:** Full downstream cost for 60 days after Billing start (resolves true economics).
2. **M1:** Randomized survey-skip trial by building-risk stratum (resolves causal effect of the skip rule).
3. **M6:** Validate the building-risk predictive rule against field-survey outcomes (resolves whether the skip rule can be fixed).
4. **M3:** Reason codes (already started; analyze weekly).
5. **M5:** Coordinator ownership vs shared-team under absence (resolves resilience).
6. **M2:** Start timestamps (already started; improves future process mining).

**Thresholds/evidence that would justify broader rollout:**
Broader (national or multi-segment) rollout of Redesign R should be approved only when **all** of the following hold for 8 consecutive weeks:
- p90 cycle time  31 days for apartments/old buildings (baseline level), and  38.5 days overall with a downward trend.
- First-time-right  82% overall and  80% for apartments/old buildings.
- Complaint rate within 30 days  6.1% overall; no segment above baseline + 1pp.
- Billing-before-activation conformance violations < 1% of cases (target zero).
- Permit check present for  99% of old permit-sensitive buildings.
- Full downstream cost (60-day)  €198 per case (baseline), or visible cost savings exceed downstream rework cost by a pre-agreed margin.
- Coordinator utilization  85% with documented backup coverage; no case stalled > 2 days during coordinator absence.
- SLA breach  13% overall.
- NPS not more than 2 points below baseline (+21).

If these thresholds are met, scale nationally with segment routing. If full downstream cost remains worse or segment harms persist despite controls, roll back the coordinator model for complex segments and retain only the merged validate/schedule step for simple houses.
