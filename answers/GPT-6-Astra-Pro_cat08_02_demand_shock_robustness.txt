## 1. Stress scenarios

**The redesign should control end-to-end workload, not maximize the output of each station independently.** It must protect Urgent deadlines without allowing Routine and Complex-panel work to accumulate indefinitely, and it must preserve required validation even when deadlines become infeasible.

The demand change is more severe than the overall volume increase suggests. Calculated from the supplied summaries, weekly arrivals increase from approximately **2,300 to 3,300 orders, or 43.5%**. However:

| Case type            | Normal orders/week | Peak orders/week | Approximate change |
| -------------------- | -----------------: | ---------------: | -----------------: |
| Routine              |              1,403 |            1,386 |                −1% |
| Urgent               |                414 |              957 |              +131% |
| Complex-panel        |                299 |              660 |              +121% |
| Repeat-after-failure |                184 |              297 |               +61% |

Thus, Routine demand is almost unchanged, while Urgent and Complex-panel arrivals more than double. Their combined arrival rate increases approximately **2.27-fold**. This is an order-volume proxy, not a medical-validator workload multiplier: assay counts, review effort, and staffing hours are not supplied. 

The historical median is already an inadequate success criterion. Normal Urgent p90 is 19 hours against an 18-hour contractual threshold; during peaks, the 17-hour median conceals a 33-hour p90. Routine p90 rises from 68 to 120 hours. 

The following scenarios should be evaluated separately and in combination. Apart from the observed peak mix and scheduled specialist absence, the disruption combinations and durations below are **stress-test assumptions**, not reported incidents.

| Scenario                                  | Demand composition                                                            | Disruption assumption                                                                                   | Principal stress                                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **S1 — Mix-driven winter peak**           | Observed peak volume and mix: 29% Urgent, 20% Complex-panel                   | Equipment and staff nominally available                                                                 | More deadline-sensitive work, multiple-assay work, and medical-review demand; Routine workload remains substantial |
| **S2 — Assay degradation or outage**      | Test both normal and peak mixes                                               | M2 performance deteriorates, or either machine is unavailable for one shift                             | Loss of compatible assay capacity; repeat workload; competition between reliable and fast processing               |

The scheduled courier waves, M2 reliability concern, specialist absence, and resource constraints come directly from the supplied evidence. 

**Operational assumptions requiring confirmation:** For the proposed controls, use registration as the provisional SLA clock origin because the contractual origin is unspecified. Replace it with the actual contractual definition before deployment, and never reset the clock after recollection, repeat testing, or outsourcing. Specimen-stability limits, machine–assay compatibility, qualified-staff permissions, and clinically acceptable Complex-panel/Repeat deadlines must also be established; they cannot be inferred from these summaries.

## 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

### Evidence boundaries

The DFGs identify routes and pressure points, but they are not a fully balanced cohort model. For example, peak Technical validation has 7,240 listed incoming transitions from Assay run but 8,080 outgoing transitions. Window boundaries, incomplete recording, and flattened multi-assay behavior are possible explanations; the data do not identify which explanation is correct. Consequently, these counts should not be converted directly into exact transition probabilities or staffing requirements. 

Likewise, edge medians cannot be added to predict an Urgent order’s turnaround: different edges represent different subsets of orders and can include processing, off-shift time, transport, or synchronization. Reported utilization of 101% should be treated as a nominal-capacity alarm, with its denominator verified—not as a literal fraction of time busy.

### S1 — Mix-driven winter peak

**Likely pressure sequence: intake and pre-analysis → correction/preparation → assay → technical validation → medical/specialist validation.**

At peak, pre-analytical utilization is 96%, machines are at 98%, and technical validation is reported at 101%. These form a likely group of interacting constraints, rather than one provable bottleneck. Accession correction is also a consequential gate: only two specialists serve a queue whose median increases from 3 to 14 hours. Medical and specialist queues reach 22 and 36 hours despite lower aggregate utilization, suggesting that case mix, availability, or synchronization matters alongside average load. 

Registration automation can therefore move work faster into an already stressed pre-analytical queue. Faster correction releases blocked work into preparation and machines. Larger preparation batches may then create concentrated assay completions that overload technical validation. Clearing technical validation exposes the increased medical-review demand.

Urgent batch-wait bypass falls from 74% to 58%, but restoring bypass indiscriminately would fragment machine schedules and displace other work. The redesign must preserve deadline-sensitive access while controlling the downstream workload pulse. 

### S2 — Assay degradation or outage

**Likely pressure sequence: machine capacity loss → prepared-work accumulation → repeat competition → technical/medical surge after recovery.**

Among the listed outgoing Assay run edges, the share leading to Repeat assay increases from approximately **6.7% to 14.8%**. These are event-transition shares, not unique-order failure probabilities or the separately reported overall rework rate. The Repeat assay → Assay run median increases from 10 to 26 hours, illustrating how returning work can compete with first attempts.   

Rigidly routing all Routine work to M2 could create a reinforcing cycle: M2 receives more work, repeats consume additional assay capacity, and Routine aging worsens. However, clustering after M2 use and night shifts is **associational evidence**, not proof that either causes the failures; assay difficulty and assignment patterns require examination.  

After restoration, releasing all prepared backlog immediately risks moving the bottleneck to technical validation, then medical review. Recovery must therefore be paced, not treated as a simple return to unrestricted processing.

### S3 — Medical-validator or specialist absence

**Likely pressure sequence: specialist/medical eligibility barrier → accumulation of technically completed orders → later medical and release backlog.**

The Technical validation → Medical validation median rises from 8 to 24 hours; Hold for specialist review → Medical validation rises from 18 to 48 hours. These edges support concern about downstream availability and handoff timing, although they are not pure service-time estimates. 

An evening medical shift helps medically ready orders, but cannot automatically resolve cases still awaiting specialist input. Conversely, temporary specialist coverage can release many held cases simultaneously and overload medical validation.

The resource notes require medical validators for Urgent and Complex-panel orders. The aggregate Technical validation → Release result shortcut is therefore **not authorization to bypass medical validation for those classes**; eligibility must be checked at order level.  

### S4 — Courier compression or delay

**Likely pressure sequence: courier intake → pre-analytical checking/correction → preparation/assay wave → late validation demand.**

Courier intake median queue increases from 1 to 7.5 hours, with arrival spikes at 10:00 and 17:00. Register order → Receive sample increases from 5 to 8 hours, while Receive sample → Pre-analytical check increases from 2 to 9 hours. These are aggregate warnings, not Urgent-specific transit estimates. 

An intake surge can generate further work if errors require correction or recollection. Recollection’s return edge already has a 60-hour peak median, and explanation work consumes clerical capacity. Extending clerical shifts is not a neutral remedy: the supplied notes associate shifts exceeding nine hours with increased errors.   

Smoothing arrivals can relieve intake but merely shift the same work into preparation and assay unless their schedules also change. Moving pickups later may improve the receiving queue while worsening collection-to-release time.

### S5 — Compound disruption

**Likely interaction: compressed intake ↔ quality/recollection work; degraded assay ↔ repeats; constrained validation ↔ unreleased completed work.**

These constraints can coexist. A lab may have a large receiving backlog, idle capacity on an incompatible machine, and a growing specialist-held queue simultaneously. Relieving the most visible queue may have little effect on completed orders.

The important recovery question is whether the constrained stations can clear accumulated work **while serving subsequent arrivals**. A policy that briefly improves Urgent turnaround but leaves Routine and Complex-panel backlogs growing after disruption recovery is not robust.

## 3. Robust redesign package

**Use a coordinated package of early defect detection, deadline-aware scheduling, protected non-Urgent progress, flexible machine routing, and downstream-aware work release.** Reserve extra staffing and outsourcing for identifiable constraints rather than adding capacity everywhere.

### A. Combine completeness-gated registration with early defect triage — modified I8 and I6

Automate registration only when required digital metadata pass completeness and consistency checks. Incomplete orders remain visible in an exception queue, with urgency overriding the digital/manual channel distinction.

Introduce a brief early specimen/metadata triage, targeting **two hours from physical receipt**, without duplicating the full pre-analytical check. Distinguish correctable accession issues from samples that genuinely require recollection. Send actionable defect information promptly to the ordering team.

I6 principally moves detection earlier; it does **not by itself reduce the underlying defect rate**. Preventing recurrence requires feedback on recurring defect categories. Overaggressive rejection could instead increase recollections, physician contacts, and intake workload.

**Assumptions and limits:** Registration automation must genuinely save clerical effort; triage must use appropriately qualified staff and valid rejection criteria. At 96% peak pre-analytical utilization, an additional inspection layer without task substitution could make matters worse.

**Next bottleneck:** Faster clean intake increases preparation and assay demand. Preserve prompt triage, but control subsequent release of eligible work into preparation according to downstream capacity.

### B. Replace strict Urgent-first with deadline-aware, anti-starvation scheduling — modified I1

For each order with an agreed deadline, calculate provisional slack:

$$
s_i=d_i-t-\widehat{R}_i,
$$

where \(\widehat{R}_i\) is a conservative estimate of **remaining end-to-end time**, including outstanding assays, required validation, queues, and coverage gaps. It must be estimated from appropriate order-level traces and current workload, not summed DFG medians.

Reserve necessary capacity for imminent Urgent deadlines, using least slack within that group. Allocate remaining compatible capacity through protected progress blocks for aging Routine and Complex-panel work. Unused protected capacity can be borrowed, but repeated displacement must trigger escalation.

Repeat work should retain its original deadline and priority where those are known; a repeat should not automatically outrank every first attempt. Complex panels need progress toward completion of the whole panel, not just rapid completion of their easiest assay.

**Trade-off:** Under severe overload, some Routine or Complex-panel work will be displaced. The policy makes that displacement visible and bounded rather than pretending every class can be first.

**Next bottleneck:** Faster Urgent assay completion can overload medical validation. Reserve downstream validation capacity when reserving assay capacity.

### C. Use bounded batching and downstream-aware work release — modified I2

Keep the existing 12-sample batch arrangement as the reference policy. Permit batches up to 24 only when compatible Routine samples are already available, the setup benefit is credible, and the run will not consume protected deadline-sensitive capacity.

Start an underfilled batch when the oldest eligible sample reaches its latest safe start. Do not wait solely to fill 24 positions. Treat runs as non-interruptible for planning unless the laboratory confirms a validated interruption procedure.

Preparation release should consider available compatible assay capacity and the expected technical/medical workload created by the run. Avoid launching several batches that will finish into the same inadequately staffed validation window.

**Assumptions and limits:** This needs assay-family, run-time, setup, and specimen-stability information. Smaller batches may increase setup losses; excessive downstream gating may move waiting upstream.

**Next bottleneck:** Technical validation is the immediate risk after assay acceleration. Count all waiting orders in end-to-end WIP, including work deliberately held before preparation, so gating cannot create a cosmetic queue improvement.

### D. Route by reliable completed output, not nominal speed — modified I3

Maintain flexible M1/M2 routing subject to assay compatibility. Evaluate expected machine minutes per valid result, including repeats and setup changes, rather than runtime alone.

Protect reliable capacity for deadline-sensitive or difficult work when justified, but lend unused capacity to other eligible orders. Do not permanently dedicate M1 to two classes or automatically send every Routine order to M2.

Investigate M2/night-shift repeat clusters by assay type, workload, failure reason, and shift. Use laboratory-approved maintenance and quality procedures where evidence supports intervention.

**Assumptions and limits:** The log must distinguish a failed assay from another assay required by the same panel. Failure-adjusted routing may be unreliable initially because these details are not supplied.

**Next bottleneck:** Fewer repeats free assay capacity and can increase first-pass output into technical validation. Retain downstream release controls even when machine reliability improves.

### E. Smooth arrivals and align existing coverage — I7

Coordinate courier windows with intake, preparation, and validation coverage, using arrival forecasts and staggered overlap of appropriately qualified staff. Avoid solving intake peaks mainly through clerical shifts exceeding the documented nine-hour error-risk point.

Smoothing must respect transport deadlines and specimen constraints. Measure collection-to-receipt and original-clock turnaround, not merely the queue after delivery.

**Assumptions and limits:** Courier routes must have practical flexibility. Some demand pulses will remain, especially during widespread delays.

**Next bottleneck:** A flatter intake profile can still create synchronized assay completions. Align preparation and run schedules as well as receiving coverage.

### F. Release completed orders promptly; do not adopt blanket I9

Do not hold a fully validated Routine order solely to wait for a batch report. The evidence does not establish release work as a resource bottleneck sufficient to justify adding a new waiting gate.

Where feasible, separate administrative batch summaries from delivery of completed results. For multi-assay orders, define order completion explicitly: a partial result must not silently become a completed-order SLA success.

**Next bottleneck:** There is no subsequent laboratory station in the supplied flow. The remaining risk is unmeasured report transmission and exception-handling capacity, which must be checked rather than assumed unlimited.

The manager-proposed interventions and their documented conflicts support using these guarded forms rather than their unconditional versions. 

## 4. Scenario-specific policies

The numerical bands below are **proposed operating thresholds**, not estimated optima. They need validation against actual staffed minutes and service-time distributions.

Use immediate activation for a confirmed outage, unavailable mandatory reviewer, or predicted deadline failure. For forecast-driven changes, require persistence across successive reviews. Step down only after capacity is restored, the at-risk cohort is covered, and the next-shift forecast remains below the warning band through an intake wave.

### S1: Activate evening medical validation only when downstream need is demonstrated — I5

Activate when the scheduled medical workforce cannot clear medically eligible work before relevant deadlines, or forecast medical workload exceeds 95% of effective next-shift capacity with a growing queue.

Plan this shift from incoming Urgent/Complex-panel demand, not only from the queue that has already formed. Coordinate technical completion times with the evening window.

Deactivate after the existing shift commitment when the backlog is recoverable with normal coverage and forecast load remains below 85%.

**Spillover:** Additional medical capacity can expose report-release delays. It will not resolve specialist-held cases without the necessary specialist decision.

### S2: Activate outage routing and paced recovery

Remove unavailable machine capacity from dispatch calculations immediately. Do not assume that losing one of two machines means exactly 50% capacity loss; speed and compatibility differ.

Restrict discretionary large batches and allocate compatible remaining capacity using deadline slack and protected aging blocks. Escalate overflow or prospective non-urgent collection rescheduling when no feasible local schedule remains, rather than allowing prepared WIP to grow unchecked.

Restore ordinary routing only after the machine is cleared for use and the accumulated queue can be processed without overwhelming technical validation.

**Spillover:** The recovery plan must include the technical and medical workload generated by the cleared assay backlog.

### S3: Activate qualified review cover and selective Complex-panel outsourcing — I10

Before known absence days, reserve qualified specialist review windows for likely held work. Additional medical cover is useful only for the tasks its staff are authorized and able to perform.

Outsource an eligible Complex-panel order only when:

$$
12\text{ h transport}
+\widehat T_{\text{partner processing/review}}
+\widehat T_{\text{result integration}}
+\text{uncertainty allowance}
<
\widehat T_{\text{remaining local path}}.
$$

The comparison should remain favorable across plausible estimates, and the partner must confirm acceptance, assay capability, and validation responsibility. The supplied lower specialist queue does not establish a shorter complete partner turnaround. 

Do not infer a 36-hour saving by subtracting 12 hours from the 48-hour local hold-to-medical edge. Those quantities do not describe equivalent end-to-end paths.

Stop diversions when the local path becomes reliably preferable, partner capacity becomes uncertain, or transport undermines the expected benefit.

**Spillover:** Outsourcing moves workload to the partner and creates local tracking, reconciliation, and result-integration work. It is not a general 18-hour Urgent solution.

### Conditional accession support: permit I4 only when the donor pool remains safe

Prepare cross-training in advance, allowing for the stated four-week training timescale and confirming the competencies needed for correction. Do not treat it as immediate emergency capacity. 

Loan a trained technical validator to correction only when a meaningful blocked queue exists **and** technical validation remains adequately covered after the loan, including expected assay completions and the work unblocked by correction. A provisional donor condition is next-shift load below 85%.

At the supplied 101% peak technical utilization, **I4 fails this guard by default unless real qualified backfill exists**. Return the worker immediately if the donor queue becomes unsafe.

**Spillover:** Correction releases work that will eventually require technical validation; the donor test must include that returning workload.

### S4: Activate arrival-wave response without merely moving the delay

When forecast receipts and existing untriaged work exceed the next two hours of qualified intake/triage capacity, activate courier re-spacing where feasible and short coverage overlaps.

For a delayed Urgent sample, reassess the whole remaining path before assigning preparation or assay priority. A fast assay slot is insufficient when the necessary medical reviewer will be unavailable.

Deactivate after the wave clears and subsequent arrivals fit planned capacity.

**Spillover:** Limit the resulting preparation/assay pulse against downstream staffing, without withholding necessary early sample triage.

### S5: Enter controlled overload management

When required work exceeds feasible local and confirmed partner capacity, escalate explicitly. Allocate scarce capacity by clinical urgency and deadline risk, preserve minimum non-Urgent progress, and communicate expected misses and recovery priorities.

Prospective changes to non-urgent collection schedules require agreement with the ordering team. They must not reset existing clocks or conceal delayed orders.

This is the boundary of the redesign: **safe, transparent degradation is preferable to unsupported promises that every deadline remains achievable.**

## 6. Residual vulnerabilities

**Capacity infeasibility remains possible.** The package cannot manufacture compatible assay capacity or specialist authority during a prolonged outage. A one-shift outage is a bounded stress test, not proof of resilience to an arbitrary disruption. Complex and repeat workloads may also contain substantially more work per order than the summaries reveal.

**The redesign can create new waiting and inequity.** Downstream gating can leave viable samples waiting upstream; protected slots can temporarily sit unused when compatibility prevents borrowing; digital registration can disadvantage incomplete-metadata orders. These are redesign-induced vulnerabilities. Monitor total order age, specimen constraints, unused reserved capacity, and channel-specific delay—not just the queue being improved.

**Efficiency gains can disappear through switching and fragmentation.** Smaller batches, frequent routing changes, and flexible staff loans may increase setup or handover work. Conversely, keeping large batches for efficiency can block urgent arrivals. The appropriate balance depends on missing setup and service-time distributions.

**External relief may fail during the same winter wave.** A partner could experience correlated demand or staffing pressure. The stated lower specialist queue is not a guaranteed capacity commitment. Outsourcing also introduces specimen/result matching and ownership risks, particularly when only part of a multi-assay order is transferred.

**Flattening limits both diagnosis and control.** The supplied log is organized by order even though an order can contain multiple tubes and Complex-panel orders require multiple assays. It cannot, by itself, reliably distinguish shared runs, parallel work, missing panel components, or the actual capacity consumed by each order.  A shared batch must not be counted as a full machine run separately for every linked order.

**Service acceptability is not yet numerically demonstrated.** The summaries do not provide service-time distributions, assay compatibility, detailed rosters, or an acceptable breach allowance. The design is a defensible candidate, not evidence that all scenarios will meet 18/72 hours.

## 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

During peaks, review the dashboard daily with operations leadership, but refresh deadline, intake, equipment, and coverage signals at least hourly. A daily-only response is too coarse for an 18-hour Urgent deadline.

The following are **leading indicators**. Warning bands are provisional control settings, not facts estimated from the supplied logs.

| Leading indicator                                        | What to measure                                                                                                                                                   | Proposed warning/action band                                                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Remaining deadline slack by class**                    | Current age plus a conservative estimate of all remaining work and waits                                                                                          | Urgent amber at 0–4h slack; red at ≤0h. Routine amber at 0–12h; red at ≤0h. Use agreed clinical deadlines for Complex/Repeat orders rather than inventing contractual SLAs          |
| **Forward workload pressure and WIP growth by resource** | Forecast required productive minutes / effective staffed compatible minutes for the next shift; show backlog separately, including work upstream that will arrive | Amber at 85–95%; red at ≥95% with growing backlog, or ≥100% regardless of trend. Examine the next station before activating an upstream intervention                                |
| **Arrival-wave and early-triage risk**                   | Forecast two-hour receipts, current untriaged work, and oldest untriaged sample                                                                                   | Amber when near-term work will exceed qualified capacity or oldest untriaged age exceeds 1h; red when the proposed 2h triage limit is exceeded                                      |
| **New defects and repeat-work arrivals**                 | Newly detected defects, failed first attempts, repeat-work minutes, stratified by assay, machine, and shift                                                       | Amber for sustained mix-adjusted deterioration; red for a confirmed quality-control failure or rising repeat workload that makes the next-shift assay plan infeasible               |
| **Validation and partner coverage gaps**                 | Cases expected to become ready before qualified coverage returns; distinguish medically ready from specialist-held work; verify partner acceptance                | Amber for a coverage gap within the planning horizon; red when a due order has no feasible qualified local or confirmed partner path                                                |
| **Starvation and synchronization**                       | Aging WIP by class, missed protected blocks, incomplete panels, and repeated dispatch deferrals                                                                   | Amber for persistent aging across two reviews; red when compatible aging work repeatedly receives no protected progress, or a specimen limit/agreed clinical deadline is threatened |

Two measurement precautions are essential. Forecast workload should include setups, repeats, required panel assays, and work expected from upstream—not just order counts. Effective capacity should reflect actual compatible machines and qualified staff on duty, including scheduled absences.

**Lagging outcomes must remain separate.** Report Urgent releases beyond 18 hours, Routine releases beyond 72 hours, p90/p95 turnaround by class, overall rework, recollection/cancellation outcomes, disruption-backlog recovery time, and validated-but-unreleased delays. Retain open and overdue orders in the report; completed-only cycle times can look better simply because the slowest orders remain unfinished. The supplied 7.5% versus 11.8% rework and 22% versus 54% incidence of queues over 12 hours provide comparison points, not proof of any intervention’s effect. 

For process-mining support, add order–tube–assay–run/batch–machine relationships, activity readiness/start/completion timestamps, qualified resource assignments, failure reasons, and courier/partner handoffs. Preserve original order identities and distinguish partial results from complete-order release.

Before broad deployment, compare the package with current practice and with isolated interventions under S1–S5 using reconstructed traces and calibrated workload/service-time ranges. Evaluate **worst-scenario deadline risk, class-specific tails, backlog growth, and recovery**, not just average cycle time. The package should be accepted only where its end-to-end gains survive the downstream workload it creates and the delay it imposes on other classes.
