# 1. Stress scenarios

Design for **on-time, valid results across the whole order journey**, not maximum throughput at any one station. The normal Urgent p90 is already 19h against an 18h deadline; at peak it reaches 33h even though the median is 17h. Routine p90 rises from 68h to 120h against a 72h target. These quantiles show the problem but do not give exact breach rates. The stress inputs below are planning cases, not claims about the frequency or duration of actual disruptions.

| Scenario | Stress input | Work that becomes disproportionately exposed |
|---|---|---|
| **S1 — Winter mix shift** | Replay the observed change from 2,300 to 3,300 orders/week (+43%), with Urgent share rising from 18% to 29% and Complex-panel from 13% to 20%. | Urgent orders rise from about 414 to 957/week and Complex-panel from 299 to 660/week, while Routine stays near 1,400/week. Rush setups, multiple assays and qualified sign-offs grow much faster than order count. |
| **S2 — Assay outage** | Take M1 out for one shift; use M2 only for work it is qualified to run. Stress-test an elevated M2 repeat rate, especially overnight, even with normal arrivals. | Machine-dependent assays, panels waiting on one missing component, and repeats that consume a second slot. |
| **S3 — Reviewer shortfall** | Replay the alternate-Friday specialist absence with a panel-heavy queue; separately remove one medical-validation shift to distinguish the two skills. | Specialist-held panels in the first case; all orders requiring medical sign-off, including Urgent orders, in the second. |
| **S4 — Courier compression** | Keep daily order volume unchanged but delay a 10:00 delivery until it overlaps the 17:00 wave. Include a Routine-heavy load and already-aged Urgent specimens. | Intake gets a burst, Urgent orders arrive with less time left, and any affected specimens may need exception handling or recollection. |
| **S5 — Compound shock** | Combine the winter mix, a one-shift M1 outage and the Friday specialist gap; test the next staffed shift as the machine returns. | Urgent and panel work compete for scarce machine time, but recovering assay capacity cannot clear orders whose reviewer is unavailable. Routine and repeat backlogs can persist after the outage ends. |

# 2. Bottleneck-migration analysis

Treat frequency DFG counts as **observed edge traversals**, not unique orders or staffing requirements. The log is flattened across tubes, assays and batches, and Release plus Cancel endpoints account for only 16,440 of 18,400 starts in the normal window and 8,580 of 9,900 at peak. Reconcile window boundaries, open cases and missing events before inferring completion or cancellation rates. Performance-edge medians are elapsed times for different subsets, not service times or quantities to sum into an order ETA. Read technical validation’s reported 101% as a warning that load may exceed nominal capacity, and verify how the metric treats overtime and carryover.

- **S1 — Intake → assay → technical validation → review.** Pre-analytical check reaches 96% utilization and a 9.5h median queue, up from 1.8h. The direct recollection edge grows from about 4% to 8% of shown check departures, and recollection-to-receipt goes from 42h to 60h; explaining and reprocessing defects adds intake work. Faster accession or preparation then feeds an assay pool already at 98% with an 18h median queue. Urgent bypass falls from 74% to 58%, and Batch wait → Assay run rises from 9h to 22h. Strict Urgent-first would help some deadlines at the expense of Routine and panels, while extra setups may reduce effective throughput. More assay completions land on technical validation (101%, 16h median queue); clearing that queue exposes medical and specialist review. About 26% of shown technical departures went directly to medical or to specialist hold in normal weeks versus 50% at peak. Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times as likely to need medical validation and 2.4 times as likely to enter specialist hold; medical queue median rises from 2h to 22h.
- **S2 — Assay compatibility and repeats → technical validation on recovery → review for the affected mix.** Moving all feasible work to the faster M2 can increase attempts without increasing valid results. Assay run → Repeat assay grows from about 7% to 15% of shown assay departures between observed periods, and Repeat assay → Assay run rises from 10h to 26h. Repeats cluster around nights and M2; test that association within assay family and shift rather than attributing it all to the machine. Permanent machine dedication could strand compatible capacity or overload M2 with Routine work. On restart, a dump of deferred runs can swamp technical validation (Assay run → Technical validation rises from 5h to 15h at peak) and send a burst of completed panels to medical review.
- **S3 — Qualified reviewer → medical sign-off or release.** Specialist review has a 36h peak median queue despite 87% reported utilization; aggregate utilization hides the scheduled gap and limited eligibility. Technical validation → Hold for specialist review grows from 290 to 610 traversals in the shorter peak window, and Hold → Medical validation stretches from 18h to 48h. Extra general medical coverage cannot clear a specialist-only hold. Restoring specialist coverage can push a batch into medical validation; if general medical coverage is missing, both direct referrals and cleared holds accumulate there instead. Clearing medical work can then bunch release and critical-result notifications (Medical validation → Release rises from 2h to 5h, a reason to check the handoff, not proof of a release staffing shortage). Keep authorized direct-release work moving separately.
- **S4 — Courier intake and accession → preparation/assay → technical validation.** Courier intake queue rises from 1h to 7.5h in the observed peak, and Register → Receive from 5h to 8h. A compressed wave first consumes receiving and check capacity, then correction time (14h peak median correction queue; Correction → Preparation rises from 5h to 16h). Defects can send demand back into the next courier wave through recollection. Smoothing delivery or automating clean registrations helps only if checks can absorb the revised arrivals. Otherwise the same burst moves downstream to preparation (11h peak queue), assay and eventually technical validation. Age at receipt matters as much as the count received.
- **S5 — Assay and review bind on different routes at the same time.** Machine time is scarce for Urgent work and incomplete panels; a specialist-dependent panel may still be blocked after all runs finish. Running those panels early without a credible review slot can consume capacity that would save Urgent deadlines while increasing held inventory. Conversely, diverting eligible panels can relieve assay and specialist work but adds transport and result reconciliation. After repair or reviewer return, throttle the catch-up wave against technical, medical and release capacity; the original disruption ending is not the same as recovery.

# 3. Robust redesign package

1. **Dispatch by remaining time and protect service for other queues (modify I1).** Use the contract-defined clock and estimate a conservative time to *valid release*, including transport if still ahead, assay compatibility, the last required panel component, and available sign-off. Give Urgent orders with least remaining slack first claim on a limited reserve at preparation, assay and required validation. Maintain a per-shift minimum of feasible Routine and panel work at their constrained steps when queued, with aging and a clinically authorized override. Lend unused reserves at the last safe cutoff. A repeat inherits the original order’s clock and clinical priority; it does not restart the clock or automatically jump the queue. A reserved assay slot without a qualified validator is not a protected path.
2. **Limit handoffs in units of work, not orders.** Plan the next shift using expected machine minutes (setups and repeats included), technical work and qualified review minutes. At preparation and assay, use downstream commitments and a bounded ready-work buffer rather than pushing whole queues forward. Leave room for near-due work and avoid starving an available machine when its downstream handoff is feasible. Track received-but-not-started and offsite work on the same board; never hide a queue outside a measured station or defer stability-sensitive processing to meet a WIP cap. This needs shift-level visibility across teams; stale estimates or overly tight caps can idle equipment and produce a larger upstream queue.
3. **Remove avoidable loop work before increasing flow (modify I6 and I8).** Screen within 2h of receipt for known acceptance defects and correctable metadata problems. Route ambiguous cases to qualified review rather than auto-rejecting; contact the sender promptly for correction or justified recollection. Give sending sites feedback on recurring defects. Automate only validated, complete digital orders, retaining an exception route and phasing in volume against check capacity. Screening itself consumes intake time, and recovering more usable samples raises preparation, assay and validation demand. Do not fund this with long clerk shifts that raise the error rate or by pulling technical validators from an overloaded queue.
4. **Smooth arrivals and batches, and keep machine routing flexible (modify I2, I3, I7).** Spread pickups around staffed receiving windows; preserve an exception route for time-critical specimens so smoothing does not simply shift waiting to the sending site. Use capped Routine fill waits and batch by compatible assay family. Reserve qualified capacity for short-slack or high-repeat-risk work, but choose between available machines by expected time and workload per valid result, not runtime alone. This depends on comparable quality and availability data and approved assay-machine combinations. Smoother arrivals and efficient batches can still generate large completion waves, so match run releases to validation headroom. Release results promptly after required sign-offs; reject I9’s blanket Routine report hold, which would move delay to the end without fixing the upstream constraint.

**Qualification before rollout.** Link orders, tubes, assay attempts, runs/batches and required signatures. Capture ready/start/end times, reasons for holds and repeats, courier ETA/receipt, staffing by qualification and downtime. Replay comparable shifts or simulate the five scenarios with observed arrivals, service and failure ranges, open carryover and recovery; do not count observed repeat work as both new demand and generated retries. Compare with current policy on identical inputs. Use deadline breach rates and tail/oldest-open age by class, plus rework, cancellations, integrity and time to clear backlog. As initial single-shock qualification goals, test p90 at or below 18h for Urgent and 72h for Routine, lower panel and repeat tails than the unmodified replay, no increase in avoidable cancellations or quality exceptions, and backlog draining by the second fully staffed recovery shift. These are pilot gates, not a waiver of individual Urgent penalties or a claim that capacity suffices. Check mature cohorts and track open and outsourced orders to avoid rewarding selective completion. If a scenario fails, report the capacity or skill deficit and use the escalation policy rather than relaxing sign-off or averaging away the failure.

# 4. Scenario-specific policies

Use the following as starting thresholds; calibrate in replay and pilot use. Review at shift handover and around courier waves, and immediately on outage, lost coverage or predicted deadline miss. For normal stand-down, require the cause to clear and the affected queue to drain across two reviews that include the next relevant arrival or handoff, not just one quiet snapshot.

| Scenario | Switch on and act | Switch off |
|---|---|---|
| **S1 — Mix surge** | If next-shift workload exceeds 90% of reliable capacity on a critical stage with a rising queue, or Urgent release slack is forecast below 4h, tighten the end-to-end Urgent reserve without dropping protected Routine/panel service. Trial batches up to 24 only on a Routine-heavy compatible queue when the fill cap, Urgent slots and downstream handoff can all be met; otherwise use smaller batches. Use trained correction cover (I4, allow four weeks for training) only if corrections are aging, e.g. oldest ready >8h, **and** technical validation has verified spare capacity after its own reserve. Time-box the loan. | Unwind surge reserves once deadline risk clears and forecast load is below 90% with queues falling; return loaned staff immediately if technical validation loses headroom. End larger batching at the first threatened fill or handoff limit. |
| **S2 — Machine loss or degradation** | On loss of a required machine or a sustained assay-specific repeat rate outside its comparable-shift control band, invoke qualified backup routing and QC/maintenance review. Limit new starts for deferrable work and prioritize according to clinical need and remaining slack; do not repeatedly feed a failing route. Pace restart against validation capacity. | Restore routing only after documented QC clearance and stable valid-output rates; remove recovery pacing only when the downstream queues can absorb the restart. |
| **S3 — Review shortfall** | Use the roster to prebook qualified specialist cover before the Friday gap when holds would age past their safe window. If ready or forecast medical-sign-off work exceeds available qualified minutes, add an evening medical shift (I5) only where the work will be review-ready and release is covered. For eligible Complex-panel overflow (I10), send only against a confirmed partner slot when a conservative end-to-end estimate beats local release by a meaningful margin **after** dispatch wait, 12h transport, partner processing and local reconciliation. Confirm custody, result ownership and return tracking before dispatch. | Stand down extra shifts and new referrals when qualified capacity covers the forecast and held work is draining. Continue tracking every dispatched case through validated release. |
| **S4 — Compressed arrivals** | If route ETAs put two waves in one staffed window or forecast receiving/check work exceeds window capacity, re-slot only eligible pickups and align receiving, check and correction coverage. Use the time-critical route where needed, pre-stage clean registrations without releasing them as an unchecked burst, and alert downstream teams to the projected wave. | Restore normal slots after ETAs separate and the received and expected waves fit the staffed intake and downstream plan. |
| **S5 — Compound overload** | If conservative forecasts still show missed deadlines or growing backlog after the above controls, appoint one shift lead to commit end-to-end slots. Protect clinically time-critical work; escalate expected breaches to sites and contract owners before the deadline. With clinical approval, reschedule uncollected elective work and divert only eligible confirmed partner work. Track deferred demand and expected breaches openly; never skip checks or sign-offs. | Resume deferred work in tranches once the affected qualified paths are restored, deadline risk clears and there is headroom to drain the backlog without a second surge. |

# 6. Residual vulnerabilities

- **A missing qualified path cannot be scheduled around.** A prolonged single-machine outage, unavailable assay compatibility, specialist-only sign-off or a full partner lab can make even an Urgent deadline infeasible. The remaining controls are early warning, safe diversion where available and approved demand deferral, not more aggressive priority.
- **The redesign can create new waits.** Reserved slots can sit empty when estimates are wrong, and pacing can strand work upstream. Lending cutoffs and aged-work minimums reduce but do not eliminate this. Early triage can also create false rejects, and courier smoothing can hide extra pre-receipt time. Audit the whole journey rather than only measured station queues.
- **A panel can look nearly done and still be far from release.** One missing component, an intermittent repeat cluster or an unmatched partner result can dominate its tail. Order-level WIP can conceal this; track the blocking component, responsible owner and next action, and audit custody and result matching.

# 7. Minimum monitoring dashboard

**Leading indicators** — review at least daily during peak, with shift and event-driven refreshes. Initial bands are operational warnings, not contractual limits.

| Indicator | Warning / action band |
|---|---|
| **Due-time risk by class and blocked stage** | Urgent forecast slack <4h or Routine <12h: review the path; slack ≤0: escalate now. Show oldest open panel/repeat and its blocking dependency; flag when it ages through a staffed review without a credible next slot. Include in-flight and partner work. |
| **Next-shift workload vs. reliable, qualified capacity** | View check/correction, assay, technical validation and medical/specialist review in work-minutes, with ready and blocked work separate. Watch at ≥90% with a rising queue; act at ≥100% or when no qualified slot exists before the latest safe start. |
| **Courier ETA and age on arrival** | Warn when waves are forecast to merge or load exceeds a staffed intake window. Act immediately on time-critical orders with an ETA that erases their release slack or on a specimen-integrity exception. |
| **Valid output and feedback loops** | Watch repeat rates and valid runs per staffed machine-hour by assay, machine and shift, alongside correction/recollection entries. Investigate sustained deterioration beyond comparable-shift bands; act immediately on downtime or a QC stop. Flag triage waiting >2h from receipt. |

**Lagging outcomes** — report actual Urgent >18h and Routine >72h rates and counts, p90 valid-release time by class (including panels and repeats), and realized rework, cancellations, quality exceptions and recovery time. Pair completed-order results with overdue open orders and cohort counts; neither an unreturned partner result nor an uncompleted order is an on-time release.
