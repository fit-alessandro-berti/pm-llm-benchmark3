# 2. Dependency and prerequisite map

- The core dependency is not linear clerical throughput but join-point readiness. KYC and financial document review are recorded sequentially but often run concurrently, so automating only one branch has limited end-to-end effect until the slower branch and the downstream join at `Committee packaging` or `Contract preparation` move as well.
- Upstream data quality dependencies run as a chain. A1 reduces duplicate noise but does not fix route quality. A2 affects the control path, the document checklist in A5, and whether the collateral branch in A7 is triggered. A5 improves completeness for `Financial document review -> Normalize financial statements -> Credit analysis`; without that, A6 accuracy and straight-through rates will be poor on low-quality or incomplete submissions. A8 and A9 depend on consistent upstream artifacts, not just faster ones.
- Legal and compliance controls are gating dependencies, not optional add-ons. A3 and A4 must preserve model suggestion, evidence viewed, human override, and final disposition. A9 must enforce committee-term-to-contract consistency and should block any signing flow that lacks KYC clearance. A10 should only be enabled after that gate is hard, because provisional signing already occurred in 6.5% of cases and 1.1% required remediation. A12 is the natural place to manage control exceptions and audit evidence.
- Human escalation capacity is a hard constraint. Compliance specialists are only 3 FTE and two seniors dominate the handover network, so A3 and A4 must be conservative and capacity-aware. Credit analysts are the central working-together node; A5 without A6 will improve document completeness but can simply increase clean inflow into analysts and lengthen the `Normalize financial statements` and `Credit analysis` queues. Legal ops is underutilized when KYC is slow but overloaded at month end, so A9 must smooth rather than batch more work.
- External valuation remains a structural dependency. A7 can improve the internal part of `Credit analysis -> Collateral valuation request -> Wait for external valuation -> Committee packaging`, but it cannot eliminate the 58-hour external wait. Its value is highest in equipment finance, where collateral is required in 71% of cases, and lower for working-capital cases.
- Core-banking integration constraints force sequencing. In the first six months, only two modules should consume deep integration capacity. A9 should be one because it needs authoritative decision, KYC, and contract data. A6 should be the second because it needs document ingestion and credit-analysis interfaces. A5 and A4 can start in portal/workflow layers with lighter API dependencies. A11 should wait until upstream controls are demonstrably reliable.

# 3. Portfolio alternatives

- Quick-win portfolio: A1, A5, A9, A10. This optimizes political visibility, feasibility, and visible customer-facing digitization. It sacrifices root-cause relief in analyst and compliance bottlenecks and risks pushing more work into KYC and analyst queues before those scarce pools are protected.
- Bottleneck/synchronization portfolio: A4, A5, A6, A9. This optimizes the two branches that most often determine readiness for downstream joins: KYC exceptions and financial-document/normalization flow. It also includes a control-strengthening module. It sacrifices some immediate political appeal and does not directly remove the external valuation wait, so equipment-finance cases retain a residual delay.
- Compliance-first portfolio: A3, A4, A9, A12. This optimizes legality, auditability, KYC integrity, four-eyes compliance, and contract/signing control quality. It sacrifices the largest broad-based throughput gain because the `Financial document review -> Request missing documents -> Financial document review` loop and analyst normalization burden remain mostly intact.

# 4. Expected process-level effects

- Quick-win portfolio (`A1+A5+A9+A10`): Cycle time improves moderately on straightforward cases through faster intake, fewer document chases, faster contract prep, and better signing follow-up, but it does little for cases constrained by `KYC check`, `Resolve entity mismatch`, `Normalize financial statements`, or `Wait for external valuation`. Waiting time falls in the visible back-office queue and later at `Contract preparation` and `Customer signing`, but can rise before `Credit analysis` and in KYC because `Receive application -> Register application` and better document prompting accelerate release into scarce pools. Rework should fall in duplicate handling, some missing-document repetition, and the `Contract preparation -> Remediate control issue -> Contract preparation` loop, but legal-sequencing risk remains if signing automation is not subordinated to KYC. Handovers decline in back office and customer reminders, while analyst and compliance handovers can rise. Resource demand shifts away from back office and toward analysts, KYC ops, and customer support. Control violations improve mainly for contract-term mismatch, with weaker effect on KYC-before-signing unless the KYC gate is hard.
- Bottleneck/synchronization portfolio (`A4+A5+A6+A9`): Cycle time should improve the most across common variants because it attacks both branches feeding downstream readiness: the KYC exception path `KYC check -> Resolve entity mismatch -> KYC check` and the financial path `Financial document review -> Request missing documents -> Financial document review -> Normalize financial statements -> Credit analysis`. It also shortens the path from `Committee decision -> Contract preparation`. Waiting time should drop materially in the 31-hour entity-mismatch queue, the 18-hour document-review wait driven by loops, the 20-hour normalization queue, and the 22-hour contract-preparation wait. Rework should fall in the 3,300/2,800 missing-document loop, the 1,750/1,160 entity-mismatch loop, and the 650/510 contract-remediation loop. Handovers should become fewer and more targeted because RM clarification loops shrink, analyst inputs are cleaner, and legal receives cleaner decision data. Resource demand shifts analyst time from manual normalization toward judgment-heavy credit work, which is desirable; compliance specialists should see fewer low-value touches if A4 only resolves high-confidence cases and escalates the rest; legal ops should experience steadier flow. Control violations should decline through stronger contract-rule enforcement, but four-eyes still requires explicit workflow separation and monitoring.
- Compliance-first portfolio (`A3+A4+A9+A12`): Cycle time improves mainly for KYC-sensitive and remediation-prone variants, especially High-exposure SME and Credit-line renewal, but ordinary SME cases waiting on documents and normalization improve less. Waiting time may initially rise in KYC because A3 should escalate ambiguous hits conservatively, but downstream remediation and rework waits should fall as control failures are caught earlier. Rework should decline in KYC hit handling, entity mismatch, provisional signing remediation, and contract-decision mismatches, while the document loop remains largely unchanged. Handovers become more formalized, with fewer informal fixes after the fact and more governance-visible exceptions. Resource demand increases in compliance, policy ownership, and control-review work, while analyst relief is limited. Control violations fall the most in KYC-before-signing, false clearance risk, contract mismatch, and same-person analysis/packaging breaches in High-exposure cases.

# 5. Risks and compensating controls

| Risk introduced by automation | Where it appears | Compensating controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| False negatives create unsafe straight-through decisions | Mainly A3 and A4 | No auto-clear for low-confidence hits, conservative recall thresholds, mandatory human review for sanctions/entity ambiguity, sampled second-line review, and immediate kill switch |
| False positives or over-escalation overload scarce specialists | Mainly A3 and A4 | Capacity-based routing, queue caps, daily backlog monitoring, fallback to manual prioritization, and threshold tuning by segment |
| Model drift or data drift degrades decisions over time | A2, A3, A4, A6 | Versioned models, monthly calibration by product/exposure/document type, drift alerts, champion-challenger testing, and rollback to rules/manual processing |
| Garbage-in/garbage-out propagates bad upstream data | A5, A6, A8, A9 | Mandatory completeness checks, source-document linking, confidence scoring, low-confidence human review, and authoritative-data precedence rules |
| Missing auditability undermines compliance responsibility | All decision-support modules | Immutable event log for input data, model version, recommendation, override, reason code, final action, and user identity; audit-friendly case timeline |
| Four-eyes violations persist or worsen under speed pressure | A8, A9, and related workflow changes | Workflow-level segregation of duties, hard block preventing same user from completing both credit analysis and committee packaging for High-exposure SME, month-end breach dashboard, and named approval for rare exceptions |
| KYC-before-signing violations continue through automated convenience | A9 and A10 | Hard KYC gate before contract release or e-sign dispatch, no bypass without compliance override, automated evidence check before sending, and exception queue in A12 |
| Contract terms drift from committee decisions because rules age | A9 | Named legal owner for the rule library, change-management approval, regression test pack on every rule update, and periodic sampling against committee minutes |
| Customer friction and abandonment increase | A5 and A10 | Segment-specific messaging, RM preview of checklist/reminder content, alternate assisted path for complex customers, and tracking of abandonment and complaint rates |
| Exception queues become invisible because “automation handled it” | All modules with human fallback | Explicit exception worklists, SLA timers, aging dashboards, and mandatory disposition codes for every escalation |
| Unsafe activation occurs despite earlier gaps | A11, if attempted too early | Defer until upstream controls stabilize; later require hard stop on incomplete controls, checklist attestation, and post-activation audit sampling |

# 6. Recommended phased rollout

1. Phase 0, months 0-1: Establish the control and measurement baseline before live automation. Lock the authoritative data sources for KYC status, committee decision, and contract terms; define the audit-log schema; codify the four-eyes rule for High-exposure SME; baseline loop frequencies, queue waits, and current control breaches. Gate criteria: rule owners assigned, event logging proven end to end, and baseline dashboards trusted by operations and compliance.
2. Phase 1, months 1-4: Deploy A5 first and bring A9 through controlled release. A5 is the fastest operational win with real process impact because it attacks the 3,300/2,800 missing-document loop and RM-driven incompleteness. A9 is deep integration number 1 because compliance requires a first-wave control-strengthening module and contract mismatch is already material. Monitoring metrics: share of cases entering `Request missing documents`, repeat-loop rate, customer abandonment/complaints, contract-remediation loop rate, KYC-before-signing breaches, legal override frequency, and audit-log completeness. Gate criteria: document-loop direction clearly down, no material rise in customer friction, zero critical contract-rule defects in pilot, and full traceability of suggestions and overrides.
3. Phase 2, months 4-6: Deploy A6 as deep integration number 2 and pilot A4 on a narrow set of entity-mismatch scenarios. A6 protects analyst capacity so A5 does not simply move the bottleneck downstream. A4 should start with high-confidence cases only because the compliance specialist pool is thin. Monitoring metrics: normalization straight-through rate by document type, low-confidence extraction rate, analyst waiting time before `Credit analysis`, entity-mismatch aging, specialist backlog, and human override reasons. Gate criteria: extraction quality acceptable on target document classes, analyst queue not worsening, and specialist workload stable enough that escalations are not aging out.
4. Phase 3, months 7-9: Expand A4 coverage and tune A5, A6, and A9 by segment, with special attention to Credit-line renewal and High-exposure SME. Use A12-like workbench features only as needed inside operating procedures if control exceptions remain noisy, but do not add a fifth module in year 1. Monitoring metrics: frequency and aging of `KYC check -> Resolve entity mismatch`, return-loop rate `Resolve entity mismatch -> KYC check`, month-end four-eyes breaches, contract mismatch by product, and exception aging by owner. Gate criteria: sustained reduction in critical-path exception waits and no increase in compliance findings.
5. Phase 4, months 10-12: Harden the portfolio, rebalance staffing, and choose the year-2 option based on residual dominant delay. If equipment finance still shows `Wait for external valuation` as the main blocker, prioritize A7 next. If KYC-gated signing remains dominant after A9 control hardening, prioritize A10 next. Monitoring metrics: cycle time by variant, waiting-time heatmap at branch joins, analyst and compliance utilization, legal month-end load, and audit exceptions. Gate criteria: upstream controls remain stable, no overloaded specialist or analyst pool, and the next module is chosen from observed residual bottlenecks rather than from frequency alone.

# 7. Final recommendation

Recommend the bottleneck/synchronization portfolio: A4, A5, A6, and A9.

This is the best first-year trade-off because it improves both branches that actually determine downstream readiness, not just the visible clerical front end. A5 and A6 reduce the costly document loop and protect analyst capacity. A4 targets a low-frequency but high-criticality exception path: when entity mismatch appears, it is on the critical path in 82% of those cases and carries a 31-hour median wait. A9 satisfies the compliance requirement for a control-strengthening module while also reducing contract remediation and enforcing cleaner signing readiness.

The highest-frequency activity should not be the lead investment. A1 has real local labor savings, but `Register application` sits before the real constraints, and back-office utilization already falls below 65% after 15:00. Automating it first mainly accelerates flow into classification, KYC, document review, and analyst work without fixing the 52% repeated missing-document loop, the 20-hour normalization queue, the 31-hour entity-mismatch delay, or the contract-control failures. If management wants a politically visible early win, A5 is a better first move than A1 because it is still feasible, highly visible, and process-level material rather than merely high-frequency.
