# 1. Model scope and state variables

Build a discrete-event, resource-constrained digital twin of the permit system, from an applicant's preparation and portal submission through decision and any modeled appeal. Its purpose is comparative: it generates prospective cases and follows them through queues, calendars, parallel work, external delays, rework and decisions under explicitly specified policies. Preparation before submission is inside the boundary when assessing D1 and D7, so that effort and delay are not simply moved outside the measured process. Track post-decision appeals and safety outcomes where data permit; otherwise report them as uncertain, unvalidated outcomes. Do not represent the building construction process itself.

Use individual cases and events, with explicit municipal resources and shared, latent groups. Each case carries ID, submission and preparation times, type, district, architect, value, area and prior-violation status when available; fire, environmental and notice requirements; related-case and neighborhood identifiers; urgency; current stage and state of each required review; queue-entry and service-start/end times; due dates, rework counts, inspection readiness, notice status, decision and appeal status. Record which attributes are observed, imputed or scenario assumptions. Area, district sensitivity, prior violations and objection status are not reliably available: they cannot be silently treated as known.

Maintain separate queues for completeness, planning, fire, environment, inspection scheduling, field inspection, drafting and legal work. A task's waiting time runs from its actual eligibility for that task to service start; service is work time, and applicant response, statutory notice, travel and calendar closures are separate. Planning, fire and environmental branches may proceed concurrently when their prerequisites hold. Drafting is enabled only after every applicable review, notice and inspection result is complete or legitimately waived. Legal pre-check may run earlier under D5, but a final legal clearance still follows the necessary evidence and policy rules. Represent rework as explicit transitions to the appropriate earlier stage, with invalidation of affected downstream approvals, and model any appeal as a conditional post-decision event.

Resources are individual or skill-equivalent workers, including five clerks with one assigned to portal support on Mondays, eight planners including two restricted to Simple renovation, two FTE-equivalent fire reviewers available Tuesday–Thursday, three environmental reviewers with 40% non-permit time, four inspectors with field visits Tuesday–Friday, and two legal officers with a council-week restriction and an urgent-case exception. Use actual reconstructed daily shifts, holidays, absences and council dates where available; otherwise specify plausible schedules and uncertainty. Fire FTE is capacity, not necessarily two full-time people: reconcile the FTE definition and part-week roster with HR before calibration. Represent environmental non-permit work as blocked time or competing workload, not both. System tasks have effectively unlimited capacity; applicants and third parties have separate response processes. Work that cannot fit in a shift is paused and resumed unless a task requires an uninterrupted appointment. Inspectors consume travel and visit time, and schedules account for no-shows.

Cross-case state includes architect workload and weekly submission bursts, neighborhood objection episodes affecting multiple cases, and inspection bundles or routes by district and architect. A shared shock can delay or trigger rework across related cases, rather than drawing all case outcomes independently. Each case retains its own legal deadlines and outcomes. Track the age of a bundle and impose a maximum hold so an early case cannot wait indefinitely. Model legal and environmental queues as coupled through shared arrivals, case dependencies and finite capacity rather than assuming they share staff.

# 2. Parameter-estimation plan

- **Prepare the log.** Preserve original event IDs and timestamps, convert times and calendars consistently to the municipality's time zone, inspect duplicates, ties, missingness and activity labels, and distinguish submission, completion and start events. Join case attributes, roster, holiday, district, appeal and related-case data where authorized. Document truncation and whether the 18-month extract includes cases already open at its start or still open at its end. Avoid treating incomplete traces as completed cases.
- **Arrivals and case attributes.** Estimate a time-varying arrival intensity and case mix by calendar date, weekday and time of day, with the stated seasonal and Monday effects as checks. Use historical date-level counts or a calendar-conditioned count model with overdispersion. Explicitly generate architect-level batches and correlated attributes, including district and case type, so that the 31% share for the top 20 architects and within-week clusters can be reproduced. Eighteen months provide limited seasonal replication; quantify uncertainty and use alternative seasonal shapes and demand trends. Do not extrapolate a stationary interarrival mean into the future.
- **Service, waits and external durations.** Where actual starts and ends exist, estimate working-time service distributions by activity, case type, district and skill, and estimate waits from process enabling to start with the correct join semantics. Compare observed work against calendars and account for interruptions. Single completion timestamps and inter-event gaps alone do not identify service versus queueing; the supplied summaries are provisional priors or calibration targets, not proof of separate observed components. Obtain samples of start/end times, work records or expert estimates and jointly calibrate service and queue parameters against observed elapsed times and utilization. Model response times, report delays, notice and travel separately; use censoring-aware estimates for unfinished responses or cases. Test distributions such as lognormal or gamma and empirical distributions with supported tails, preserving heterogeneity and dependence. Avoid adding historical waiting-time draws on top of simulated queues.
- **Routing and rework.** Estimate conditional probabilities and loop counts from complete traces and enriched case attributes, pooling sparse categories and reporting uncertainty. Respect known mandatory fire, environmental and notice rules; infer unknown area and sensitivity with documented distributions or bounds, rather than equating a missing flag with no requirement. Distinguish the legal-check option in the supplied tree from its mandatory use for particular decisions. Classify a sampled set of unstructured rework notes with domain review, then estimate stage-specific return destinations and counts, including appeals where records exist. Rare removed emergency decisions require an explicit exclusion or separate scenario; the model should not pretend they are represented in the log.
- **Calendars and dependencies.** Validate reconstructed shifts with HR and staff, including part-time capacity, leave, legal closures, priority exceptions and inspection travel. Infer architect clusters from IDs, district and time, and use scheduling records to estimate bundle sizes, dispatch delays and travel savings. Use neighborhood and objection records if obtainable; otherwise fit a latent objection/extension model to notice-duration mixtures, subject to the 14-day minimum, with broad scenarios. A long notice is only a proxy, not a recorded objection. Flattened traces can miss shared events and causal links, so validate cluster assumptions externally.
- **PM4Py's role.** Use pandas and PM4Py to format the event log, inspect variants, discover DFGs and performance DFGs, parse and visualize the supplied process tree, and compare traces or alignments with a suitable model. The tree is a simplified control-flow reference, not the simulator: its labeled loop-redo activities do not fully encode the stated Planning/Legal/appeal returns, conditional attributes, external delays, capacity or inter-case coupling. Reconcile these differences with domain experts and document deviations. Performance DFG edge durations are elapsed times between adjacent recorded events, not identified service times or reliable parallel-branch waits. Feed estimated inputs to a separate calendar-aware discrete-event engine; PM4Py discovery alone does not supply a calibrated twin.

# 3. Assumptions

These are starting assumptions to test, not established facts.

- **Arrivals:** Portal submissions can occur outside office hours. Arrival counts and attributes are generated jointly by day and architect, with bursts and overdispersion. Forecast totals and type/district mix are scenario inputs, not necessarily identical to the historical 31,200-case mix.
- **Service times:** Activity work is positive and right-skewed, varies by case and resource skill, and excludes queueing, applicant response and off-shift time. Where starts are absent, use ranges anchored by the supplied median/p90 summaries; distributions and correlation are uncertain. System actions may consume finite elapsed time without constraining a human pool.
- **Routing:** Required reviews follow the stated rules and sampled missing attributes. Parallel branches join before drafting; notice has a minimum of 14 calendar days unless policy specifies otherwise. A no-inspection decision requires Simple renovation, value strictly below EUR 50,000 and verified absence of prior violations. Unknown eligibility is conservatively inspected until a verified alternative is specified. Reject and permit probabilities are conditional on case evidence, not chosen just to match throughput.
- **Resources/calendars:** Staff obey their skills, reconstructed shifts, holidays and legal restrictions; unspecified daily hours and council dates are provisional inputs. Monday clerk capacity is reduced by the portal assignment. Dispatch is FIFO within priority and skill constraints unless an intervention changes it. An inspector's working time includes travel; group visits do not erase service time.
- **Rework:** Completeness, planning, inspection, legal and appeal loops have stage-specific probabilities, destinations and work requirements. They may repeat; simulate a documented maximum iteration or explicit escalation state to prevent pathological infinite loops, and test sensitivity to it. Rework can invalidate already completed dependent work. Rare pathways are uncertain.
- **Applicant/third-party response:** Document returns, readiness, consultants and appeals are external processes, possibly heavy-tailed and correlated within architect or neighborhood; they do not occupy municipal capacity while waiting. D1 can add preparation time and cost and may change submission timing or abandonment. Treat those effects as uncertain scenarios rather than claiming free improvement.
- **Cross-case dependencies:** Relatedness uses architect, district and timing as imperfect proxies; independent and strong-clustering cases bound uncertainty. Objection shocks are shared within a plausible neighborhood and period, with durations and affected cases sampled from uncertain assumptions. Bundling requires compatible locations, readiness and appointments, subject to a maximum hold.
- **Non-stationary demand:** Seasonal, weekday and peak-month patterns persist only as a forecast scenario, with annual level, mix and shocks varied. Preserve calendar alignment across policies and include evolving demand and policy adoption; do not assume a steady state applies throughout.
- **Missing data:** Missing area, prior violations, district sensitivity, starts, objection events and rework reasons are imputed probabilistically or bounded and flagged. Account for missingness that may depend on case complexity; compare plausible missing-data mechanisms. Removed emergencies and unobserved post-decision harms limit generalization. Reconstructed calendars and imputed attributes are not ground truth.

# 4. Experiment matrix

Use the same forecast horizon, initial backlog, stochastic demand scenarios, staffing assumptions and outcome definitions for each comparison. Calibrate the baseline before interpreting intervention effects. Exact levels below are proposed design points for stakeholder agreement, not observed effectiveness estimates. An implementation has a start date, adoption/ramp and costs; run both immediate full adoption for mechanism testing and realistic phased adoption for decisions. Compare each intervention with the baseline and the combined policy with its constituents.

| Experiment | Factors and example levels | Expected trade-offs |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | Current rules, staffing, calendars and dispatch; no new intervention | Reference backlog, service, costs and unequal outcomes. |
| D1: document checker | Adoption 50%/90%; eligible missing-document probability reduced by 10%/30% relative; added preparation 0.5/2 days and applicant effort | Fewer loops and clerk requests versus pre-submission burden, digital exclusion and shifted demand. |
| D2: no-inspection rule | Apply specified value and verified-violation rule; adoption 50%/100%; vary missed-defect risk | Frees inspectors versus potential safety and equity effects; unknown history remains inspected. |
| D3: district batching | Minimum bundle 2/3, maximum hold 2/5 working days; group compatible architect/district cases | Less travel and higher capacity versus waiting for first cases and inequity in sparse districts. |
| D4: temporary inspector | Add one qualified inspector in March–May and September–November; vary actual availability 70%/100% | Shorter peak queues versus salary, onboarding and idle or shifted bottlenecks. |
| D5: parallel legal pre-check | Commercial and Complex variance: pre-check in parallel with reviews, with 50%/100% uptake; final clearance remains required; vary duplicate-work fraction | Earlier issue discovery versus more legal congestion and discarded work after changes. |
| D6: aging-case planner | Reserve one eligible planner each day for cases older than 30/60 calendar days; release unused capacity after a fixed period | Better tail and fairness versus longer waits for newer cases and reduced specialization flexibility. |
| D7: architect appointment slots | Target top-20 architects; 25%/50% of their Monday submissions shifted to later weekdays, with 50%/90% participation | Smoother queues versus applicant scheduling burden, delayed submission and possible access inequity. |
| D8: environmental triage | Prioritize by district and project attributes using two calibrated risk thresholds; retain all legally required reviews | Faster high-risk or straightforward cases versus misclassification, subgroup disparities and triage work; measure false classifications against an independent audited sample. |
| D9: objection early warning | Detection sensitivity 50%/80%, false-positive rate 10%/25%; advance engagement and vary its effect on extension length | Earlier resolution and planning versus staff work, false alarms and uncertain effectiveness. Preserve statutory notice minimum; warning does not mean objections are observed in the historical log. |
| D10: combined | D1 + D2 + D3 + D4 using their selected individual levels; also evaluate low/high efficacy combination | Possible synergy or congestion shift versus combined cost, eligibility and implementation complexity. |
| Additional combinations | D2 + D4; D3 + D4; D1 + D7; optionally a small factorial around D10 | Estimate interaction, such as whether staff reduce the benefit of batching; avoid assuming effects add. |
| Stress S1: demand | Baseline and leading policies under 25% higher annual volume and a 50% peak-month surge with unchanged capacity | Tests backlog persistence and capacity resilience. |
| Stress S2: capacity | Baseline and leading policies with 20% inspector absence plus an extra legal closure week | Tests sensitivity to service disruption and bottleneck migration. |
| Stress S3: external shocks | Baseline and leading policies with a shared neighborhood objection surge, longer applicant/consultant response tails and stronger architect clustering | Tests correlated delays and exposure of related cases. |
| Stress S4: adverse implementation | Baseline and leading policies with low adoption, weaker efficacy, high no-show rates and delayed temporary-inspector onboarding | Tests practical rather than idealized benefit. |

Stress cases are alternative plausible futures, not probability forecasts unless supported by evidence. Include normal and adverse parameter sets within each scenario, and compare policy differences within the same scenario. Apply policy to existing backlog only where legally and operationally appropriate, with an explicit transition rule.

# 5. Warm-up, replications, and uncertainty treatment

Initialize with the observed age, type, district and stage distribution of open cases and in-progress work at the forecast date when possible; this is preferable to inventing an empty system. For a synthetic initialization, simulate at least 12 months of prehistory with seasonally aligned arrivals and calendars, compare starting backlog, age and utilization at the same calendar date after 12 and 24 months, and extend if they remain materially different. Discard only the prehistory, not the beginning of the policy evaluation. If the system has a growing backlog, there may be no stationary warm-up; initialize from observed backlog and report transient results instead.

For each scenario simulate a two-year policy horizon, including at least one full seasonal cycle after ramp-up. Report calendar-period outcomes for all work and submission-cohort outcomes separately. Continue processing the cohort through a documented follow-up period, such as 12 months, without counting later submissions as cohort members; retain ongoing competing arrivals during follow-up. Report still-open cases and use censoring-aware estimates, rather than dropping unfinished long cases or assuming all have completed. Report backlog at horizon end and at follow-up. Examine whether results change with a longer follow-up.

Start with 100 independent replications per scenario/policy, then increase until 95% intervals for principal paired differences are decision-useful, e.g. half-width at most 2 percentage points for SLA compliance and 5% of baseline for p90 duration, or report when computational limits prevent this. Give each replication a reproducible seed and record code, data, parameter and policy versions. Use common random numbers for paired comparisons: key independent random streams to replication, case or shared group and event purpose, so a policy that changes routing does not accidentally reassign unrelated draws. Preserve within-group dependence. Compute paired differences within replication and report means and 95% intervals across independent replications; use replication-level or cluster-aware bootstrap for quantiles, fairness gaps and skewed outputs. Do not count cases from the same replication as independent replications. Present distributions and risk of adverse outcomes, not just averages.

Separate simulation noise from input and structural uncertainty. Draw parameter sets from bootstrap samples, fitted uncertainty distributions or elicited ranges, then run multiple stochastic replications per set; compare alternate objection, missing-data, dependence and demand models. Report within- and between-input variation and uncertainty due to model form. Vary influential factors jointly as well as one at a time, especially rework rates, service/wait split, objection prevalence and duration, travel savings, eligibility, no-shows, staffing and demand. For heavy tails, compare empirically supported lognormal, gamma and heavier-tail alternatives, estimate censoring explicitly, and test tail cutoffs or escalation assumptions. Report medians, p90/p95 and open-case proportions with uncertainty; avoid an arbitrary cap that hides long cases. If a finite cap is operationally required, state and stress-test it.

# 6. Validation strategy

- **Face validation:** Walk through representative simple, parallel-review, objection, bundled-inspection, legal rework and appeal cases with applicants, clerks, planners, inspectors, legal staff and policy owners. Verify required joins, calendars, escalation, policy eligibility and measured preparation burden. Check conservation of cases, no double booking, no work in prohibited hours and no release before required approvals.
- **Event-log replay:** Replay observed case attributes and event sequences against the reconciled control-flow model, checking conformance and the handling of concurrent branches, loops and incomplete cases. Inspect alignments or deviations by variant and case type. Replay can test whether observed paths are possible, but completion-only timestamps cannot reconstruct exact service starts, resource contention or missing objections; it does not validate counterfactual effects.
- **Distributional validation:** Generate historical-period simulations with observed calendar and demand inputs. Compare case-type and variant shares; median, p90 and full duration distributions; activity and inter-event gaps; queue ages, backlog over time, rework counts and notice durations; and cluster sizes and within-architect correlations. Compare both completed cohorts and censored open cases. Use uncertainty bands and prespecified tolerances, such as a 10% relative deviation for major-variant median/p90 and 5 percentage points for major routing shares, alongside graphical checks. Sparse rare variants need wider intervals.
- **Resource utilization:** Compare predicted busy time, queue length, throughput and overtime by role, weekday and season with rosters, timesheets and sampled observations, including the Monday clerk constraint, part-time fire capacity and legal closures. Unobserved utilization is an elicited or uncertain check, not a measured target. Verify realistic travel, idle time and specialization; calibrating durations alone can hide implausible utilization.
- **Bottlenecks:** Compare predicted buildup and delay at planning, environment, inspections and legal with staff reports and independent queue snapshots, including seasonal changes and interacting dependencies. Check that changing capacity in a controlled test produces plausible bottleneck movement rather than mechanically assuming a fixed bottleneck.
- **Out of sample:** Fit using an earlier time window and evaluate on a later held-out window with actual arrivals, calendars and policy conditions; also test another seasonal split if data allow. Keep tuning separate from final evaluation, and compare by case type, district and architect group. Monitor drift after deployment and recalibrate with newly measured starts, objections and outcomes. Backtests validate predictive behavior under historical conditions, not the causal effect of a novel policy; use a phased pilot or suitable quasi-experiment to estimate intervention effects and update the twin.

# 7. Decision criteria

Agree these illustrative thresholds with the municipality before looking at results, and use legally prescribed SLAs and safety rules where they exist. Measure duration both from submission to final decision and, for D1/D7, from start of preparation or request for a slot to decision; separately report applicant effort, abandonment and external waiting. Measure completed-case quantiles alongside cohort completion and censoring so a growing backlog cannot improve apparent performance by leaving difficult cases unfinished.

A candidate should show a practically meaningful improvement relative to baseline, such as at least 10% lower overall median or p90 submission-to-decision time and no more than a 5% increase in either for any case type. Report p95 and longest-open-case age as well. For each type, report the percentage decided within the applicable legal SLA; if no SLA exists, use agreed illustrative targets of 30, 60, 90 and 180 calendar days respectively for Simple renovation, New residential, Commercial and Complex variance. Aim for at least a 5 percentage-point gain in the share meeting the relevant target without a greater than 2 percentage-point loss in any type; never present these example thresholds as law.

Report the same duration, SLA and backlog measures by district, architect size and participation/eligibility group, with sample sizes and intervals. Aim for no more than a 5 percentage-point worsening of the worst district or architect-group SLA gap versus baseline, and no material degradation for small architects or applicants unable to use digital tools. Examine sparse groups over longer periods rather than interpreting noisy point estimates as precise. Track staff utilization by pool and season, overtime, queue age and inspector travel; investigate sustained utilization above roughly 85–90% as a congestion warning, not a universal hard cap. Require no sustained growth in age-adjusted backlog at the end of the evaluation and compare rework and repeat-inspection rates by case type.

Include implementation and recurring costs, applicant effort, error and appeal rates, safety outcomes, audit burden and statutory compliance. D2 and D8 need independent evidence on missed defects or triage errors; if those outcomes are unobserved, show bounds and treat safety as unresolved rather than inferring it from faster throughput. Select a feasible policy only if uncertainty intervals and adverse scenarios make its benefits credible, it meets legal and agreed equity/safety constraints, and gains persist under plausible parameter and demand changes. If options trade off outcomes, present the Pareto trade-off and recommend a monitored pilot rather than asserting an exact optimum.
