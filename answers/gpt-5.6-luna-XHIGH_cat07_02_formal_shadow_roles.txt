# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

Core activities are the stable work that defines a resource's operating responsibility. Peripheral activities may be important, but they are not sufficient by themselves to establish a formal role; they may be caused by handoffs, backup coverage, case ownership, or a temporary absence.

**Automated intake and entitlement precheck — R14_Nora.** A1 and the automated part of A2 are core because they dominate Nora's event profile and occur continuously. Human-facing routing is peripheral in the sense that Nora does not perform the downstream resolution. Its high frequency and low complexity should be measured as automation throughput, not compared directly with human productivity. Nora has no substitution episode or human decision authority in the evidence.

**Routine front-office service agents — R01_Amira and R02_Ben.** A2, A4, and A12 are core, with A1 and routine A13 also part of normal case ownership. A3, A5, A7, A8, A10, A11, and A14 are peripheral touches. Amira's higher medium/high exposure makes her a useful complexity tier or escalation-ready peer; Ben's distribution is more routine. Neither the similar profile nor the counts prove identical authority.

**Temporary front-office load support — R11_Kai and R12_Lina.** A1, A2, A4, and A12 are core for the low-complexity queue. Their few technical or negotiation events are peripheral and should not be interpreted as certification. Temporary substitution is evidence that they covered capacity, not evidence of permanent competence. The reopened cases show that entitlement documentation and closure quality need a control, possibly a permanent-agent review.

**Technical resolution analysts — R03_Chen and R04_Dina.** A3, A5, and A10 are core. A2, A6, A11, A12, A13, and A14 are peripheral but operationally meaningful: they reflect intake context, security adjacency, quality handoff, and rework or knowledge capture. The 0.94 similarity supports a common technical role, while complexity data supports differentiated assignment rules. Chen's critical-case exposure should not be averaged away with Dina's more moderate portfolio.

**Security risk specialist — R05_Erik.** A6 is the defining core activity. Other technical or case-maintenance activities are peripheral and support the security decision. The small event count is expected for a scarce risk gate; the high critical share makes the role operationally central. Mateo's low-risk filtering reduced false escalations but also coincided with two critical cases waiting more than two days, so the boundary between peripheral triage and required security review needs explicit safeguards.

**Commercial exception and negotiation specialist — R06_Fatima.** A7 and A8 are core. A2, A3, A9, A10, A12, and A13 are peripheral activities that provide context, prepare a decision, or manage the case after negotiation. Fatima's pricing certification and concentrated A7/A8 work support the functional role, but A9 remains a supervisor decision. Her absence is evidence of a substitution need, not evidence that Giorgio is a permanent equivalent.

**Senior front-office agent with commercial shadow capability — R07_Giorgio.** Routine front-office work remains core to the formal job, while A7/A8 become conditional core work for the shadow assignment. The 31 pricing reviews and 18 negotiations during Fatima's absence demonstrate useful coverage; the increase in median pricing duration from 38 to 74 minutes demonstrates a cost of substitution. Giorgio's complexity distribution suggests he can handle more than a routine agent, but does not establish that he has Fatima's full decision rights.

**Supervisor and approval authority — R08_Hana.** A9 is the core control point. A7/A8, A11, and A13 are peripheral review or escalation context. The 13 rechecks after Mateo's preliminary approvals are direct evidence that authority and work execution are separable. Staffing this role requires signed decision accountability and coverage, not merely finding someone with a similar activity vector.

**Quality gatekeeper and knowledge steward — R09_Ivan and R10_Julia.** A11 and A14 are core. Technical, security, implementation, closure, and reopening events are peripheral inspection or feedback work. Their 0.97 similarity supports a shared role, with late/day coverage providing resilience. High quality-check counts should not be treated as production throughput: the activity exists to control the output of other roles.

**Incident coordinator and escalation broker — R13_Mateo.** Coordination is a cross-case function visible in A13, specialist handoffs, and preliminary A9 activity; if the literal `Coordinate` activity exists in the raw log, it should be retained rather than collapsed into A13. A1-A12 and A14 are peripheral context or routing work. Mateo's high critical exposure and broad activity reach indicate brokerage, while the Hana rechecks show that brokerage does not confer final approval authority. The Enterprise Security episode shows both the value and the risk of this peripheral triage function.

# 3. Shadow and bridging roles

**R07_Giorgio as a commercial shadow.** Giorgio is formally a Senior Service Agent, but his pricing certification, A7/A8 activity, and 0.71 similarity to Fatima make him more than an ordinary front-office backup. The absence episode is strong evidence of a functioning shadow arrangement: he performed 31 pricing reviews and 18 negotiations. It is not evidence that he permanently owns commercial exceptions. The longer median duration under substitution suggests a workable resilience measure with a capacity and training penalty. Management should define which pricing decisions Giorgio may prepare, which he may recommend, and which still require Fatima or Hana.

**R13_Mateo as a cross-functional escalation broker.** Mateo connects technical, security, commercial, quality, and supervisory work. He can reduce unnecessary escalation by filtering low-risk security referrals and can keep reopened cases moving, which explains the reduced false-escalation complaints. The two critical cases waiting more than two days show the risk of an over-centralized broker or an overly aggressive filter. Mateo's 21 preliminary approvals during Hana's absence and the 13 later rechecks demonstrate delegated preparation, not equal approval authority. His role should have explicit triage criteria, a critical-case override, and service-level monitoring.

**R05_Erik as a scarce critical specialist and risk boundary.** Erik is not a high-volume cluster member; he is a rare intervention point with 96 security reviews, median complexity 0.86, and 55 critical cases. His role bridges technical resolution and formal risk control. Treating him as a general technical analyst would obscure the need for on-call capacity and independent security sign-off. The benefit of routing only appropriate cases to him is focus and lower false escalation; the risk is that a filter or shortage delays a genuinely critical review.

**R14_Nora as an automation boundary role.** Nora bridges the external request to human work, but the bridge is a system control rather than a human organizational role. Its high volume should be separated from staffing calculations. The process owner should monitor precheck false positives/negatives and the handoff to R01/R02/R11/R12, because a clean automated A1/A2 profile can hide downstream entitlement rework.

**R09/R10 as quality bridging roles.** Ivan and Julia connect resolution work to closure and organizational learning. Their A11 and A14 work is a gate and feedback loop, not merely another task cluster. They provide a useful independent check on whether technical, commercial, and temporary front-office work is complete and correctly documented.

# 4. Ambiguous assignments and alternative interpretations

The following assignments should remain hypotheses until policy, authorization, outcome, and handover data are checked.

**R07_Giorgio.** One interpretation is a senior generalist who occasionally assists commercial work. Another is a formally designated commercial deputy with authority to review or negotiate within limits. The current data supports the first as the formal title and the second as a plausible shadow capability: pricing certification, 72 A7, 88 A8, and the Fatima substitution support the deputy hypothesis, while the longer substitution duration argues against assuming full equivalence. Delegation records, price thresholds, signed decisions, exception outcomes, and post-substitution rework would decide between them.

**R13_Mateo.** One interpretation is a coordinator who routes work without decision authority. Another is a de facto incident manager who makes risk and approval decisions under time pressure. His broad activity reach, 76 critical cases, low-risk security filtering, and preliminary A9 events support strong influence; Hana's 13 rechecks argue against final approval authority. Approval signatures, escalation policy, queue-routing timestamps, and whether Mateo's recommendations are binding would decide the assignment.

**R05_Erik.** One interpretation is a narrowly scoped security reviewer invoked only at A6. Another is the organization's critical-security risk owner who should participate earlier in diagnosis and implementation. His A6 concentration and 55 critical cases support a scarce reviewer, while his occasional A3/A5/A10/A13/A14 work could indicate a broader risk-owner remit. Security sign-off requirements, case-level referral rules, and the consequences of cases resolved without Erik would distinguish the two.

**R03_Chen and R04_Dina.** One interpretation is one technical role with day/late-shift coverage. Another is a senior complex-case analyst (Chen) and a standard technical analyst (Dina). The 0.94 similarity supports the common role, but Chen's 42 critical cases and 0.71 median complexity versus Dina's 0.48 support a capability tier. Assignment rules, certification level, review rights, and complexity-adjusted resolution outcomes would decide whether the tier is formal.

**R01_Amira and R02_Ben.** One interpretation is a single interchangeable routine service-agent role. Another is a generalist/mentor tier (Amira) and a high-volume queue tier (Ben). Their 0.93 similarity supports common membership; Amira's larger medium/high portfolio and the title note that she is an experienced generalist support a distinction in practical responsibility. Training records, queue permissions, coaching assignments, and complexity-adjusted quality and service-time measures would decide.

**R09_Ivan and R10_Julia.** One interpretation is two equivalent quality analysts providing shift coverage. Another is a differentiated quality role in which Julia is the stronger knowledge-base steward and Ivan handles somewhat more complex or late-shift inspections. The 0.97 similarity and nearly identical A11 volume support the peer interpretation; the A14 split (96 versus 104), shift difference, and modest complexity difference leave the second possible. Ownership metadata for KB changes, sign-off permissions, defect-detection outcomes, and shift handovers would decide.

**R06_Fatima and R07_Giorgio.** One interpretation is a specialist plus a backup. Another is a distributed commercial capability in which both are permanent commercial decision makers. Fatima's pricing title, certification, and concentrated A7/A8 work support specialist-plus-backup; Giorgio's pricing certification and substitution support a real secondary capability. Approval thresholds, commercial outcome quality, and whether Giorgio's events are signed as recommendations or decisions would decide.

**R14_Nora and human entitlement agents.** One interpretation is that A2 is a homogeneous entitlement activity and Nora belongs with front office. Another is that Nora performs only deterministic prevalidation and humans retain substantive entitlement authority. The system contract, 24/7 pattern, low complexity, and lack of downstream work support the boundary interpretation. Rule-exception rates, manual overrides, and audit responsibility for failed entitlement checks would decide.

**R11_Kai and R12_Lina.** One interpretation is a temporary version of the permanent front-office role. Another is a deliberately restricted queue-closure role with no entitlement discretion. Their near-identical low-complexity work and temporary contracts support either; the 17 reopened cases traced to incomplete notes favor restricted work plus a stronger review gate. Training completion, queue permissions, note-quality audits, and case assignment policy would decide.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

The following is illustrative PM4Py-style code. Exact keyword names can vary slightly by PM4Py release; the important point is to pass the non-default keys explicitly and to treat the outputs as behavioral evidence. The code assumes a raw event CSV with the columns in the question.

```python
import networkx as nx
import pandas as pd
import pm4py

CASE = "case:concept:name"
ACTIVITY = "concept:name"
TIMESTAMP = "time:timestamp"
RESOURCE = "org:resource"

# Keep the case attributes on every event if they are already present there.
events = pd.read_csv("events.csv")
events[TIMESTAMP] = pd.to_datetime(events[TIMESTAMP], utc=True)
events["case_complexity"] = pd.to_numeric(
    events["case_complexity"], errors="coerce"
)

# Normalize common CSV representations of the substitution flag.
events["temporary_substitution_bool"] = (
    events["temporary_substitution"]
    .astype(str).str.strip().str.lower()
    .isin({"true", "1", "yes", "y", "t"})
)

events = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    events,
    case_id=CASE,
    activity_key=ACTIVITY,
    timestamp_key=TIMESTAMP,
)
log = pm4py.convert_to_event_log(events)

params = {
    "case_id_key": CASE,
    "activity_key": ACTIVITY,
    "timestamp_key": TIMESTAMP,
    "resource_key": RESOURCE,
}

# 1. Discover behavioral roles. The result is a hypothesis, not a formal
#    organization chart.
role_result = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(log, parameters=params)
roles = pd.DataFrame(role_result)

# Normalize the common PM4Py output naming. Inspect roles.columns in a local
# installation if a release returns a differently named role column.
roles = roles.rename(columns={"role": "discovered_role"})
if "discovered_role" not in roles.columns and "org:role" in roles.columns:
    roles = roles.rename(columns={"org:role": "discovered_role"})
if RESOURCE not in roles.columns and "resource" in roles.columns:
    roles = roles.rename(columns={"resource": RESOURCE})

role_map = roles[[RESOURCE, "discovered_role"]].drop_duplicates()

# 2. Activity-based similarity. Depending on the PM4Py version this may be a
#    graph, an edge table, or a matrix-like object; retain and inspect it.
similarity = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(
    log, parameters=params
)
print("activity-based similarity:")
print(similarity)

# 3. Handover and working-together networks describe relations, not authority.
handover = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(log, parameters=params)
working_together = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(
    log, parameters=params
)

def network_edges(network):
    if isinstance(network, nx.Graph):
        return nx.to_pandas_edgelist(network)
    if isinstance(network, pd.DataFrame):
        return network
    # Useful for releases that return an iterable of edge records.
    return pd.DataFrame(network)

handover_edges = network_edges(handover)
working_edges = network_edges(working_together)
print("handover edges", handover_edges.head(20))
print("working-together edges", working_edges.head(20))

# Optional focused view of substitution events. This is descriptive only:
# substitution is not proof of a permanent role or certification.
substitution_log = pm4py.filter_event_attribute_values(
    log,
    attribute="temporary_substitution",
    values=[True, "True", "true", 1, "1", "yes"],
    retain=True,
)
print("substitution variants", pm4py.get_variants(substitution_log))

# 4. Join the discovered resource role to case-level complexity and
#    substitution indicators. Case attributes are reduced to one record per
#    case before joining to avoid double-counting them.
case_features = (
    events.groupby(CASE, as_index=False)
    .agg(
        case_complexity_median=("case_complexity", "median"),
        case_complexity_max=("case_complexity", "max"),
        temporary_substitution=("temporary_substitution_bool", "max"),
    )
)

annotated = (
    events.merge(role_map, on=RESOURCE, how="left", validate="many_to_one")
          .merge(case_features, on=CASE, how="left", validate="many_to_one",
                 suffixes=("", "_case"))
)

# Resource-level summaries make the complexity distinction visible.
resource_summary = (
    annotated.groupby(RESOURCE, as_index=False)
    .agg(
        discovered_role=("discovered_role", "first"),
        events=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
        median_case_complexity=("case_complexity_median", "median"),
        high_complexity_events=("case_complexity_median", lambda s: (s >= 0.66).sum()),
        substitution_event_share=("temporary_substitution", "mean"),
    )
)

role_activity_complexity = (
    annotated.groupby(["discovered_role", ACTIVITY], dropna=False)
    .agg(
        event_count=(ACTIVITY, "size"),
        median_case_complexity=("case_complexity_median", "median"),
        substitution_share=("temporary_substitution", "mean"),
    )
    .reset_index()
)

# Compare similarity within complexity strata rather than only on totals.
annotated["complexity_band"] = pd.cut(
    annotated["case_complexity_median"],
    bins=[-float("inf"), 0.33, 0.66, float("inf")],
    labels=["low", "medium", "high"],
)
stratified_profile = (
    annotated.groupby([RESOURCE, "complexity_band", ACTIVITY], dropna=False)
    .size().rename("events").reset_index()
)

# Formal authority must be joined from policy/HR data, not inferred from
# role discovery. This small example shows the separation explicitly.
formal_authority = pd.DataFrame({
    RESOURCE: ["R05_Erik", "R06_Fatima", "R08_Hana", "R13_Mateo"],
    "formal_authority": [
        "security review/sign-off",
        "commercial recommendation or exception review",
        "final supervisor approval",
        "coordination and delegated preliminary review",
    ],
})
resource_summary = resource_summary.merge(
    formal_authority, on=RESOURCE, how="left"
)

# Do not call a discovered cluster a role until these are reviewed together:
# complexity-stratified profiles, substitution/rework, handover direction,
# working-together ties, formal permissions, and quality/service outcomes.
print(resource_summary)
print(role_activity_complexity)
print(stratified_profile)
```

In practice, I would also inspect variants, service time, and reopened-case outcomes by discovered role. A role with many events but low complexity, such as Nora or the temporary agents, should not be ranked above a low-volume role with a high critical-case share, such as Erik. Handover and working-together edges can show who routes work to whom or who collaborates, but they do not by themselves establish reporting lines, authorization, or accountability.

# 6. Operational interpretation

For staffing, maintain separate capacity plans for automated throughput, routine front office, technical resolution, commercial exceptions, quality control, supervision, and critical security work. Schedule day/late coverage deliberately: R03 and R09 provide late-shift capability, R04 and R10 provide day coverage, and Nora provides continuous intake. Keep protected availability for Erik and Hana rather than reallocating them solely because their event counts are small. Use Giorgio as planned commercial backup and Mateo as a broker with a critical-case escalation override, not as an unbounded single point of failure.

For training, give Kai and Lina stronger entitlement-note and closure training, followed by permanent-agent review until reopen rates are acceptable. Cross-train Giorgio on documented pricing thresholds, negotiation boundaries, and escalation to Hana. Give Mateo explicit security triage rules and critical-case detection training. Use Ivan and Julia's quality findings to update training for front office and technical staff, and preserve their independence as a gate.

For risk control, separate preparation, specialist review, and final approval in the audit trail. Record whether an event is a recommendation, a delegated preliminary check, or a binding decision. Put a service-level timer on critical security referrals so low-risk filtering cannot delay them. Monitor substitution duration, reopen rates, missing entitlement notes, quality defects, and critical-case waiting time; the Fatima and Hana episodes show that continuity can be achieved while decision time or rework worsens.

For resilience, maintain a documented deputy matrix with expiry and scope: Giorgio for bounded commercial coverage, a named security backup or on-call arrangement for Erik, and a supervisor coverage procedure for Hana. Review the matrix after absences rather than converting every successful substitution into a permanent role. Use complexity-stratified workload forecasts so the organization has enough ordinary capacity without sacrificing rare critical expertise.

Two cautions are especially important. First, mined activity profiles describe observed behavior, not formal authority, competence, or accountability; similar profiles for Giorgio and Fatima, or Hana and Mateo, can conceal very different decision rights. Second, temporary substitution proves that work was performed during a coverage episode, not that the substitute is permanently certified. More generally, historical volume is confounded by automation, queue assignment, absences, and opportunities to receive critical cases, so staffing changes should be validated with policy records, complexity-adjusted quality, service time, rework, and risk outcomes before implementation.
