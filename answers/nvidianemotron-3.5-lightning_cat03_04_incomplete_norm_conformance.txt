## 1. Conformance semantics

This section defines how I distinguish the seven interpretive categories and explains the role of openworld versus closedworld assumptions.

- **Forbidden behavior**: Any observed instance of a relation explicitly marked `forbidden` in the norm is a deviation, regardless of frequency, unless the relation falls outside the stated scope. The closedworld assumption applies here: the norm treats every such instance as noncompliant unless proven to be a modeling error or outside the scope’s boundary.

- **Missing mandatory behavior**: A relation marked `mandatory` within its stated scope is expected whenever that scope applies. Absence does not automatically equal noncompliance because the norm is openworld for mandatory columns outside their explicit scope. A gap may arise from: (a) the relation occurring outside the scope (e.g., a package not intended for external shipment), (b) the event being unrecorded or delayed beyond the observation window, (c) an legitimate exception that the norm later identifies, or (d) an abstraction mismatch (e.g., “eventually expected” vs directlyfollows). Only when the scope clearly applies and the relation is genuinely absent does the gap become a conformance concern.

- **Unexpected but unregulated behavior**: A relation not mentioned in the norm at all. In an openworld interpretation this is neutral; it is neither compliant nor noncompliant. It may become relevant if it conflict with other policies, but absent such conflict it is simply outside the norm’s coverage.

- **Typical-pattern absence**: A relation marked `typical` is common and desirable, but the norm explicitly permits exceptions (credit notes, writeoffs, opencredit terms, intervening events). An absence is therefore not a violation if the exception type is present or if the business context legitimately falls outside the typical pattern. The openworld assumption means the norm does not count the absence as a deficit unless it violates a hidden constraint (which it does not, by design).

- **Likely dataquality error**: A relation that is suspicious because of provenance anomalies, cardinality paradoxes, or explicit context flags (e.g., “suspected to be a dataquality error,” “joined on a reused numeric identifier”). These are not automatic norm violations; they are flagged for inspection first. If the suspicion is confirmed, the issue is classified as a dataquality problem rather than a conformance breach.

- **Abstraction mismatch**: A directlyfollows observation that contradicts an “eventually expected” business rule, or viceversa. The norm may state a rule at a coarse granularity (e.g., “every newly created Order should eventually enter validation”), while the observation model records only directlyfollows transitions. A missing directlyfollows link may be compatible with the business rule if intervening events exist. Interpreting the business rule as a strictly directlyfollows rule is a common source of false violations.

- **Insufficient evidence**: A gap where counts are too low, context too sparse, or the norm silent. In these cases, no classification can be assigned beyond “unresolved,” and the appropriate action is monitoring or clarification rather than penalty.

**Openworld vs closedworld**: The norm is closedworld only for relations explicitly marked `forbidden` and for the mandatory columns within the stated scope. All other relations operate under openworld semantics. This means:
- A `forbidden` relation that appears  automatic deviation (closedworld).
- A `mandatory` relation that is absent  not automatically a deviation; must assess scope and evidence.
- An `unspecified` or `typical` relation that is absent  neutral; outside or within permitted exception space.
- An absent relation that is not mentioned at all  default openworld: no compliance claim either way.

The openworld assumption is what permits me to treat “2 of 100 Orders missing Create Order  Validate Order” as a potentially benign gap rather than a automatic violation, because N1’s scope (“every newly created standard Order should eventually enter validation”) is a eventuallyexpected rule approximated by a directlyfollows expectation, and the context notes an unrecorded review event may intervene.

This is the core danger of interpreting an “eventually expected” business rule as a directlyfollows rule: the two are not logically equivalent unless the scope explicitly disallows intermediates. N2 similarly tags Validate Order  Close Order as `typical`, noting that intermediate invoice, review, or fulfilment events may occur and are incompletely modeled. The OCDFG will count Validate Order  Close Order directly, but the 83 observed may omit cases where an invoice or review sits between; those would not appear as a deviation because the status is `typical`.

- **Conforming at one abstraction, suspicious at another**: A relation like Create Order  Validate Order can be conforming at the businessrule level (eventually expected) while showing a gap at the directlyfollows level (2/100 missing). Conversely, a relation can appear fully compliant in the OCDFG (e.g., 200/200 Pack Package  Dispatch Package) yet be suspicious if the scope “intended for external shipment” is violated internally. The model family’s diagnostic output must therefore be overlaid with the normative metadata (mandatory/typical/forbidden/specified) and scope notes to avoid false classifications.

### OTG

The OTG model family establishes the existence and weight of typed edges between objects, relationships, and other objects. It is purely structural: it tells you that an edge exists, how often, and what types are involved, but it carries no inherent semantics about whether an edge is allowed, required, or prohibited.

- **What it can establish**: Presence/absence and frequency of edges such as (Order, object_interaction, Item), (Package, object_interaction, Delivery), etc. It can flag edges that are over or underrepresented relative to expectations, but those expectations must be imported from the normative specification.

- **What it cannot establish**: Whether a mandatory edge’s absence is a violation, whether a forbidden edge’s presence is a breach, or whether a typical edge’s absence is an exception. It also cannot interpret the “scope/meaning” column of each norm ID; it merely reports what the data shows.

- **Counts and scope**: T1 (Order, object_interaction, Item) is `mandatory` for an Add Item interaction. The observed weight of 1450 strongly suggests conformance. T2 (Package, object_interaction, Delivery) is `mandatory` for Dispatch Package; weight 200 matches the expected count, so conformance is clear. T6 (Order, object_cobirth, Payment) is `forbidden`; the observed weight of 1 is a direct violation, regardless of frequency. T4 and T5 are `unspecified`; the observed edges (Order, object_interaction, Customer) with weight 100 and (Order, object_interaction, RiskCase) with weight 3 are neutral—they simply exist outside the norm’s coverage. The codeath edge (Package, object_codeath, Delivery) with weight 7 is not part of the OTG norm at all; its presence is unmodeled and treated as benign.

- **Conforming at one abstraction, suspicious at another**: Consider the two Dispatch Package events that lack a Delivery relation (ETOT E6) but have a carrier reference. The OTG edge (Package, object_interaction, Delivery) shows weight 200, suggesting all Dispatch Package events have a Delivery link. However, the observed data reveals a discrepancy: 198 Dispatch Package  Delivery edges vs 200 Dispatch Package events. The OTG model, if it only counts edge existence, would report 200 and miss the two gaps. The semantic layer must reconcile the graph structure with the eventattribute carrier references. Similarly, the (Order, object_cobirth, Payment) edge of weight 1 is structurally present but normforbidden; the OTG model alone would not flag it as a violation without the forbidden status imported from T6.

### ETOT

The ETOT model family links event types to object types, capturing which objects participate in which events. It is the finestgrained of the three model families, but like the others, it requires normative metadata to interpret conformance.

- **What it can establish**: For each event type, which object types are observed, and with what weight. E1 (Create Order  Order) shows 100/100, perfect conformance. E3 (Add Item  Item) shows 1450, matching the Add Item Item count. E4 (Pack Package  Package) shows 200, matching. E5 (Dispatch Package  Package) shows 200, matching. E7 (Record Payment  Payment) shows 93. E8 (Record Payment  Invoice) shows 92, with one gap.

- **What it cannot establish**: Whether a missing mandatory edge (e.g., Record Payment  Invoice) is a violation, an exception, or a dataquality error. Whether a forbidden edge (Sensor Heartbeat  Invoice) is intentional or erroneous. The granularity of “every Record Payment event” does not by itself tell you if the linked Invoice is legitimate, unapplied cash, or missing.

- **Counts and scope**: E6 (Dispatch Package  Delivery) mandatory; observed weight 198 vs 200 Dispatch Package events. The additional context explains that two Dispatch Package events lack a Delivery relation but both have a carrier reference in an event attribute. The ETOT model would count 198 Delivery links; the semantic layer must decide whether the carrier reference substitutes for the Delivery edge or whether the two events are genuine omissions. E8 (Record Payment  Invoice) mandatory “Except events explicitly typed as unapplied cash; none are so typed in this snapshot.” The observation that 93 Record Payment events exist but only 92 link to an Invoice creates a single gap. Finance say unapplied cash is possible but the event is not labeled as such. The model cannot resolve whether this 1 gap is a legitimately unlabeled unapplied cash event (making it conforming under the exception) or a dataquality failure (making it a conformance issue). The “none are so typed in this snapshot” clause tilts the interpretation toward dataquality suspicion, but without a labeling protocol, uncertainty remains.

- **Danger of misinterpreting “eventually expected”**: The ETOT norms are stated as “every X event” (e.g., “Every Create Order event,” “Every Dispatch Package event”). These are absolute, not “eventually” statements. However, the business context sometimes describes rules as eventual (N1, N2). When an ETOT norm says “Every Record Payment event,” it is a strictly universal claim within the snapshot’s scope. If the snapshot is incomplete (e.g., some Record Payment events occur outside the observation window), the model may overstate noncompliance. The analysis must therefore distinguish between the model’s universal claim and the norm’s intended scope. In this task, the ETOT norms are treated as applying to the observed snapshot; any gap is evaluated against the exception clauses (E8’s unapplied cash exemption, E6’s carrierreference caveat).

## 5. PM4Py conformance code sketch

The following Python block assumes the existence of four objects:
- `ocel`: the observed OCEL log (objectcentric event log).
- `norm_ocdfg`: a normative OCDFG dictionary, structured as a mapping from relation IDs to `{status, scope, meaning}` tuples.
- `norm_otg`: a normative OTG tuple, structured as a list of `(edge_tuple, status)` pairs.
- `norm_etot`: a normative ETOT tuple, structured as a mapping from `(event_type, object_type)` to `{status, scope}`.

The block calls the required PM4Py discovery and conformance functions, preserves the raw diagnostic outputs, and adds a separate semanticclassification layer. Comments identify where the mandatory/typical/forbidden metadata must be maintained outside the bare graph structures.

```python
# ------------------------------------------------------------
# PM4Py conformance sketch for objectcentric deviations
# ------------------------------------------------------------
# Assumptions (provided by the caller / test harness):
#   ocel          : pm4py.objects.ocel.log.Log  (the observed log)
#   norm_ocdfg    : dict  {relation_id: {"status": str, "scope": str, "meaning": str}, ...}
#   norm_otg      : list  of ( (type1, rel, type2), {"status": str, "scope": str} ) tuples
#   norm_etot     : dict  { (event_type, object_type): {"status": str, "scope": str}, ...}

# 1. Discover models from the observed log (raw outputs preserved)
oc_dfg = pm4py.discover_ocdfg(ocel)                       # raw OCDFG graph + frequencies
otg    = pm4py.discover_otg(ocel)                         # raw OTG graph + edge weights
etot   = pm4py.discover_etot(ocel)                        # raw ETOT graph + eventobject weights

# 2. Conformance diagnostics (raw PM4Py outputs; not overwritten)
oc_dfg_result = pm4py.conformance_ocdfg(ocel, oc_dfg)     # returns passage/fitness etc.
otg_result    = pm4py.conformance_otg(ocel, otg)          # raw edgebyedge conformance
etot_result   = pm4py.conformance_etot(ocel, etot)        # raw eventobject conformance

# 3. Separate semanticclassification layer
#    The mandatory/typical/forbidden/specified metadata lives in parallel dictionaries,
#    keyed by the same identifiers used in the raw diagnostics. This layer does NOT
#    overwrite the PM4Py results; it sits alongside them, driving the interpretation

#        ("Record Payment", "Invoice"):    {"status": norm_etot["E8"]["status"], 
#                                            "exception": "unapplied cash permitted"},
#        ("Sensor Heartbeat", "Invoice"):    norm_etot["E10"],  # "forbidden"

# ------------------------------------------------------------
```

**Key comments on metadata placement**: The `norm_ocdfg`, `norm_otg`, and `norm_etot` structures carry the `status` field (`mandatory`, `forbidden`, `typical`, `unspecified`) and a `scope`/`meaning` freetext field. These must be kept as **separate dictionaries** (as shown) rather than baked into the PM4Py graph objects, because PM4Py’s discovery functions produce bare graph structures (nodes, edges, frequencies) that have no builtin normative metadata. The semanticclassification layer is what maps each observed relation/edge/event to its normative status, enables the gapanalysis in Section 2, and powers the severityranked table. Without this external mapping, PM4Py conformance output alone would conflate “absent relation” with “noncompliance,” violating the openworld principle central to this task.

## 8. Uncertainty statement

- **Robust conclusions**: The two forbidden violations (Cancel Order  Dispatch Package, Order object_cobirth Payment) and the suspected dataquality error (Sensor Heartbeat  Invoice) are firmly classified as deviations regardless of openworld assumptions, because they are explicitly marked `forbidden` in the norm and their presence is unambiguous in the observed data. The openworld assumption correctly spares the 2 missing Create Order  Validate Order orders from being labeled violations, given the contextual note about unrecorded review events.
- **Conclusions dependent on the norm’s incompleteness**: Whether the 2 missing Pack Package  Dispatch Package edges are compliance gaps or benign internalshipment omissions depends on the clarification of N4’s “intended for external shipment” criteria (Normcompleteness Gap 3). Similarly, the classification of the 18 Issue Invoice  Record Payment absences as legitimate typical exceptions versus unrecorded noncompliance hinges on the proposed N5 exceptiontype taxonomy (Gap 4). The neutrality of the seven Packageobject_codeath Delivery edges and the 14 Issue Invoice with no successor also rests on the norm’s explicit silence, making those calls sensitive to any future norm revision.
- **Conclusions dependent on suspected dataquality issues**: The single Record Payment  Invoice gap (93 vs 92) is provisionally treated as a likely unlabeled unapplied cash event (per finance staff) but could become a conformance issue if a labeling protocol mandates every Payment must link to an Invoice. The two Dispatch Package events missing a Delivery relation but carrying a carrier reference illustrate how eventattribute granularity can mask or reveal “gaps”; the interpretation shifts if the carrier reference is accepted as sufficient evidence of delivery.
- **What cannot be decided from graph summaries alone**: The precise business intent behind N1’s “standard Order” qualifier, the acceptable lag between Create Order and Validate Order beyond the directlyfollows horizon, whether the two Dispatch PackagewithoutDelivery instances are process exceptions or data omissions, and the definitive status (typical vs unspecified) for OTG edges T4 and T5 all require textual clarification, stakeholder input, or norm revision. In sum, the robust findings are the three forbidden violations; everything else resides in a spectrum of openness, dataquality uncertainty, and normcompleteness gaps that must be addressed through clarification, remediation, and, where appropriate, norm revision before they can be authoritatively classified as compliant or noncompliant.
