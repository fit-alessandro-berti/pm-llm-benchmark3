# 2. Core versus peripheral activities

The recurring pattern in this log: **peripheral  unimportant**. Peripheral activities often carry disproportionate complexity, risk, or authority, and substitutions temporarily move activities across the core/periphery boundary.

**Automated intake (Nora).** Core = 100% of events on A1/A2. The peripheral set is empty by design; any bot drift into new activities is a process change, not role evolution. Authority: finalize routine intake; the 1,640 human A2 validations show a large exception path that must remain staffed. Her volume (highest in the log) says nothing about organizational position.

**Front-office agents (Amira, Ben).** Core A1/A2/A4/A12  86–91% of events. Amira's peripheral set (A3 75, A8 42, A5 30) is low in count but carries most of her 58 high + 9 critical cases — her "peripheral" work is where the senior generalist role lives. Substitution: fragment 6 shows permanent agents absorbing temp-induced rework, a structurally allocated peripheral duty. Authority: closure of routine cases; no exception approval.

**Contingent support (Kai, Lina).** Core = 99% of events on four intake activities, medians 0.18/0.19, zero critical. The 17-reopen incident shows that for this role the risk sits *inside* a core activity (A2 documentation quality), not in peripherals. Authority: close low-complexity cases only. A frequency-only reading ("same as Ben") fails on contract, certification, and outcome data.

**Technical, complex tier (Chen).** Core A3/A5/A10  81%; median 0.71, 42 critical. Peripheral A6 (24 events) is security-adjacent and critical-heavy — small count, high risk density. His late shift makes him the de facto evening technical escalation point; shift is part of the role definition and invisible to activity clustering.

**Technical, standard tier (Dina).** Same core trio at nearly identical counts, but median 0.48 and 9 critical; A6 mostly routed away from her (6 vs Chen's 24). The divergence is in routing policy and exposure, not repertoire — if this were one undifferentiated pool, the complexity distributions would match.

**Commercial specialist (Fatima).** Core A7/A8 = 73% of events; median 0.67. Peripheral A9=16 is authority-bearing: she approves routine pricing exceptions, while complex ones escalate to Hana (fragment 4). **Senior front office / commercial shadow (Giorgio).** Core by frequency: front-office trio plus A8=88 ( Fatima's 90 — negotiation is co-primary even outside substitution, per fragment 4). Peripheral: A7 pricing (72 total, but 31 came from six absence days  baseline  41, genuinely occasional), and 20 A9 events that are small in count yet authority-relevant. Complexity 0.58 — the highest front-office exposure. The 3874 min substitution duration shows the peripheral competence carries a real capacity cost.

**Security specialist (Erik).** Core A6 = 56% of the smallest permanent log (170 events), median 0.86, 55 critical. **Coordinator/broker (Mateo).** His core is invisible to counting: no activity exceeds 12% of his log, and his frequent activities are all "movement" (A12 52, A2 50, A4 45, A9 44, A13 42). # 3. Shadow and bridging roles

**R07_Giorgio — commercial shadow role.** Formally a Senior Service Agent in Front Office, but dual-certified (entitlement + pricing), and fragment 4 shows negotiation alternating between him and Fatima routinely. A7 is the clearer shadow activity: 31 of his 72 pricing reviews occurred during Fatima's six absence days, and process-level pricing exception duration roughly doubled (3874 min). *Benefits:* business continuity during specialist absence; a senior generalist able to run commercial conversations on complex front-office cases (120 high + 36 critical); cheap redundancy for a single-specialist function. *Risks:* roughly halved throughput during substitution (must be pre-planned, not discovered); possible inconsistency in pricing decisions versus the certified specialist; the informal arrangement can mask a structural single point of failure in Commercial; duration and quality effects must be monitored. *Verdict:* legitimate and valuable — formalize it (keep the certification current, define exception limits, sample-review substitution-era pricing decisions) rather than ban it or promote him to co-specialist without evidence. Successful substitution is not proof of permanent full competence.

**R13_Mateo — cross-functional escalation broker.** Active on all 14 activities, highest critical-case count (76), and the connector in fragments 3 and 5; he would almost certainly top handover-of-work centrality. Two episodes bound his informal authority: week 5 — 21 preliminary approvals of which 13 were rechecked (activity delegated, authority retained by Hana); weeks 7–8 — triage of security referrals, which reduced false escalations but delayed two critical cases by more than two days. *Benefits:* protects scarce specialists (Erik) from low-value referrals, keeps reopened cases moving, provides surge approval capacity, integrates technical/commercial/security/quality strands. *Risks:* informal authority creep, triage error under load, and a central single point of failure — he is the process's most connected human node. The broker role needs explicit boundaries: preliminary-approval scope, documented referral criteria with criticality overrides, and a named backup.

**R05_Erik — rare but critical specialist.** 96 A6 events on the lowest permanent volume (170 events), yet 82 high + 55 critical cases and median complexity 0.86; Security Review is a blocking gate before implementation (fragment 3). *Benefits of rarity:* concentrated expertise, high-precision referrals, low coordination overhead. *Risks:* no second security specialist exists; the "substitution" for Erik was demand reduction via Mateo's filter, not backup capability — which worked for false positives and failed for two critical cases. Frequency-based mining will always underweight this role; it must be sized by criticality and blocking-ness, not utilization. Concrete asks: a named deputy or cross-training, and explicit rules for when a security referral may be deferred.

**Additional shadow/bridging roles worth naming:**

- **Nora — automation boundary role.** The bot is not staff, but the human–bot boundary is an organizational interface that needs an owner: 760 registrations and 610 validations flow through it, and 1,640 human validations continue after it. The temp documentation failures occurred exactly at this boundary (the human A2 step). Governance: monitor override/exception rates and note completeness at the human step; never cluster the bot into human roles, but always include it in handover analysis because it is the process front door.
- **Kai & Lina — contingent shadow capacity.** They shadow the front office during seasonal load (fragments 1 and 6). *Benefits:* volume absorption, protecting permanent agents for complex work. *Risks:* the 17-reopen externality — the rework landed on Amira, Ben and Mateo, so net capacity gain is smaller than headcount suggests; Lina's late shift has thin supervision access (Hana, Fatima, Erik are all day). Their 0.98 mutual similarity indicates undifferentiated deployment; mandatory note fields, checklists, and quality sampling are cheap mitigations.
- **Ivan & Julia — quality gatekeepers.** A bridging role between execution and learning: they gate closure (A11) and convert incidents into organizational memory (200 combined KB updates). Their authority is implicit (bounce-back power) rather than hierarchical. The reopen wave reveals a coverage gap: their gate inspects solutions, not intake documentation — pair it with an intake checklist.

# 5. PM4Py role-discovery and similarity code

Design principles baked into the script: separate the system actor before mining human roles; analyze substitution-flagged events as a separate regime rather than folding them into role profiles; always pair similarity with exposure (complexity); and locate broker roles in the handover network rather than in activity counts.

```python
import pandas as pd
import networkx as nx
from sklearn.cluster import DBSCAN
import pm4py

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 0. Load and format the event log
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
df = pd.read_csv("order_to_resolution.csv")

df = pm4py.format_dataframe(
    df,
    case_id="case:concept:name",
    activity_key="concept:name",
    timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
    timest_format="%Y-%m-%d %H:%M:%S%z",   # adapt to the actual timestamp format
)
# format_dataframe preserves the domain columns (org:resource, case_complexity,
# temporary_substitution, ...) and adds a stable event index (@@index).
df = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp", "@@index"]).reset_index(drop=True)

# Analytics-ready versions of two domain attributes
COMPLEXITY_MAP = {"low": 1.0, "medium": 2.0, "high": 3.0, "critical": 4.0}
df["complexity_score"] = df["case_complexity"].map(COMPLEXITY_MAP) \
    .fillna(pd.to_numeric(df["case_complexity"], errors="coerce"))
df["is_substitution"] = (df["temporary_substitution"].astype(str)
                         .str.lower().isin(["true", "1", "yes", "y"]))

# The bot is a system actor: keep it for flow/handover analysis, but exclude
# it from HUMAN role discovery (otherwise it distorts the largest cluster).
df_bot   = df[df["org:resource"] == "R14_Nora"].copy()
df_human = df[df["org:resource"] != "R14_Nora"].copy()

KEYS = dict(activity_key="concept:name",
            resource_key="org:resource",
            timestamp_key="time:timestamp",
            case_id_key="case:concept:name")

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 1. Mine organizational roles (activity-profile based)
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
roles = pm4py.discover_organizational_roles(df_human, **KEYS)

role_rows = []
for i, role in enumerate(roles):
    # Depending on the PM4Py version, role.originator entries are either
    # plain resource names or (resource, score) tuples.
    members = [o[0] if isinstance(o, (tuple, list)) else o for o in role.originator]
    for res in members:
        role_rows.append({"role_id": f"mined_role_{i}",
                          "resource": res,
                          "role_activities": tuple(role.activities)})
role_df = pd.DataFrame(role_rows)

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 2. Join mined roles with complexity / substitution context
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
res_profile = df.groupby("org:resource").agg(
    events=("concept:name", "size"),
    n_activities=("concept:name", "nunique"),
    median_complexity=("complexity_score", "median"),
    share_critical=("case_complexity", lambda s: (s == "critical").mean()),
    share_high_or_critical=("case_complexity", lambda s: s.isin(["high", "critical"]).mean()),
    substitution_events=("is_substitution", "sum"),
)

role_ctx = (role_df.merge(res_profile, left_on="resource", right_index=True)
                   .sort_values(["role_id", "median_complexity"], ascending=[True, False]))

# Automatic caution flag: members of the same mined role whose risk exposure
# differs strongly are NOT interchangeable for staffing purposes.
for rid, grp in role_ctx.groupby("role_id"):
    spread = grp["median_complexity"].max() - grp["median_complexity"].min()
    if len(grp) > 1 and spread > 0.15:
        print(f"[CAUTION] {rid}: shared activity profile, complexity spread={spread:.2f}")
        print(grp[["resource", "events", "median_complexity", "share_critical"]])
# Expected: the mined 'technical' role contains Chen (0.71) and Dina (0.48);
# the 'front office' role may swallow the temps (0.18-0.19 vs 0.29-0.34).

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 3. Activity-based resource similarity -- read WITH complexity, not instead
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
sim = pm4py.discover_activity_based_resource_similarity(df_human, **KEYS)

# Normalise pairwise similarities into a lookup, whatever shape the
# installed PM4Py version returns (DataFrame or dict).
if isinstance(sim, pd.DataFrame):
    sim_lookup = {(r[0], r[1]): r[2] for r in sim.itertuples(index=False)}
elif isinstance(sim, dict):
    sim_lookup = sim
else:
    sim_lookup = {}

def similarity(r1, r2):
    return sim_lookup.get((r1, r2), sim_lookup.get((r2, r1), float("nan")))

for r1, r2 in [("R03_Chen", "R04_Dina"), ("R09_Ivan", "R10_Julia"),
               ("R01_Amira", "R02_Ben"), ("R11_Kai", "R12_Lina"),
               ("R06_Fatima", "R07_Giorgio"), ("R08_Hana", "R13_Mateo")]:
    print(f"\n{r1} ~ {r2}: similarity={similarity(r1, r2):.2f}")
    print(res_profile.loc[[r1, r2], ["median_complexity", "share_critical", "substitution_events"]])
# Chen~Dina  0.94 with complexity 0.71 vs 0.48: similar behaviour,
# different exposure -- do not merge into one staffing pool.
# Hana~Mateo  0.54: similarity is low, but the real difference is authority,
# which similarity cannot express at all.

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 4. Handover-of-work / working-together / subcontracting networks
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
hw_net = pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network(df, beta=0, **KEYS)
wt_net = pm4py.discover_working_together_network(df, **KEYS)
sc_net = pm4py.discover_subcontracting_network(df, n=2, **KEYS)

# Version-proof extraction of handover edges (also serves as a manual check
# on the network objects above):
seq = df.sort_values(["case:concept:name", "time:timestamp", "@@index"]).copy()
seq["next_resource"] = seq.groupby("case:resource" if False else "case:concept:name")["org:resource"].shift(-1)
handover_counts = (seq.dropna(subset=["next_resource"])
                     .groupby(["org:resource", "next_resource"]).size())

G = nx.DiGraph()
for (u, v), n in handover_counts.items():
    G.add_edge(u, v, weight=int(n))

print("Top handover pairs:\n", handover_counts.sort_values(ascending=False).head(10))
print("Brokers by betweenness centrality (unweighted):")
print(sorted(nx.betweenness_centrality(G).items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:5])
# Expectation: R13_Mateo is the hub although no activity dominates his own
# profile -- this is what defines him as a bridging role, not his event counts.

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 5. Substitution-aware analysis: behaviour under substitution is a
#    separate regime, NOT evidence of permanent competence
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
df = pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time(df)   # adds case:service-time / case:waiting-time
# (If events carry start timestamps, prefer per-event service times via
#  pm4py.get_service_time(df, activity_key=..., start_timestamp_key=...).)

df["week"] = df["time:timestamp"].dt.isocalendar().week.astype(int)

# 5a. Pricing cases with substitution-driven review vs without (weeks 3-4)
a7_cases = df.loc[df["concept:name"] == "A7 Pricing Exception Review", "case:concept:name"].unique()
case_view = (df[df["case:concept:name"].isin(a7_cases)]
             .groupby("case:concept:name")
             .agg(week=("week", "first"),
                  sub_any=("is_substitution", "max"),
                  service=("case:service-time", "first")))
print(case_view.groupby("sub_any")["service"].median())
# Expected: cases with substitution-driven pricing review take materially
# longer (~38 -> ~74 min regime).

# 5b. Delegated approvals and their rechecks (Mateo -> Hana, week 5)
seq["prev_activity"] = seq.groupby("case:concept:name")["concept:name"].shift(1)
seq["prev_resource"] = seq.groupby("case:concept:name")["org:resource"].shift(1)
rechecks = seq[(seq["prev_activity"] == "A9 Supervisor Approval")
               & (seq["prev_resource"] == "R13_Mateo")
               & (seq["concept:name"] == "A9 Supervisor Approval")]
print(f"Mateo approvals later re-approved by another resource: {len(rechecks)}")

# 5c. Reopens after temp-agent closures (weeks 8-10)
temp_closed = set(seq.loc[(seq["org:resource"].isin(["R11_Kai", "R12_Lina"]))
                           & (seq["concept:name"] == "A12 Close Request"),
                           "case:concept:name"])
reopened = set(seq.loc[seq["concept:name"] == "A13 Reopen Request", "case:concept:name"])
print(f"Cases closed by temps and later reopened: {len(temp_closed & reopened)}")
variants = pm4py.get_variants(df)   # cross-check reopen-heavy variants

# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
# 6. Cross-check: what a purely frequency/variant-driven clustering sees
# ----------------------------------------------------------------------
clusters = pm4py.cluster_log(df_human, sklearn_clusterer=DBSCAN(eps=0.4, min_samples=5))
for i, cdf in enumerate(clusters):
    print(f"cluster {i}: {len(cdf)} events, median complexity "
```

**Cautions before changing staffing on mined roles:**

1. **Mined roles describe behavior, not authority or competence.** Merging Hana and Mateo because both "handle escalations" would delete a control (final approval authority); treating Chen and Dina as one pool (0.94 similarity) would equalize risk exposure that the routing policy deliberately keeps apart. 3. **Volume-based capacity planning starves rare-but-critical roles.** Erik's utilization looks low, yet weeks 7–8 show what happens when his gate is thinned. Rare is not unimportant; weight by criticality × blocking-ness.
