# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness but low precision.** Fitness measures how well the discovered model covers observed traces; precision measures how many traces allowed by the model actually appear in the log. The pilot model has high fitness because many cases follow the simplified coordinator path. It has low precision because the model also allows many combinations that are not observed or not desirable, such as skipped survey plus manual activation plus second visit plus billing before activation. The model is therefore too permissive: it fits the dominant pilot pattern but does not show a tightly controlled process.

**Improved median but worse tail duration.** The median improves because the central mass of cases, especially simple low-risk houses, benefits from merged validation/scheduling and fewer handovers. The tail worsens because exceptions become more frequent and longer: failed visits, second visits, manual activation, missing permit checks, and billing-before-activation complaints. The DFG shows higher shares of installation  failed visit, failed visit  second visit, manual activation  billing start, and billing start  complaint escalation. Temporal profile deviations show installation  activation worsening for apartments and failed visit  second visit delay increasing where coordinator utilization is high. The pilot also had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, so the same redesign is applied to a harder case mix.

**Fewer handovers but lower resilience.** Fewer handovers can reduce coordination overhead and clarify ownership. However, the handover-of-work network became simpler while the working-together network became more centralized around coordinators and activation specialists. If one coordinator owns the context, absence can stall cases because context is not shared. Reduced handovers can therefore improve normal-flow efficiency while reducing exception resilience.

**Lower visible cost but higher rework or downstream cost.** Visible cost before complaint handling falls because there are fewer handovers, fewer surveys in some cases, and faster median completion. But rework rises: second visits, manual activation, complaints, and billing disputes. The cost estimate is incomplete because downstream complaint handling after week 4 is excluded. The visible saving may therefore overstate the true economic benefit.

**Improvement for simple houses but deterioration for apartments/old buildings.** Simple low-risk houses align well with merged scheduling and coordinator ownership. Apartments and old buildings require more validation, survey, and permit handling. The survey-skip rule appears to misclassify medium-risk apartments, and permit checks are missing for older apartment buildings. These segments experience more failed visits, second visits, manual activation, and complaints. The redesign is not uniformly bad; it is segment-dependent.

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: E — Hybrid targeted deployment with controls and measurement.** Do not scale nationally. Do not roll back entirely. Preserve the simple-house benefit while stopping the risky variations that are driving tail duration, rework, complaints, and activation/permit issues.

**Immediate action:**  
- Gate Redesign R to **simple, low-risk houses only**.  
- For apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, business customers, and rural cases, revert to the baseline field-survey/permit/activation sequence or apply explicit controls.  
- Add coordinator backup, shared case notes, and absence escalation.  
- Start a conformance dashboard for **billing before successful activation** and **missing permit checks**.

**What to pause:**  
- Pause **survey skipping** for medium-risk apartments and old permit-sensitive buildings.  
- Pause **post-billing activation** except for tightly logged low-risk exceptions where activation test is completed or signal is verified. For all other cases, require successful activation before billing start.

**What to scale:**  
- Scale **coordinator ownership** and **merged validate/schedule** only for simple low-risk houses.  
- Expand to additional regions only for simple low-risk cases after 2-4 weeks if early conformance and segment metrics remain acceptable.

**What to measure next:**  
1. **M7** immediately: conformance monitoring for activation order and permit absence.  
2. **M4**: full 60-day downstream cost after billing start.  
3. **M1 + M6**: randomized survey-skip test by risk stratum and validation of the predictive rule.  
4. **M2**: start timestamps for installation, activation, and manual activation.  
5. **M3**: reason codes for failed visits, manual activation, second visits, and complaints.  
6. **M5**: test shared-team ownership or backup coverage during coordinator absence.  
7. **M8**: separate customer experience for speed to appointment and working service after installation.

**Thresholds to justify broader rollout:**  
For a 4-6 week controlled period after controls are in place, broader rollout should require, by segment:

- **Duration:** p90 and p95 cycle time no worse than baseline for the same segment; overall tail returns toward baseline or segment-adjusted baseline.  
- **Quality:** first-time-right at least baseline segment level; simple houses maintain at least +2pp improvement.  
- **Customer impact:** 30-day complaints no worse than baseline segment level; overall complaints not materially above baseline.  
- **Compliance-like controls:** billing before successful activation below 0.5% with logged exceptions; permit check completion at 100% for older apartment/permit-sensitive cases.  
- **Rework:** second visit and manual activation no worse than baseline +1pp.  
- **Cost:** full downstream cost at or below baseline EUR 198, or visible savings clearly exceed downstream cost.  
- **Resilience:** coordinator utilization below 85% or absence coverage prevents case stalls.

If these thresholds are met, expand coordinator ownership to medium-risk segments with a validated survey rule and activation-before-billing controls. If not, roll back the risky components—survey skipping and post-billing activation—and retain only the simple-house benefits.
