```
def cif_at(d, horizon=72, cause_of_interest=1, time_col="time", cause_col="cause"):
    event_times = sorted(d.loc[(d[cause_col] > 0) & (d[time_col] <= horizon), time_col].unique())
    S = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = (d[time_col] >= t).sum()
        d_total = ((d[time_col] == t) & (d[cause_col] > 0)).sum()
        d_cause = ((d[time_col] == t) & (d[cause_col] == cause_of_interest)).sum()
        if n > 0:
            cif += S * d_cause / n
            S *= (1 - d_total / n)
    return cif, S
```

# 1. SLA estimands and time definitions

**Time origin:** `Submit`, so each case has process time \(t=0\) at `Submit`.  
**SLA horizon:** \( \tau = 72 \) hours elapsed time. I use the convention that a `Resolve` at exactly 72 hours counts as resolved by 72; breach is `Resolve` after 72, or no internal `Resolve` by 72.  
**Observed terminal outcomes:** `Resolve` is operational success; `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` are other internal terminal dispositions. `Transfer` sends the case outside internal observation, so eventual external resolution is unknown.  
**Censoring:** `open` cases are censored at `duration_or_age_h` for time-to-terminal-outcome analyses. For an SLA-at-72 estimand, an open case below 72 hours is censored before the horizon and its 72-hour outcome is unknown; an open case at or above 72 hours is already observed to have no `Resolve` by 72 under the internal-record view.  
**Already-observed breaches:** A case with `Resolve` at 76 hours, or an open case at 92 hours, has already failed to resolve by 72. These are not ordinary right-censored cases for the “not resolved by 72” estimand.

Useful estimands:

1. **Actual probability of not being resolved by 72 hours**  
   \[
   P(\text{no internal Resolve by }72)=1-\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)
   \]
   where other terminal outcomes and still-open/no-resolve cases count as not resolved. This matches SLA breach/backlog governance. It is not the same as Kaplan-Meier time-to-`Resolve` with competing outcomes censored.

2. **Cumulative incidence of resolution after 72 hours**  
   Unconditional version:
   \[
   P(\text{Resolve in }(72,\infty])=\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(\infty)-\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72).
   \]
   Conditional tail version, useful for active backlog:
   \[
   P(\text{Resolve in }(72,72+s]\mid \text{no Resolve and no competing terminal before }72).
   \]
   This matches recovery/capacity planning for cases already in the tail.

3. **Probability of still having no terminal disposition at 72 hours**  
   \[
   P(T_{\text{any terminal}}>72)
   \]
   where terminal means `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer`. This matches active internal workload and “still sitting somewhere” caseload.

4. **Cause-specific cumulative incidence at 72 hours**  
   \[
   \text{CIF}_{\text{Withdraw}}(72),\ \text{CIF}_{\text{Reject}}(72),\ \text{CIF}_{\text{Transfer}}(72).
   \]
   This matches competing-risk governance: how often cases leave the success pathway through withdrawal, rejection, or external transfer.

How specific cases contribute:

- **Resolved at 76 hours** e.g. S03.  
  - Not resolved by 72: yes, it breaches the SLA/no-success-by-72 estimand.  
  - Resolution after 72: contributes as a `Resolve` event at 76 in the unconditional after-72 estimand, and is at risk at the 72-hour landmark.  
  - No terminal at 72: yes, because its terminal event is after 72.  
  - Cause-specific: no competing event before 72; `Resolve` at 76.

- **Open at 92 hours** e.g. S09.  
  - Not resolved by 72: yes, because it is open at 92 and therefore had no `Resolve` by 72.  
  - Resolution after 72: at risk at 72, censored at 92 if no later `Resolve`/competing event is observed.  
  - No terminal at 72: yes, no internal terminal disposition by 72.  
  - Cause-specific: censored at 92 for terminal causes unless additional data are available.

- **Withdrawn at 27 hours** e.g. S06.  
  - Not resolved by 72: yes, it did not reach `Resolve` by 72.  
  - Resolution after 72: under competing-risk logic, it cannot later resolve internally; it is a competing event before the landmark.  
  - No terminal at 72: no, because `Withdraw` is a terminal disposition at 27.  
  - Cause-specific: `Withdraw` cause at 27.

- **Transferred at 68 hours** e.g. S08.  
  - Not resolved by 72: depends on transfer assumption. Under competing/composite failure, yes, it did not resolve internally by 72. Under non-informative censoring, it is censored before 72 and should not be silently treated as resolved or unresolved. Under optimistic external-success assumption, it could count as external resolution at 68.  
  - Resolution after 72: under competing, no; under censoring, unknown; under optimistic external success, already resolved before 72.  
  - No terminal at 72: under internal-termination view, no, because `Transfer` is terminal at 68; under “still under internal observation” view, it would be censored.  
  - Cause-specific: `Transfer` cause 4, or censored, or external `Resolve`, depending on sensitivity scenario.

# 2. Event and censoring encoding

Let general competing-risk cause codes be: `1=Resolve`, `2=Withdraw`, `3=Reject`, `4=Transfer`, `0=open/censored`.

| Observed status | General cause code | Event indicator | Censoring rule | Sensitivity alternatives |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| `resolved` | 1 | 1 at `duration_or_age_h` | Not censored | Always success. If time >72, still a `Resolve` event but a breach for the by-72 estimand. |
| `withdrawn` | 2 | 1 at `duration_or_age_h` | Not censored as a competing event | Treat as competing; treat as composite failure; censor non-informatively for hypothetical no-withdrawal world; separate cause-specific CIF. |
| `rejected` | 3 | 1 at `duration_or_age_h` | Not censored as a competing event | Same alternatives as withdrawal. |
| `transferred` | 4 | 1 under competing/composite-failure; 0 under censoring | Under primary competing-risk view, not censored: internal observation ends and internal `Resolve` is precluded. | 1) Competing internal termination. 2) Non-informative censoring at transfer. 3) Optimistic external resolution: treat as `Resolve` at transfer time. 4) Conservative composite failure: transfer counts as failure/no-success. 5) Missing external outcome with best/worst-case bounds. |
| `open` | 0 | 0 | Censored at `duration_or_age_h` for general time-to-terminal analysis. For 72-hour SLA estimand: if age <72, censored before 72 and 72-hour outcome unknown; if age 72, already observed no `Resolve` by 72. | Non-informative censoring; worst-case all open below 72 do not resolve by 72; best-case all open below 72 resolve by 72; informative-censoring adjustment using `last_event_gap_h` in landmark/multi-state models. |

Important distinctions:

- **Right censoring:** an `open` case whose future terminal event is not observed by cutoff.
- **Competing event:** `Withdraw`, `Reject`, or `Transfer` before `Resolve`; this prevents the `Resolve` event from being observed internally.
- **Already-observed breach:** `Resolve` after 72, or open age 72 with no `Resolve`. These are not censored for “not resolved by 72”; they are known non-resolution by 72.

For transfer, at least three plausible treatments:

1. **Competing event:** `Transfer` is an internal terminal outcome that prevents internal `Resolve`. This is conservative for operational success.
2. **Non-informative censoring:** case leaves internal observation at transfer and external outcome is assumed independent. This is risky because transfers occur after escalation, e.g. S08, S21, S32, so censoring is likely informative.
3. **Optimistic external resolution:** treat transfer as if the external organization resolved the case at transfer time. This is a best-case sensitivity bound, not a primary assumption.
4. **Conservative composite failure:** transfer is counted as non-success for SLA governance. This is operationally simple and aligns with “internal operational success = Resolve.”

# 3. Why naive completed-case analysis is biased

A naive completed-case analysis would often keep only `resolved` rows, or only rows with a terminal event, and then summarize duration. That is biased for SLA risk.

- **Survivor bias:** Keeping only `resolved` cases conditions on reaching `Resolve`. Cases such as S09 open at 92 hours, S11 open at 81 hours, S25 open at 79 hours, and S26 open at 104 hours are excluded even though they may represent the slowest, highest-risk tail. S26 has `last_event_gap_h=72`, suggesting long inactivity; excluding it hides a likely tail-risk case. Resolved cases S03 at 76 and S13 at 110 are useful, but they are not enough to represent unresolved or competing-terminal cases.

- **Informative censoring:** Open cases are censored at the observation cutoff. If open cases are open because they are stalled or complex, censoring is not independent of future resolution. S11 has an 81-hour age and 55-hour last-event gap; S25 has 79-hour age and 48-hour gap; S26 has 104-hour age and 72-hour gap. Treating these as ordinary censoring assumes their future hazard is like recently active cases, which is unlikely.

- **Competing risks:** Withdrawal, rejection, and transfer preclude internal `Resolve`. S06 withdrawn at 27, S12 withdrawn at 15, S22 withdrawn at 44, S30 withdrawn at 70; S05 rejected at 9, S14 rejected at 63, S23 rejected at 11; S08/S21/S32 transferred. If these are censored in a Kaplan-Meier time-to-`Resolve` analysis, the estimate becomes a hypothetical resolution probability in a world without those competing outcomes, not the actual SLA success probability.

- **Cohort imbalance:** Cohort B began after an automated-screening and escalation-policy change, but assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete. B has more P1 cases than A (5/17 vs 3/15) and more `AutoCheck`-before-`Screen` paths. A naive cohort comparison confounds policy, priority, process variant, and case mix. For example, S18 is P1 in B and resolves in 12 hours, while S04 is P1 in A and resolves in 29 hours; this is not causal evidence without adjustment.

- **Open below versus above 72:** Open cases below 72, such as S10 at 47, S15 at 35, S24 at 67, S29 at 51, and S31 at 23, still have a chance to resolve before 72 and should contribute to the risk set until censoring or resolution. Open cases above 72, such as S09 at 92, S11 at 81, S25 at 79, and S26 at 104, are already observed not resolved by 72 for retrospective breach counts. Mixing these two groups as ordinary censored cases would distort both retrospective breach and prospective tail-risk estimates.

# 4. Statistical analysis plan

Compare the candidate approaches:

| Method | What it estimates | Strengths here | Weaknesses here |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaplan-Meier | Time-to-`Resolve`, treating other outcomes as censored | Simple, familiar | Biased for actual SLA success if withdrawal/rejection/transfer are competing or informative. Only useful for hypothetical resolution. |
| Aalen-Johansen / cumulative incidence | Cause-specific cumulative incidence with competing risks | Primary fit for actual SLA breach, cause-specific withdrawal/rejection/transfer, and no-terminal risk | Nonparametric; small cohort strata have wide uncertainty. |
| Cause-specific hazards | Hazard of `Resolve` and each competing event separately | Good for covariates and time-varying process features | Does not directly give absolute risk without combining all hazards. |
| Subdistribution hazards / Fine-Gray | Direct model for cumulative incidence | Useful for risk prediction and governance | Less natural for complex time-varying process states; small data limit. |
| Restricted mean time | Expected time in states or event-free time up to 72 | Good for SLA burden and cohort comparisons | Needs clear state definition; competing events require multi-state framing. |
| Landmarking | Conditional risk after a landmark such as 72 hours or current stage | Handles post-baseline events and dynamic prediction; avoids immortal-time leakage | Depends on landmark choice; smaller risk sets. |
| Multi-state model | Transitions among `Screen`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`, terminal states | Best process-aware representation of stage hazards | Many parameters; with 32 cases, exploratory unless strongly regularized. |

Recommended primary approach:

- Use **Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence** with competing risks. Primary transfer assumption: `Transfer` is a competing internal terminal outcome. `Withdraw` and `Reject` are competing terminal outcomes. `open` cases are censored at cutoff, but for the by-72 estimand open cases above 72 remain event-free at 72. Report:
  - \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\),
  - \(1-\text{CIF}_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\) = actual no-resolve-by-72 risk,
  - \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Withdraw}}(72)\), \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Reject}}(72)\), \(\text{CIF}_{\text{Transfer}}(72)\),
  - \(P(\text{no terminal by }72)\) using any-terminal event outcome.

Recommended secondary approach:

- Use **landmarking at 72** and, if data grow, a **multi-state model** with current process state and inactivity gap as time-varying covariates. This supports operational tail-risk alerts: given a case is still active at 72, what is the subsequent chance of `Resolve` versus competing exit?
- Use **cause-specific hazards** for exploratory covariate analysis of cohort, priority, escalation timing, rework, and current stage. Do not interpret these as causal effects because cohort assignment was not randomized and process events are post-treatment.

Justification for these data: the sample is small, censoring and competing risks are central, and the process is stage-based. Nonparametric cumulative incidence is robust and transparent. Multi-state and cause-specific models are valuable but should be secondary or exploratory because of parameter count and confounding.

# 5. Process-aware features and stage hazards

Features derived from prefixes:

- **Baseline covariates:** `cohort`, `priority`. These are known at or before `Submit`. `cohort` is policy-era group, not randomized treatment.
- **Initial path ordering:** `AutoCheck` before `Screen` versus `Screen` before `AutoCheck`. This may be known only after the first few events, so it is safer as a time-varying or landmark feature. Examples: S16 `Submit>AutoCheck>Screen>Resolve`, S01 `Submit>Screen>AutoCheck>Resolve`.
- **Current stage:** `Screen`, `AutoCheck`, `Review`, `Escalate`, `Rework`. Time-varying state. Important for stage hazards: hazard of `Resolve` likely differs in `Review` versus `Escalate`.
- **Repeated `Review`:** count of `Review` events by time \(t\). Time-varying or cumulative. S03 and S13 have repeated `Review`; S13 also has `Escalate`.
- **Rework:** `rework_count`, or indicator/state `Rework`, or transition `Review -> Rework -> Review`. Post-baseline and time-varying. S07 has rework count 1, S13 has 1, S27 has 1.
- **Escalation timing:** time from `Submit` to first `Escalate`, or current escalation state. Post-baseline. S03, S04, S08, S10, S11, S13, S18, S21, S25, S28, S29, S32 are escalated. “Ever escalated” cannot be a baseline covariate because a case must survive to escalation.
- **Inactivity gap:** `last_event_gap_h` at cutoff. For open cases, this is time since last recorded event. It is time-varying/current-status information, useful in landmark models. It is not baseline.
- **Path pattern/variant:** full prefix or full variant is future information if used at `Submit`. Use only as a descriptive outcome/process feature, or use observed prefix up to time \(t\).
- **Leakage risks:** Using `Resolve`, `Transfer`, final state, total duration, full prefix, rework that occurs later, or escalation that occurs later as baseline predictors induces leakage. “Ever escalated” also creates immortal-time bias because only cases that survive to escalation can be labeled escalated.

Proposed state-transition diagram in text:

`Submit` -> `Screen` or `AutoCheck`  
`Screen` -> `AutoCheck` or `Review` or `Reject` or `Withdraw`  
`AutoCheck` -> `Screen` or `Review` or `Escalate` or `Resolve`  
`Review` -> `Resolve` or `Withdraw` or `Reject` or `Escalate` or `Rework`  
`Escalate` -> `Review` or `Resolve` or `Transfer`  
`Rework` -> `Review`  
`Review` -> `Escalate` -> `Review` loops are possible, e.g. S03.  
Terminal states: `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, `Transfer`.  
`open` is not an absorbing process state; it is administrative censoring at cutoff.

A multi-state model would estimate transition hazards such as `Review -> Resolve`, `Review -> Escalate`, `Escalate -> Transfer`, `Review -> Rework`, and `Rework -> Review`, with `Resolve`, `Withdraw`, `Reject`, and `Transfer` as absorbing or semi-absorbing states.

# 6. Sensitivity analysis

At least three sensitivity analyses should be run.

1. **Transfer treatment sensitivity**  
   Compare:
   - primary: `Transfer` is a competing internal terminal outcome;
   - alternative A: `Transfer` is non-informative censoring at transfer;
   - alternative B: optimistic external resolution, treating `Transfer` as `Resolve` at transfer time;
   - alternative C: conservative composite failure, treating `Transfer` as failure/no-success.  
   Cases S08 transfer at 68, S21 transfer at 26, S32 transfer at 96. S08 and S21 occur before 72. If transfers are censored, those cases are removed from the 72-hour risk set and the apparent no-resolve risk can fall. If transfers are competing/failure, they count as non-success. If transfers are optimistic external success, S08 and S21 can count as resolved before 72. This can change whether cohort B appears better or worse, because B contains S21 and S32.

2. **Withdrawal/rejection treatment sensitivity**  
   Compare competing risk versus non-informative censoring versus composite failure for `Withdraw` and `Reject`. Censoring S06, S12, S22, S30, S05, S14, S23 removes real non-resolve outcomes and can inflate the estimated `Resolve` probability. Competing-risk treatment preserves the actual SLA success interpretation. If one cohort has more early withdrawals/rejections, cohort conclusions can reverse under censoring.

3. **Informative censoring of open cases**  
   Compare standard non-informative censoring of `open` cases against worst-case and best-case bounds for open cases below 72. Relevant cases include S10 age 47, S15 age 35, S24 age 67, S29 age 51, S31 age 23. Worst-case: all open below 72 fail to resolve by 72. Best-case: all open below 72 resolve by 72. Also use `last_event_gap_h`: S11 gap 55, S25 gap 48, S26 gap 72 suggest stalled cases. If long gaps predict lower future hazard, standard censoring underestimates breach risk.

4. **Cohort/policy-effect sensitivity**  
   Compare unadjusted cohort B vs A with adjustment/stratification by priority and initial process variant. Because assignment was not randomized and rollout compliance was incomplete, a naive “B improves SLA” conclusion could reverse after adjusting for priority and case mix. If B’s apparent advantage is driven by more `AutoCheck`-first paths or by transfer handling, the conclusion is fragile.

5. **Horizon/tie sensitivity**  
   Define whether `Resolve` at exactly 72 is success or breach. No supplied case resolves exactly at 72, so this does not change these data, but it matters for governance.

Qualitative conclusions that could reverse: whether cohort B reduces tail risk; whether escalation is associated with longer or shorter resolution; whether open cases are low-risk backlog or high-risk stalled cases; and whether the SLA breach rate is acceptable under transfer assumptions.

# 7. Operational interpretation

Convert survival outputs into operational tools:

- **Alerts:** At each event or daily landmark, compute dynamic predictions such as \(P(\text{no Resolve by }72)\), \(P(\text{no terminal by }72)\), and cause-specific exit risks. Alert on high predicted breach risk, especially for P1 cases, cases in `Review` or `Escalate`, cases with rework, and cases with long `last_event_gap_h`. For already-open cases above 72, shift from “will they breach?” to “will they recover?” using landmark-after-72 probabilities.

- **Workload actions:** Triage high-risk active cases to review/expedite queues. Manage stalled `Review` and `Escalate` states with inactivity thresholds. Track transfers separately because external resolution is unknown; operational actions include external follow-up or treating transfers as governance exceptions.

- **SLA governance:** Report cumulative incidence of `Resolve` by 72, actual no-resolve-by-72, and cause-specific withdrawal/rejection/transfer rates. Do not report only average duration among resolved cases. Separate retrospective breach counts from prospective tail-risk forecasts.

- **Calibration and fairness checks:** Evaluate predicted breach probabilities against observed breach by risk bin, overall and within priority and cohort. Check false-alert and missed-breach rates by P1/P2/P3 and cohort A/B. Because cohort reflects a policy change, monitor performance disparities by cohort but do not interpret cohort coefficients causally.

- **Causal caution:** Risk scores and covariates such as `Escalate`, `Rework`, and current stage are prognostic, not causal. Escalation is post-treatment and likely confounded by case severity. Do not present “Escalate increases risk” as a causal effect without a time-aware design, such as landmarking, multi-state modeling, or a randomized/rollout-based identification strategy.

```python
    """Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence at horizon for one cause."""
    event_times = sorted(d.loc[(d[cause_col] > 0) & (d[time_col] <= horizon), time_col].unique())
    S = 1.0
    cif = 0.0
    for t in event_times:
        n = (d[time_col] >= t).sum()
        d_total = ((d[time_col] == t) & (d[cause_col] > 0)).sum()
        d_cause = ((d[time_col] == t) & (d[cause_col] == cause_of_interest)).sum()
        if n > 0:
            cif += S * d_cause / n
            S *= (1 - d_total / n)
    return cif, S

        n = (d[time_col] >= t).sum()
        d_event = ((d[time_col] == t) & (d[cause_col] > 0)).sum()
        if n > 0:
            S *= (1 - d_event / n)
    return S
```

# 9. Limitations and additional data

The strongest missing items are:

- **Exact event timestamps:** only total duration/age and `last_event_gap_h` are supplied. Exact timestamps for each activity are needed for time-varying covariates, multi-state transition times, stage-specific hazards, and calendar-time effects.
- **External outcomes after transfer:** S08, S21, and S32 leave internal observation. Without external linkage, the treatment of transfers dominates sensitivity analyses. Needed: whether the external organization resolved, rejected, withdrew, or is still open, and when.
- **Cutoff and start dates:** calendar dates for `Submit` and cutoff are needed to distinguish administrative censoring from dropout, adjust for seasonality, and align with the policy change.
- **Time-varying workload and resource state:** staffing, queue length, priority changes, case complexity, and concurrent caseload affect resolution and inactivity gaps.
- **Assignment mechanism and policy rollout compliance:** cohort B is not randomized. Needed: actual exposure to the automated-screening/escalation policy, rollout timing, compliance, and reasons for non-compliance. Without this, cohort B is an intention-to-treat or exposure-misclassified group.
- **Reason codes:** why cases withdraw, reject, rework, or transfer. These determine whether competing events are informative and whether transfer should be treated as failure, censoring, or external success.
- **Larger sample and repeated cohorts:** 32 cases produce unstable cause-specific CIFs and very wide uncertainty in priority/cohort strata. More cases and longer follow-up would support multi-state and landmark models.
- **Informative-censoring diagnostics:** post-censoring information or external follow-up for open cases would allow sensitivity bounds rather than assuming non-informative censoring.
- **Severity/case-mix variables:** priority alone may not capture complexity. Needed for fair comparison of escalation, rework, and cohort effects.
