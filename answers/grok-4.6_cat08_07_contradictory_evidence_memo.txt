# 1. Executive memo

**To:** Leadership, Fiber Access Operations  
**From:** Process Intelligence / Operating Model  
**Subject:** Do not scale Redesign R as piloted. Capture the simple-house gain, stop the high-risk shortcuts, and buy the measurements that decide national design.  
**Recommendation:** Alternative **E**, executed with selected controls from **C** and a short measurement program from **D**.

Redesign R did what it was designed to do for the easy majority path: a coordinator, a merged validate-and-schedule step, and fewer handovers cut median cycle time from 14.2 to 10.8 days and cut cases with three or more handovers from 34% to 12%. Visible unit cost before complaint handling also fell (EUR 184 to EUR 161). That is real operating value, concentrated in simple low-risk houses (34% of the pilot): median −6 days, p90 −8 days, first-time-right +3pp, complaints −1pp.

It is not a national operating model. The same redesign lengthened the tail (p90 31.0 → 38.5 days; p95 45.0 → 62.0), reduced first-time-right (82% → 76%), raised 30-day complaints (6.1% → 9.8%), second visits (11% → 18%), manual activations (7% → 15%), and overall SLA breach (13% → 16%). Estimated full cost including complaint and revisit handling moved the wrong way (EUR 198 → EUR 207) even though that estimate is incomplete. NPS slipped from +21 to +17. Complaint language shifted from “waiting for an appointment” to “installed but not working” and “billed before activation.”

Those are not random noise. Process-mining artifacts point at three design choices, not at coordinator ownership as such.

First, survey skipping and the predictive rule. Appointment → field survey fell (31% → 18%) while appointment → install and failed-visit / second-visit edges rose. Apartments (medium risk) and permit-sensitive old buildings—already over-represented in the pilot—lost first-time-right and gained a long tail. Alignments show repeated installs without a preceding survey and missing permit checks on older apartments.

Second, activation after billing. Installation → activation test fell (78% → 60%); manual activation and manual-activation → billing rose. Frequent move-on-log: billing before a successful activation test, then complaint after billing. That is a compliance-like and customer-trust defect, not a scheduling win.

Third, concentration. Coordinator utilization hit 93% in two regions; second-visit utilization rose to 92% while survey capacity sat idle (78% → 52%). Failed-visit → second-visit delay grew where coordinators were busiest. The handover network simplified; the working-together network centralized. When a coordinator was absent, cases stalled. Fewer handovers bought accountability and lost spare capacity.

The evidence cannot support **A**. Scaling R nationally would export a worse tail, more rework, more post-billing activation, and a brittle single-owner bottleneck into a mix that is *less* apartment-heavy than the pilot—and we still lack complete segment/risk attributes, start timestamps, and 60-day cost. The evidence also cannot support **B**. Rolling back would throw away a clean, segment-specific gain on speed, handovers, and SLA for simple houses.

**What to do now**

1. **Scale only the proven slice:** coordinator ownership plus merged validate/schedule for simple, low-risk houses, with SLA and first-time-right guardrails already observed in that segment.
2. **Pause immediately:** survey skipping on medium-risk and unknown-risk addresses; activation or billing start before a successful activation test; any path that omits permit check on older or permit-sensitive buildings.
3. **Add hard controls:** restore survey and permit gates for apartments and old buildings; keep billing start downstream of a passed activation test; cap coordinator load and define a backup owner so absence does not freeze the case.
4. **Measure before any further scale:** validate the building-risk rule against actual survey outcomes; randomize any residual skip policy by risk stratum; instrument start times and reason codes; capture 60-day cost after billing; monitor the two forbidden deviations in near real time; split customer experience into speed-to-appointment versus working service.

Do not treat the pilot as a single “R works / R fails” vote. R is a bundle. The median improved because the easy path got shorter. The tail, rework, precision collapse (fitness 0.94 / precision 0.51), and complaint mix worsened because the bundle deleted surveys, permits, and activation discipline for the cases that needed them, then overloaded the remaining owner. The next decision is not courage versus caution. It is whether we will scale a *gated* design that keeps the simple-house factory and puts the exception factory back under control.

Broader rollout is justified only when the paused shortcuts, if reintroduced at all, do so under a validated risk rule; when p90/p95, first-time-right, second visit, manual activation, and 30-day complaints in apartments and old buildings are at least back to baseline; when billing-before-activation and missing-permit rates are near zero; and when fully loaded 60-day cost is no worse than baseline.

# 3. Explanation of metric conflicts

**High fitness, low precision.** Fitness asks whether observed cases can be replayed on the model. In the pilot, a large share of cases travel the new coordinator / combined-validate-schedule / skip-survey path, so replay succeeds more often (0.94 vs 0.87). Precision asks whether the model allows behavior that is *not* a sensible or intended process. The pilot model admits many legal-looking combinations of skipped survey, repeated install, manual activation, and billing-before-activation. Alignments confirm those combinations actually occur. The model therefore “fits the log” while describing a much larger language than the intended process (precision 0.51). Generalization also falls (0.70 → 0.56): the discovered structure is overfitted to a short, exception-heavy pilot rather than a stable process.

**Improved median, worse tail.** The median is dominated by the 34% simple-house segment, which became shorter (median −6 days, p90 −8). The right tail is dominated by apartments, old permit-sensitive buildings, failed visits, second visits, and complaint-after-billing. Those segments lengthened sharply (apartment p90 +12 days; old building p90 +25). A left-shifted body plus a heavier right tail is exactly the pattern of a design that accelerates the easy path and removes gates on the hard path. Sparse start timestamps also make waiting time inside visits harder to separate from calendar cycle time, so tail inflation is more trustworthy as an ordering of cases than as a precise wait-time diagnosis.

**Fewer handovers, lower resilience.** Handovers are a count of work passing between roles. Coordinator ownership mechanically cuts that count (34% → 12% of cases with 3+ handovers) and simplifies the handover-of-work network. Resilience is about whether the case can continue when a person or team is unavailable. Context now sits with one owner at 93% utilization; the working-together network is centralized on coordinators and activation specialists; absence stalls the case. Recovery work also moved to a now-saturated second-visit team (92%). The process has fewer interfaces and fewer spare interfaces.

**Lower visible cost, higher rework / downstream cost.** The visible cost series stops before full complaint handling and, after week 4, excludes that handling entirely. Survey capacity was taken out of the path (utilization 78% → 52%), which removes a recorded cost. Failure cost re-enters as second visits, manual activations, activation-specialist exceptions, and 30-day complaints—partly outside the visible ledger. Hence EUR 184 → 161 can be true on the incomplete definition while EUR 198 → 207, first-time-right, and complaint rate all worsen. This is a measurement-boundary conflict, not a paradox in the economics.

**Simple houses improve; apartments and old buildings deteriorate.** R’s beneficial components—merged validate/schedule, fewer queues, a single owner—match the failure mode of simple houses (appointment wait, extra handovers). R’s harmful components—predictive survey skip, optional activation-after-billing, missing permit checks—match the failure mode of apartments and older buildings (hidden plant risk, permits, failed first visits, “works on paper, not in the dwelling”). The pilot over-samples those harder buildings, so the *average* of R looks worse on quality and tail than a national mix would, while still hiding that the average is a mixture of a genuine win and a genuine loss. SLA “better for simple houses, worse overall” is the same mixture stated as a service metric.

# 4. Decision alternatives

**A. Scale Redesign R nationally**  
*Advantages:* Fastest capture of median-time and handover gains; simpler org chart; visible unit-cost reduction on the current incomplete definition.  
*Risks:* Exports a worse tail, more second visits, more manual activations, more post-billing billing, missing permits, coordinator overload, and a low-precision process into every region. Pilot mix is *harder* than baseline, but even after that bias the quality metrics are unacceptable; national scale would still include large apartment and old-building stocks. Cost and attribute data are not decision-grade.  
*When reasonable:* Only if leadership were willing to accept higher complaints and SLA breach as the price of median speed *and* had already closed the activation-before-billing and permit gaps. That condition is not met. **Not recommended.**

**B. Roll back to the baseline process**  
*Advantages:* Restores activation-before-billing discipline, survey/permit practice, a less centralized network, and the better baseline quality/tail profile. Stops an under-measured pilot from becoming the default.  
*Risks:* Forfeits a large, clean gain for simple low-risk houses (speed, handovers, SLA, slightly better FTR and complaints). Returns appointment-wait complaints that R actually fixed. Treats a bundle failure as a total failure.  
*When reasonable:* If the organization cannot segment work by building risk, cannot enforce activation and permit gates, or if coordinator load cannot be staffed above a safe utilization. That is a capability constraint, not what the evidence requires today. **Too coarse.**

**C. Scale only coordinator ownership; suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation**  
*Advantages:* Keeps the accountability mechanism that cut handovers; removes the two design choices most tightly linked to alignments, DFG exception edges, apartment/old-building loss, and the new complaint mix. Lower implementation complexity than a full hybrid factory.  
*Risks:* Leaves merged validate/schedule and simple-house skip value on the table (or, if merge is kept, still needs a risk gate). Does not by itself fix 93% coordinator utilization or absence risk. Still a bundled national change without a cleaner experiment.  
*When reasonable:* If IT/process systems cannot maintain two front-door paths, but operations *can* restore survey and activation gates immediately. A sound fallback if E is judged too complex.

**D. Controlled regional experiment with better segment/risk measurement**  
*Advantages:* Honest about confounding (pilot mix, missing attributes before week 3, sparse start times, truncated cost). Can identify which component of R causes which effect. Highest scientific quality.  
*Risks:* Delays harvest of the already-observed simple-house gain; if the current pilot continues unchanged in those regions, customers keep paying the tail/rework price. “More study” can become a way to avoid stopping known-harmful shortcuts.  
*When reasonable:* As the *measurement layer* under E or C, not as the only action. Reasonable as the sole next step only if leadership refuses any partial scale until causality is identified.

**E. Hybrid: R for simple low-risk houses; controls for apartments/old buildings; measure before wider scale**  
*Advantages:* Matches the segment evidence rather than the average. Scales what improved (coordinator + merge on simple houses). Reinstalls survey/permit/activation controls where the tail, alignments, and complaints degraded. Creates a natural contrast set for the measurements that still matter. Compatible with a later, narrower reintroduction of skip rules.  
*Risks:* Two operating modes increase routing error if risk attributes remain incomplete (they were incomplete before week 3). Coordinators may still overload if simple-house volume is large. Hybrid designs decay unless conformance on the forbidden edges is monitored.  
*When reasonable:* When segment effects point in opposite directions, when some components are already implicated by alignments, and when coverage gaps are large enough to block national scale but not large enough to hide a simple-house win. **That is the present case.**

# 6. Final recommendation

**Recommended decision: E**, with C’s suspensions built in as hard gates, and D’s measurement agenda run on a fixed clock—not as a substitute for action.

**Immediate action**
- Route **simple, low-risk houses** to Redesign R: coordinator ownership after address validation and merged validate/schedule.
- Stand up daily conformance on two forbidden traces: **billing start before successful activation test**, and **permit check absent** on older / permit-sensitive buildings.
- Cap coordinator utilization and name a deputy owner so absence does not stall cases.
- Rebalance staffing: pull idle survey capacity back onto apartment and old-building work; relieve the second-visit queue.

**What to pause**
- Survey skipping for medium-risk, unknown-risk, apartment, and permit-sensitive addresses.
- Any activation check moved after billing start, including “signal looked acceptable.”
- National or additional-region rollout of the full R bundle.
- Interpretation of incomplete visible cost as proof of savings.

**What to scale**
- Coordinator ownership **and** merged validate/schedule **only** for the simple low-risk house segment, where median, p90, first-time-right, complaints, and SLA already move together in the right direction.
- Do **not** scale skip-survey or post-billing activation.

**What to measure next** (in order)
1. M7 conformance rates on the two forbidden edges.  
2. M6 validation of the building-risk rule against survey outcomes.  
3. M3 reason codes on fail / manual activation / complaint / second visit.  
4. M4 60-day fully loaded cost after billing start.  
5. Then M1 (randomized skip by stratum) only inside strata that M6 says are truly low risk.  
6. M2, M8, M5 as supporting instrumentation.

**Thresholds for broader rollout**
Broader rollout beyond simple low-risk houses—or any reintroduction of skip / late activation—requires **all** of the following, measured on a post-fix window at least as long as the complaint horizon:
- Billing-before-successful-activation and missing-permit rates at or near zero (M7).
- Apartment and old-building p90/p95, first-time-right, second-visit rate, manual-activation rate, and 30-day complaints **at least back to baseline**, and SLA breach no worse than baseline in those segments.
- Fully loaded 60-day cost (M4) ≤ baseline on a like-for-like definition.
- Building-risk rule, if used at all, validated (M6) and then confirmed in a stratified experiment (M1) with no material rise in fail/second-visit/complaint rates.
- Coordinator utilization with a working backup path that does not reproduce absence stalls (M5).

If those gates fail, keep E’s simple-house factory and treat the rest of R as rejected, not as “needs more time.” If they pass only for additional well-measured low-risk strata, widen E by stratum—not by slogan.
