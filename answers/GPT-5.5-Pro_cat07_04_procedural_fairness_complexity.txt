# 1. Fairness dimensions

**Outcome parity.** The raw approval gaps are large: Group A approval is 78%, versus 63% for Group B, 69% for Group C, and 58% for Group D. But raw approval parity is not the right fairness test here because groups differ strongly in measured risk, documentation completeness, channel, language needs, accommodation needs, and prior case history. The raw outcome gaps are evidence of disparity, not by themselves evidence of discrimination.

**Adjusted outcome disparity.** After controlling for measured case-mix variables, residual approval gaps remain: Group B is -5.5 percentage points, Group C is -2.2 points, and Group D is -3.1 points relative to Group A. These adjusted gaps are more concerning than the raw gaps because they remain after controlling for several legitimate complexity factors. Still, they do not prove discrimination because some controls may be biased, incomplete, or post-treatment mediators.

**Procedural burden.** Procedural fairness includes whether applicants must go through more steps, handovers, document requests, reviews, or appeals. Group B and Group C face especially high support-related burden: Group B is much more likely to go through Language Support Contact, and Group C is much more likely to go through Accommodation Review. These steps are legitimate and rights-preserving, but they appear to add substantial delay and handover burden.

**Timeliness.** Raw median cycle time is 8.1 days for Group A, compared with 13.7 for Group B, 15.9 for Group C, and 12.1 for Group D. After adjustment, Group B still has +2.6 days, Group C +4.1 days, and Group D +0.9 days. The adjusted time gap is especially concerning for Group C and Group B because it appears tied to support-step bottlenecks rather than only to case complexity.

**Consistency of routing.** Some routing differences are legitimate: high-risk cases should receive Fraud Review; accommodation requests should receive Accommodation Review; minority-language applicants should receive Language Support Contact. The fairness question is whether these rules are applied consistently and whether comparable applicants receive comparable routing. The adjusted increases in Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents for Group B and Group C suggest possible residual inconsistency or extra scrutiny.

**Quality and accessibility of support.** Language support and accommodation review should make the process more accessible, not slower or more burdensome. A fair process would treat those steps as part of equal access, with sufficient staffing, clear service targets, and low-friction handoffs. In the current evidence, support steps seem to function partly as bottlenecks.

**Appeal and rework burden.** Group B, Group C, and Group D all have higher reopen/rework rates than Group A. Appeals are especially high for Group B and Group D, and adjusted appeal probability remains +4.4 points for Group B and +5.7 points for Group D. This could reflect more complex cases, more denials, poorer explanations, inconsistent decisions, or applicants needing appeals to correct procedural problems.

**Resource-allocation fairness.** Group B cases are concentrated among 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. Group C cases are concentrated among 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also do supervisor review. This is a major procedural fairness issue: even if routing to specialists is legitimate, under-capacity in those specialist pools can impose unequal waiting costs on groups needing legally or ethically required support.

# 2. Adjusted comparison strategy

The analysis should separate four concepts:

**Disparity** means groups experience different outcomes, paths, waits, or burdens.

**Legitimate routing** means the difference follows an explicit policy-relevant need, such as high risk requiring Fraud Review, accommodation requests requiring Accommodation Review, or language needs requiring support.

**Potential discrimination or unfair burden** means similarly situated applicants, or applicants with legitimate support needs, receive slower, more burdensome, less consistent, or less favorable treatment without adequate justification.

**Uncertainty** means the observed data cannot fully identify the causal source because of missing variables, biased measurements, non-random assignment, and limited workload data.

A reasonable strategy would use several linked analyses rather than one adjusted approval model.

**Matching or stratification variables.** Build matched or stratified comparisons using risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, and month. Also examine language_preference and disability_accommodation_requested in two ways: first as legitimate need indicators, and second as possible sources of procedural burden. Do not blindly control them away when estimating the burden of accessing support.

Useful strata include:

| Comparison purpose          | Suggested strata                                                                                           |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| General case-mix adjustment | risk_score_band, documentation_completeness, channel, household_size_band, prior_case_count, region, month |
| Language-support fairness   | language_preference, channel, documentation_completeness, region, month                                    |
| Accommodation fairness      | disability_accommodation_requested, household_size_band, region, month                                     |
| Risk-routing fairness       | risk_score_band, prior_case_count, region, month                                                           |
| Channel fairness            | online vs in-person within group, documentation_completeness, region, month                                |

The analysis should check common support. For example, if Group C has many accommodation-request cases and Group A has few, direct matched comparisons may be weak. In those strata, the better question is whether accommodation cases receive timely and effective support, not whether they look like non-accommodation cases.

**Path and variant comparison.** Compare variant frequencies by group before and after matching. For example, compare the probability of V2, V3, V4, V5, and V6 conditional on the relevant need indicators. The main question is not merely “which group has more complex paths?” but “within comparable need and risk strata, does one group experience more document requests, reviews, handovers, or appeals?”

**Resource-assignment comparison.** Model the probability of assignment to overloaded officers, scarce specialist pools, or high-handover teams conditional on case mix, region, month, channel, and support need. For Group B, compare applicants needing language support across regions and across bilingual caseworkers. For Group C, compare accommodation-request cases handled by different specialists and by specialists who also carry supervisor-review duties.

**Waiting-time decomposition.** Decompose total cycle time into transition-level waits:

* Submit Application → Identity Verification
* Completeness Check → Request Missing Documents
* Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment
* Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment
* Risk Screening → Fraud Review
* Supervisor Review → Decision Draft
* Decision Draft → Applicant Notification

Then estimate how much of each group’s total cycle-time gap is due to path mix, queue time within the same path, specialist-resource assignment, regional backlog, and residual unexplained differences. This matters because Group B and Group C may have longer total times not only because they take different paths, but because the support-related transitions themselves are slow.

**Sensitivity analysis for biased covariates.** Risk_score_band and documentation_completeness should not be treated as unquestionably neutral. Risk scores may encode historical enforcement, prior surveillance, or differential access. Documentation completeness may be affected by language barriers, disability, channel, literacy, or confusing forms. Run alternative models:

* with and without risk_score_band;
* with risk_score_band replaced by finer observable risk components, if available;
* with and without documentation_completeness;
* treating documentation_completeness as an outcome of accessibility rather than only as a control;
* excluding post-intake or post-support variables from the main causal adjustment;
* bounding how large unobserved bias would need to be to explain the residual group gaps.

**Subgroup checks by region and channel.** The scenario specifically mentions two regions with more handovers for Group B and Group C. The analysis should therefore estimate group-by-region and group-by-channel interactions. A process may look moderately fair in aggregate but be procedurally unfair in specific regional offices or in-person channels.

**Uncertainty.** Report confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals for adjusted differences, variant shares, transition-level wait gaps, and resource-assignment gaps. Cluster uncertainty by caseworker, region, and month where possible. Avoid treating a single adjusted model as definitive.

# 3. Path and resource disparities

The most important disparities are procedural, not only outcome-based.

**Fast standard path V1.** V1 has the shortest median cycle time, 5.8 days. Group A uses this path in 42% of cases, compared with 19% for Group B, 22% for Group C, and 18% for Group D. Some of this is probably legitimate because Groups B, C, and D have more incomplete documentation, language-support needs, accommodation requests, or higher risk. But the size of the gap means access to the simple path is highly unequal.

**Request-documents path V2.** V2 is much more common for Group B, 34%, and Group C, 29%, than for Group A, 24%. Group B also has a much higher incomplete-documentation share, 46%, which partly justifies more document requests. However, the adjusted probability of Request Missing Documents is still +9.5 points for Group B and +4.2 points for Group C. That residual gap is concerning because it may indicate unclear instructions, language barriers, accessibility problems, or stricter interpretation by staff.

**Language-support path V4.** V4 is used by 24% of Group B but only 1% of Group A, 3% of Group C, and 2% of Group D. This difference is mostly expected because 57% of Group B needs language support. The fairness problem is not the existence of Language Support Contact. The problem is that V4 has a 19.2-day median cycle time and Language Support Contact → Eligibility Assessment has a 3.8-day median wait. Group B is also concentrated among only 7 bilingual caseworkers and 3 overloaded regional officers. That suggests support capacity is not proportional to need.

**Accommodation path V5.** V5 is used by 31% of Group C but only 2% of Group A, 3% of Group B, and 3% of Group D. This is largely legitimate because 62% of Group C requests accommodation. But V5 has the longest median cycle time among the listed non-appeal paths, 21.6 days, and Accommodation Review → Eligibility Assessment has a 4.7-day median wait. A rights-preserving accommodation step should not become a queue penalty.

**Fraud-review path V3.** V3 is used by 28% of Group D, much higher than the other groups. This is partly justified because Group D has a 51% high-risk-score share and 49% high prior-case-count share. The Risk Screening → Fraud Review wait is also only moderately different across groups: 2.8 days for Group D versus 2.2 for Group A. However, this conclusion depends heavily on whether risk_score_band is valid and unbiased. If the risk score reflects historical disparities or differential surveillance, then apparently legitimate fraud routing may still reproduce unfairness.

**Appeal path V6.** V6 is more common for Group D, 13%, and Group B, 8%, than for Group A, 4%. Appeals have a 30.7-day median cycle time. The adjusted probability of appeal remains especially high for Group D, +5.7 points, and Group B, +4.4 points. This is concerning but ambiguous. It may reflect more denials, weaker explanations, higher applicant disagreement, inconsistent decisions, or a need to use appeals to correct initial procedural deficiencies.

**Supervisor Review.** Group B has an adjusted +8.0 percentage-point probability of Supervisor Review, and Group C has +5.5 points. Group D’s +2.0 points is easier to explain by risk. The Group B and Group C differences are more concerning because they may reflect extra scrutiny, specialist bottlenecks, or supervisor review being used as a compensating control for language/accommodation cases.

**Handovers.** Group B has more handovers from general caseworker → bilingual caseworker → general caseworker. Group C has more handovers from general caseworker → accommodation specialist → supervisor. These handovers may be operationally necessary, but they create delay, information loss, repeated explanation burden, and a higher risk of inconsistent treatment.

Overall, the strongest procedural fairness concerns are not about the existence of specialized routing. They are about the capacity, waiting time, and handover burden attached to rights-preserving routes.

# 4. Plausible mechanisms

Several mechanisms could generate the observed patterns.

**Legitimate complexity and policy routing.** Group D has higher measured risk and prior-case counts, so more Fraud Review and some additional delay are expected. Group B has more incomplete documentation and language-support needs, and Group C has more accommodation requests, so additional support steps are also expected.

**Specialist-capacity bottlenecks.** Group B depends on only 7 bilingual caseworkers. Group C depends on 5 accommodation specialists, two of whom also perform supervisor review. This can create queues even when frontline staff act neutrally.

**Support steps treated as add-ons rather than integrated access.** Language support and accommodation review appear to be sequential handoff steps rather than embedded capabilities. That design can convert rights-preserving support into extra waiting time.

**Channel effects.** Group B has a high in-person channel share, 38%, compared with 9% for Group A. If in-person intake requires scanning, appointment scheduling, manual verification, or regional-office routing, this could explain some Submit → Identity Verification delay.

**Documentation-accessibility problem.** Higher missing-document rates for Group B and Group C may not only reflect applicant behavior. They may reflect forms, instructions, portals, or evidence requirements that are less accessible to minority-language applicants or applicants needing accommodation.

**Regional backlog.** The scenario notes that two regions have backlogs and more handovers. If Group B and Group C are concentrated in those regions, regional workload can create group disparities without individual-level discriminatory intent.

**Risk-score feedback loop.** Group D’s routing may be formally consistent with policy, but if risk_score_band encodes historical disparities, past enforcement intensity, or biased labels, then “risk-based” routing may reproduce unfair procedural burden.

**Discretionary extra scrutiny.** The adjusted increases in Supervisor Review and Request Missing Documents for Group B and Group C could reflect staff uncertainty, inconsistent standards, language misunderstandings, or more conservative decision-making for some applicant groups.

**Poor explanation quality.** Higher appeals for Group B and Group D may reflect unclear or inaccessible decision notices, not only incorrect decisions. Appeal_flag alone cannot distinguish justified disagreement from poor communication.

# 5. Limitations of available attributes

The available attributes are useful but insufficient for a definitive fairness judgment.

**Measurement bias.** risk_score_band may not be neutral. It may be trained on historical outcomes affected by prior enforcement patterns, differential documentation, or selective investigation. documentation_completeness may also be partly caused by the process itself: confusing instructions, lack of translation, inaccessible forms, or inadequate pre-submission support can make an application appear incomplete.

**Missing applicant needs.** The log does not capture literacy, digital access, disability type, severity of accommodation need, immigration or housing instability, caregiver responsibilities, urgency of benefit need, quality of submitted evidence, or whether the applicant understood instructions. Without these, adjusted comparisons may still be confounded.

**Unobserved resource workload.** Workload is observed only at daily granularity. That may miss hour-level queues, sick leave, part-time schedules, appointment slots, batch processing, caseworker skill differences, and actual caseload complexity. Resource assignment is not random, so overloaded-resource effects may be entangled with case difficulty.

**Appeal quality and appeal reason.** appeal_flag says that an appeal occurred, but not whether the appeal succeeded, whether the initial decision was wrong, whether the applicant received a clear explanation, or whether the appeal was caused by procedural confusion.

**Outcome quality.** case_outcome does not capture whether the benefit amount was correct, whether the applicant received timely interim help, whether communication was respectful, or whether the process imposed avoidable stress.

**Historical bias in risk scores.** If the risk score was calibrated on historical fraud findings, prior case counts, or past administrative attention, it may encode earlier unequal treatment. Controlling for it may understate unfairness.

**Post-treatment controls.** Some variables may be affected by earlier process steps. For example, documentation_completeness could be measured after an interaction with staff. If so, controlling for it can hide procedural barriers.

**Event-log incompleteness.** Informal calls, translation difficulties, failed contacts, repeated applicant attempts, quality of language interpretation, or internal staff discussions may not be recorded as events.

Therefore, the evidence supports a strong procedural fairness concern, especially around support-step burden, but not a definitive causal finding of discriminatory treatment.

# 6. Mitigation options and side effects

| Mitigation                                                                           | Why it helps                                                                                                               | Possible side effects or trade-offs                                                                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Add capacity for language support and accommodation review                           | Reduces the clearest bottlenecks: 3.8 days after Language Support Contact and 4.7 days after Accommodation Review          | Requires budget, hiring, training, and quality assurance; scarce specialists may be hard to recruit; demand may vary by region |
| Cross-train general caseworkers in basic language-access and accommodation workflows | Reduces dependence on a tiny specialist pool and lowers handover burden                                                    | Risk of lower-quality support if training is superficial; complex cases still need specialists                                 |
| Add service-level targets for support steps                                          | Makes rights-preserving steps visible in performance management rather than invisible queues                               | Targets can create gaming or rushed reviews unless quality measures are included                                               |
| Redesign routing to reduce handovers                                                 | Keeps a single case owner while specialists advise or join in parallel; reduces repeated explanations and information loss | More complex coordination; may blur accountability; specialist time may be fragmented                                          |
| Parallelize support and eligibility work where legally possible                      | Eligibility preparation can continue while language or accommodation support is arranged                                   | Risk of premature assessment if support input is essential; requires clear safeguards                                          |
| Improve decision explanations                                                        | Plain-language, translated, and accommodation-sensitive notices may reduce appeals and rework                              | More drafting effort; legal review needed; better explanations may initially increase appeals by making rights clearer         |
| Provide pre-submission document checklists and assisted intake                       | May reduce missing-document requests for Group B and Group C                                                               | Could slow intake if not well designed; online-only tools may disadvantage applicants with low digital access                  |
| Monitor adjusted disparities continuously                                            | Detects whether Group B and Group C remain slower after controlling for case mix, region, channel, and month               | Requires governance and privacy safeguards; small-cell subgroup monitoring can be noisy                                        |
| Audit risk_score_band                                                                | Checks calibration, false-positive rates, proxy effects, and whether risk-based routing is justified                       | May reduce apparent fraud-screening efficiency; requires access to model inputs, outcomes, and historical labels               |
| Audit documentation_completeness                                                     | Determines whether incompleteness reflects applicant complexity or inaccessible forms and instructions                     | May reveal need for costly form redesign, translation, or staff training                                                       |
| Balance regional workloads                                                           | Addresses the two-region backlog affecting Group B and Group C handovers                                                   | May shift delays to other regions unless capacity is increased; remote reassignment may reduce local knowledge                 |
| Review supervisor-review criteria                                                    | Ensures Supervisor Review is triggered consistently and not used as vague extra scrutiny                                   | Too much standardization may reduce discretion for genuinely complex cases                                                     |

The key principle is to preserve language support and accommodation review while removing the delay penalty attached to them. Removing support steps would make cycle time look better while making access less fair.

# 8. Bottom-line assessment

The evidence does not justify saying that the process is discriminatory merely because approval rates differ. The groups have different measured case mix, and some routing differences are legitimate.

However, the evidence also does not support a clean finding of procedural fairness. After adjustment, Group B and Group C still experience longer cycle times, higher probabilities of extra review or document requests, and substantial support-step delays. The most concerning pattern is that rights-preserving steps — Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review — appear to impose extra waiting time, handovers, and resource bottlenecks on the applicants who need them.

Group D’s additional Fraud Review is more plausibly justified by measured risk and prior-case count, but that conclusion remains conditional on the validity and neutrality of risk_score_band. The high adjusted appeal probability for Group D also deserves review.

The calibrated conclusion is: **procedural fairness is indeterminate with respect to intentional discrimination, but the process shows serious procedural fairness risks, especially for Group B and Group C.** The strongest evidence points to unequal procedural burden caused by specialist-capacity constraints, support-step delays, regional handovers, and possibly biased or incomplete complexity measures.
