# 1. Network-by-network interpretation

- **Handover-of-work network.** This directed network captures who tends to be followed by whom in the event sequence, so it is useful for seeing routing paths and likely coordination interfaces. Mira_Coordinator's high out-degree (0.82), in-degree (0.51), and betweenness (0.77), plus frequent handovers to adjusters and specialists and return handovers from them, make her a prominent connector in the observed exception flow. Bot_Rules → Mira_Coordinator is consistent with an automated exception route; Mira → specialist/adjuster → Mira patterns are consistent with assignments and answers returning through a coordination point. This does not establish that Mira performs the substantive claim work, has formal authority, or causes delays. Edge frequencies are affected by how often a resource is eligible to handle a case, and elapsed time between events includes queueing, other work, and logging gaps rather than only service time. Bot_Ingest → Bot_Rules and the team-lead → Vendor_Pay paths also show that automation and vendors are important parts of the operating network.

- **Working-together network.** This undirected network measures resource co-occurrence on cases (here, the reported co-case rate), not sequential handoffs or the quality of collaboration. Mira's low weighted degree (0.16) and 4–8% co-case rates with several adjusters and team leads are compatible with her brief routing, clarification, or reopening events and subsequent absence for multiple events. Her low score therefore does not negate her connector role: a person can route a case without doing sustained work alongside the receiving resource. Conversely, co-occurrence alone does not prove close teamwork; it depends on case mix, graph thresholds, and the denominator used. The stronger team-lead/adjuster and adjuster-pair edges (19–27%) fit more sustained within-team work. Compare rates among cases with similar activities, teams, and exception status before interpreting them as organizational closeness.

- **Subcontracting network.** This network detects patterns where one resource's work is followed by another resource's work and then a return to the first, within the chosen event window. Mira's high subcontracting betweenness (0.69) and the Mira → Omar/Priya → Mira motifs fit a broker who requests expertise and receives the result to route, reopen, or clarify the case. Such motifs can also represent a control loop, rework, or a process rule that assigns follow-up to the original coordinator. The n=2 patterns do not prove intentional delegation, supervision, or that Mira caused rework; event ordering, activity mix, and the selected window can create or suppress motifs. The team-lead/adjuster return patterns and Temp_1/Vendor_Verify pattern show similar loops elsewhere in the process.

- **Performance/time statistics.** Mira touches only 22% of all claims but 64% of reopened claims and 71% of complaints, has a 48% exception-flag case share, and has high-variance cycle-time contribution. Together these point to concentrated exposure to difficult work, not automatically low performance. The absence-period increase in the exception cohort's 90th-percentile cycle time (8.4 to 13.9 days), while ordinary low-value claims stayed stable, is evidence that the exception flow may depend on her capacity. It is not a causal estimate without adjustment for incoming volume, staffing, case severity, and other concurrent changes. Her 4.2-minute median event service time is short and consistent with coordination events; it says little about end-to-end case waiting. The long vendor service times and elapsed gaps around Vendor_Verify and Vendor_Pay must also be considered when locating delays. Event counts, distinct cases, case-level exception shares, event service times, and case cycle-time contributions have different denominators and should not be compared as if interchangeable.

- **Role-discovery or activity-profile similarity.** Activity-based similarity can show whether Mira's activity mix resembles coordinators, adjusters, team leads, or another resource group. Given her routing, missing-information, reopen, and complaint work, she may form a distinct profile or cluster with exception-handling resources. Organizational-role discovery may identify a role from repeated activity and handover patterns, such as exception coordination or triage brokerage. These methods infer roles from recorded behavior; they do not establish a job title, reporting line, authority, or whether the observed assignment is appropriate. A distinct profile could reflect a genuine specialized function, a logging convention, or a workload concentrated on exceptional cases. Compare roles across time and case strata, and inspect whether bot and vendor resources are represented consistently.

# 2. Competing role hypotheses

1. **Formal coordinator / exception desk**
   - **Mechanism:** Bot_Rules and team leads route exceptional cases to Mira, who clarifies missing information, assigns a suitable specialist or adjuster, handles complaints and reopens, and receives responses for the next routing decision.
   - **Evidence supporting it:** The named cross-team coordinator role; the Bot_Rules → Mira edge; her high shares of reopened claims and complaints; numerous Mira-to-specialist/adjuster handovers; return motifs; and the reported accumulation of exception queues during her absence.
   - **Evidence limiting it:** She appears on only 22% of claims, ordinary low-value cycle time did not change during the absence, and there is no supplied policy or assignment record proving that these decisions are formally part of her role. The owner describes her as a queue cleaner, while adjusters describe broader expertise.
   - **Future-log predictions:** Exception cases should have a repeatable route into Mira's queue, recognizable assignment or clarification activities, and a fairly stable return path. After controlling for case mix and workload, her involvement should correlate with more complete routing and fewer unmanaged escalations. A documented backup performing the same activities should produce similar paths.

2. **Hidden bottleneck / key-person dependency**
   - **Mechanism:** Exception work depends on Mira's capacity or tacit knowledge, so incoming cases wait for her decisions even when other resources could perform some routing tasks.
   - **Evidence supporting it:** Her high handover and subcontracting centrality, high-variance cycle-time contribution, concentration in reopened claims and complaints, and the rise in exception P90 cycle time during her six-day absence.
   - **Evidence limiting it:** The absence comparison is short and uncontrolled; ordinary work was unaffected; centrality may reflect a designed queue interface rather than a capacity constraint. High event service time is not reported for Mira, and vendors or specialist queues may account for substantial elapsed time.
   - **Future-log predictions:** Queue age and exception P90 should rise with Mira's backlog or absence, especially before her first routing event. Cases waiting on vendors or specialists should be distinguishable from cases awaiting Mira. A trained backup or additional coordinator capacity should reduce Mira-specific waits and tail cycle time without simply moving the queue downstream.

3. **Organizational boundary spanner / triage broker**
   - **Mechanism:** Mira translates between automated routing, teams, adjusters, and specialist functions, matching unusual cases to the right expertise and bringing answers back into the claim flow.
   - **Evidence supporting it:** The Bot_Rules-to-Mira path, cross-team label, high betweenness across resources, specialist return motifs, and the adjusters' account that she knows where hard cases should go. Low co-case rates are expected when brokering is episodic rather than sustained co-production.
   - **Evidence limiting it:** Network paths do not reveal the substance or quality of her judgment. Team leads also route work and release payments, and the data do not show whether Mira's assignment choices outperform predefined rules or other coordinators.
   - **Future-log predictions:** Her involvement should be concentrated at transitions between activity or expertise domains. Cases with similar complexity routed through her should show fewer inappropriate specialist referrals or fewer repeat handoffs, after case-mix adjustment. Activity descriptions or assignment records should show matching and translation work, not just queue movement.

4. **Quality gatekeeper or automation monitor**
   - **Mechanism:** Mira reviews exceptions generated by Bot_Rules, resolves ambiguous flags, and either clears them or sends them for manual/specialist review.
   - **Evidence supporting it:** Bot_Rules → Mira is frequent; the bot handles triage and fraud screening; exception cases dominate Mira's workload; and a Bot_Rules/Mira subcontracting motif includes a fraud-screen update.
   - **Evidence limiting it:** No decision outcomes, override reason codes, false-positive rates, or explicit review activity are provided. Routing after a flag is not itself evidence of validation or quality control.
   - **Future-log predictions:** Bot flags followed by Mira should have a recorded disposition, override or escalation reason, and subsequent bot or case outcome. Her involvement should be associated with measurable changes in flag disposition, referral accuracy, or downstream correction rates.

5. **Data or workflow artifact**
   - **Mechanism:** A system may assign a nominal resource to routing events, record replies under the original coordinator, or serialize parallel work in a way that exaggerates Mira's apparent network position.
   - **Evidence supporting it:** Her short routing-like events, low co-case rates despite many handovers, and the sensitivity of subcontracting patterns to ordering and event-window choices leave room for logging effects.
   - **Evidence limiting it:** The role descriptions, reported operational experience, and absence-period queue accumulation provide independent evidence that a real coordination function exists, even if the measured magnitude is distorted.
   - **Future-log predictions:** Audit trails may show that some Mira-attributed events were system-generated, bulk-entered, or timestamped at a different point from the actual decision. Alternative resource attribution or timestamp handling should materially change her centrality if the artifact is substantial.

# 3. Risk assessment

- **Bottleneck risk:** Concentrated routing authority can create queues, particularly for reopened claims and complaints. The absence-period signal warrants checking queue age and workload, but it does not prove that Mira is the sole cause of delay.
- **Coordination risk:** If assignments and specialist responses pass through one person, unclear handoff ownership can lead to dropped follow-ups, duplicate requests, or cases parked between teams. The low co-case rate makes explicit case ownership and return-to-queue rules especially useful.
- **Key-person risk:** Tacit knowledge about where difficult cases belong may be hard to replace during leave or turnover. This is a resilience concern even if the role is performing well. A trained backup and documented routing logic would reduce single-person dependency.
- **Compliance/control risk:** The reviewer raises a legitimate segregation-of-duties question if the same person detects an issue, chooses the reviewer, and controls follow-up. The evidence does not establish misconduct. Preserve reasons for overrides and specialist selection, define approval boundaries for consequential decisions, and use risk-based independent review or sampling. Avoid treating network centrality as proof of inappropriate discretion.
- **Workload fairness risk:** Mira's workload is not well represented by her share of all claims: exceptional and complaint cases are likely more complex and emotionally demanding. Allocation comparisons should account for complexity, waiting, rework, and interruption load, not just case counts. Adjusters and specialists also carry different activity types and service burdens.
- **Data-quality risk:** Incorrect resource attribution, missing lifecycle start/complete events, inconsistent timestamps or time zones, case merges, reopens, parallel work serialized into a single order, and bot/vendor events logged differently can distort paths, service times, and centrality. Validate these before making staffing or control decisions.

# 4. Further process-mining analyses

1. **Handover-of-work network:** Format and validate the event data, then discover handover networks separately for exception-flagged and non-exception cases and for reopened/complaint variants. Compare Mira's outgoing and incoming handovers, betweenness, edge rates per eligible case, and transition delays across weeks. Inspect whether paths differ by case type, priority, region, and channel; retain Bot_Rules, Vendor_Verify, and Vendor_Pay so the analysis covers the full service chain.
2. **Working-together network:** Discover the co-case network by exception status and case type, with consistent case denominators. Test whether Mira's low co-occurrence persists within comparable exception cases and whether it changes when cases are defined as reopened episodes rather than whole claim lifecycles. Report both raw co-case rates and normalized association measures so common resources do not dominate solely through high case volume.
3. **Subcontracting network:** Discover motifs for several event windows (for example, n=1, 2, and 3) and stratify by activity and exception status. Check whether Mira → specialist → Mira sequences remain common after deduplicating repeated events and accounting for eligible opportunities. Measure whether return loops end in resolution, another referral, or reopening; distinguish necessary consultation from repeated rework.
4. **Activity similarity and role discovery:** Use activity-based resource similarity and organizational role discovery on comparable time periods. Compare Mira with team leads, temporary staff, adjusters, and bots; repeat after restricting to human resources or exception cases. Inspect the activities and handover patterns that drive each inferred role rather than interpreting a cluster label as an official role.
5. **Filtering and cohort comparisons:** Use event-attribute filters for `exception_flag`, activities such as `Reopen Claim` and `Escalate Complaint`, and case attributes such as priority and amount band. Filter variants to compare common routing paths and use time-range filters to compare the Mira absence with matched periods. For an absence analysis, control or match for incoming volume, case mix, staffing, and vendor load, and report uncertainty rather than a simple before/after causal claim.
6. **Service and waiting time:** Estimate service time only where lifecycle or timestamp semantics support it; compare that with elapsed time between events and case-level cycle time. Use service-time and case service/waiting-time analyses to separate time attributed to human, bot, and vendor work from queue waits, especially waits before Mira's routing and after specialist/vendor handoffs. Validate how the log represents starts, completions, and parallel activity, and compare median and tail percentiles by exception status and resource. The supplied per-event medians and handover gaps alone cannot locate the cause of end-to-end delay.

# 5. Recommendation

- **Change immediately:** Keep the exception coordination function available while documenting its intake criteria, assignment and escalation rules, ownership of follow-up, and decision reasons. Name and train a backup, and make queue ownership visible so a case does not wait for an undocumented personal handoff. Add a lightweight, risk-based review of specialist selection or overrides for high-impact cases, with an audit trail; use the review to clarify controls rather than presume a problem.
- **Monitor:** Track exception queue size and age, Mira-specific waits, case cycle-time P50/P90, reopen and repeat-referral rates, complaint resolution, specialist and vendor waits, formal escalations, and workload by complexity. Compare matched cohorts and periods and include the automated and vendor stages. Review whether backup coverage changes those measures without creating specialist overload.
- **Do not change until more evidence is available:** Do not remove Mira, infer poor performance from centrality, or conclude that she has excessive discretion from network structure alone. Do not collapse her role into the team leads' jobs or change automated routing based only on raw frequencies. First validate event attribution, quantify delays by stage, evaluate routing outcomes, and test backup coverage. If the coordination function is later redesigned, preserve its routing knowledge and exception ownership in a staffed, auditable replacement.

# 6. Short executive summary

Mira appears to coordinate exceptional claims across automation, adjusters, and specialists. Her high handover and subcontracting centrality reflects frequent routing and return paths; her low working-together score is compatible with short, episodic coordination events rather than sustained joint case work. She touches a minority of all claims but most reopened claims and complaints. The increase in exception-case tail cycle time during her absence suggests a dependency worth investigating, although one short absence cannot establish causation. Network measures do not show whether her decisions are correct, whether she has formal authority, or whether she causes delays. Keep the function in place while documenting routing and follow-up rules, training a backup, and recording reasons for consequential referrals. Monitor exception queues and cycle-time percentiles alongside specialist and vendor waits. Validate timestamps and resource attribution, then compare similar cases across periods. Avoid removing or redesigning the role until its outcomes and the effect of backup coverage are measured.
