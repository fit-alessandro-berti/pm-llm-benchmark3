You are advising a pharmaceutical wholesaler after a cyber incident disabled its warehouse management system and parts of its order-to-delivery workflow. The event log shows before, during, and after periods. During the incident, teams improvised: they skipped system checks, used manual pick lists, rerouted critical orders, and added temporary controls. Some adaptations preserved throughput and protected hospitals; others created hidden compliance and traceability risk.

There is no single hidden ground-truth answer. Your answer will be judged by an LLM-as-a-judge for temporal reasoning, abductive interpretation, distinction between resilience and concealed noncompliance, and concrete recovery redesign.

Question:
Determine which adaptations indicate resilience, which create hidden risk, and how the recovery process should be redesigned.

Process context:
Cases are customer orders. Customers are Hospital-critical, Pharmacy-standard, and Wholesale-bulk. Products include Ambient, Cold-chain, Controlled-substance, and Recall-sensitive items. The normal process is:
Receive order -> Validate customer/product authorization -> Inventory reservation -> Pick -> Pack -> Quality/release check -> Dispatch -> Proof of delivery -> Invoice close.
Cold-chain and controlled-substance orders require additional traceability and temperature/security checks.

Adaptation patterns observed:
A. Hospital-critical fast lane: orders for hospitals were triaged first, sometimes before full electronic authorization was available.
B. Manual authorization check: trained compliance staff checked customer/product eligibility using offline lists and phone confirmations.
C. Skip system authorization check: some teams shipped ambient products using customer history and assumed standing authorization.
D. Manual inventory snapshot check: inventory was checked against a printed snapshot from the morning of the incident.
E. Manual pick list: pickers used spreadsheets and paper labels; scans were later reconstructed.
F. Temporary release control: pharmacists manually checked a sample of orders and all controlled-substance orders.
G. Dispatch without scan: courier handoff occurred without the usual system scan.
H. Phone proof of delivery: delivery confirmation was collected by phone and entered later.
I. Defer noncritical order: non-urgent bulk orders were paused to preserve capacity.
J. Backlog prioritization: after systems returned, backlog was ordered by customer criticality and product risk.
K. Reconcile manual reservation: inventory and order records were corrected after system recovery.
L. Audit remediation: cases with missing scan, proof, temperature, or authorization events were remediated after operational close.

Risk and value observations:
- A protected critical hospital supply and appears aligned with mission priorities, but sometimes bypassed system authorization.
- B preserved control quality but consumed scarce compliance capacity.
- C preserved throughput for known ambient products but created a possible authorization gap.
- D was reasonable early in T2 but became risky as inventory became stale.
- E maintained physical flow but caused downstream traceability reconstruction.
- F reduced controlled-substance errors but missed some cold-chain temperature documentation.
- G accelerated dispatch but created the largest traceability gap.
- H recovered customer delivery evidence but is weak for controlled products.
- I protected critical capacity but increased cancellations and customer dissatisfaction among pharmacies.
- J appears resilient if priorities were explicit; possibly unfair if rules were informal.
- K and L restored compliance evidence but created a long hidden workload after cycle-time metrics appeared recovered.
