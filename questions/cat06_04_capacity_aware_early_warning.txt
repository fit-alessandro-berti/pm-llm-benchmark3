ROLE
You are designing a portfolio-level early-warning policy for live process cases. The goal is not merely to rank breach probabilities. You must decide when to intervene, which cases receive scarce capacity, how repeated alerts are handled, and how intervention spillovers and fairness are monitored. There is no single optimal answer because intervention effects and calibration are uncertain. Your answer will be judged for decision-theoretic coherence, operational realism, and explicit treatment of uncertainty.

PROCESS AND INTERVENTION
An enterprise service provider handles critical incidents through:
Incident Opened -> Triage -> Diagnosis Started -> Diagnosis Complete -> Resolution Work -> Customer Validation -> Closed.

The prediction target is breach of the contractual resolution deadline.

The intervention is a 45-minute “Senior Diagnostic Swarm.” It is useful only before `Diagnosis Complete`. It may accelerate diagnosis, expose missing information, or redirect the case. Once `Diagnosis Complete` has occurred, the same intervention has almost no effect on the breach probability.

Operational consequences
- Four swarm slots remain today.
- Two senior specialists deliver the swarm. A specialist interrupted for a swarm stops ordinary diagnosis work.
- Each used slot is expected to add 8–20 minutes of aggregate delay to other, non-intervened cases sharing the diagnosis queue.
- Repeated swarms on the same case are normally forbidden unless a materially new event has occurred.
- A swarm costs €450 in labor and coordination.
- A false alarm creates customer disruption and case-specific burden listed below.
- The intervention’s absolute breach-risk reduction is uncertain and heterogeneous; ranges are supplied per case.
- Using a slot on one case may increase breach risk for untreated cases. Under the base spillover model, each used slot increases the breach probability of each of six non-intervened diagnosis-stage cases by approximately 0.01–0.03, with strong dependence on congestion.

MODEL RELIABILITY NOTES
- For the SME segment, recent backtests suggest the model underpredicts breach risk by about 0.04–0.09.
- For Strategic Enterprise cases, it overpredicts by about 0.05–0.12 after a recent workflow redesign.
- For Public Sector cases, calibration is broadly acceptable but sample size is moderate.
- For cases using Non-Native-Language Support, the validation sample is small and both calibration and intervention-effect estimates are uncertain.
- The predicted probability is observational. It is not automatically the probability under intervention.

- One unlisted regulatory incident may arrive before 15:00. Management prefers to keep one slot in reserve, but this is a policy preference rather than an absolute rule.

EFFECT AND SPILLOVER SCENARIOS
Use at least these three portfolio scenarios:
1. Optimistic: selected cases receive the upper half of their risk-reduction range; spillover is near the low end.
2. Base: selected cases receive approximately the midpoint effect; each used slot creates the stated 0.01–0.03 spillover on six untreated diagnosis-stage cases.
3. Adverse: effects are near the lower bound; congestion causes 15–30 minutes of aggregate delay per slot; at least one selected case receives little or no benefit because the model’s reason for alerting was wrong.

TASK
Design an intervention policy for the current portfolio and for repeated use over time. Do not use a single breach-probability threshold without considering effect heterogeneity, stage eligibility, case value, false-alarm burden, calibration uncertainty, and spillovers.

You may use an expected-value calculation such as:
expected direct benefit ≈ estimated absolute risk reduction × value of avoiding breach − swarm cost − false-alarm burden,
but you must discuss uncertainty, non-monetary obligations, and queue externalities. Do not treat supplied effect ranges as known causal effects.

REQUIRED OUTPUT
Use exactly the following six sections.

## 1. Decision rule
State a deployable rule that determines eligibility, alert persistence, minimum evidence change, and prioritization. Include how you adjust or robustify probabilities for known calibration issues.

## 2. Capacity allocation for the current cases
Provide a table with one row for every case I-01 through I-10 and columns:
`Case | Eligible now? | Priority score or tier | Intervene / wait / do not intervene | Slot rationale | Main uncertainty`

Then name the selected portfolio of at most four cases and whether one slot is reserved.

## 3. Expected-impact table
For the selected portfolio, provide at least three rows: Optimistic, Base, and Adverse. Use columns:
`Scenario | Direct breaches plausibly avoided | Direct economic benefit range | Intervention cost | Spillover harm range | Net-impact interpretation | Confidence`

Approximate ranges are acceptable. Show enough arithmetic to make the table auditable, but do not claim unwarranted precision.

## 4. Repeated alerts and temporal policy
Define cool-down, suppression, escalation, and re-entry logic. Explicitly compare I-05 and I-07.

## 5. Fairness and feedback-loop risks
Discuss at least:
- value-based prioritization potentially starving lower-value cases;
- uncertainty for Non-Native-Language Support;
- differential calibration across segments;
- selective intervention changing future training labels;
- the possibility that spillover harms systematically fall on untreated groups.

Propose concrete safeguards, not just general principles.

## 6. Monitoring and recalibration plan
Specify operational, predictive, causal-effect, capacity, and fairness monitoring. Include a plan for estimating intervention effects, detecting policy-induced drift, and deciding when to change the threshold or allocation rule.

QUALITY CRITERIA
Strong answers refuse to spend a slot on I-03 merely because its risk is high, treat I-05’s repeated unchanged alert differently from I-07’s evidence-changing alert, and account for harm to untreated cases. Weak answers rank only by `p_now`, assume the intervention effect equals the observational prediction, or ignore segment-level uncertainty and feedback loops.
