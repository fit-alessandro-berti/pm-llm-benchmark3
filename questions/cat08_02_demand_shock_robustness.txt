You are advising the operations team of a regional diagnostic-lab sample process. The process is normally stable but becomes unstable during winter infection waves, equipment outages, courier delays, and specialist absences. Historical averages are misleading: different case types stress different resources and interventions that help one step often overload the next step.

There is no single hidden ground-truth answer. Your answer will be judged by an LLM-as-a-judge for robust process reasoning, bottleneck-migration analysis, use of process-mining evidence, and the ability to avoid optimizing only the historical average.

Question:
Redesign the process so that it remains acceptable under several plausible demand and disruption scenarios.

Process context:
Cases are lab orders. Each case may include one or more sample tubes, but the log below is flattened by lab order. The normal process is:
Register order -> Receive sample -> Pre-analytical check -> Test preparation -> Assay run -> Technical validation -> Medical validation -> Release result.
Some orders are Routine, Urgent, Repeat-after-failure, or Complex-panel. Complex-panel orders require multiple assays and a medical validator. Urgent orders carry contractual penalties if release exceeds 18 hours. Routine orders have a 72-hour target.

Variant behavior:
- Urgent cases bypass Batch wait in 74% of normal cases and 58% of peak cases. The bypass accelerates Urgent cases but fragments machine schedules.
- Complex-panel cases are 3.1 times more likely to need Medical validation and 2.4 times more likely to enter Hold for specialist review.
- Repeat-after-failure cases consume assay capacity twice and are clustered after night shifts and M2 usage.
- Routine cases tolerate batching but are sometimes delayed by priority rules for Urgent cases.
- Recollection loops reduce available intake capacity because clerks spend time explaining errors to physicians.

Candidate interventions proposed by managers:
I1. Add a strict Urgent-first dispatch rule at preparation and assay.
I2. Increase batch sizes from 12 to 24 for Routine samples.
I3. Dedicate M1 to Urgent and Complex-panel cases; route Routine cases to M2.
I4. Cross-train two technical validators to do accession correction.
I5. Add a temporary evening medical-validation shift during peaks.
I6. Create an early pre-analytical defect triage that rejects poor samples within 2 hours.
I7. Shift courier pickup slots to smooth arrival peaks.
I8. Automate registration for orders with complete digital metadata.
I9. Hold Routine releases until a batch report is ready, reducing release work but adding waiting.
I10. Outsource Complex-panel overflow to a partner lab with 12h transport delay but lower specialist-review queue.

Important conflict hints:
- I1 helps Urgent cases but can starve Routine and Complex-panel cases, increasing total work-in-process.
- I2 improves preparation efficiency but may push Urgent cases into longer waits unless priority exceptions are well controlled.
- I3 relieves M1 congestion for critical cases but can increase repeats if M2 gets all Routine volume during high-load periods.
- I4 relieves accession correction but may create a shortage in technical validation.
- I8 may relieve registration but expose pre-analytical check and assay-machine bottlenecks.
- I10 relieves specialist review but increases transport delay and traceability complexity.

Required output format:
Use Markdown. Produce exactly the following top-level sections.

2. Bottleneck-migration analysis
