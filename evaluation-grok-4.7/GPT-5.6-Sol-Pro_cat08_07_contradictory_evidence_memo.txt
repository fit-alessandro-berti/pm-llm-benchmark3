7.8

The memo is a strong, constraint-compliant decision piece: it chooses a segment-gated hybrid, refuses both national scale-up and blanket rollback, separates most facts from interpretations, and ties measurements to decisions. It is not nearly flawless.

The clearest error is process-mining literacy. The memo says low precision and lower generalization both show that the model permits many combinations of skipped surveys, manual activation, repeat visits, and early billing. Low precision supports that claim. Lower generalization does not; under standard discovery metrics it points toward overfitting, not a more permissive model. Section 3 then treats generalization as transportability to new regions, and the rollout gate later asks precision and generalization to rise while warning against permissiveness. Those uses are mutually uneasy and partly wrong. Alignments are also flattened: move-on-log and move-on-model are different conformance directions, and permit absence was frequent, not merely occasional.

The gates look more precise than their basis. Two days, one percentage point, 3%, 99%, and 85% are not tied to baseline rates, harm, power, or queueing evidence. Using discovered-model precision and generalization as business rollout KPIs confuses a mining diagnostic with an operating target. Survey skipping and ownership get real experiments, but intentional post-billing activation—the complaint shift the memo itself emphasizes—is mostly banned and monitored rather than tested. “All-in” cost is also a poor label for an estimate that still excludes complaint cost after week 4.

The recommendation, segment logic, confounding caveats, and value-of-information ranking remain defensible, including the selection-bias warning on survey-rule validation. Those strengths keep this high; the metric mistake and false precision keep it out of the top band.
