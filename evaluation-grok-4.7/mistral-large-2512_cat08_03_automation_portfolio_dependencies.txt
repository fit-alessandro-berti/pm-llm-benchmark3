4.5

The write-up has the required seven sections and a usable twelve-row matrix, and it correctly treats the 1,750 entity-mismatch cases (31-hour median wait, critical path in 82% of those cases) as more important than registration volume. It is not a coherent portfolio recommendation. The implementation plan and the stated choice contradict each other, one hard constraint is missed, and a central resource effect is reversed.

The decisive failure is that sections 6 and 7 prescribe different programs. Section 7 selects A4, A5, A6, and A7. Section 6 rolls out A1, A5, A4, A9, A6, and A10, six modules against a four-module first-year budget, and drops A7. Phase 4 then audits only A1, A4, A6, and A9. A bank cannot tell which set is authorized. The rollout also places contract generation and e-sign ahead of financial-statement normalization, so a downstream legal artifact is automated while the inputs it depends on are still manual.

The recommended four-pack misses the requirement that one first-wave module strengthen controls rather than only cut labor. A4, A5, A6, and A7 are queue, rework, and routing tools. The answer’s own compliance portfolio shows that it knows A3, A9, and A12 are the control modules, then recommends a set that excludes them. Later claims that cleaner files and faster mismatch handling reduce violations are indirect. They do not implement the KYC-before-signing rule, the committee-to-contract match, or four-eyes segregation, and they never use the measured baselines: provisional signing 6.5% (1.1% remediated), contract remediation 8.5% overall and 14% on credit-line renewal, or four-eyes breaches in 5.8% of high-exposure cases at month-end.

A6’s workload effect is backwards in the matrix, the dependency map, the bottleneck effects, and the recommendation. Normalization is the activity that removes about 75 minutes of analyst work and is the stated protection against analysts being flooded by cleaner inflow. The answer instead lists A6 as a high downstream risk that may overload the nine analysts, then adds temporary staffing to fix a problem the module is meant to prevent. A5 can raise completed-file arrivals; A6 is the offset. That causal pair is the resource shift the evidence highlights, and it is inverted.

Section 4 does not meet the requirement to use variants and directly-follows relations. It substitutes unsupported cycle-time cuts of 5–10%, 20–30%, and 10–15%. It says skipping classification and KYC automation increases misclassification and KYC violations; leaving a rate unchanged is not an increase. It says A1 can increase classification errors, A9 can overload four-person legal ops despite high labor savings and a “low” downstream-bottleneck rating, and collateral triage yields only a moderate wait reduction even though the external wait is 58 hours, the longest synchronization point in the table. KYC and financial-document review are concurrent in practice and sequential only in the log; every cycle-time claim treats the paths as additive. Register-in-position-2 (98%), the mismatch loop (1,750 forward, 1,160 back to KYC, 380 straight to contract preparation), the document loop (3,300 out, 2,800 back), the remediation loop (650 / 510), the collateral split (71% of equipment finance, 18% of working capital), and the weekly high-exposure committee cadence are unused. A7 is also described as applying to equipment-finance cases only.

The dependency map has the right headings and a few right links, notably checklist before extraction, and it correctly says triage cannot remove the external wait. Several mappings are wrong or invented. KYC clearance is legally required before customer signing; the text makes A3/A4 a predecessor of contract generation. Four-eyes is between credit analysis and committee packaging, then parenthetically attached to A6 and A8, so statement extraction is treated as credit analysis. The committee join needs KYC status, normalized statements, collateral when required, and credit notes; the map reduces that to valuation. Core-banking depth is limited to two modules, but the claim that those two are registration and contract generation is not in the evidence. Loan activation is the obvious core-banking write, and it is absent from the integration discussion. Concurrency, the two-senior specialist dependency inside a three-person compliance pool, afternoon back-office slack below 65% utilization, and month-end legal overload are also absent, so labor “savings” on registration are overstated.

Gates are not operational. Entity-mismatch “resolution time” mixes a 55-minute handling time with a 31-hour queue; a limited-coverage assistant cannot be given a 70% reduction target with no coverage assumption. The contract gate is remediation under 10% while the current overall rate is 8.5%, so the gate allows a worse process. The document gate allows rework to rise by 5% for a module aimed at a 52% repeat rate. Other thresholds (90% registration success, 60% faster normalization, 80% e-sign adoption, 90% KYC confidence) have no baseline.

Risk controls mention audit samples, rule versioning, drift fallback, and segregation alerts, which is the right control family. Four-eyes is then reused as a classification check, which is a different control. The e-sign compensators are authentication and video checks; the missing control is a hard stop until KYC is cleared. False positives that would flood the two senior specialists are thin relative to false negatives. Relationship managers who issue incomplete document instructions are not given a control, even though that behavior feeds the loop A5 is supposed to break. The auditable chain of model suggestion, human override, and final decision is stated in the findings and only partly reflected.

The frequency trade-off in the closing is the strongest passage, because it uses the mismatch volume, wait, and critical-path share. It still calls financial-document review “A5/A6,” leaves human accountability for KYC, credit judgment, and the committee implicit, and does not reconcile the political demand for one visible win with a four-slot budget. A defensible pack would have kept the synchronization work and spent one slot on a real control module. This answer argues for that standard and then fails it twice, in two incompatible lists.
