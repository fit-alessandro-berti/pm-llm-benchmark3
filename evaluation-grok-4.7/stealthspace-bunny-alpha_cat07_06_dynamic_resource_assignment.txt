6.3

The answer is complete and often careful, but it is not nearly flawless. Several defects sit in the assignment rule itself, the queue table, and the baseline comparison.

The urgency formula is directionally wrong. With \(U\) dominated by \(1-e^{-h_i/\tau_i}\), more SLA time remaining raises “urgency,” while a nearly exhausted deadline scores near zero on that term. The smaller lateness term cannot repair this. The same section says components lie in \([0,1]\), yet \(p_i\) can push \(U\) above 1, and continuity can be negative. The tie-break margin of “two points” has no meaning on that scale.

Continuity scoring is inverted. The queue defines handover cost as the cost of changing owner, and high-complexity, high-cost cases are supposed to stay put. The stated \(q\) values do the reverse: keeping the owner is worth 1.0 when the transfer is cheap and 0.2 when it is expensive. Complexity never enters the formula despite the prose. The reassignment penalty is also inert within a case, because it is subtracted from every candidate. Workload, overtime, training, and resilience remain verbal, so two auditors could not reproduce a score.

C006 is factually wrong. Its owner is R9_Iris, not R10_Jonas. The reason “current owner” is false, and the claimed 11 retained owners follows from that mistake. The correct retention count is 10 of 16. That wrong 11/16 is repeated in the comparison. Moving C006 to Jonas can still be justified by Iris’s high overtime risk and by legal batching with C012, but an explainable policy cannot cite a false owner.

C001 is under-reasoned. It is urgent, 3 hours remain, complexity is 0.88, handover cost is high, and the prompt’s own evidence says an owner change on a high-complexity case adds about 2.7 hours of waiting, while a weak owner adds about 3.3 hours of service. R3_Celine is Security level 1, so continuity is preferred unless supervision by R7 or R8 is actually infeasible. The answer transfers the case to R8_Hugo without that demonstration or that arithmetic. The global hours caveat does not replace a case-level escalation.

The baseline comparison misstates first-available routing. That baseline ignores specialization except skill 0, so it cannot send Security Review to R10_Jonas. It can still violate the Security, Approval, supervision, and batch-cap rules; the skill-zero example is the wrong violation.

The worker text says ownership may be changed when a repeat handover would cause delay. That condition is a reason to avoid a transfer. Elsewhere, supervision is stacked on R3_Celine and R6_Farid while the batch table counts only primary assignments against the cap of four. The text admits a later hours check, then still presents the cap as satisfied.

The PM4Py sketch names the requested analyses and separates measurement from authorization, which is right. The calls themselves are not reliable: `format_dataframe` is given `timestamp` and `activity` instead of `timestamp_key` and `activity_key`, against `time:timestamp`; `filter_event_attribute_values` is called with a dict rather than an attribute name and value list; `discover_performance_dfg(..., variant="sequence")` is not a trustworthy signature. The version hedge does not make those calls correct.

What keeps this above a weak score is real constraint handling. Skill 0 is never assigned. R5_Eva and R10_Jonas are correctly barred from C015. R2_Bruno is correctly treated as Approval level 2, not a trainee. Urgent and high-priority work stays at skill 2–3. C007 and C016 keep high-cost continuity. High-overtime resources receive no new primary work. The normal/low training share, 2/9, is calculated on the right denominator and uses actual training priorities. The worker note, audit indicators, causal caution, and refusal to invent a false optimum are substantively strong. Those merits do not cancel an inverted objective, a false owner, or a misstated baseline.
