6.5

The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision and covers every required section, but it is not reliable enough for an executive decision because several claims overreach the evidence and the recommended action is not stable across sections.

The central strategic move is right: do not scale Redesign R nationally, and do not roll it back wholesale. Segment results, the tail, rework, and billing-before-activation are treated as binding constraints. The five metric conflicts are all addressed in the correct direction, and the evidence table really does split facts, interpretations, and assumptions.

The decision itself is muddled. The memo pauses survey-skipping and post-billing activation everywhere, then says that path still preserves the simple-house 6-day median gain. Section 6 pauses those rules only for medium- and high-risk segments and reinstates mandatory survey for “other” segments. Those are different decisions. The 6-day, first-time-right, and complaint gains were observed under the full bundle, including merged validation/scheduling, survey skipping, and later activation. Nothing isolates coordinator ownership as the cause. Claiming the gain survives after removing the other levers treats an interpretation as a fact.

Aggregate results are also under-qualified. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline, risk attributes are missing before week 3, start times are sparse, and complaint cost stops at week 4. None of that constrains the recommendation to deploy ownership nationally. Composition can worsen the tail, first-time-right, and complaints even before treatment effects. NPS (+21 to +17), manual activation (7% to 15%), generalization (0.70 to 0.56), and the handover-of-work versus working-together networks are essentially unused.

Process-mining concepts slip. Low precision means the model allows many unobserved traces; it does not itself mean non-compliant behavior or weak governance. That is what the alignments show. Temporal-profile deviations and most directly-follows edges are barely used. “Cases with 3+ handovers” is shortened to “handovers,” 34% becomes 35%, and “skipping is currently false” is stronger than “often wrong for apartments.”

Value of information is incomplete. M6, M4, and M1 are sensibly high, but several items never say which result would change the choice among A–E. M5 is ranked last even though resilience under coordinator absence is a pillar of the recommendation and directly tests whether ownership should scale. M7 is treated as regulatory control rather than information, and no regulatory finding is in the evidence.

The rollout gate is not operationally coherent. An SLA-breach ceiling near 1% cannot sit beside a 13% baseline. “Precision of the conformance model ≥ 0.70” mixes discovery precision with conformance. The 80% load cap, “permit class = green,” and 3,000 cases per stratum are unargued thresholds. Alternative C is called the core recommendation while the conclusion adopts a narrowed E.

Format and direction are solid. Attribution, consistency of the action, confounding, and artifact coverage are not.
