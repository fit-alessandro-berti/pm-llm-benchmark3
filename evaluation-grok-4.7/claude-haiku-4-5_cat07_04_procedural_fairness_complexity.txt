The answer covers every required section, keeps raw approval gaps separate from discrimination, and treats language support and accommodation review as possible burdens rather than as steps to remove. The verdict is more careful than the calculations that support it. Several of those calculations are wrong, one required sensitivity test is reversed, and the PM4Py plan would not estimate the quantities it names.

The language-support caseload is invented. Group B has 1,250 cases and a 57% language-support need, about 713 cases, while V4 is 24%, about 300 cases. The answer uses “~400,” then “57 cases per bilingual worker,” then an unexplained “expected queue of ~8 days” from an assumed one-day service time. A stock of cases and a service time do not produce that wait. The later claim that 3.8 days is shorter than the expected queue, and therefore evidence of prioritization, rests on that invalid model. The same section compares a 23-point V1 share gap with a 25-point incomplete-documentation gap and treats those as the same kind of quantity. They are not, so the conclusion that case mix explains most of the V1 gap is not supported. The 57% need versus 24% V4 share is the sharper coverage question, and it is not examined. Accommodation is handled better: 31% on V5 versus 62% requesting accommodation is noticed, although “ad-hoc accommodation” is only one reading.

Resource figures contradict each other. Group C is given as about 160 cases per specialist (820/5) and later as 102 (accommodation requests only). Neither figure follows from “disproportionately assigned.” Group A’s 3,600/52 = 69 is presented as caseload per worker, and V1 is called a one-caseworker path. The log summary does not show exclusive assignment or one resource per case. Mitigation effects are similarly over-precise: cutting the language wait from 3.8 to 1.5 days cannot take about two days off Group B’s overall adjusted cycle time when only a minority of cases traverse that edge. The same share problem affects the claimed three-day Group C reduction and the two-to-three-day fraud-review reduction. The salary range is not in the evidence.

The sensitivity rule in section 2.5 runs in the wrong direction. For these data, controlling for risk shrinks the raw approval gap. Removing `risk_score_band` should enlarge that gap if the band is what accounts for it. The answer says the disparity shrinking after exclusion would show that the band explains the gap. The complete-documentation restriction check is more coherent. The appeal interpretation in section 1.7 is also inverted: lack of resources to contest would lower appeal volume, so it does not explain Groups B–D having higher appeal rates. Adding hypothetical reversals to the 58% approval rate assumes `case_outcome` is pre-appeal and that a reversal rate can be added in percentage points. The attribute does not establish either point. “Case mix explains 60–70%” flattens residuals that differ sharply by metric and group, especially Group C cycle time.

Section 7 names the right families of techniques, then implements several of them incorrectly. Case identifiers are never stored. Variant approval logic calls string methods on events, reads `case:outcome` rather than `case_outcome`, and stores one trace duration as a median. The performance DFG is treated as an object with `.graph.edges`; PM4Py returns a tuple whose edge map carries statistics directly. `get_service_time` is called with an activity name where the aggregation argument belongs, and it cannot separate service from waiting without start timestamps. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is given activity names it does not take. The logistic model encodes group as one ordered integer, fits `approval_rate > 0.5` on stratum aggregates, and then indexes four group coefficients on a three-column design. That is not an adjusted case-level comparison, and the no-risk rerun repeats the indexing error.

Section 8 restates the findings at length instead of giving the requested concise judgment. The design-versus-decision split is still the right strength of claim: thin bilingual and accommodation capacity, extra handovers, missing timeliness targets, and residual adjusted gaps are fair concerns, while individual discriminatory intent and the neutrality of the risk band remain open. The grade would be much higher if the numeric inferences, sensitivity direction, and executable analysis matched that caution.
