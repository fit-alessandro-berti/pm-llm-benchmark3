6.5

The answer is structurally complete and often sharp, but it is not nearly flawless. Several constraint, dependency, and process-mining errors are material.

The year-one plan breaks a hard limit. Phase 1 already uses A3, A4, A6, and A9, then Phase 2 (months 7–12) adds A10, A7, A5, and A1. That is eight modules inside the first year, against a cap of four. The final recommendation still “layers on” those extra modules. The two deep core-banking integrations are handled correctly; the budget is not.

Dependency reasoning contradicts the portfolio. A2 is called a quality prerequisite for A3, A7, A8, and A9 because an 8% misroute rate corrupts control paths, yet the recommended set is A3, A4, A6, and A9, and A2 is deferred even past Phase 2. The matrix also says A1 depends on “clean intake fields from A2,” which reverses the flow: registration precedes classification. Section 2 then says A2 depends on A1. Those claims cannot all be true.

Process-level effects are overstated. Median wait sits before an activity; it is not time the activity itself consumes. The write-up repeatedly treats automation as if it removes those waits: 31.0h before entity resolution, 22.0h before contract preparation, 44.0h before signing, and 58.0h before external valuation. Signing has 0 minutes of service time, so reminders cannot be assumed to erase a 44-hour customer wait. A7 is elsewhere correctly described as unable to compress the partner SLA, then later credited with a large cycle-time cut. Aggregate wait mass is also misread. Entity mismatch is about 1,750 cases at 31 hours; credit analysis is about 8,900 cases at 28 hours and is omitted from the dominance claim. Customer signing, contract preparation, and financial-document review likewise outweigh entity mismatch in total case-hours.

Explicit evidence is missed or misused. KYC and financial-document review are concurrent in practice and must not be treated as a recorded sequence; the answer never uses that join. The weekly high-exposure committee cadence is ignored, so faster packaging does not automatically shorten cycle time. Back-office utilization below 65% after 15:00 is unused, which weakens the claim that registration is the visible constraint. A6 is said to cut the 24% document-review loop “indirectly,” but normalization is downstream of that loop. Four-eyes violations occur at credit analysis versus committee packaging; Portfolio C does not automate packaging, yet enforcement is assigned to contract generation.

Smaller consistency problems add up. The required table is in A1–A12 order, not ranked. A2 is rated high first-year suitability and then excluded. The opening says every portfolio strengthens controls; Q and B are later described as only weakly doing so. “Low-variance” is not in the data. A8’s garbage-in risk and A4’s limited coverage are acknowledged, then underweighted when Portfolio C is called a large, durable cycle-time win while valuation, signing, and the analyst queue remain.

The strongest parts are real: A1 is not chosen first, A11 is gated, audit trails and false negatives are treated seriously, specialist and analyst load are discussed, and the three portfolios do state sacrifices. Those merits do not offset the budget breach, the broken A2 prerequisite, the wait-time mechanism error, or the ignored concurrency.
