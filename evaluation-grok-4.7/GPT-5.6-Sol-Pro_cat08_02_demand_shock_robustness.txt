7.5

The answer is a strong, usable redesign and it clears every required section. Weekly normalization is done correctly (about 2,300 versus 3,300 starts; Routine flat at about 1,403 versus 1,386 per week; Urgent about +131%, Complex-panel about +121%, Repeat-after-failure about +61%). The pre-analytical exception rates (about 7.0% to 12.3%) and assay-exit repeat shares (about 6.7% to 14.8%) match the DFGs. Tail targets, utilization, queues, and the cited performance edges are accurate. I1, I2, and I3 are modified rather than adopted, I9 is rejected using the 5-hour Medical-validation → Release edge, and downstream overload is discussed for the package. Policies have on and off triggers, and several residual risks are created by the redesign itself.

The deductions are for analytical and control-logic gaps, not missing sections.

Calling 2,300 and 3,300 “throughput” treats starts as output. Over eight weeks, releases plus cancellations are about 16,440 of 18,400 starts; over the peak window they are about 8,580 of 9,900. That open-case gap is never used. Peak assay entries (preparation, batch release, and reruns) are about 10,250 against about 8,500 assay exits, while technical-validation exits (8,080) exceed assay arrivals into technical validation (7,240). Those imbalances change how hard the “technical validation is already accumulating” claim can be pushed, and they are absent.

The main-path migration over-weights accession correction. Peak correction is 420 of 9,730 pre-analytical cases, about 4%. The long 14-hour queue is a severe local constraint on a small pool, but the system delays sit on batch/assay, technical validation, medical validation, and specialist review (median queues 18h, 16h, 22h, and 36h). Utilization and queue rankings are also left unreconciled: technical validation is the only pool above 100%, while the longest waits are specialist review at 87% and medical validation at 93%.

Several high-value DFG facts never enter the argument. Urgent batch bypass falls from 74% to 58%. Batch-wait incidence rises from about 4.4% to about 12.7% of preparation exits, and the batch path median moves from 6h + 9h to 14h + 22h. Specialist-review volume rises from about 36 cases a week to about 203. Recollection cancellations rise from 290/830 to 370/890. Peak urgent median is still 17h, under the 18-hour penalty line, while urgent p90 moves from 19h to 33h. That last contrast is the clearest warning against managing the center of the distribution, and it is only implied.

The control design is incomplete where it claims to be general. Slack is defined from a contractual deadline, but only Urgent (18h) and Routine (72h) have targets. Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure have no stated SLA, yet they are inside the same token and slack rules. Mode interactions conflict: mix-surge mode permits 24-sample Routine batches, assay-degraded mode requires smaller batches, and precedence is defined only after compound-red is already on. The lagging line “overall rework plus assay-repeat rate” uses 9% and 11% bands calibrated only to overall rework of 7.5% and 11.8%. If repeats sit inside overall rework, the sum double-counts; if they do not, those bands are the wrong scale. “Sole required specialist” is stronger than the evidence, which only says one specialist is absent every second Friday. Setting acceptable Urgent performance at p90 ≤ 18h still leaves about 10% of Urgent orders past a per-case penalty.

The capacity dashboard would also alarm late. Red only above 100% of effective capacity would leave the observed peak’s assay pool (98%), pre-analytical pool (96%), and medical pool (93%) in amber, even though that peak already produced 33-hour Urgent p90s and a 54% long-queue rate. Queue-age reds would catch it, but the leading capacity band would not. Flex activation at an 8-hour correction queue versus a dashboard red at 12 hours, and mix-surge activation at 90% forecast load versus a dashboard red at 100%, would give operators two different signals for the same state.

The skeleton is sound: mix-aware scenarios, migration, conditional interventions, explicit non-priority of every case type, and a split leading/lagging dashboard. It is not nearly flawless, because a few control rules are underspecified, one outcome metric is inconsistently defined, and the process-mining evidence that would most tightly pin the policies is only partly used.
