6.5

The case-level fork-join arithmetic is largely right, and the main conclusion — B controls completion more often than A despite A’s longer service — is sound. The write-up is not reliable enough for a high score: several published aggregates are wrong, those errors are reused in the redesign claims, and the final-station analysis ignores the F1/F2 evidence the scenario supplies.

Section 1 is the strongest part. Upstream, branch queue, service, join-ready as the max of branch ends, sync wait as join minus branch end, downstream queue, final service, and flow time are defined correctly, and flow time is the sum of upstream, fork-join elapsed time, downstream queue, and final service. The double-count argument is right: each branch’s queue + service + sync wait equals the same fork-join interval, so summing the three branches yields three times elapsed stage time. Tie-breaking (A, then B, then C) is explicit. No completion-time ties occur in this log, so the rule does not change the counts.

Section 2 matches the log. All 12 rows check out, including critical branch, downstream queue, and flow time, and the worked arithmetic for F02, F04, F07, and F11 is correct. Sync waits and fork-join elapsed time are defined but not shown case by case; the required columns are present.

Section 3’s ranking is directionally justified and the critical counts are right: B 8/12, A 2/12 (F01, F11), C 2/12 (F04, F08). Mean A service 50, mean B service 37.5, mean B queue 19.17, mean A sync 11, and mean B sync 4.33 are correct, as is mean flow time 115.25. These figures are not:

- C queue mean is 125/12 = 10.42, not 10.0, so mean C duration is 30.58, not 30.17.
- Mean C sync wait is 365/12 = 30.42, not 30.50.
- Sample standard deviation of B queue is about 9.49, not 9.26. The other standard deviations match the sample definition, so this is not a consistent population-versus-sample choice.
- “Tail contribution to join-ready” is not measured (no critical excess over the runner-up, no percentile of branch lateness). A and C are tied on critical frequency; that tie is visible in the table but not stated.

Section 4 gets two of the three counterfactuals right and mishandles the third. Cutting A service from 50 to 40 saves 5 minutes on F01 and 10 on F11, and 0 elsewhere: 15 minutes total, mean 1.25. Halving B queue saves 10, 10, 10, 15, 15, 12.5, 5, and 15 on F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, and F12: 92.5 minutes, with four zero-benefit cases. The path-shift note is slightly wrong: after the cut, F02 and F06 tie A at 50 minutes; only F05 and F10 fall strictly below A. Downstream queue sums to 176 minutes, mean 14.67, not 166 and 13.83. The 166 figure is the sum with F12’s 10 minutes omitted and still divided by 12. CF1/CF2 are join-time savings, while the summary header “Total Saved (Join)” also covers CF3. The masking discussion is only qualitative: it does not recompute `final_end` under an F1/F2 occupancy rule, and it treats earlier arrival as increasing downstream queue minute for minute whenever capacity is tight.

Section 5 overclaims relative to both the counterfactuals and the resource trace. A 50% B-queue cut is not a reduction of mean B queue from 19.17 to under 5, yet the 7.71-minute join saving from that cut is attached to the stronger claim. Fully removing B queue leaves C critical on F04 (duration 57) and F08 (duration 55), so A becomes the floor on 10/12 cases, not 91.7%. Final service is 20 minutes on every case, and the resource column shows the downstream gap is mixed: F05, F09, F11, and F08 start exactly when the same resource frees; F03, F07, F06, F12, and F01 still wait while that resource is already idle; F04 and F10 are only partly blocked. A notification rule or extra capacity therefore cannot remove the whole gap, and it cannot “unlock 100%” of upstream savings. The batch-release model, the 09:08 shift start, and the 5-minute pre-alert are stated as operating facts. Throughput is not estimated. The pilot order is clear, but it skips measuring which downstream waits are resource waits before trying to delete all of them. Mean flow falling from 115.25 to 101.42 simply subtracts the wrong 13.83.

Section 6 would calculate the case table, critical branch, sync waits, and the three counterfactuals, including a correct CF3 sum of 176, so the script contradicts the prose. The printed counterfactual summary is aggregate only; per-case savings are computed and not printed. `join_ready` is omitted from the printed decomposition. The interval log has one A/B/C/F row per case, calls `format_dataframe` and `get_service_time`, and the comment correctly says a directly-follows graph cannot separate sync wait from downstream queue. `numpy` is unused. Section 3’s standard deviations are not produced by the script.

Section 7 covers the requested limits: queue cause, unknown branch capacity and outside workload, service time that may hide preemption, downstream wait that may be a release rule, and an opaque upstream interval. It does not state the assumption the counterfactuals need: each case’s branch delay can be scaled independently even though the branches overlap in time, so this is not a shared-resource simulation. B is not a single server; around 10:50–10:55 four B intervals overlap. That sits uneasily with the cross-training recommendation.

The definitions, the case table, the max-based critical-path logic, and CF1/CF2 are good process-mining work. The wrong C aggregates, the wrong downstream total reused in the recommendation, the unsupported F mechanism, and the unused resource schedule keep the answer well short of nearly flawless.
