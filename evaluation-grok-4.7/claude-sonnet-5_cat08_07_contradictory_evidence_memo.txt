6.8

The memo reaches the right decision family (segmented hybrid, not national scale and not full rollback), hits every required section, and explains the five metric conflicts in mostly coherent terms. It is not nearly flawless. Several claims are stronger than the evidence, and a few links between evidence and action are wrong or inconsistent.

The largest analytical gap is case mix. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline, so aggregate moves (p95 45 to 62 days, first-time-right 82% to 76%, complaints 6.1% to 9.8%) are confounded. The memo still says the redesign “drove” those results. The assumption that tries to capture this is misplaced: mix bias threatens the aggregate comparison, not the within-segment deltas, and “skew fully explains segment-level results” would undercut the decision if taken seriously. Segment tags are also missing before pilot week 3, yet simple-house results are later called unambiguous.

Those simple-house results are not “unambiguously positive across cost, speed, and satisfaction.” The segment table supports faster duration, higher first-time-right, and slightly fewer complaints. Cost and NPS are not reported by segment; NPS fell in aggregate (+21 to +17). “No segment where post-billing activation looked net-positive” is likewise unidentified. That practice is bundled with the rest of R, and simple houses improved on the reported quality metrics.

Redesign R has four changes. The memo repeatedly calls it three and drops merged validate-and-schedule. Section 3 credits that merge for the median gain, then section 6 scales only coordinator ownership. That is an internal contradiction, and it leaves the clearest simple-house mechanism out of the action list. “Reinstate permit checks” also treats a conformance deviation as a confirmed design deletion.

Precision is mishandled in the decision itself. Fitness 0.94 means observed pilot behavior is replayed well; precision 0.51 means the model allows too much unobserved behavior. “Does not reliably predict pilot behavior” says nearly the opposite. Section 3 corrects the definition, then overreaches by calling the precision collapse purely real variability and “not a modeling artifact.” Generalization also fell (0.70 to 0.56) and is never interpreted. “Fewer visits, less checking” is inaccurate: failed visits and second visits both rose; only surveys fell.

Value of information is complete but conceptually soft. M7 is ranked first because it monitors a pause already chosen; decision value belongs to information that could change the choice. M5, which could reverse scaling coordinator ownership given 93% load and absence fragility, is ranked sixth. The rollout bar (90% rule agreement, near-zero conformance breaches, full cost at or below baseline) never requires tail duration, first-time-right, or complaints to recover. The 90% figure is arbitrary, and calibration is not the same as an acceptable false-negative rate. Rural and business expansion is gated on survey-rule studies (M1/M6) even though the business note points to manual activation and billing disputes.

Fact, interpretation, and assumption are separated in the table, and alternatives A–E have real trade-offs. The recommendation is operational and appropriately refuses both blanket scale and blanket rollback. The grade is held down by causal overclaim, the missing fourth intervention, the precision error in the decision section, the incorrect visit mechanism, and a threshold that does not test the harms that justified caution.
