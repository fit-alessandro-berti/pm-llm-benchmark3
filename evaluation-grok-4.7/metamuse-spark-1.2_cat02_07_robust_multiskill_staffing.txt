The write-up is complete and often directionally alert, but its capacity arithmetic is not coherent, and the scenario table is presented as simulation evidence the code neither implements nor could have produced. Under a strict reading those are central failures, not presentation nits.

The service-time expectations are right: Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.30, Electrical 32.82, Software 38.50, Validation 15.74, and the U4 doubled-tail means (7.38, 29.60, 37.14, 44.50, 17.48) follow. Block diagnostic loads are also mostly right, and 42 cases, 450 minutes for an eight-hour shift minus a 30-minute break, and Farah’s 240 minutes are right. The interpretation of those loads is not.

Every multiplier is at least 1, so expected person-minutes are at least the sum of base minutes. That sum is about 476 + 797 + 495 + 292 + 661 ≈ 2720. Validation is 42 × 15.74 = 661, not the stated 561. Staff time on shift is 5 × 450 + 240 = 2490, or 2670 with every resource’s full 30-minute overtime. Expected service alone exceeds both, before handover, setup, coordination, or any multiplier above 1. The claimed daily utilizations of 33% / 45% / 50% / 10% / 24%, and the sentence that they “suggest spare capacity on average,” treat each multi-skilled person as fully available to every skill at once. Ada’s 450 minutes are counted in mechanical, electrical, intake, and validation simultaneously. Intake capacity is also mis-added: dedicated intake base-minutes are about 2367, not 2919. The ≤90% individual-utilization constraint is therefore not shown to be feasible, and a single empty-day delay of 65–110 minutes is not evidence the day clears.

The same error dominates the block table. Correct 08:00–10:00 offered work is about 444 base minutes against 480 person-minutes, so the morning is already near saturation, not an intake/validation regime “under 20%.” At 14:00–16:00, offered work is about 650 base minutes against about 570 person-minutes after Deepa’s break. Reading electrical as “203/460 ≈ 44%” is the dedicated-capacity illusion again. Several block capacities are also locally wrong. Mechanical labels for 12:00–14:00 and 14:00–16:00 include Chen and Emil, who have no mechanical skill. The recurring 346 figure is Emil’s full-day electrical capacity, 450/1.30, pasted onto two-hour software and electrical cells. After 16:00 Emil has about 90 minutes once the 16:00–16:30 break is removed, while software load is about 92 and electrical, intake, and validation also sit on Emil and Deepa. The “mechanical at 57%” evening diagnosis misses a single-server overload. The 18:00–20:00 electrical and software caps of 138 and 173 do not match Emil’s 120 minutes (92 electrical base-minutes at 1.30, 120 software).

U2 is internally inconsistent. Doubling the E and ES shares and renormalizing with fixed block arrivals raises electrical load by roughly 15–20%, not the stated +45–50%. The code implements that renormalization; the prose estimates do not match it. Absolute doubling of E/ES counts would raise volume by about two-thirds and would not reduce mechanical load. The write-up asserts both a fixed-arrival renormalization and a large electrical jump.

Section 4 then treats those bounds as if a 100-replication discrete-event study had ranked the policies. The code uses 20 replications, approximates arrivals with a truncated normal because `random.poissonvariate` does not exist, and never builds a queue. Cases are list-scheduled in arrival order, so P1 versus P2 versus P3, earliest-deadline, and shortest-expected-remaining-time do not reorder waiting work. `penalty` stays 0, so the 10% restart rule is not applied. Parallelism is a fragile timestamp adjustment, and the code’s family map charges a setup between electrical and software while assumption A5 puts them in one family. The parallel test uses ordinary means (23 / 28.5 / 32.5), not the 26.3 / 32.8 / 38.5 means named in the text. The printed metrics were not produced by a run. Ranges that overlap are still marked “best” (U5 P1: B 58–72% versus C 65–78%; U1 P1 similarly). Claims that simulation “shows” a 7–10 minute base gap, P3 tails two to three times P1, Jain 0.92, and 0.6 idle FTE are unsupported observations.

The required PM4Py surface is incomplete. `format_dataframe`, `get_service_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network` appear, inside a blanket `try/except`. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is discussed and never called. `discover_bottlenecks` is not a reliable PM4Py entry point. The comment that case-level service/waiting must not be treated as stage queue time is right, and stronger than the code.

The policy prose is the best part of the answer. Specialization, full pooling, and reserved-P1 hybrid are specified with eligibility, scores, overflow thresholds, parallel rules, and preemption limits. The lexicographic robustness objective is explicit, and the text does say when pooling or strict specialization would be preferable. Section 7 names the requested log fields and separates handover, drift, service-time change, and bottleneck migration. The pilot has P1 guardrails. Those sections still over-quote the same unrun model, and the power statement conflicts with itself: about 200 cases per arm versus 1000–1500 total, with about 150 P1 cases for a 10-point SLA gap, which is short of a two-arm comparison near 90% versus 80%.

A score of 5 reflects a full, serious attempt whose auditable load math reverses the slack conclusion, whose policy ranking is not identified with its simulator, and whose uncertainty bands are then read as if they separated the policies.
