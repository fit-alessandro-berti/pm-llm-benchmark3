6.6

The answer covers every required section and is often directionally sound, but it is not nearly flawless. Several bottleneck claims are causally loose, one priority mechanism contradicts the given conflict evidence, and the process-mining use stays mostly at the level of quoted summaries rather than flow rates computed from the DFGs.

What holds up. The six scenarios vary mix and disruption type, not only volume. Urgent-first dispatch and routine release-holding are correctly rejected as defaults. M1/M2 dedication is qualified by M2’s higher failure rate. I8 is tied to a metadata gate and to downstream queues. I10 is compared with transport plus partner time, not with the 12-hour haul alone. The trade-off table has the required columns, triggers have off-ramps, and section 6 includes redesign-caused failures (lane starvation, validator cannibalization, automation pushing defects downstream, outsourcing dependency). Several figures match the log: bypass 74% to 58%, tech validation 101%, specialist queue 36 hours, hold-to-medical edge 48 hours, recollection return 60 hours, medical-to-release edge 5 hours. The implied pre-analytical non-straight-through rate (~7% normal, ~12% peak) and assay-exit repeat share (~15% peak) are consistent with the frequencies.

Main faults.

The S1 migration paragraph treats observed normal-to-peak queues as the effect of relieving one station. Pre-analytical 1.8h to 9.5h, assay 18h, technical validation 16h, and medical validation 22h are whole-peak outcomes under volume, mix, defects, and arrival spikes together. They are not the measured result of automating registration or speeding preparation. The direction matches the I8 hint; the magnitude attribution does not.

S1 is a mix shock, but its bottleneck sequence starts at courier and registration. Registration’s peak median queue is only 2.8 hours at 85% utilization. Urgent and complex work stress assay, medical validation, and specialist review. Specialist review is the longest queue in the table (36 hours) at only 87% utilization, yet the utilization argument that is supposed to prove the migration omits it and also omits accession correction (14 hours, two specialists, 91%). That flattens a mix scenario into a generic front-to-back flood. S4, not S1, is the arrival scenario.

I1 is mis-specified in S3. Urgent-first can push Urgent work into medical validation and, per the stated conflict, starves Routine and Complex-panel upstream. It does not “dump” Complex-panel cases into medical validation. Later lane language corrects the design, but the migration analysis itself is wrong on the case type that specialist review actually constrains.

Repeat-after-failure is placed in the Routine batch lane even though it is the worst cycle-time class (peak median 145 hours, p90 240 hours), consumes assay capacity twice, and clusters on M2 and night shifts. Batching that class with Routine is a redesign hazard the residual-risk section does not own.

The “robust” package still contains contingent tools. I4 is unsafe when technical validation is already over 100%. I10 is explicitly not robust in ordinary flow. I5 depends on scarce named people. Those belong in the trigger table; calling them robust blurs the distinction the output format requires.

Process-mining evidence is thinner than the DFGs allow. Weekly volume is about 2,300 versus 3,300, but medical-validation completions rise by roughly 2.6×, specialist holds by roughly 5–6×, batch-wait entries by about 4×, and recollection cancellations by about 3×. Direct technical release falls from about 74% of technical-validation exits to about 50%. Assay entries exceed assay exits by far more per week in the peak window, which is backlog formation, not just an 18-hour median. Recollection cancellations rise from about 35% to about 42% of recollection outcomes. None of these rates are computed. Arrival-compression bands (25%, 30%, one-third, 35%) and an Urgent p80 have no basis in the log; the log gives p90, and normal Urgent p90 is already 19 hours against an 18-hour penalty. That baseline tail breach is never stated.

The median-versus-tail pattern is the clearest reason averages mislead: Urgent median stays near 17 hours while p90 goes to 33; Routine median stays under 72 hours while p90 goes to 120. The answer cites p90 in places, then centers control on median queues and a made-up p80.

Early triage is recommended on a pre-analytical pool already at 96% without saying that the same station must do the triage, so good samples can wait behind the screen. The dashboard’s red bands sit on the historical peak medians, so a repeat of the observed peak paints almost everything red and stops discriminating which constraint is worsening.
