6.6

The forecast covers every required section, separates East from North and South, and treats tails, fairness, learning, and conditional rollout as real outcomes. The grade stays well below excellent because several quantitative claims are wrong or unsupported, the pilot does not test the architecture that is recommended, and the PM4Py script is presented as runnable while calling APIs and return values that do not exist.

**Mechanism map.** The eight requested mechanisms are all named, and the Legal/Risk saturation risk is real. The map still misstates the proposal’s own handover table. Summing management’s frequencies gives about 343 handovers per 100 cases today and about 359 after the change. Specialist referral rises from 36 to 41, the return hop from 31 to 36, approval from 42 to 49, and legal/risk from 9 to 12. Only intake-to-caseworker and cross-team handovers fall. “Nominal handover reduction” is therefore not what the management estimate shows. The single-server Kingman formula is the wrong congestion model for a multi-specialist pool, and it is undefined once utilization exceeds 1. Legal volume rising from 9 to 12 per 100 cases at 93% utilization implies a load near \(0.93 \times 12/9 \approx 1.24\) if service time stays constant; Risk is similarly above 1. That is an unstable queue, not a Kingman waiting-time curve. Subcontracting is the A-B-A pattern; calling it “reciprocity” mixes it up with mutual handover edges. Resilience is described almost only as a pooling benefit, without the new shared failure mode in which one absent central specialist now stops all three teams.

**Scenarios.** Optimistic, central, and adverse cases include the required dimensions, and the central case correctly lets East improve while North and South slow. The optimistic case quietly adds cross-skilling of technical staff onto legal triage, which is not part of the proposal, so that scenario is not a forecast of the stated plan. The summary table and the prose disagree: central ping-pong is 8–12% in the table and 12–15% of high-complexity cases in the text; adverse technical wait is “>12h” in the table and a median above 14h in the text. Several percentages, including the claim that simple cases are 60–70% of volume, are stated as facts although the log summary does not contain them.

**Indicators.** Leading versus lagging separation is useful, and queue ratio, bounce-back, reassignment, and percentile cycle time are the right early and late signals. `outcome` is then treated as a CSAT score; the log does not define it that way, and customer satisfaction is not an event-log field. “Role partitioning modularity” is not a result of `discover_organizational_roles`. Many cutoffs (inflow ratio 1.15, reassignment 1.35, utilization 88%, overlap 0.30/0.75) are presented as operational triggers without a baseline distribution.

**Hidden risks and recommendation.** Section 4 does cover tail latency, high-complexity cases, local knowledge, fairness, learning, accountability, and exception paths, including a genuine Simpson-style aggregation problem. The recommendation correctly refuses an unmonitored full rollout, protects East’s capacity problem, and says early mean cycle time, high utilization, short-run rework, and fewer named handovers can all mislead. It then tells management to judge a two-tier hybrid, with part-time local champions, retained local supervisors, a legal scope cap, 0.5 extra FTE, and a “>$100k” approval cutoff, “strictly through” the Section 5 pilot. That pilot instead pools all of South and East tech, centralizes their approvals, and widens legal/risk routing. Those are different interventions. The $100k rule has no basis in the columns or the scenario. The pilot also leaves North, the strongest local-expertise team, untreated, and it routes more East/South work into the existing global Legal/Risk queue, so North’s waits and rework change too. The control is contaminated, and that is not discussed.

**PM4Py sketch.** The pre/post case split, stratified percentiles, and the intended network list are the right analysis shape. The code as written would not run against current PM4Py. `discover_handover_of_work_network`, the working-together and subcontracting calls, role discovery, and `insert_case_service_waiting_time` are passed `resource_key` or `resource_column` arguments those wrappers do not take. Their results are read as `.matrix` and `role.resources`; handover results expose connections or a graph, and roles expose `activities` and `originator_importance`. `discover_performance_dfg` returns one float per edge, so `stat['mean']` and `stat['median']` are invalid; median requires a separate aggregation. On a dataframe, `get_variants` returns counts, so `len` on the count fails. `get_rework_cases_per_activity` returns sets of cases in the standard implementation, which the arithmetic then treats as integers. The SLA cutoff of 240 hours (10 days) contradicts the given data: North’s 90th percentile is 16.4 days while its SLA miss rate is only 6.5%, so the threshold is beyond 16.4 days, and East’s figures place it near the low-20-day range. A declared transition list is never used, and it mixes resource labels (“Local Caseworker”, “Shared Approver”) with activities. Segment filters aggregate case metrics; they do not rediscover handover, subcontracting, or performance DFGs inside team, complexity, specialty, and customer-segment slices. `get_service_time` is cited in the indicator table and then never called.
