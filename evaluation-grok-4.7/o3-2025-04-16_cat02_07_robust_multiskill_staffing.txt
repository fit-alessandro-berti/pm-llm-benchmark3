The write-up has the required nine-part shape and a real specialization-versus-pooling argument, but its load math, scenario claims, comparison table, and code do not support one another. Under a strict reading it is not a reliable policy analysis.

The only fully shown service-time calculation is wrong. Mechanical tail probability is 0.15, so the mixture mean is \(0.15\times45+0.85\times23=26.3\), not 25.2 (that figure is what you get with \(p=0.10\)). The claimed “weighted harmonic mean ≈ 27” is neither the harmonic nor the arithmetic mean of the resource times that follow from 25.2. Intake is then valued at the ordinary midpoint 6.5, dropping the 0.08 tail the assumptions just defined, and validation is taken as 12 rather than the ordinary midpoint 14 or the mixture mean ≈15.7.

Skill load omits combined cases. Mechanical work is M+ME, electrical is E+ME+ES, and software is S+ES. Using only the pure-type shares understates 08:00–10:00 diagnostic demand by roughly 60 percent (about 444 base minutes versus the stated 274). Handover, setup, breaks, and speed multipliers are not applied, so the “2.3 FTE” conclusion is not an offered-load result. The same section says the worksheet is in the code; it is not.

Shift mapping is also off. Emil starts at 12:00, so “Chen+Emil” cannot be the 10:00–12:00 electrical bottleneck, and Farah is already off by 14:00–16:00. Deepa’s electrical multiplier is 1.00, so she is core electrical capacity, not an overflow. U1 does not specifically push intake and mechanical past 140 percent: extra arrivals raise total load, while mechanical-capable base capacity in 08:00–10:00 still covers the mechanical share. U4’s doubled diagnostic tails raise mean service about 12–16 percent, not 15–20 percent. “P1 is unattainable” in U5 conflicts with the later claim that hybrid still reaches 82 percent.

The objectives are not operational. O-tail adds SLA gaps to raw p95 minutes, and the printed weights are unreadable. O-fair likewise mixes utilization with handover minutes and never aggregates across U1–U5, so it is not a robustness objective. The hybrid overflow rule fires when queue workload exceeds 25 minutes, which is below one mean software job, so “controlled” overflow is effectively always-on. Policy P is called EDD but the formula is a critical ratio, and “sampled” remaining time gives the dispatcher the random draw. Policy S leaves morning software without Emil and does not treat Farah as software-capable.

Section 4 does not meet the required table. As printed, `mean_delay` and `p90_or_p95_delay` are empty. The SLA and fairness entries are two-digit precision that the simulator cannot produce. Hybrid U1’s failure mode is “post-outage backlog,” but only U5 has an outage. Section 5 says hybrid never exceeds 105 percent utilization, including in U5; the table marks U5 as VH, defined as above 105 percent. The 12 percent versus 20 percent handover comparison is not calculated anywhere.

The Python is not a three-policy study. `policy` is unused; U2’s `E_mult` is unused; U5’s `combine` is unused, so U5 is only an intake outage, not U1+U3. Shifts and breaks are coded and then never applied. ME/ES lose the second branch. Priority is sampled and ignored. Handover, setup, coordination, preemption, reservation, and overtime are absent, and the closing note does not identify all of those omissions. One unseeded NumPy stream is shared across runs, with no repeated seeds. `get_service_time` is indexed as `svc['mean']`, waiting time is quantile-aggregated on an unspecified and likely event-level column, and the P1 line matches case IDs that never contain “P1”. That does not satisfy the required case-versus-stage PM4Py distinction.

Section 6 and the pilot are the most usable parts: P3 aging, skill-hour erosion, shadow mode, and a staged release are concrete. They cannot rescue an unsupported ranking. The recommendation for hybrid reservation may be directionally sensible, but on this evidence it is not earned.
