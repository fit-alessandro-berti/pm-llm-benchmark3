6.2

The answer is structurally complete and arithmetically careful, but it is not nearly flawless. Several identification, dynamic, and reproducibility failures are large enough that the cautious headline does not rescue the analysis.

The central identification problem is understated. For every treated branch-month, automation availability is perfectly bundled with the routing campaign: Alpha and Beta get both in April, Gamma gets both in July, and Delta and Epsilon get neither. No contrast separates automation from that demand shock. Calling a staggered event study “partially” identifying an availability effect, then saying month fixed effects plus adjustment for arrivals, FTE, and complexity address the confounders, overstates what these data can support. Arrivals are the pathway of the routing bundle, and Alpha’s May FTE loss and Beta’s April FTE gain can be responses to rollout. Conditioning on them changes the estimand and can be a bad control. That issue is never named. “3 treated cohorts” is also wrong: Alpha and Beta are one timing cohort, Gamma a second. The same error is repeated in the code warning.

The executed comparisons do not match the stated design. The primary strategy says not-yet-treated Gamma is a control for Alpha and Beta. The DiD actually uses only never-treated Delta and Epsilon, and only April–June, when Alpha/Beta median cycle time rose by about 2 hours and the “benefit” is that controls rose by about 6 hours. July–August, the highest fast-path months and the only months when early-adopter medians actually fall, is omitted without justification. Section 7 then mislabels that contrast as “vs not-yet-treated.”

Dynamics are averaged away. Alpha and Beta rework drops only in the adoption month (13.2 to 11.2; 12.8 to 10.4) and then sits at or above baseline (about 14.5 and 12.7). The April–June rework DiD of about −2 pp is therefore a one-month dip plus controls hit by the May case-mix shock. The within-adopter intensity slopes have the opposite sign, about +0.8 to +1.2 rework points per 10 pp fast-path use. Those two results are never reconciled, and section 7 still calls rework a suggestive reduction at confidence 40. The p90 intensity “average” is likewise a poor summary: Alpha and Beta tails worsen as use rises, while Gamma’s two-point slope dominates the average. Workload is not computed. Alpha’s arrivals per FTE rise by roughly 30 percent, which is exactly the queueing alternative for a flat median and a worse p90. Substitution versus net capacity is discussed only qualitatively.

The rollout choice, `expand with an embedded evaluation`, is directionally reasonable, but the design contradicts itself. A 1 September Delta start cannot produce the required six months of pre-period case-level logs. A two-month holdout of Epsilon is too short for the learning path the answer itself describes, and a wild-cluster rule on two new branches will not be decisive. There is no within-branch or case-level randomization, which is the design that would actually separate automation from branch shocks and from the shared fraud desk. Guardrails are arbitrary point thresholds with no sampling uncertainty.

Section 9 does not reproduce the argument. As written, `blocks = ''`, so the sparkline throws `IndexError` on the first outcome and the contrasts, event-time indicators, and models never run. The slope printer labels the three branch slopes with outcome names. Relative-time dummies are built and then unused. The fitted models are static two-way fixed effects, the specification the prose correctly warns against, not the event study claimed as primary.

What keeps this above a failing analysis is real: availability is separated from use, median is not collapsed into p90, CSAT nonresponse is treated as fatal, the single “reduces cycle time by X%” claim is rejected, the main pre/post and DiD magnitudes check out, and the threat list covers the required biases. Those are necessary strengths, not enough to offset the bundled-treatment overclaim, the hidden rework reversal, and code that does not execute the stated design.
