4.9

The answer has the right skeleton—policy bundle rather than a magical flag, interventional effects instead of natural effects, interference, and a pilot—but it is not reliable on the event log, and several estimands contradict their own labels.

**Estimands.** The required well-defined column is empty on every row. E2 labels a total effect of P1 on triage time as a “direct effect.” E3’s outcome is `Triage_done – Close` (a reversed interval) and mixes a principal-stratum population with an interventional indirect effect. E4 claims an effect via review waiting but the outcome is `Manual_start – Manual_done`, which is service time, not queue time. E5 is described as both “mediated by escalation” and a controlled direct effect that fixes review while letting escalation vary; those are different estimands, and “isolating the escalation gate” while letting escalation vary is internally inconsistent. Interventional indirect effects are named but the mediator distribution is never specified. P4 is never pinned to a concrete escalation rule.

**Timestamps.** Headline flag contrasts for triage wait (~14 vs ~149 min), triage service (~36 vs ~57), and conditional manual wait (~390 vs ~113) are roughly right, as are review rates (10/10 vs 4/10) and escalation rates (6/10 vs 2/10). Much of the rest of the table is wrong. Low complexity is n=4, not 6. Mean cycle time is about 991 min (flag 1) and 490 min (flag 0), not 985 and 530; high-complexity cycle time is about 1187, not 910; flagged cases without escalation average about 549, not 820. Residuals are about 67 and 71 min, not 80 and 55. Several escalation and manual waits in the load and escalation splits are off by 30–160 min. Section 3’s commentary is under the 100-word minimum and treats escalation as a “7 h longer chain,” while conditional escalation waits are similar (~420 vs ~390). The extra time is mostly occurrence (60% vs 20%), which is exactly the occurrence-versus-waiting distinction the task requires. Section 9 then contradicts section 3 by calling the manual-wait gap “six-hour longer” (the flagged level is 6.5 h; the contrast is about 4.6 h) and calls unflagged cases “similar.”

**Identification facts missed in the sample.** Risk scores do not overlap: flagged scores run from 0.64 to 0.93 and unflagged from 0.28 to 0.61. Flag and score are separated in these 20 cases, so conditioning on `risk_score` has no positivity, and a threshold design has no local mass. The answer still proposes g-formula adjustment for `risk_score` and an RD as if overlap existed, and only lists positivity as a future reversal. M2 is not faster: M2 service times are about 180–210 min on high-complexity cases, while M3 is about 120 min. Reject rates are 2/10 in both flag groups; all four rejects are high-complexity escalations. “After the flag was introduced” is not observed—there is no pre-period.

**Graph and falsification.** `underlying_risk` is unobserved but not marked `U_`. `cycle_time → decision_outcome` reverses the substantive direction. There is no arrow from `risk_score` or complexity into review or escalation decisions, so P1 (hide the flag, keep the score) is not even representable. The exposure-induced-confounding sentence describes between-case queue interference. In the falsification table, a queue adjustment that explains the delay would support the queue story, not change it, and a null effect of hiding the flag would implicate confounding by risk, not “behavioural not informational” effects. Hour-level partial interference is already implausible: review and escalation waits run for many hours and overnight.

**Pilot and code.** P3’s evidence is called “strong” with no capacity model. Individually randomizing flagged cases into a protected pool contaminates the control half through the shared queue the answer itself emphasizes, and weekday day-shift eligibility misses the overnight waits in the log. Reversal 2 is inverted (extra flagged capacity should reduce unflagged delay if the queue is shared), and reversal 3 attacks dropping review even though the preferred policy keeps review. The code avoids inventing absent stages and calls `format_dataframe` with a start-timestamp key, but it fabricates a `SeniorTeam` resource, prints only `head()` rather than stage summaries and support counts, and warns only on flag groups of size 10, so the actually small strata never trigger the warning.
