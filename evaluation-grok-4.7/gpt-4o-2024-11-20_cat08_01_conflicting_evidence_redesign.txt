The writeup follows the required section order and cites several real statistics, but it is not a defensible synthesis. Multiple figures are wrong, two redesigns fight the mining evidence, and the experiment would not test the claim it is meant to test.

Diagnosis. The modal path is identified, and the 14.1% fast-path reopen rate, the 31%-then-9% cost erosion, R1/R2, the premium high-value 22% vs 5% split, the 96 h / p90 180 h dispatch-to-complaint tail, 12.5% high-value repair rework, and the 26% repeat-evidence rate are used correctly. That is not enough. “Median ~5 hours” for the fast path contradicts the segment table, where the fastest reported median is 8 hours (premium low-risk) and standard low-risk is already 13. “Intake-to-approval 2–3 hours” is also wrong: those two edges are median 0.5 h and 0.4 h. “Median 5–26 hours… up to 100 hours” glues three different edges together and assigns the complaint-to-review p90 to the evidence loop. The fast path is treated as generally bad for premium customers. For premium low-risk it is 8 h with a 6% vs 3% complaint gap, and R2 explicitly exempts that segment from evidence-before-dispatch. The real contradiction is segment-specific: a small quality penalty on accessories versus an 18–22% vs 5–6% penalty, R1 exposure, and EUR 410 wrong-part cost on high-value devices. Fitness 0.91, 68% fitting traces, low precision, and R3 are unused, as are the DFG volumes that show how large the auto-approve-and-replace path is.

Redesign A. Segment-aware screening, a high-value pre-dispatch gate, and an evidence portal are the right levers, and the stated sacrifices match the prompt. The design is still a bundle of the three options already listed in the resource notes, not a specified flow. “No more R1/R2 violations” is not supported. “Will not overwhelm manual review” conflicts with the same section’s +15–35% inflow and with the 82% utilization cliff. The gate’s 6–18 h hit is not checked against the 24 h premium service-credit threshold, and idle inspectors are not used to staff it.

Redesign B. Differentiating premium and standard is reasonable. The mechanism is not. Premium low-risk is pulled off an 8 h path that policy already privileges and put through fraud/risk review, whose edges are median 8–16 h and whose team is two specialists already bottlenecked on Mondays and after campaigns. That is likely to breach the 24 h credit line for the segment it claims to protect. High-value premium is then described as going straight to repair quote, while the sacrifice, the failure mode, and the “fast-track mis-approval” story still talk about those cases bypassing review into fast approval. The table says quality gets worse for standard customers, although sending them through review/repair is exactly what the outcome data associates with lower reopen rates (8% to 4%, 18% to 6%). Evidence handling for the 22% / 140 h segment is explicitly left unchanged and not named as a sacrifice.

Redesign C. Pooling seven EUR 34 eligibility reviewers with two EUR 52 fraud specialists ignores the specialization that makes fraud the scarce role. Pre-inspection by the three technicians does not match the stated repair-quote delay (waiting on part price) or the dispatch complaint mechanism (wrong replacement). Quality inspectors are underused and are the natural gate resource. Fewer escalation referrals, with reopens sent to “faster resolutions,” risks violating R3, which never appears in the answer. This is the generic capacity-pooling option the task warned against.

Table. All required columns are present and exact euro ROI is avoided. “Current dominated? Yes” is not tenable: their own cells show each redesign giving something up, low-risk fast paths are not dominated on the outcome data, and the prompt forbids treating dominance as knowable here. A’s “compliance low” and C’s “compliance low” are stronger than the designs. B’s quality and resource cells do not follow from B’s flow. Best-fit labels are broad enough to be non-discriminating.

Failure modes and pilot. Each redesign has two failures and a migrated bottleneck, and A’s portal-abandonment and manual-queue risks are real. B’s headline failure describes a flow B does not actually specify. The pilot sequence is the clearest break. Pilot A is run on standard low-risk accessories, where the fast path is already tolerable out to 72 h, while the go/no-go metric is a 10%+ complaint cut on high-value claims, which that pilot cannot observe. B and C have no thresholds. Complaint edges have medians of 50–70 h and p90s of 150–180 h, but there is no observation window, no segment split, and no before-versus-after-rework cost check. “Builds on earlier pilots” is false: A pushes work into manual review, B bypasses it, and C dissolves the specialist pools. Downstream-versus-shifted learning is one clause, which misses the central lesson of the case.
