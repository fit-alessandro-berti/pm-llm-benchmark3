7.3

The headline split is right: April is a schema and feature-pipeline failure, June is a routing-mix change with stable within-variant breach rates, August–September combine supplier performance, concept shift, and Partner Z, and October’s 0.16 breach rate is not comparable to mature months. The six required sections, dual-hypothesis tests, training quarantine, and four failure modes are all present. The score is held down by mechanism errors, overconfident claims, a population comparison that ignores label maturity, and a monitoring sketch that would not run or would suppress the wrong alarms.

The timeline math that is checked is accurate, including the June mixture \((0.30 \times 0.16) + (0.52 \times 0.09) + (0.18 \times 0.35) \approx 0.16\), the variant shares, parts-edge hours, and metric table. Within-path stability is a sound reason not to call June concept drift. Quarantining unrepaired April and immature October is the correct training decision.

Several claims are stated as fact without evidence. April’s predicted risk of 0.28 is attributed to missing-value imputation; the logs support bad or missing prefixes, duplicates, and `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY`, not a specific imputer. May’s residual AUROC of 0.78 is blamed on the cached “events before diagnosis” feature and on early-May cases; the audit only says that feature was not fully backfilled for April. August’s V3 share rising from 22% to 35% is explained by “delayed cases accumulated parts dependencies.” On a case-start cohort, supplier delay does not reclassify a case into V3. June is called pure covariate drift with zero concept drift and “historical base-rate priors,” which is stronger than stable \(P(Y \mid \text{path})\). A post-diagnosis routing policy can change \(P(Y \mid X)\) at the diagnosis timestamp even when path-conditional breach rates do not move. Parts transit was already creeping from 28 h in January to 42 h in June; July is not the first movement.

October is both “high-confidence ongoing concept drift” and a censoring artifact whose variant rates are “insufficiently mature.” Those cannot both be high-confidence findings, and the concept-drift row never lists that insufficiency. “True underlying breach rate is likely \(\ge 0.32\)” is an unsupported number. The censoring story also equates a 3.8–4.6 h diagnosis-to-remote-fix edge with immediate non-breach labels. Baseline median label delay is already 32–35 days when V2 is common, so V2 cases are not labeled in four hours. Differential delay by long paths is supported; instant labeling is not.

Partner Z is mishandled relative to the maturity criterion. The 0.45 versus 0.27 September gap is used as evidence of population shift, as a physical fact in the failure-mode section, and as the target of reweighting, without the given fact that Partner Z labels mature more slowly. Test 6 does not require equal-maturity cohorts, so a remaining gap would not separate demographics from censoring. Every drift row is “High” confidence, and section 2 says three phenomena and then lists four.

Retraining guidance conflicts with itself. July is marked safe even though matured V3 breach has already moved from about 0.33 to 0.46. August is “safe if supplier features are added” at 93% maturity, while the same section requires \(\ge 95\%\). The remaining immature slice is likely the long parts cases. A June–July Platt/isotonic fit encodes a 52% V2 mix that is already gone by August (V2 40%, V3 35%) and September (V2 36%, V3 42%). The +25% parts markup is an arbitrary constant. The two-stage model is a reasonable response to variant-specific risk, but the V2 branch is not deterministic, and nothing shows V2 breaches are driven purely by queue depth.

The monitor list omits an intervention or deployment monitor (schema migration, routing policy, model version, partner go-live). Putting outcome-API backlog in Tier 0, then suppressing Tiers 1–3 on any Tier 0 alarm, hides the label-maturity path during the October failure and can block scoring when features are fine and only labels are late. A label-delay alarm is prevented from causing retraining only by collapsing it into a data-engineering suppress rule.

The code sketch calls the requested libraries and separates case-start from event-calendar cohorts, but `pm4py.format_dataframe` renames `case_id` / `activity` / `timestamp` to the standard `case:concept:name`, `concept:name`, and `time:timestamp` columns. The later `groupby("case_id")` and `timestamp` logic would raise. `get_variants` values are treated as lists (`len(v)`); current PM4Py often returns counts. `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` is computed once on the pooled log and only its first five column names are stored, so there is no monthly temporal summary. Unknown-activity share is a single global count after rows are dropped, so the function cannot report April’s 17% monthly spike. Duplicate removal keeps only exact same-timestamp consecutive copies.

The failure modes themselves are the right four, and the April recalibration trap and the immature-label retrain are well specified. The Partner Z mode still treats 0.45 versus 0.27 as the structural breach gap and never gates that comparison on matched label maturity.
