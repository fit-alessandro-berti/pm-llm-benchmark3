The write-up follows the required nine-section outline and transcribes the input tables, but its quantitative core is not auditable and its simulation does not implement the process it claims to study.

Section 2’s only worked load example is arithmetically incoherent and unit-inconsistent. For 08:00–10:00, \(7 \times 0.30 \times 23\) minutes is 48.3 minutes (0.81 hours), not 4.81 hours; the electrical, software, and validation lines do not share one conversion either (validation’s 0.98 hours is roughly \(98/100\)). Multi-skill incidence is omitted: mechanical work is \(M+ME\), not \(M\) alone, and electrical work is \(E+ME+ES\). Complex-tail expectations, speed multipliers, the 7-minute handover, 5-minute team and setup penalties, and the 8-minute parallel coordination delay are absent from the load. Ada’s “7.5-hour mechanical capacity” assigns a full shift, including a break outside the block, to one 2-hour block and to one skill. Those figures imply surplus capacity, yet the text still calls mechanical and validation the bottlenecks, so the conclusion contradicts the arithmetic.

The shift structure that actually drives bottlenecks is missed. Farah stops at 12:00; Deepa is the fast electrical resource and the afternoon mechanical resource; after 18:00 only Emil is on duty and Emil cannot do mechanical work, so M/ME arrivals in the last block depend on overtime or carry-over. U3’s main failure mode is therefore not “mechanical overload”: Chen has no mechanical skill, and his absence removes morning software cover that Farah only partially and slowly replaces. U1’s +8 priority-point shift and U2’s doubled E/ES shares are never renormalized.

Section 4 reports exact delays, SLAs, and utilizations with no ranges, seeds, or derivation. Hybrid strictly dominates every column in every scenario, which erases the specialization–pooling trade-off the task is about, conflicts with the “balance” claim in §5, and cannot follow from §2. Delay, utilization (including 105%), fairness, and handover minutes are undefined. Hybrid’s failure mode is “P1 preemption conflicts” in every row even though all three policies preempt under the same vague rule. The fallback trigger—abandon the P1 reserve when that reserve exceeds 90% for two hours—can cut P1 protection in the stress case it is meant to handle. Recommending strict specialization for 18:00–20:00 ignores that the only on-shift resource cannot perform mechanical work.

Policies are not operational. “Strict specialization” still has Ada doing intake, mechanical work, and validation; fixed queues, eligible sets, and overflow thresholds are not specified. The preemption rule (“penalty less than time saved”) leaves “time saved” undefined and ignores the victim case. Parallel versus sequential is not a state-dependent rule about two free qualified resources versus one cross-skilled resource. The fairness objective—maximize minimum utilization subject to a 90% cap—pushes load upward rather than equalizing burden or protecting robustness across U1–U5. Three different fairness definitions appear in the objective, the table, and the code.

The Python does not run and is not an identified scaffold. `demand_df.loc['08:00-10:00']` is a KeyError; scenarios mutate global frames in place; scaled arrival rates are not integer and priority vectors sum above 1; ES cases are given a mechanical activity; reservations are floats compared with resource names, and the software key is missing. Shifts, multipliers, queues, contention, handover, setup, coordination, parallel branches, overtime, and the 10% restart penalty are never applied. Intake is always the first qualified name. Completion timestamps can precede start timestamps. SLA filters search for `"P1"` inside `case_1`, compare with `Timestamp('now')`, and `min_utilization` is undefined. Required calls are missing: `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `pm4py.insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `pm4py.get_service_time`, and `pm4py.discover_handover_of_work_network`. Substituted handover and `dataframe_utils.insert_case_*` APIs are wrong, and case-level service/waiting time is not separated from activity duration. Printed “resilience” is a hard-coded label that does not match §4.

§6–§7 name the requested risks and log fields, but the PM4Py drift and utilization functions are not real, and case-level waiting time is treated as if it were stage-specific. §9 does not state that rankings cannot be identified from means and ranges without a calendar-aware queueing model, and the pilot does not say how P1 cases are protected during a limited rollout.
