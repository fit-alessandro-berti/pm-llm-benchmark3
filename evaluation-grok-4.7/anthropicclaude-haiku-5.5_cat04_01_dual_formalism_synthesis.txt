7.5

The models themselves are largely right, and the witness verdicts match them. The write-up still contains several concrete logical and example errors, so it is not close to flawless.

The POWL partial order is the right canonical model. Registration precedes both streams; containment precedes planning; root-cause analysis precedes design, the risk XOR, and the optional supplier XOR; the trial loop joins planning and design; release joins the loop, the chosen route, and the supplier operator; archive follows release. Unordered pairs that should be concurrent are left unordered, including approval versus trial and supplier versus design, planning, approval, and trial. `LOOP(ET→IT, AP)` yields `(ET IT)(AP ET IT)*`, so adjustment cannot follow the exiting inspection. The process tree is a real conservative under-approximation: phase two forces the route and supplier consultation to wait for both planning and design. That preserves every intended precedence and still allows approval and consultation to overlap the trial. W3 and W4 correctly expose exactly that loss, and F1–F3 are actually rejected by both models.

The reasoning around those models is not equally exact.

The concurrency section contradicts itself. It says the trial loop and containment are “not ordered,” then correctly notes the path containment → planning → trial. In a partial order that path is an order. That pair is not concurrent, and it does not belong in an account of why unconnected nodes may overlap. Direct-edge absence was treated as concurrency, which is the central POWL distinction.

D10 is classified twice. The tree section first lists “CS after root cause” as exact, then lists the same dependency as approximated because consultation cannot start until planning and design finish. D18 and D19 are likewise split between the exact list and the approximation list without a clean partition. D11 (consultation before release) is exact; early start of consultation is not.

F1 is mislabeled. Design before root-cause analysis breaks an intra-stream precedence, not a cross-branch join. F2 is the actual cross-branch witness. The coverage claim still holds only because of F2.

The pass/fail discussion fights itself. Exit after an inspection that is not followed by adjustment is defined as the pass, which is the right control-flow abstraction. The same sections then say the model cannot stop an exit after failure and that a data engine must guarantee it. Once failure is identified with the redo branch, there is no extra exiting behavior left to forbid.

The validation examples do not test what they name. The duplicate-label mutant trace `RD, CM, RC, PV, CA, ET, IT, IT, RM, AC` omits every review. Adding a second `Inspect Trial` does not remove the risk route, so that trace is still refused, and the example does not show label aliasing. The “adjustment after the final inspection” trace also omits the approval route, so absence of the trace would not isolate a broken loop. The soundness, subset, and positive-trace plan is otherwise sound, and the admission that it was not executed is appropriate.

Lesser slips: W2’s reason says the two reviews “run concurrently” when the trace only orders them after the trial; D8 and D15 call loop structure a choice; the concurrency write-up never actually covers every unordered pair. None of those overturn the models. They do show the answer is less precise than its structure suggests.
