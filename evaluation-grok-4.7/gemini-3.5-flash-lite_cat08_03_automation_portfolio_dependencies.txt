5.5

The write-up follows the required seven-part structure, uses the activity, DFG, resource, and control figures, and lands on a four-module set that respects the year-one cap and includes a control-oriented module. Those merits do not offset several causal errors and an unsupported final claim.

The candidate table is not ranked; rows stay in A1–A12 order. Several ratings invert the mechanism they cite. A6 is the analyst-time release the case says is required before cleaner inflow is safe, yet its downstream-bottleneck rating is High because it “can overload” analysts. “Prerequisite for upstream credit and committee tasks” points the dependency the wrong way: normalization sits before credit analysis and committee packaging. A5 is rated High suitability in the same row that says it will flood those analysts. A10 is treated as dependent on automated contract generation; a contract must exist, but A9 is not a prerequisite. A7’s process effect is discounted as “tertiary” despite the 58-hour external wait the case flags as a synchronization constraint.

The dependency section invents a scope item: A9 is described as “automated accounting ledger updates.” It is contract generation with control-rule checks. A3 is tied to the 31-hour / 38% entity-mismatch statistics, which belong to the mismatch activity and to A4. The claim that a hit pre-classifier will “paralyze” the three specialists ignores the distinction between fewer routine hits and more false-positive escalations, and it ignores that the handover concentration is two senior specialists inside that pool. A6 is also over-stated as heavily dependent on A5; completeness helps extraction, but normalization already occurs on 5,200 cases.

Portfolio 2 is labeled a synchronization portfolio and still omits the 58-hour valuation wait, the weekly high-exposure committee cadence, and recorded-versus-actual concurrency of KYC and financial-document review. Portfolio 3 claims it will eliminate four-eyes breaches and provisional signing. None of A3, A6, A9, or A12 is a signing gate or a segregation rule between credit analysis and committee packaging. Section 4 then says Portfolio 1 overloads Legal Ops while that portfolio includes A9, whose direct effect is to remove legal-ops preparation work. Portfolio 2 is called control-neutral even though A10 can accelerate the existing 6.5% provisional-signing path. “Severing” the 3,300 and 1,750 DFG edges overstates what a checklist and an assistant can do; the return edges (2,800 and 1,160) and the 52% repeat rate remain. Entity-mismatch is also mislabeled high-frequency.

Section 5’s controls are generic thresholds (90% confidence, 3% drift) with no basis in the case. The four-eyes control is implemented by blocking A8, a module that is not in any chosen wave.

The rollout and the recommendation diverge. Section 3’s bottleneck portfolio is A4, A5, A6, A10. Section 6 deploys A5, A12, A4, A6. Section 7 calls that an “augmentation” of Portfolio 2 with A12. Adding A12 without dropping a module would make five modules and break the budget; A10 is dropped without a trade-off against the 44-hour signing wait. Section 4 never evaluates the set that is actually recommended. Both first-half deep core-banking slots are assigned to a portal nudge and a remediation workbench, while entity resolution, which needs customer-master data, is left for later and described as capacity “freed up,” as if completed integrations release the quota. Gates of a 30% rework cut, zero four-eyes violations, a 52%-to-under-20% loop cut, and a 25% end-to-end cycle-time cut are unsupported, and the cycle-time gate is implausible once signing, external valuation, credit-analysis queue, and the weekly committee are left untouched.

The closing argument cites the 58-hour valuation wait as a constraint the chosen modules target. None of A5, A4, A6, or A12 touches it. It also says high-frequency intake automation would overwhelm back office. Registration is back-office work; automating it reduces that pool’s load and shifts arrival pressure downstream. Faster registration does not create more applications. Receive application is already 72% digital with a 0.2-hour wait, so arguing against automating it is easy and does not answer the politically attractive candidate, which is A1.

The directional preference for document loops, entity-mismatch assistance, statement normalization, and a control workbench is defensible. The reasoning that is supposed to justify it misstates resource effects, analyzes a different portfolio from the one it recommends, and treats the largest synchronization delay as both decisive and unaddressed.
