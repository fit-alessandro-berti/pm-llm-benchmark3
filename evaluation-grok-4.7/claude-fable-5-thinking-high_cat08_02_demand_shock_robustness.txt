7.0

The redesign is structurally complete and often sharp, but it is not nearly flawless: several load-bearing figures are wrong, one flagship mechanism misreads the event log, and a scenario policy is ambiguous in a way that can send critical work to the worse machine.

The required skeleton is all present. Five scenarios vary mix and disruption type, not just volume. Bottleneck migration is scenario-specific and usually names the downstream station that gets worse. The standing package favors rules, smoothing, defect timing, and repeat reduction over blanket hiring. Case types are not treated as simultaneously prioritizable. Triggers and shutoffs are explicit. The trade-off table has every required column. Residual risk includes failures created by the redesign itself, and the dashboard separates leading signals from lagging p90/SLA outcomes.

The data handling is not at that standard. Weekly volume in the observed peak is about 3,300 versus 2,300 cases (+43%). Calling that “~20–35%” while also anchoring S1 to the observed peak log is an internal contradiction. “Hold for specialist review doubles as a share (290 → 610 on roughly half the volume)” is false on every reading: 290/18,400 versus 610/9,900 is about a 4× per-case rate, and 290/15,890 versus 610/8,080 of technical-validation exits is also about 4×. The medical-validation share is likewise miscomputed. Normal 3,900/15,890 = 24.5% is a real share of technical-validation exits. Peak exits are 3,420 + 4,050 + 610 = 8,080, so 3,420/8,080 = 42%, not 47%. The cited 7,240 is Assay run → Technical validation, an inflow, and it is mislabeled “technically validated cases.” The same frequency DFG shows assay entries far above assay exits and technical-validation exits above technical-validation entries. The claim that technical validation is an unstable queue that “grows without bound” is not reconciled with that balance. That is a material process-mining miss, because the accumulation evidence points first at the assay/repeat subsystem.

The causal account of recollection is also off. Request recollection → Receive sample is 42h/60h after the request already exists. Early triage can shorten receipt-to-request delay (Receive → Pre-analytical check 2h/9h and Pre-analytical check → Request recollection 3h/7h). It does not shorten the return edge, and it does not justify “same-day recollection.” S4 repeats that mistake by blaming detection latency for a post-request edge that courier delay could affect and detection delay cannot. Recollection is also tied too tightly to Repeat-after-failure tails. Those are different paths: pre-analytical redraw versus assay repeats clustered on M2 and night shifts. R1 remains a sensible intervention; the stated mechanism overclaims what the edges can show.

P3 says to reserve the “healthy/faster-turnaround” machine. M2 is faster and less reliable; M1 is the one worth protecting when M2’s repeat rate rises. The slash merges those attributes, so the outage rule can dedicate the failing machine to Urgent and Complex-panel work. P5’s wording also slips: Routine p90 is already 120h in the peak log, so the harm is a larger share past 72h and a worse tail, not p90 newly crossing 72h.

Elsewhere the judgment is strong: raw I1/I2/I3/I9 are correctly demoted, I4 is made conditional so it cannot drain technical validation at 101%, I10 is kept off Urgent work because 12h transport consumes most of an 18h limit, and the float-pool and outsourcing-traceability risks are genuine self-inflicted fragilities. Those merits support a good, not excellent, score once the numerical and mechanism errors are weighted as they should be in an evidence-based process redesign.
