7.4

The answer covers all seven required sections, stays numerically faithful to the timeline, DFGs, and conformance rates, and correctly separates mission-preserving adaptations from control loss and after-the-fact cleanup. That foundation is strong. It is not nearly flawless.

The main conceptual problem is classification. Section 3 places K and L under “true resilience” even while arguing that reconciliation and audit remediation are delayed cleanup and not real-time compliance. A, D, E, H, I, and J are then also listed as both settled categories and ambiguous, so the required three-way distinction stays blurred. “Parallel authorization” in the adaptation table conflicts with the later hard stop that hospital ambient orders cannot dispatch if authorization is missing. The Manual-Emergency trigger treats snapshot age, an order-level condition, as a whole-mode trigger, and “emergency pharmacy” is not one of the given customer segments.

Several conformance signals are underused. Move-on-model deviations are not stated, the backlog-to-pick p95 breach is not used, and the fact that 2,100 of 5,600 workaround cases end at spreadsheet invoice hold is not analyzed. The 1.25× p90 rule is also applied inconsistently: 18.75 becomes ≤19, while 61.25 becomes ≤61. Proposed time and fill-rate thresholds are concrete, but several are unanchored by baseline evidence.
