4.2

The write-up has the required seven sections, a twelve-row matrix, three portfolios, and a year-1 recommendation that correctly refuses to automate registration alone and correctly leaves the activation bot out. That structure does not survive the process evidence. Several load-bearing claims are factually wrong, control concepts are repeatedly misassigned, and the rollout breaks the four-module cap it claims to respect.

The matrix is not a ranking. It stays in A1–A12 list order. Several cells misread the event log. A4’s “38% rework” is that activity’s own exception rate, not coverage of rework. A8’s “8.5%” is the contract-remediation rate; committee-packaging exceptions are 15%. The 8.0h figure is median wait before “Request missing documents,” not the customer-response delay the notes say dominates, and not a wait A5 removes. The 75 minutes is current normalization service time, not labor A6 saves. The 58h external wait is later treated as something triage can largely remove, although internal automation feasibility is low and the wait sits with external valuers. A1’s downstream note (“offload KYC if duplicates rise”) reverses the stated risk: faster registration increases inflow to classification, KYC, and document review. A5’s exception risk is rated low and friction “minimal,” against both the candidate note and relationship-manager resistance.

Dependencies are drawn as a single chain and equate modules with process states. A3 is not KYC clearance, and A4 does not require A3. The directly-follows graph shows KYC → entity mismatch (1750), a return to KYC (1160), and a leak to contract preparation (380); none of that is analyzed. A6 is normalization, not credit analysis, yet four-eyes is defined as separating A6 from A8. Four-eyes is a person-segregation rule on credit analysis versus committee packaging in high-exposure cases (5.8% month-end violations). Collateral need is a product rule (71% of equipment finance, 18% of working capital), not a consequence of normalized statements, so A6→A7 is false. Concurrency of KYC and financial-document review is ignored despite being called out. Section 2 also states the external wait as 23h, then uses 58h later.

The bottleneck portfolio (A4, A6, A7, A8) misses the large missing-document loop (3300, of which 2800 return) and the 44h signing wait, while claiming A8 “guarantees” a clean pack despite the garbage-in risk. It also has no clear control-strengthening module. The compliance portfolio says A3 “directly addresses four-eyes,” which it does not. The quick-win set (A1, A5, A9, A10) puts contract generation and e-sign ahead of reliable KYC, classification, entity resolution, and committee artifacts. A9 can strengthen the contract-versus-decision rule; A10 does not. E-sign and reminders can scale the existing provisional-signing variant (6.5% of cases, 1.1% remediated) unless a hard KYC gate is added. That variant is never mentioned. Nor are credit-line renewal’s 14% contract loops, the weekly high-exposure committee cadence, legal month-end overload, or back-office utilization already falling below 65% after 15:00.

Section 4 then assigns unsupported precision (about 12–15% cycle time, 30–35% wait, 15–20% back-office FTE, and similar bands for the other portfolios) after the task barred unsupported ROI. Causal direction is also wrong: packs are downstream of credit analysis, so cleaner packs do not cut credit-analysis rework; contract automation should not increase legal-ops demand; A10 does not “eliminate” the 21% reminder loop or enforce four-eyes. The required ties to variants and directly-follows relations are mostly absent.

In the risk table, the KYC “false-negative” row describes over-filtering and a blocked loan. That is a false positive. A false negative lets a true hit through. Four-eyes never appears as its own compensating control. The recommended portfolio’s main resource shift is also missing: A1 and A5 send cleaner, faster work to nine central credit analysts while normalization stays manual, which is the overload the resource note warns about. Scarce capacity is three compliance specialists, with handovers concentrated on two seniors, not the back office that already has afternoon slack.

The phase plan contradicts itself. A1, A5, A9, and A10 are said to use the full four-module budget, then months 6–12 add either A4 and A6 or A2 and A3. Gates such as a 60% drop in contract loops and at least 98% term alignment are ungrounded. Treating A9 as light integration, so that two core-banking slots can be spent on A1 and A5 inside six months, is asserted rather than shown.

The closing argument cites “33k” missing-document cycles; the frequency is 3300. It calls signing a lower-frequency delay even though it occurs on about 7300 cases, and it defers entity mismatch and external valuation because they are lower frequency. That reinstates the frequency heuristic the case rejects. Entity mismatch is on the critical path in 82% of cases when it occurs, with a 31h wait, and it blocks KYC and signing. A1 is also described as shrinking the KYC queue, which the same answer’s bottleneck logic does not support.
