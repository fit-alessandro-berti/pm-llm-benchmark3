The write-up has the required nine sections, a complete case table, and the right vocabulary (competing risks, censoring, immortal time, escalation leakage, non-random cohort rollout). Those strengths do not survive a strict reading of the estimands, the encoding, or the code.

The two “estimands” are complements of one event, not two decision-relevant targets. Cumulative incidence of resolution by 72 hours and “probability of not being resolved by 72 hours” add to one if they share a definition of resolution. The workload story (cases still needing attention at 72 hours) is a different quantity: withdrawal, rejection, and transfer already have a disposition and should not sit in that risk set. Calling the second quantity \(S(72)\) with \(P(T_{\text{terminal}}>72 \cup \text{terminal}\neq\text{Resolve})\) misnames a survival function. A case resolved at 76 hours (S03), open at 92 hours (S09), withdrawn at 27 hours (S06), and transferred at 68 hours (S08) is never given a separate contribution under each estimand. S09 is described as censored even though age 92 makes non-resolution at 72 observed; that erases the distinction among right censoring, competing events, and already-observed breaches. Open cases still younger than 72 hours (S10, S15, S24, S29, S31) are not contrasted with open cases already past 72 (S09, S11, S25, S26).

Transfer is not given three coherent treatments. Scenario C says count a transfer as resolution, then as a breach, then as a competing event that “counts toward non-compliance,” which collapses into scenario B. The code makes that collapse literal: `breach` returns the same `(0, 3, t)` as `competing`. The section 2 table also codes `open` as competing-risk 0, while the code uses 4.

The analysis plan omits subdistribution hazards and restricted mean time, both required. Kaplan–Meier and Aalen–Johansen are characterized acceptably, and Aalen–Johansen plus a landmark is a defensible recommendation for \(N=32\), but the landmark at 48 hours is unmotivated and the recommended estimator is never built.

The state diagram misses observed Cohort B flow `Submit → AutoCheck → Screen`, and also `Screen → Reject`, `Screen → Withdraw`, and `Screen → Escalate`. “Length-biased sampling” is the wrong name for dropping short competing outcomes. Escalation is correctly treated as time-varying, but baseline versus post-treatment features (priority, cohort, end-of-case rework) are not classified, and repeated review and path pattern are thin.

Sensitivity stays qualitative. It never states which conclusion would reverse, skips S25 (`last_event_gap_h` 48) in the long-gap rule, and does not use the 32 rows to bound breach rates. Priority fairness is named, not checked. Cohort differences are only weakly separated from causal effects of the screening change.

The single code block embeds the data accurately and does not invent intermediate timestamps, but it does not meet the analysis spec. It never builds a risk set or Kaplan–Meier/cumulative-incidence table. Open cases younger than 72 hours stay in the denominator as non-breaches, so unknown outcomes are treated as successes. The competing-risk denominator is computed and then overwritten. Stratification is cohort-only, not priority. If PM4Py imports, the log keeps only Submit and the last activity, so `get_variants` cannot recover the prefixes; the pandas fallback does. Printed “breach rates” are therefore not the estimands defined in section 1.

Limitations (timestamps, post-transfer outcomes, cutoff dates, workload, non-random assignment) are the most reliable part of the answer.
