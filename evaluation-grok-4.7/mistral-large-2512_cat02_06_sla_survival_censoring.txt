4.4

The write-up has the required nine sections and some real competing-risk vocabulary, but the estimands, the four required case contributions, and the code contradict each other and would misclassify the SLA endpoint.

Section 1 is not coherent. Estimand 1 is a conditional probability, yet S09 (open at 92h) is called censored at 72h even though unresolved status at 72h is already known. Estimand 2 then drops that same post-72 follow-up. Estimand 3 defines \(P(T>72)\) for any terminal event and then says S03, resolved at 76h, was not open at 72h. The opposite is true: a terminal time of 76h means no disposition had occurred at 72h. S09 is treated differently from S03 despite both having \(T>72\).

Section 2 censors every open case at \(\min(\text{age}, 72)\). That erases the distinction, required by the task, between right censoring before 72h and an already observed breach after 72h. The three transfer stories are named, but “informative censoring” is still listed as an ordinary censoring alternative rather than a different estimand.

Section 3 is the strongest analytical passage. S15/S31 versus S09/S26 correctly separates open cases still able to meet the SLA from open cases that have already missed it, and withdrawals/transfers are not waved through as harmless censoring. It also mixes completed-case deletion with the different bias from coding competing events as censored.

Section 4 names the right families, then misstates them for these data. Restricted mean time limited at 72h is aligned with the horizon; calling it unaligned is false. Kaplan–Meier is appropriate for time to any terminal disposition and inappropriate for resolution under competing risks; only the second use is acknowledged. Aalen–Johansen and a multi-state model are both marked primary, while the prose later demotes the multi-state model. Transition times are not in the extract, so that multi-state model is not identifiable here. \(n=32\) is never treated as a constraint.

Section 5 labels full-path pattern and any occurrence of `AutoCheck` as baseline features with no leakage. Both are future relative to `Submit`. The worked example says S13’s current stage is `Escalate`; that prefix ends in `Resolve`. The diagram omits observed transitions that define the policy change and the rest of the log, including `Submit → AutoCheck`, `Screen → Escalate`, `Escalate → Review`, `AutoCheck → Resolve`, and `Rework → Resolve`.

Section 6 lists real sensitivities, but censoring at \(72 - \text{last\_event\_gap\_h}\) is not a coherent observation scheme and is 0 for S26. The claimed direction for breach probability does not follow from the base encoding, where open cases past 72h are not events.

Section 7 sets an arbitrary 50% alert, treats S11 and S25 as predicted risk after they have already breached, and proposes a 48-hour escalation rule with no event times. The non-causal caveat and the priority/cohort check are appropriate but undeveloped.

Section 8 transcribes the table, then clips every time at 72 while leaving `event_resolution = 1` for all resolved cases. S03, S13, S20, and S28 therefore become resolutions at 72h. The three-scenario dictionary is unused and would mark every case resolved. The “transfer as failure” fit actually models any non-open terminal status. Priority is not stratified. Identical fabricated timestamps are added despite the instruction not to invent timestamps, and there is no fallback if `lifelines` or PM4Py is absent. String event labels do not encode censoring as the required zero code for an Aalen–Johansen fit.

Section 9 covers the important missing data, but it describes `duration_or_age_h` as time to the last event. For open rows that field is age at cutoff; time of the last event is age minus `last_event_gap_h`.
