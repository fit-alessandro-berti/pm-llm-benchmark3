The memo covers every required section, stays inside the four-module cap, and correctly refuses to lead with registration or an activation bot. The recommendation is directionally usable. The support is not reliable enough for a higher score.

The candidate table is an ID list, not a ranking. Several process-benefit claims treat pre-activity waiting time as time the module removes. Contract generation is a 35-minute legal task with a 22-hour upstream wait; it does not cut that 22-hour wait. Normalization’s 20-hour wait and the 31-hour entity-mismatch wait are mostly queues, and entity resolution has only a 55-minute service time on three specialists. “Removes 31 hours” and “cuts the 22-hour contract wait” overstate the mechanism. A12’s exception risk is rated low even though the candidate is defined as exposing policy ambiguity.

Section 4 then puts unsupported precision on that mistake: signing wait 44h to ~12h, remediation 8.5% to under 3%, analyst inflow +11–13%, entity wait 31h to 6h, doc-loop 52% to ~20%, collateral “15h spread,” KYC queue −40%, specialists freed 25%, analyst touch time −35%. Analyst minutes do not yield a 35% reduction from normalization alone. No baseline cycle time exists, yet overall effects are given as 10–15%, 12%, and, in the recommendation, ~25%. Those last two figures describe the same portfolio and contradict each other. On the memo’s own quick-win arithmetic, a 32-hour signing reduction on most cases would beat the bottleneck portfolio on cycle time, but the recommendation never reconciles that.

Explicit process evidence is largely unused. KYC and financial-document review run concurrently, so the slower branch is the critical path; that split is never analyzed. Collateral is required on 71% of equipment finance and 18% of working-capital, committee volume is 3,900 against 7,600 contracts, and credit-line renewals remediate at 14% versus 8.5% overall. None of this case mix appears. DFG structure is also missed: 1,160 entity mismatches return to KYC versus 380 continuing toward contract, and 2,800 of 3,300 document requests loop back. Aggregate delay still sits more in signing, credit-analysis wait, document review, and contract wait than in the low-frequency entity queue. Back-office utilization below 65% after 15:00, which undercuts the visible registration queue, is unused.

Dependencies are inconsistent. The map says A4 needs A3 or clean KYC data, then the chosen portfolio and rollout deploy A4 without A3. Deep core-banking candidates are listed as A1, A9, A3, and A11, while Phase 1 spends the two integration slots on A4 and A5. Four-eyes is a same-person rule on credit analysis and committee packaging; the map ties it to contract sequencing. The compliance portfolio is credited with a four-eyes monitor and a provisional-signing hard stop that A3, A4, A9, and A12 do not implement. The false-negative control reviews scores below threshold, which is the flagged population, not the auto-cleared population where false negatives sit, and a 100% review cancels the automation benefit.

The rollout recreates a risk the matrix already names. A5 is fully integrated in months 2–6 while A6 covers 15% of statements, which is the cleaner-inflow path that overloads the nine analysts. The compliance requirement is met by calling a workflow configuration a non-module. Gates such as analyst median queue “not above 20 hours” sit at today’s normalization wait and omit four-eyes, provisional signing, specialist WIP, and renewal remediation.

The final trade-off shows why an 8-minute registration step is a weak first bet. It does not show why A4, A5, A6, and A7 beat a portfolio that attacks the 44-hour signing wait, nor why that set satisfies the control-module rule. A7 is also asked to smooth legal month-end load that the resource note attributes to upstream KYC timing, not valuer routing.
