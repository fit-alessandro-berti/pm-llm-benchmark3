7.5

The memo is a real decision document: it selects a segmented hybrid, refuses both national scale and blanket rollback, and ties that choice to tail risk, rework, billing-before-activation, permit gaps, and case-mix confounding. All six required sections are present, the five metric conflicts are explained in substance, alternatives A–E have advantages, risks, and conditions, and the measurement ranking mostly says what would change the decision. Numbers that are cited match the brief.

The operating rule for survey skipping is ambiguous. The opening decision suspends survey skipping outside a controlled experiment. The immediate-policy and final-action bullets then require surveys for apartments, old buildings, business, rural, and any address outside validated low-risk eligibility, which reads as leaving verified simple houses off that requirement. Those two instructions cannot be executed together. Business and rural cases are also placed under permit-and-survey controls even though the brief ties permits to old and apartment buildings, business harm to manual activation and billing disputes, and rural harm to rescheduling under coordinator load.

The fitness/precision explanation is mostly right, then misstates the alignments. Frequent move-on-log means logged behavior the model does not replay cleanly (billing before successful activation, repeat installation without a survey, complaint after billing). The memo treats those mismatches as behavior a low-precision model absorbs. That confuses permissiveness with conformance failure. Generalization falling from 0.70 to 0.56 is unused, despite being directly relevant to whether the pilot model can travel. Of the temporal-profile findings, only the failed-visit to second-visit delay is used explicitly; the worsened installation-to-activation path for apartments and the new billing-to-complaint long tail are thin. The complaint-type shift, from waiting for an appointment to “installed but not working” and billing before activation, is unused, so the median-versus-experience conflict is less grounded than the evidence allows.

The evidence table keeps categories apart and exceeds eight items, with one bad classification: “full 60-day economics are unfavorable” is listed as an assumption, while the recommendation correctly treats that cost result as incomplete and orders a 60-day cost study. The fact line on alignments also softens the brief’s “frequent” deviations to “common enough to appear.”

Value-of-information ranks are explained, and refusing to randomize billing-before-activation is sound. The order is still soft. The building-risk rule is described as what makes the survey-skip experiment interpretable, yet that validation is ranked below the experiment. Coordinator ownership is something the memo would scale now, while the absence test that could reverse that scale sits at rank 6, below reason codes. The rollout gates add unmotivated cutoffs — p90 within 2 days, complaints within 1 percentage point, utilization under 85% — beside a p95 test that only says “materially.” The 6-week, 9,200-case window versus a 10-week, 24,000-case baseline, and sparse start timestamps, are not treated as threats to the cycle-time comparison itself. Kill criteria for the limited simple-house deployment, as opposed to gates for later expansion, are unspecified.

Taken together, the recommendation is strategically defensible and carefully hedged, but the survey rule contradicts itself, one core process-mining link is wrong, and several decision thresholds are sharper than the evidence.
