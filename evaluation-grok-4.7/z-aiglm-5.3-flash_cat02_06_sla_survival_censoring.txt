6.4

The estimand design is strong: time origin, the 72-hour horizon, competing withdrawal/rejection, administrative censoring of opens, already-observed breaches, and three transfer regimes are separated before estimators are chosen. Case roles for a late resolution, an old open case, an early withdrawal, and a pre-horizon transfer are right, and escalation is correctly treated as time-varying rather than a baseline covariate. Method choice (Aalen–Johansen plus bounds, landmarking, no Fine–Gray at this n) fits the data.

The headline survival arithmetic does not. With withdrawals and rejections censored, the Kaplan–Meier probability of resolution by 72 hours is about 0.32 (survival about 0.68), not 0.68. The competing-risk cumulative incidence is about 0.31, so there is no swing from “32% miss” to “69% miss.” Both readings of “not resolved by 72” are near 68–69%; the cited contrast confuses S(t) with F(t). The code then prints that survival value as Pr(resolve ≤ 72) and folds in the first event after 72, so it cannot verify the prose.

The pathway reversal is also wrong. Broad breach bounds are about [50%, 83%] if escalated and [50%, 65%] if not, not [80%, 95%] for the non-escalated cases. Still-active-at-72 ranges are about [6, 8]/12 versus [3, 6]/20, not [6, 7]/12 versus [2, 5]/20. Cohort intervals such as 26.7–73.3% mix a narrow floor with a broad ceiling. P2 escalation is 4/14, not 4/15; P3 is 0/10, not 0/9. S01 is Screen-then-AutoCheck, not AutoCheck-first, and S26’s 72-hour gap is not an escalation stall.

Those errors hit the quantitative conclusions the design was built to support. The qualitative frame remains usable; the reported probabilities and rank reversals are not.
