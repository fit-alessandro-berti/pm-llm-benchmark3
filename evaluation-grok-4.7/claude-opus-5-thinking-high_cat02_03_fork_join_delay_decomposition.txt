The delay decomposition is correctly defined and numerically reliable. The additive identity \(T_c=U_c+G_c+D_c+V_c\), the demonstration that \(\sum_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci}+W_{ci})=3G_c\), the case table, the four worked examples, critical-branch counts (B 8 / A 2 / C 2), attributable margins (120 / 15 / 12), and the joint-floor split (585 + 147 = 732) all match the log. Ranking B by cycle time, last-completion frequency, slack, tail, and attributable join time, while refusing to rank by mean service, is the right analysis. The counterfactual simulator is also internally consistent: the reported savings (5, 122, 20, 73, 91, 106, 129.5, 204) reproduce under the stated lag and resource rules, including spillover and the F11 masking result. Assumptions, the 18–73 capacity bound, and the non-identifiability of the pre-F gap are handled at a high level. The required sections and PM4Py-versus-pandas distinction are present.

The score is held down by concrete slips. F10 did not wait for F2 while F1 was free: F1 is idle only from 10:40 to 11:10, and F10 is not join-ready until 11:20. F11’s 20-minute wait overlaps a free F2 for about 10 minutes, not the whole wait. “Nine cases unaffected, and two get worse” contradicts the same section’s table: seven are unchanged, two worsen, and three improve. The super-additivity paragraph credits F11’s 10 minutes for beating the sum of CF1 and CF2, but that 10 is already inside CF1; the extra join minutes are F01 (+2.5), F05 (+2.5), and F10 (+5). After the C-queue cap, F04 is held by both residual lag and resource blocking, not lag alone. Setting every lag to 5 also raises the four baseline lags of 0, which “cutting lag to 5” does not say.
