5.5

The write-up has the required seven sections, separates case types, and correctly treats technical validation and an M2 repeat spiral as redesign-created risks. Those merits do not offset several decision-level errors in the evidence.

The scenarios are too thin to stress a redesign. The winter-wave case restates the observed peak (Urgent 29%, Complex-panel 20%) without a volume, duration, or service target. The specialist case treats a medical validator and the specialist as interchangeable, despite separate pools, utilizations (93% vs 87%), and queues (22h vs 36h). No scenario states what “acceptable” means against the 18-hour Urgent penalty and 72-hour Routine target.

The migration analysis misses the main process-mining shift. Technical validation releases directly in 11,700 of 15,890 normal exits, but at peak direct release (4,050) and medical validation (3,420) are nearly equal. That mix-driven swing is already present; it is not something that appears only after assay capacity is relieved. Calling assay the first winter bottleneck also ignores peak queues: specialist review 36h, medical validation 22h, assay 18h. Adding an evening medical shift cannot move the constraint upstream into technical validation, because technical validation is already at 101% and sits before medical validation. I10 is a Complex-panel overflow valve, so it does not answer an M2 outage. Accession correction is 500 of 18,400 normal cases and 420 of 9,900 peak cases; a 14-hour queue there is painful, but clearing it does not push the main wave downstream.

The “robust” package is not robust. I4 pulls people out of the station already at 101%, and cross-training takes four weeks, so it cannot be switched on inside a wave. I7 and I8 are front-door measures; they do little for an assay outage or a specialist absence. Early triage helps a branch that is about 4% of flow normally and 8% at peak, which cannot relieve 98% assay utilization.

The conditional policies conflict. Policy A sends all Routine work to M2 in larger batches precisely when M2 is most failure-prone. Policy C then stops Routine work on M2. A and C can be on together. Policy A turns on if either assay queue or Urgent mix trips, but turns off on queue alone. Policy B applies a medical-validation shift to a specialist-review trigger, and neither B nor C has an off switch. Policy C’s 12% “repeat-after-failure” trigger names an incoming case type (~8–9% of orders), not the Assay → Repeat assay loop (about 6% normally, about 13% at peak). I9 adds Routine waiting and does not reduce M2 load. Larger preparation batches also block Urgent work on a shared bench; Urgent batch-bypass already falls from 74% to 58% at peak, and the policy never installs the exception that conflict I2 requires.

The table drops Policy C, marks I4 as covering the demand shift that makes I4 harmful, and calls the Complex-panel effect of I10 an unblock while omitting the 12-hour transport penalty. Section 7 then sets the M2 alarm at 10% against the policy’s 12%. An Urgent-bypass warning above 60% is on in normal operation (74%) and off in the peak failure mode (58%), so it flags the healthy pattern and misses the breakdown. The dashboard also omits queues for technical validation, medical validation, and specialist review, which are the constraints the earlier sections themselves name.
