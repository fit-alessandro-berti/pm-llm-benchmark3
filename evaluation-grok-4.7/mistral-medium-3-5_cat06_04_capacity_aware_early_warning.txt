**3.5**

The write-up names the right constraints and then does not follow them. It excludes I-03, treats I-05 differently from I-07, adjusts segments in the correct direction, and has a monitoring outline. The deployable rule, the portfolio, and the impact table contradict each other, and the euro figures are not the expected values they claim to be.

The score is not the expected benefit the prompt specifies. It multiplies adjusted probability by breach value by the midpoint absolute reduction, then subtracts swarm cost and the full false-alarm burden. Absolute reduction is already a probability change, so multiplying by `p_now` again has no decision-theoretic meaning. The printed scores also do not equal that expression. I-01 is about €755, not €14,500. I-02 is about €1,100, not €22,000. I-09 is about €2,060, not €30,000. I-04’s €4,500 and I-08’s €3,000 are roughly the products before costs. I-08’s own robustness check is stated backwards: \(0.05 \times €100{,}000 = €5{,}000\), which is less than €5,450, so the stated rule would reject I-08. The table still keeps it as “wait.”

The allocation does not implement the top-4 rule. On the numbers printed in the table, I-09 is first and I-07 is fifth, yet the action is to swarm I-07 and wait on I-09. The non-native “default to wait unless the lower bound exceeds 0.20” appears only later, and I-09’s lower bound is 0.18. Correcting the arithmetic would rank I-09 and I-04 at the top and I-07 near the bottom. Using all four slots, including the €5,000 SME case and the imputed I-06 jump, while calling that set the high-value set, is inaccurate: I-04 and I-08 are the high-value cases. The regulatory hold is dismissed as “low risk” with no basis, and once four slots are spent there is nothing to reprioritize into. Section 5 then asks to reserve a low-value slot, which Section 2 refuses.

Section 3 measures a different quantity from the scenarios it labels. “Upper half,” “midpoint,” and “lower bound” of risk reduction would put expected breaches avoided near 1.1, 0.9, and 0.6, and direct benefits near €13–15k, €11k, and €5–7k before one selected case fails. The table instead books 4, 3, and 1 certain breaches and €54k, €45k, and €25k, i.e. the full contractual values. A note even computes about €2.15k for I-02 and then scales it up. Spillover is an expectation, so subtracting it from a tail “all selected cases are saved” outcome is not a net. The six affected cases are described as including I-04, I-08, and I-10, which are in triage, not the diagnosis queue. The extra €2k for 15–30 minutes stacks a second monetization on the probability spillover the delay is already meant to represent. False-alarm burden is in the score and missing from the nets. All three scenarios are reported as clearly positive. Under midpoint effects, four slots, and 0.02 on six diagnosis-queue cases, the sign is ambiguous, and the adverse case can be negative. Confidence marked “high” on that adverse row is unsupported.

I-05 versus I-07 is the right contrast, but the temporal rule is thinner than the operating constraint. A two-hour cool-down does not stop a 30-minute refresh from returning the same no-event case, and SLA under two hours reopens a case with no new event. That is a different rule from “another swarm only after a materially new event.” I-07’s probability fell by 0.03; the text treats the change as 0.03. There is no check that a 45-minute swarm can still move a 1.5-hour triage case, or that long-horizon cases should wait because the slot has option value.

The fairness section lists the required risks and then mishandles them. The starvation example says high-value cases crowd out I-01 and I-07; the chosen portfolio does the reverse. A hard wait rule for Non-Native-Language Support is itself unequal treatment, and it is not in the decision rule. Raising the Strategic Enterprise penalty because that segment exceeds 30% of swarms mixes a calibration backtest with a quota. A 10% random withhold is a real causal-identification idea, but it is absent from the allocation rule, and “>5% of untreated cases breach due to delay” has no attribution method.

Monitoring names the right families, then inherits the bad accounting. A €20k base-net trigger is scaled to the inflated €36k. False-alarm burden is an input, not an outcome that can be observed to “exceed €1k.” Calibration flags at 0.10 and rule changes at 0.15 sit on top of a Strategic error that is already about 0.05–0.12. Drift is checked by comparing predictions, which misses the label problem: prevented breaches teach the model that swarmed-looking cases are safer. There is no negative control using post-diagnosis cases, no propensity log, and almost no non-monetary treatment of the regulatory incident or public-sector duty. The closing claim that the policy is robust to uncertainty is not supported by the arithmetic.
