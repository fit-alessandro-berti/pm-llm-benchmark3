7.2

The design is coherent and covers every required section, with the right high-level architecture: a calendar-aware discrete-event core, waiting time produced by queues rather than sampled from the log, latent objections, non-stationary arrivals, shared resources, and PM4Py kept on the log/model side. Those choices are sound. Under a strict reading it is still materially flawed in several places that would change results or misstate what the evidence can support.

The process-tree treatment is weaker than it looks. The excerpt repeats `Completeness check` and never emits `Completeness rework` or `Inspection rework`, so the loop-redo labels in the supplied tree are not the observed rework encoding. Conformance will systematically flag that structural mismatch; treating those deviations as ordinary rework parameters mixes a labeling mismatch with genuine looping. The parallel block’s join also sits before public notice and inspection, not immediately before decision drafting. Calling decision drafting the AND-join gate blurs control flow the tree already fixes by sequence. Fire capacity is left ambiguous: “2 FTE equivalent, Tuesday–Thursday only” can mean two weekly FTE compressed into three days, or two people present only three days. Those are different capacities, and the design never picks one.

Service versus delay is handled well in prose and then undercut. The uniform claim that a service duration is working time advanced only inside resource calendars cannot be applied to the statutory public-notice minimum, which is elapsed calendar time, nor to the holiday and council-meeting interruptions the brief says affect that period. Applicant delay is separated, which is correct, but the same calendar-time carve-out is missing for notice periods. The skeleton comment then proposes `waiting = flow_time - assumed_service`, which re-imports historical congestion into the split the narrative correctly refuses to treat as an input. The comment describes a calculation the function does not even perform.

Cross-case structure is real, but D3 is misspecified. District batching in the brief is geographic and can group different architects; the model bundles only `(architect, district)` and assumes bundling is beneficial only inside that pair. That shrinks batch size, travel savings, and the first-case delay, so the D3 trade-off is not the intervention named in the matrix. Consultant-report delay on environmental review is also left independent across cases, despite architect-linked applications being a stated dependency. D2’s “no prior violations” rule has no data source, and that gap is absent from the missing-data assumptions. D7 rescales the Monday factor without saying whether displaced submissions move to other weekdays or disappear.

The experiment and uncertainty sections overclaim the statistics. A resolution-IV fraction on four factors aliases two-factor interactions with each other; it does not estimate them, and a full 16-run factorial is cheap here. Effect sizes such as “−50% missing-document loops,” “10–25% legal conflict,” and “−40% notice tail” are counterfactual and cannot be identified from this log; the estimation plan never separates them from log-calibrated baseline parameters. Stress windows (“6 months,” “3 months”) are not anchored to peak versus off-peak seasons, which matters in a seasonal system. Welch’s method and MSER-5 assume a transient into steady state, which the same section says does not exist; a seasonal phase comparison would match the cyclo-stationary setup. The outer Latin-hypercube loop is a sensitivity envelope, not a credible interval, unless a prior is actually specified. A Kolmogorov–Smirnov gate on tens of thousands of cases will reject trivial discrepancies. Bottleneck validation ranks environmental review strictly above site inspection although both median waits are 8 days, and unconditional median wait is not bottleneck severity for optional activities such as fire review.

Validation, common random numbers, tail metrics, and the refusal to treat the twin as an optimum are strong. The PM4Py calls that are actually written (`read_csv` via pandas, `format_dataframe`, `parse_process_tree`, variants, DFG, performance DFG, `get_case_arrival_average`) match current conventions, and the layer split is clear. The remaining code is an honest skeleton, but the case object drops most of the state variables section 1 declares, and the experiment list drops several scenarios the matrix treats as required.

Taken together, the answer is a serious design with the right boundaries and several errors that a simulator implementer would copy into biased comparisons.
