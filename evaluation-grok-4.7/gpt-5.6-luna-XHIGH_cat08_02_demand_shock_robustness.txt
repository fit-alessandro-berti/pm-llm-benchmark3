7.4

The answer is a strong, scenario-based redesign with the required seven sections, real downstream-overload reasoning, and conditional use of the manager interventions. It is not nearly flawless. Several evidence and mechanism errors are material because bottleneck migration and process-mining fidelity are central to the task.

The clearest logic error is in Scenario C. If a medical validator is absent, cases that need medical review still leave Technical validation and accumulate in the medical queue. The claim that Technical validation “appears to improve because fewer cases leave it” reverses that mechanism and contradicts the next clause, which correctly says the output piles up in front of Medical validation. The later point about moving technical validators into accession correction is sound; the departure mechanism is not.

The batch-wait evidence is mislabeled. “Batch wait increases from 6 to 14 hours, followed by 22 hours” assigns the Test preparation → Batch wait edge (6h / 14h) to batch wait itself. The Batch wait → Assay run edge is 9h normal and 22h peak. The 22h figure is cited correctly, but the 6-to-14 claim is the wrong arc. Median edge times are also treated as if they can be read sequentially as one wait.

“A small increase in defect rate” does not match the peak log. Pre-analytical recollection rises from 760/18,120 received cases (4.2%) to 780/9,730 (8.0%). Combined diversion to recollection or manual correction rises from about 7% to about 12%. The harmful feedback onto intake capacity is real; the increase is not small.

Specialist hold volume is understated. Peak has 610 holds in 3 weeks versus 290 in 8 weeks, about 5.6 times the weekly rate. “More than twice … after adjusting for the shorter period” is technically true and analytically weak.

The redesign also has internal control gaps. The default batch size is already 12, while the mix-shift policy says to “reduce” it to 12. Accession-correction flex depends on Technical validation being below a safe floor, yet that pool is already at 101% utilization with a 16-hour peak queue, so the flex is unavailable in the state where the 14-hour correction queue is worst. That infeasibility is not carried into residual risk. The courier policy turns off only when no Urgent case has under six hours of slack; with peak Urgent median 17h and p90 33h, that off-switch would rarely fire.

Headline anchors are thinly used. Normal Urgent p90 is already 19h against an 18h penalty, peak Urgent p90 is 33h, rework moves from 7.5% to 11.8%, and the share of cases with a queue over 12h moves from 22% to 54%. The answer tracks the right classes and warns against averages, but it never uses those comparisons to show that the normal tail is already outside the contractual target.

What keeps the score up is the overall control logic: class-aware reservations instead of permanent Urgent-first dispatch, WIP caps, conditional M1 protection, selective partner overflow compared with the 36h/48h specialist delay, explicit redesign-caused starvation, and a leading-versus-lagging dashboard. Those conclusions mostly survive the local errors, but the errors are in the reasoning the task is meant to test.
