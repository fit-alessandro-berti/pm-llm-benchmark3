7.2

The memo is well structured, hits every required heading, and reaches a defensible policy conclusion: no single “X%” cycle-time claim, median and tail kept separate, and expansion only with a phased comparison. The main DiD arithmetic checks. That is not enough for a high score. Several quantitative and identification claims are wrong, overstated, or not produced by the analysis that is shown.

The pre/post table is mostly accurate. Alpha/Beta/Gamma/Delta/Epsilon baseline, May–June, and July–August means for median and p90 match the panel, as do the Gamma-versus-Delta/Epsilon shock-to-mature contrasts (about −5.1 h and −3.3 h for the median; about −9.4 h and −5.9 h for p90) and the April-cohort mature-minus-baseline contrasts versus Delta. Escalation differentials of roughly +3.3 to +3.8 pp versus +2.5 to +2.8 pp are also right. Small slips remain: Epsilon rework rises about +0.2 pp, not +0.3, and Gamma’s satisfaction change versus June is about +0.18, not +0.17.

Section 5.3 then leaves the data. “Median falls ~4–10 h as use rises 25 pp” is not what the rows show. April-to-August median changes are about −5 h (Alpha), −2 h (Beta), and, for Gamma, −2 h on only +7 pp of use. The −10 h figure is the June-to-August drop, which never-treated Epsilon largely shares (−8.6 h median, −16 h at p90) while its fast-path share stays at 4%. The unfitted claim that branch-and-month fixed effects “yield a negative coefficient” and that the p90 coefficient is “smaller/positive in early months” is not a calculation. The code fits one linear intensity term for each outcome. It has no early-month interaction, and no coefficient is computed in the memo. That is the largest quantitative-discipline failure.

The top “Observed” bullet repeats the exaggeration. July–August recovery is not largest in adopting branches, nor in the highest-use adopters. Epsilon’s June-to-August median and p90 drops (−8.6 h, −16.1 h) exceed Beta’s (−4.0 h, −7.5 h) despite Beta reaching 50% fast-path use, and they nearly match Alpha and Gamma. Beta is the counterexample to a use–recovery story, and it is labeled as observed fact.

Identification is directionally right and incompletely executed. The preferred design is a staggered event study against not-yet-treated and never-treated branches, with a clear warning about two-way fixed effects under heterogeneous timing. The code builds relative-time indicators and then ignores them. The only panel models are single-coefficient two-way fixed effects in `automation` or `automated_fast_path_pct`. Those are the specifications the narrative says not to treat as the estimand. `arrivals` and `high_complexity_pct` are entered as controls with no bad-control discussion, even though routing is bundled with rollout and the memo itself says registered complexity may move with triage. The prose also says month effects absorb the May–June complexity surge. They absorb only the common part. Alpha goes 28→36, Epsilon 30→38, Beta falls 25→23 in April, and Delta/Gamma rise by 5 pp. The 30–50% “discount” that turns −5.5 to −8.8 h into −2 to −6 h is an arbitrary haircut, and p90 is not discounted the same way.

The reproducible script is not reliably reproducible. In current pandas, same-frequency period subtraction returns an integer number of months, so `.apply(lambda x: x.n)` should raise. Cluster-robust warnings are printed, which meets the letter of the requirement, but five clusters are then still used as the only uncertainty display. Validation never checks that `automation` dates match the stated rollout or that fast-path share is already 4–6% when `automation=0`. That pre-availability mass is a measurement problem for every dose estimand and is never treated as one. Piecewise-constant rework and escalation (identical values for three consecutive months) are likewise unremarked, so month-to-month process signatures look more precise than the reporting grid.

Estimands cover availability, dose, median, tail, rework, escalation, satisfaction, and a dynamic/heterogeneous contrast, and interference through the fraud desk is explicit. Gaps that matter for this decision remain. There is no transport estimand for Delta and Epsilon. E4 and E5 are both named as routing effects although rework is not routing. “No reliably” is left as the identifiability judgment for satisfaction. The mechanism table is substantively good, but several “expected signatures” are calendar-wide (May–June p90 spikes, satisfaction dips) and cannot identify automation.

The rollout choice, `expand with an embedded evaluation`, fits the evidence, and Delta-then-Epsilon is a reasonable sequence. The design still overstates what two branches can identify. A stepped wedge does not escape a two-cluster assignment, and the shared fraud desk contaminates Epsilon if Delta sends it more borderline cases. Guardrails are specific, but “rework rises” is not a threshold, and the decision rule treats a single control branch as if it were a stable counterfactual. Power, interference, and case-level versus branch-level inference should have been stated as limits on the learning the pilot can produce.

The claim ladder’s middle rungs are appropriately hedged. The conditional escalation effect of +1 to +3 pp is in a plausible range only after the common March +2 pp fraud-desk jump, which the memo does use. Confidence 60 on a “probable” median reduction is high relative to two adoption dates, selected early adopters, and co-moving volume, staffing, and mix. The safe leadership claims are better than the qualitative labels beside them.

Overall: complete, process-aware, and right to refuse a pooled percentage effect, but not nearly flawless. Uncomputed regression signs, an observed recovery ranking the panel contradicts, a recommended event study that the code does not estimate, and informal bounds presented as sensitivity keep it well below the top of the scale.
