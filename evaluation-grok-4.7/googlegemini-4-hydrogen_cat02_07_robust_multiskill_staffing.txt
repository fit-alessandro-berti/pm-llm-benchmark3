6.7

The capacity arithmetic is mostly right and the required nine-part structure is present, but the policy comparison is not evidence. Section 4 reports 24 cells as 20-seed Monte Carlo behavior, while Section 8 never runs that experiment (it sets `n_seeds=12`) and no simulation output is shown. Those ranges are judgmental estimates labeled as observations. That is a direct failure to separate estimates from measurements, and it is the basis for the robustness claim.

The audited load math holds up. Mixture means are correct (Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.30, Electrical 32.82, Software 38.50, Validation 15.74). Activity volumes 18.09 / 24.30 / 12.85 and 2,720.6 base minutes against 2,490 regular minutes are correct, as are shift calendars (480, 600, 510, 570, 210, 120) and the U4 increment of about +242 minutes. U1 and U3 block loads follow from those figures. U2 does not: after doubling E and ES and renormalizing, 14:00–16:00 is about 655 minutes, or 115% of 570, not 116.5%, and 12:00–14:00 is about 90.0%, not 90.4%. The stated “E = 240 min” is consistent with renormalization; the headline load is not. The 3,300–3,800 “effective” band is also tighter than the author’s own 1.05–1.15 multiplier and 14–25 min/case handover assumptions.

The structural story is the strongest part: afternoon electrical peak, 16:00 capacity cliff, Emil’s mechanical hole after 18:00, and Farah as the only software resource before noon under U3/U5. The Farah check, 1.7 × morning software base × 1.40 ≈ 366 minutes on a 240-minute shift, is correct. Uniform mixtures, the U1 −4/−4 priority split, cross-team coding, and per-day overtime are labeled as assumptions.

Policy text and code diverge. P2_POOL is specified to fall back to sequential ME/ES and avoid the 8-minute coordination delay; the code leaves `branch_mode` as Parallel unless P3/P4 explicitly merges, so sequential execution still pays coordination. The U5 outage only postpones arrivals inside 09:30–10:00. Intake already queued or in progress can still run, so the shock the recommendation treats as decisive is under-modeled. Utilization is charged at task start from planned active time, not subtracted on preemption, and charged again on restart, so `max_util` is biased upward exactly where preemption differs across policies. Planned overtime is consumed when the completion is scheduled, not restored if the task is preempted. The “once per case” rule is enforced per task. The P3 gate counts any on-shift coworker, not a qualified one.

Objective B imposes `U_max ≤ 0.90` on BASE, then recommends P3_HYBRID with a stated BASE maximum of 86–92%. P4_FAIR_BAL is the policy that actually fits that constraint. The P3 anti-starvation trigger (`slack < 180` on a 1,440-minute clock) opens only about three hours before a 24-hour deadline, which the 12-hour overnight closure can consume. Section 6 names starvation but does not fix the control.

Section 7 is substantively good: the required log attributes are defined, and the overnight-span versus stage-wait distinction is the right PM4Py warning. The code does call `format_dataframe`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_service_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network`, and it prints stage-level means separately. It does not show that those calls succeed, and `.connections` plus the divide-by-60 assumption are unverified. The emitted log omits queue entry, eligible set, planned service, and pause/resume even though Section 7 makes them the monitoring design.

The recommendation is directionally coherent — preserve fast primary skills, retain Validation, and parallelize only for P1 — but its claimed dominance is not reproducible from the code as written. A scaffold would have been acceptable if omitted mechanisms were named. Here they are implemented incorrectly and then cited as scenario results.
