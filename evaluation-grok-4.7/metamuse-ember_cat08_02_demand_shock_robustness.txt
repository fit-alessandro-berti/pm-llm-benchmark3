7.5

The answer covers all seven required sections, uses the logs carefully, and avoids a blanket capacity increase and average-only optimization. Its weekly mix arithmetic, transition shares, and utilization citations are mostly accurate, and the scenario triggers, downstream-overload discussion, and redesign-induced risks are substantive. It is not nearly flawless.

The clearest factual slip is treating 11,700 of 15,890 normal technical transitions as evidence that Routine cases release directly. That count is all direct releases. At peak the direct-release share also falls to about 50%, so the normal-period figure is a weak basis for the peak claim. I9 still deserves rejection because it adds Routine waiting without relieving assay or validation.

In S4, 9h and 13h are performance-edge medians, not the station queues of 9.5h and 11h, yet they are grouped under “peak queues.” The drop in Urgent batch bypass from 74% to 58% is real, but assigning it specifically to courier compression is unsupported. Early two-hour rejection is also not reconciled with a 9.5h pre-analytical queue: unless triage jumps that queue, the proposed clock cannot be met.

Repeat-after-failure orders and in-process repeat-assay loops are blurred. The worst case class, with peak p90 of 240h, never receives a distinct policy. The 18h Urgent target is already missed in the normal period, where p90 is 19h, and the package never checks whether reserved slots can restore it once peak edges and the medical queue are considered. Aging promotion, the 6-hour batch cap, and the 20–30% outsourcing cap are stated without a failure mode for the case where most of the WIP ages together.
