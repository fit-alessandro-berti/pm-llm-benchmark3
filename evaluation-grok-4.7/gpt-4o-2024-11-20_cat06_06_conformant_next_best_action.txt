5.5

The answer reaches the right family of conclusions: Actions A and B stay last, a second-officer review stays mandatory, P-901 is not ignored, and re-screening is not treated as a substitute for that review. The implementation does not hold together, and several required comparisons are missing or contradicted by the answer’s own numbers.

The ranking is internally dominated. Rank 1 is sequential screening then S4, with earliest completion 50–110 minutes, one scarce API call, and the same €20,000–€35,000 benefit and €15,000–€80,000 harm as Rank 2. Rank 2 is faster (40–80 minutes) and uses no API call. On the table as written, Rank 2 dominates Rank 1. Screening is valuable only if it runs in parallel with the wait for S4, because 15–30 minutes of screening fits inside a 35–65 minute wait. The answer never makes that concurrency explicit, even though the task asked for sequence, concurrency, and fallback, and even gave parallel screening-plus-S4 as the model composite. Section 6 still writes the plan as “screening → wait for S4.”

Review duration is inconsistent and drives the recommendation. Action C states a 35–75 minute review plus 5–15 minutes to record and, if approved, execute (40–90 minutes total). Section 2 then gives S2 and S4 about 10 minutes. Under the stated 35–75 minute review, S4 arriving at 35 minutes finishes near or after the 90-minute deadline, and S4 at 65 minutes misses it. The “High” confidence primary plan depends on the unsupported shorter figure. Section 5 also calls S2 interruption “Rank 2,” while the table has it at Rank 3, and it does not actually compare an S4 arrival at 35 minutes with one at 65 minutes. It also skips the required point that waiting on an override can consume the deadline without enabling anything.

Benefits and harms are not comparable. Ranks 1–3 are all credited with the full on-time value even when much of the time range is past 90 minutes. The €15,000–€80,000 customer-harm range is pasted on as if it were the expected harm of those plans, and it is mislabeled as SLA-breach loss. P-901’s spillover is given as €5,000–€20,000, a figure the case does not supply; the case supplies only a 0.12–0.28 rise in breach probability from a 45-minute interruption of a payment with a 70-minute deadline. Approval probabilities do not form a distribution: 55–75%, 25–30%, and 10% cannot occur together. The “under 0.3” match threshold is invented. Rule 6 is an unresolved fuzzy match or a score of at least 70, and the current score is 72 and 12 minutes old. That stale score is never used.

Action F’s stated benefit, “eliminates compliance risk,” is not a gain relative to Ranks 1–3, which already avoid unlawful execution. With conformant approval still at 0.55–0.75, rejecting now is more likely to be a false rejection than a justified one. The €80,000 top of customer harm versus the €100,000 floor of unlawful-execution harm, plus non-monetary consequences, is the dominance argument that should retire A and B. It is only gestured at. Observational predictions may not transport under intervention; that limitation is never discussed. Suspicious Activity Report, hold-versus-execution, and non-disclosure to the customer never appear, despite report being a required outcome.

Section 4 does not map every action. C, D, and E are missing. Action A is tied to Rule 10 rather than to Rules 6 and 9: skipping review is not a failed override. What would make F or G valid is left as “threshold” and “justified,” without the documented rejection criterion, an actual director authorization, a real incident identifier, or a warning against a pretextual incident. Confidence “Low” on A and B reads as confidence in the action rather than confidence in the ranking. Those two rows are the most certain part of the table.

The normative ordering is sound enough that the answer is not a failure. The plan that is actually recommended is slower than the answer’s own second option, timed with two incompatible review models, and over-confident relative to the evidence.
