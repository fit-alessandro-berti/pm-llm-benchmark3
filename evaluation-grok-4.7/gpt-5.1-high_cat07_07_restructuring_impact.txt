6.5

The write-up covers every required section, keeps East distinct from North and South, and correctly refuses a full rollout. It is not a reliable forecast: several central quantitative claims conflict with the tables given in the scenario, and the measurement plan would misread the log.

The mechanism map says total handovers per case may fall slightly. Summing the listed handover types gives about 343 per 100 cases now and about 359 under management’s own post-change table. Specialist edges rise (36/31 to 41/36), approval edges rise (42/39 to 49/43), and legal/risk edges rise (9 to 12). Only cross-team cover and intake-to-caseworker edges fall. Section 2.2 then says nominal handovers decrease, which repeats the same error. The real structural change is substitution toward central edges plus a higher approval load, not fewer handovers.

Two capacity shocks are left unquantified. Legal and risk are already at 93% and 91% utilization while referral frequency rises by about a third; a 10.5-hour median wait is not feasible on that pool unless technical staff absorb the extra work, which is the bottleneck staff already flagged. Approval volume rises about 17% while one of three supervisors is removed. The optimistic path still treats a 4.0-hour technical wait and a 3.0-hour approval wait as compatible with flat North/South performance. Those waits are already higher than today’s 3.2-hour and 2.4-hour medians, so “flat” and “close to the management estimates” cannot both be true. Calling the mixed case “most likely” is an undefended prior given these load increases.

Cycle time is also on the wrong scale. The extra handover waits are on the order of an hour per case; median cycle time is about 7–9 days and the 90th percentile is 16–23 days. A 5–10% cycle-time improvement cannot come from those waits. It would have to come from rework and processing time, which the scenarios assert without using the handover-specific rework rates (9%, 8%, 7%, 14%, 18%) or team rework rates (8.2%, 9.0%, 11.5%).

The handover-of-work network is a resource-to-resource graph. Pooling N_Tech, S_Tech, and E_Tech leaves three resource ids unless they are explicitly relabeled, and North caseworkers handing work to S_Tech or E_Tech will increase cross-location resource edges even if absence-cover handovers fall. The map treats “Technical Shared Service” as a network node and reads centralization off in-degree. That requires an activity performance DFG, or a resource mapping the sketch never builds. Utilization is likewise misstated as the share of events done by specialists, which is volume, not busy time over available time.

The pilot does not identify the treatment. Routing East plus a high/medium-complexity subset of North and South into the shared model, then using “the rest of North/South” as a difference-in-differences control, confounds treatment with complexity and with East’s already worse baseline (8.6 days, 11.5% rework, 82% E_Tech utilization, 3.8/5 satisfaction). Merging the embedded specialists also changes the control cases, because the old model needs N_Tech and S_Tech. Guardrails stay at “agreed thresholds” instead of the given baselines. Rollback ignores that local skill lost during a 3–6 month pilot is not restored by switching the routing rule back. `priority` never appears, even though shared approval is defined for medium/high-value cases; “vulnerable” is not a value supplied in the log.

The indicator split is useful, but the 90th-percentile cycle time cannot be a days/weeks leading indicator when the current 90th percentile is 16–23 days. Completed-case percentiles in a short window are right-censored toward fast cases. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` returns case-level service and waiting totals, not the wait from caseworker to shared service; that wait belongs on a performance DFG. In-queue ageing is the measure that actually works on open cases.

The code sketch names the right family of techniques and then will not run as comparison logic. `format_dataframe` is given a `resource_key` argument it does not take. The SNA discovery calls are unpacked into `(net, metrics)` although each returns one SNA object. `pm4py.get_case_duration` is not the API (`get_all_case_durations` is). Event-level filters on `team` or `case_complexity` drop events rather than keep whole cases, so post-change traces lose shared-service events and cycle time is biased in the direction of the policy being tested. Pairwise waits, SLA misses, edge-weight diffs, and utilization are comments rather than calculations. Security is in the routing rule and missing from the segment filters.

Section 4 and the recommendation are the strongest parts: tails, local knowledge, learning, accountability, and exception handling are separated from the mean, and early average cycle time is correctly treated as non-decisive. Those judgments sit on top of a forecast that never checks the proposal against its own frequencies and utilizations.
