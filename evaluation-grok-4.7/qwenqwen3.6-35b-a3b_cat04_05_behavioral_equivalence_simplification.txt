The final write-up keeps the required outline and a few sound methodological caveats (observation-relative equivalence, bounded checks are not a proof, equal footprints or a small EMD do not yield trace equivalence). Those points do not rescue the comparison: the models themselves are misread, and the decisive claims are false.

`+` is concurrency and `X` is exclusive choice. That is required by the statement that Model B runs one check concurrently with the revision loop, and by optional approval as `X('Approve Revision', tau)`. The verdicts instead treat `+` as a global or per-iteration XOR and explicitly dismiss concurrency. Under that reading, every later conclusion is about different languages.

Correct behavior is the opposite of the structural table. Model A’s loop body is `(Analyze Revision ∥ Check Revision)` followed by optional approval, then optional `Rework Case`. Each iteration therefore has exactly one analysis and exactly one check, and approval cannot start until both have finished. Model B places exactly one `Check Revision` in parallel with the whole loop; each iteration is `Analyze Revision` then optional approval. Neither model can skip analysis or the check, and neither can fire `Rework Case` without another loop body afterward. The table’s claims of zero checks, a check-only path, and mutual exclusion of analysis and check are all false.

The verdicts follow that mistake. P1 is correctly labeled not equivalent, but for the wrong reason (pairing fails in B because there is one shared check, not because check and analysis cannot co-occur). P2 is wrong: after hiding approval and `tau`, collapsing checks to a Boolean, and keeping only the analysis count and whether any rework occurred, both models realize the same observations (at least one analysis, a check always occurs, rework occurs exactly when there is more than one analysis). P3 is also wrong. Short complete traces already separate the visible languages inside the stated bounds, so the bounded verdict is not equivalent, not “equivalent only under an abstraction.”

The four traces do not repair this. None is admitted by the model the table marks as admitted:

- `Open, Analyze, Rework, Check, Close` and `Open, Check, Rework, Analyze, Close` put `Rework Case` where the loop cannot fire it, and Model A cannot leave an iteration without both analysis and check.
- `Open, Analyze, Rework, Analyze, Close` and `Open, Check, Close` omit a mandatory check or a mandatory analysis.

There is therefore no valid witness in either direction, no two-analysis counterexample, and no proved inclusion to excuse that. Real separators are immediate: A admits `Open, Analyze, Check, Rework, Analyze, Check, Close` and rejects a single trailing check; B admits `Open, Analyze, Approve, Check, Close` and `Open, Analyze, Rework, Analyze, Check, Close`, which A rejects because approval is sequenced after that iteration’s check and every iteration needs its own check before rework.

The Python does not implement the requested check. The trees are rewritten with an illegal `@(` wrapper, so they are not the given strings. Enabling, firing, and final-marking tests use non-APIs instead of `get_enabled_transitions` and explicit marking equality. Silent steps, visible bounds, and repeated markings are named, but the exploration would not run. Complexity figures are presented as exact and are wrong: both trees have 12 nodes, 6 visible leaves, and 1 silent leaf, not 11 vs 10 and 6 vs 5 visible leaves. The simplicity comparison is qualitative and unexplained. The simplified tree is a reasonable stricter P1 candidate (analysis then check, then optional approval, per iteration), but the code is again unparsable, simplicity is not compared with both originals, and the negative trace `Open, Check, Analyze, Close` is legal in true Model A. The recommendation that A allows unpaired checks and that neither original can produce both an analysis and a check is false, so the replacement advice is not supported.
