The answer is structurally complete and causally mature: it separates availability from intensity, rejects a single “X%” claim, treats median and p90 separately, names interference and treatment-version problems, and chooses an embedded evaluation rather than immediate expansion. That judgment is right. It is not nearly flawless, and the quantitative core is not trustworthy enough for a high score.

Section 5 does not match its own windows. With Jan–Mar versus Apr–May, Alpha’s median change is 0.0 hours, not about +0.3; p90 is about +6.5, not +7.1; escalation is about +2.6, not +2.3. Jul–Aug versus Jan–Mar gives Alpha p90 about +6.9, not +5.1, and escalation about +3.1, not +2.8. Beta’s later escalation is about +2.6, not +2.3. Some of those figures only appear if February is dropped, so the arithmetic is inconsistent across columns. June, Alpha’s worst month (median 53.5, p90 119.9), is in neither post window, which softens the early-adopter tail result even though the prose mentions mid-post deterioration.

The intensity comparison is mislabeled. “auto_fast ≥40 (Jun–Aug) versus the first adoption month” is not what is shown. Alpha’s June share is 39, and 53.5→43.3 / 119.9→101.2 is June to August, not April (48.3 / 97.3) versus high-intensity months. The Gamma-versus-control contrast is only a verbal sketch; the actual median difference-in-differences is about −4 hours and the p90 contrast about −8 hours. Calling [−8, +3] hours a pooled ITT interval overstates a mix of uncontrolled scenarios.

The code meets the checklist and is properly caveated, but Model C is not a valid event study: Gamma’s January–March leads (relative times −6 to −4) are coded as the omitted reference, and calendar month effects are dropped despite the May shock being the main threat. The primary Sun–Abraham / cohort estimator is never fit; the reported panel model is the naïve two-way fixed-effects specification the text itself disclaims. Arrivals and FTE are used as adjustments without a bad-control discussion, and E4–E5 collapse availability and intensity into one contrast.

Section 1 is appropriately non-causal except for the parenthetical that the March fraud desk raised escalation. The rollout design is the right policy, but it offers three assignment schemes at once and runs long of the 250–400 word limit. The conditionally causal bullet both states a near-zero-to-modest median effect and says the conditions are not credible.
