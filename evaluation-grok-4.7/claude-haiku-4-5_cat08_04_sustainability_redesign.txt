4.7

The answer covers every required section, stays attached to the named paths and policies, and tries to separate justified emergency transport from slack-rich stock shipments. That structure is not enough to carry the analysis. Core quantities contradict the log, contradict each other, and are then added into decision-scale totals.

The modal baseline is wrong. Ship-air is 1,420 of 12,000 orders, about 11.8%, and dedicated van is 620 orders, about 5.2%. The indicator set treats air as 5.8% and dedicated van as 5.1%, mixing the 5% stock-to-standard-to-air path share with the network modal share. S4 then applies that 5.8% to Standard-stock Service-contract and gets about 110 air orders. S1, for the same segment, uses a 22% air share. The 22% figure is the SP1 violation rate, not an observed air share, and the same paragraph multiplies a 3 kg saving by 6,000 orders. Those are different populations. The resulting transport opportunity is alternately about 1.65 t, 3–5 t, and 18 t for one overlapping change.

Other denominators break the same way. Pick-to-standard-pack (6,040) plus consolidated truck (850) double-counts, because the 850 trucks already leave from standard pack. Dedicated-van volume is stated as both 620 orders and 8% of orders; 8% is 960 orders and is the critical emergency path share, not the van count. S8’s “50–60% of vans” is then calculated on 200–250 orders. Cancel-due-to-delay is 530 cases, about 4.4%, so non-cancellation is about 95.6%, not the stated 96.6%. Weighted made-to-order energy is about 19.0 kWh, not 19.3. Inspection to rework production is 470 events, about 4% of all orders and roughly 13% of inspection entries, not 4% of inspected orders. Packaging rework is 620 events, later 650, and the avoided material is both 5–6 kg and 0.6 kg per event.

Overlapping options are summed as if independent. S1, S4, S5, and S12 all claim credit for the same stock air-to-ground move. S8 and S9 both claim the dedicated-van reduction. S10’s green path reuses the batching and consolidation already counted in S2 and S5. Section 4 totals 41–65 tCO2e, section 6 totals 30–40 t, and section 7’s monthly baseline of 4.5–5.5 t with a 25–35% cut implies roughly 13–23 t/year. Segment averages imply about 123 t of transport across the 12,000 orders, so the 50–65 t baseline is also low. The 12,000-order extract is never shown to be one year, yet the results are reported as annual tons.

S10 is not available at the stated scale. The immediate-CNC-plus-air path is 6% of orders, about 720 cases, with 24 kWh and 23 kgCO2e. Made-to-order Regular as a whole already averages 18.5 kWh and 8.0 kg, close to the batched ground path. Shifting 700–1,300 Regular orders off the fast path, saving 8 kWh and 17 kg each, treats a small variant as if it were a third to half of the segment. The offered 120-hour promise sits on a path whose median is 118 hours and whose late rate is 21%, with segment p90 at 180 hours. That promise recreates the lateness it is meant to negotiate away.

The Service-contract stock recommendation has the same service problem. Air is about 24 hours faster than ground. Moving a segment whose median is 18 hours and p90 is 41 hours to a 36-hour median places the new center on today’s tail. “Within historical variation” does not preserve a 5% late rate. SP1’s rule is a probability threshold above 85%, not permission to move 85% of the segment. The evidenced unjustified subset is the 22% SP1 violation, and only after slack is checked order by order. S4 also assumes a 2–4 hour consolidation wait while S5 assumes 12–24 hours for the same truck product.

Several mechanisms invent the quantities that drive the recommendation. The 60–70% true-downtime split, the 20% downtime-probability cutoff, 2.5 kWh per rework cycle, 0.3 kg baseline packaging, an 8-hour rework labor time, and a 20:1 transport-to-packaging dominance ratio are not in the attributes. Per-item CNC energy is interpolated and then mixed with per-order energy. Risk 4 writes 16 kWh plus 23 kgCO2e as equal to 4,600 kgCO2e. The boundary includes 11,200 closed cases and then analyzes 12,000; post-delivery returns are outside the boundary and later used as an in-process quality KPI. The low precision of the discovered model, explicitly given because emergency and partial paths tangle during disruptions, never widens the effect ranges.

The indicator targets fight the later estimates: overall energy is targeted down 15–20%, while the package claims 2–4%. Conformance is summarized as 78–69% compliant by subtracting the 22% and 31% findings from 100, even though those findings have different denominators. The effect table mixes t/year, percentages, kilograms, hours, and percentage points, and several direction markers do not say whether the metric rises or falls. Phase 4 accepts stock lateness of 8% after section 2 set the stock Regular ceiling at 7%.

Rebound coverage is broad, and batching, obsolescence, modal shift, rework drift, queue displacement, customer response, and logging avoidance are real process risks. Packaging is not given a distinct displacement path despite reinforced packs doubling material. Risk 1 analyzes longer batch waits, while S2 is a wait cap whose direct rebound is smaller batches and higher energy per item. The proposed fix of reserving 2–3% of batch output for samples creates scrap in order to manage scrap. Risk 5 says not to stack batch wait and consolidation on made-to-order work; S10 and phase 3 do that stack anyway.

The phased sequence is the soundest part: conformance and packaging compatibility first, machine and inspection quality next, slack-gated mode shift after that, and true downtime left on a fast route. The package does not survive its own numbers. A distributor following the stated tonnage, the 85% Service-contract shift, or the 120-hour green promise would be acting on inconsistent evidence and an understated service risk.
