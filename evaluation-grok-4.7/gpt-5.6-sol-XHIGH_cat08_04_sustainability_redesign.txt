7.5

The answer is strong on boundary-setting, path grounding, rebound control, and governance, but it is not nearly flawless. The effects table, which is a required product, does not reliably state direction, and a few effect claims conflict with or outrun the log.

**What holds up.** The order-to-delivery boundary correctly keeps kWh, scrap, packaging, all shipment legs, rework, obsolescence, post-close returns, and cancellation displacement in scope, and it refuses an unsupported single score and a made-up grid factor. Indicators are tied to real attributes and paths and are split by segment, route, machine, and disruption. The hotspot arithmetic is right: made-to-order is about 34% of orders and about 87% of logged energy and 85% of scrap; critical-downtime is 11% of orders and about 41% of transport kgCO2e. Air and dedicated vans are treated as slack- and segment-dependent, SP1/SP2/Q1 are operationalized, and R1 alternatives are preserved. Twelve options cover mechanism, service/quality/resilience, and monitoring evidence. Rebound coverage is concrete across batching, obsolescence, mode shift, packaging, escaped defects, inspection queues, machine concentration, downtime misclassification, customer behavior, inventory, and metric gaming. The package is sequenced so quality and rework are fixed before logistics are loosened, and validation correctly separates intention-to-treat from conformance and original requested dates from renegotiated promises.

**Defects that block a high score.**

- Section 4’s legend is empty (` means lower/higher/little change), and many cells are blank or reduced to `****`. Direction is the point of that table. Prose saves some rows; it does not save the table.
- Selective critical stock is marked as a cycle-time and late-delivery improvement with no caveat. The only supporting path is slower and worse on service than emergency van fulfillment: 23h and 9% late versus 14h and 3% late, at much lower energy and transport. Calling that a service gain contradicts the log. Material/obsolescence risk is acknowledged; the service columns are not.
- Dynamic batching presents the CNC_A gradient (2.8 kWh/item at batch 1–3 versus 1.3 at 12–20) as if it were the intervention effect. Most planned work already waits for a batch (3020 of 3780). Due-date caps release smaller batches, so kWh per item can rise relative to today’s large batches while wait, lateness, and the 6% 14-day non-shipment rate fall. The cross-section is not the treatment effect.
- “Up to about 24h slower” misstates the evidence. Air cuts median transport time by about 24h; 24h is a median difference, not a ceiling. Stock cycle time only moves from 17h to 34h, so transport time and order cycle time are also blended.
- The 1,010 partial-shipment events are tied to the 7% backorder path. Those are not the same set: 790 partials go straight to standard packaging, and only 220 enter backorder wait.
- Heat-treatment waiting has a 1.7× late association whenever cases wait, not only “if caps fail.” The showcased CNC_A path includes heat treatment; the bad CNC_B path does not. Like-for-like routing is mentioned, but part-driven heat treatment is not treated as a constraint on S3.
- Section 1 says not to collapse criteria into one score; section 6 then says to minimize “expected end-to-end burden” across kWh, kgCO2e, scrap, packaging, returns, and obsolescence without weights or a dominance rule.

Those are interpretation and communication failures inside an otherwise careful redesign, not generic sustainability advice. They are enough to keep this well below a near-flawless mark.
