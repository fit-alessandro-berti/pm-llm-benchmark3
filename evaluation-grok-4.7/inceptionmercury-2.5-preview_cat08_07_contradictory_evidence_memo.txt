5.5

The memo reaches a defensible family of decisions (hybrid E, neither full national scale nor full rollback) and includes every required section, but it is not rigorous enough for a high score. Several errors are material to the judging criteria, not cosmetic.

The fitness/precision explanation is conceptually wrong. High fitness does not mean cases follow the simplified coordinator path; it means the discovered model can replay observed behavior. Low precision means the model allows many traces that were not observed. “Billing start before activation” is a frequent move-on-log (observed behavior the reference does not expect), so it cannot illustrate low precision. Generalization (0.70 to 0.56), temporal-profile deviations, and the handover-of-work versus working-together distinction are largely unused.

The median/tail account is only partly right. Apartments improved on the median (−2 days) while their p90 worsened (+12 days), so the split is not only between simple houses and everyone else. The pilot mix (more apartments and older buildings), incomplete segment and risk attributes before week 3, truncated cost data, and sparse start timestamps are never treated as threats to the comparison, yet the memo still recommends national action.

Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are labeled separately, but the table is the minimum eight rows and the assumptions are poor. “Estimated costs are accurate” is muddled with the fact that post-week-4 complaint cost is missing. “Coordinator bottlenecks would resolve if volume increased” is the opposite of 93% utilization and is not an assumption the decision depends on. Survey skipping, missing permit checks (move-on-model), and post-billing activation are repeatedly collapsed into one cause despite different evidence.

Option C is misread: C suspends survey skipping and post-billing activation, so “does not fix survey/billing errors” is not its risk. Option D is reduced to a delay used only when cost data are missing, rather than the response to confounding and non-random regions. The memo says deploy Redesign R for simple low-risk houses; the final section scales only coordinator ownership. Those are different packages. Simple-house speed is tied in the temporal profile to merged validation and scheduling, which is not what gets scaled. Immediate national coordinator rollout is also ungated on absence resilience, even though stalls when coordinators are absent are already in the evidence and second-visit utilization rose from 66% to 92%.

Value of information is incomplete. M1, the randomized skip-rule test by risk stratum, is absent. That is the measurement that would most directly test the causal claim behind suspending the skip. M5 is ranked last even though the recommendation scales the ownership model whose resilience is unproven. M8 is dismissed as PR despite the complaint shift from waiting to “installed but not working” and billing before activation. M3 is undervalued: reason codes could show whether rework is skip error, permits, customer reschedule, or coordinator delay, which changes C versus E versus rollback. The 90% rule-precision gate is arbitrary and the wrong operating characteristic (the decision needs the false-skip rate). Requiring every segment’s complaint rate to sit at the aggregate baseline of 6.1% misapplies a portfolio number to segments whose baselines are unknown. Tail, first-time-right, SLA, permit conformance, and utilization never appear as rollout gates.

The visible thresholds are also syntactically incomplete (“cost is Baseline cost”; “remains 6.1%”), so the decision rule is not operational as written.
