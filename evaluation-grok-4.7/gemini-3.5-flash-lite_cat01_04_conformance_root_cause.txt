The write-up keeps the required eight-part shape and the right broad story: June’s fitness drop is mostly relabeling, `Queue Routed`, and ingestion-time order, August adds an unmodeled sign-off, and staff blame is not the first explanation. Those merits are outweighed by factual mistakes, broken causal links, over-claimed certainty, and a code section that misses its specification.

The evidence ledger has the right columns and twelve rows, but several rows point the wrong way. The appeal-rate row both supports and weakens employee noncompliance. The 40-case UI audit is treated as evidence against noncompliance, although it only checks a screen label. The August policy is treated as weakening the label hypothesis, and the rise in events per case from 5.4 to 6.8 is treated as weakening model obsolescence. That rise splits: June–July is mostly one extra technical event, while August–September also contains 210 sign-offs. Reliability scores of 100 sit next to unresolved dependencies, and “code commits” and “deployment logs” are not in the record. The 11-of-70 statement reverses the given audit: 70 June–July traces have `Notify` before `Decision`, and screenshots cover 11 of those 70. Missing-check counts are called stable, which erases the April–May baseline of 180 enhanced reviews, all with a compliance check. No row uses the DFG counts the question required the ledger to span. `Standard Review -> Supervisor Sign-off` (60) and the jump in events missing a resource (1.2% to 6.4%) are unused.

The ranking order for the measured drop is sensible, and the scores are not. Posteriors of 99, 98, 95, and 90 ignore the listed residuals and the staged costs: label mapping alone reaches 58%, removing `Queue Routed` reaches 81%, and source order reaches 89% only in June–July. The same preprocessing leaves August–September at 66%. “The real process changed” at 15 cannot sit beside “obsolete model” at 90, because the 1 August rule is a real process change whose conformance effect is an outdated tree. The July memo is counted as support for noncompliance even though it reports no known increase in violations. The table never separates cause of the fitness collapse from a remaining operational concern.

The adjacency list includes the named nodes, then wires them incorrectly. `true_process -> normative_model` reverses the failure to update the tree. `reviewer_behavior -> recorded_labels` assigns a UI rename to reviewers. The gateway that introduced `Queue Routed` is absent, as are hidden variables for the EUR 50,000 attribute, missing compliance events, and incomplete detection. The prose says the August policy occurred concurrently with the June logging changes and that stable outcomes prove the operation did not change. The question asks for the opposite reading: unchanged approval, cycle time, and appeals neither prove a pure data error nor exclude noncompliance. “Entirely a product of logging” also conflicts with the later 70% compliance-risk claim and with section 2’s 30 posterior. Calling the UI release a failure to edit the process tree further confuses a semantic map with model obsolescence.

Section 4 has eight rows and recovers the published 58, 81, 89, and 86 figures, but those are restatements, not new predictions. One technical-event row actually reverts timestamps. The low-value stratum is predicted to reach 100% fitness without saying that overrides, missing checks, and reversed pairs remain. The sign-off falsifier does not follow from the hypothesis. Discovery fitness is offered as a test of real change, which is the overpermissive-model trap.

The four-phase program names labels, sequence order, DFGs, stratification, and provenance, yet it does not isolate move-on-log versus move-on-model, never uses precision, and gives no real PM4Py method for several phases. It also omits a manual audit of the enhanced-review traces that still lack a compliance check.

The code parses a tree and converts it, and the optional sign-off is in a plausible position, with a printed EUR 50,000 caveat. It does not call `pm4py.parse_event_log_string` or `pm4py.discover_dfg`. Unpacking `dfg_discovery.apply` as a triple mismatches the low-level return. `pm4py.conformance_eval_alignments` is not a PM4Py function. Alignment results and the DFG are discarded, precision is never computed, source ordering is absent, and the candidate model is not compared with the normative net on the same August log. The script is not a coherent executable reproduction.

Section 7 is inside the length band and gives useful actions, but it calls the evidence unambiguous, treats 89% as the restored fitness, and says stable outcomes confirm normal operation. That 89% is June–July only. The 95% and 70% confidences conflict with section 2 and with the unaudited missing checks. Section 8 is the cleanest part: six rules cover versioning, semantic contracts, technical events, timestamp provenance, data-aware guards, and post-release checks, still without pinning a model version to each reporting period.
