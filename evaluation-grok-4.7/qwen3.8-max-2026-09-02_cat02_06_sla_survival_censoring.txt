The final document is a strong, process-aware survival analysis: time origin and the 72-hour horizon are correct, right censoring is separated from competing events and from already-observed breaches, and the primary estimands are usable. E1 as \(1-\mathrm{CIF}_R(72)\) and E2 as internal occupancy at 72 hours are well matched to SLA reporting versus workload. The case arithmetic checks: 13 resolved cases, 9 resolved by 72 hours, 4 resolved later, 18 known non-resolutions, 5 open cases younger than 72 hours, and breach bounds \([18/32,(18+5)/32]\). Transfers are given three coherent treatments, completed-case bias is illustrated with real IDs, and the recommended Aalen–Johansen primary analysis plus landmark/occupancy secondary analysis fits \(n=32\) and missing intermediate times. The state diagram uses only observed transitions, immortal-time leakage from `Escalate`/`Rework` is handled correctly, and the single code block encodes both estimands, four transfer scenarios, open cases, stratified CIF/KM, and a PM4Py-compatible variant extraction without invented timestamps.

The main defect is Estimand E3. \(P(T_R>72\mid\text{still not closed before 72})\) is tautological: anyone still open at 72 hours already has \(T_R>72\). The quantity needed for recovery is \(P(\text{eventual Resolve}\mid\text{open at 72})\), i.e. the inequality and conditioning are reversed. The later examples treat late resolution correctly, but the stated formula is not corrected. Smaller slips remain: cohort B noncompliance omits `S17` (`Screen` before `AutoCheck`); best-case external resolution cannot rescue `S32` (transfer at 96 hours) for a 72-hour endpoint; and “censoring removes transfers from the risk set” ignores that they stay at risk until the transfer time. Those keep the answer short of nearly flawless.
