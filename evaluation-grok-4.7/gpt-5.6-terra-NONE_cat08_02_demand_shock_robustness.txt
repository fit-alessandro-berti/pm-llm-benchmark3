7.2

The redesign is structurally complete and directionally sound: it separates mix, machine, validation, and courier stress; rejects unlimited Urgent-first, permanent M1/M2 segregation, and blanket larger batches; and it ties most actions to a downstream overload. It still has quantitative and design flaws that keep it well short of nearly flawless.

The batch-wait comparison is misstated. `Test preparation -> Batch wait` is parallel to `Test preparation -> Assay run`, not a subset of it. The relevant shares are 760/(16550+760) ≈ 4.4% normally and 1180/(8140+1180) ≈ 12.7% in peak, not 760/16550 and 1180/8140. The conclusion that the share rises survives, but the cited ratios are wrong. “1,260 out of 8,500-ish assay entries” also uses the wrong base: 7240+1260 = 8500 is assay exits; peak assay entries are 8140+1030+1080 = 10250. That inflates the repeat share from about 12% to about 15%.

Unequal windows are not handled consistently. Peak accession correction is 420 in three weeks versus 500 in eight, about 2.2× the weekly rate, so “only 420” understates the surge. The same problem affects raw repeat and hold counts. The strongest direct-follows-graph evidence is left unquantified: technical-validation exits to medical validation rise from about 25% to about 42%, and specialist holds rise from about 1.8% to about 7.5% of those exits (roughly 5.6× weekly). Those shares explain the medical and specialist queues better than utilization alone.

Flow balance is ignored where it complicates the story. In peak, assay inflow exceeds outflow by about 1,750, while technical validation shows 8080 exits against 7240 entries. Scenario E’s claim that technical validation simply accumulates after assay is therefore only partly consistent with the log. Normal Urgent p90 is already 19 hours against an 18-hour contract, so the tail breach is not only a peak phenomenon. Overall rework of 7.5% and 11.8% is also used as the threshold for pre-analytical defect/recollection rate; those are not the same metric.

The centerpiece controls are under-specified. Lane WIP caps and protected slot shares never get even an illustrative split, while later triggers are given to the hour. Repeat-after-failure, the worst cycle-time class (peak median 145 hours, p90 240), is placed in the Routine lane despite consuming assay capacity twice and clustering on M2 and night shifts. A two-hour defect-triage promise is not reconciled with a pre-analytical station already at 96% utilization and a 9.5-hour median queue; pulling triage forward would delay good samples, and that within-station tradeoff is missing. The clerk error rise after 9-hour shifts is unused, and M2’s faster runtime is barely weighed against its failure rate.

The dashboard does not meet the “few” / minimum requirement. Fourteen leading indicators, several with unanchored cut-points (6 hours of Urgent slack, 12% repeats, 130% of plan, 90 minutes late), is a full control room. Red bands set at observed peak medians are recovery alarms; that is acknowledged at the end, but the core package still depends on those alarms plus unnamed caps.

Migration reasoning and constraint discipline are otherwise strong: I1, I3, I4, I5, I8, and I10 are all given a next bottleneck, and the redesign-caused risks (borrowed technical validators, fragmented protected slots, automation shifting defects, partner traceability) are real. The grade is held down by wrong ratios, missed flow accounting, an infeasible triage target, and the mis-placement of the worst case class.
