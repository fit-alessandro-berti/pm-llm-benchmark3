The write-up keeps the required seven-section shape, the four disruption types, and a complete trade-off table, and it correctly leaves blanket urgent-first dispatch, larger routine batches, and delayed routine release out of the standing design. The reasoning underneath is not reliable enough to treat the package as a robust redesign.

The scenarios are labels with a sentence of impact, not operating cases. They do not use the measured mix shift (urgent 18% to 29%, complex-panel 13% to 20%, weekly volume about 2,300 to 3,300), the urgent 18-hour penalty against a peak p90 of 33 hours, or the routine 72-hour target against a peak p90 of 120 hours. Scenario 1 invents “stricter defect tolerance.” Scenario 4 turns registration (peak utilization 85%, median queue 2.8 hours) into the crisis and adds “dropped samples,” while courier intake is already at 7.5 hours and pre-analytical check at 9.5 hours, with arrivals spiked at 10:00 and 17:00. Medical-validator loss and the known every-second-Friday specialist gap are collapsed into one case, so their different queues (22 hours versus 36 hours) never drive different responses. No scenario stacks the winter combination the process actually faces: mix shift plus absence plus courier disruption.

Bottleneck migration reverses the event log in several places. Under observed stress, urgent batch-wait bypass falls from 74% to 58%, and the batch-wait branch rises from about 4% to about 13% of preparation exits. The outage scenario predicts the opposite, more bypass. Repeat-assay outcomes rise from about 6.7% to about 14.8% and are tied in the evidence to M2 and night shifts. Scenario 1 instead makes medical-validation delay flow backward and create those repeats. The M1-dedication risk then says pressure on M1 raises repeats. The given conflict is the reverse: putting routine volume on higher-failure M2 raises repeats. Smoothing courier arrivals is treated as something that overloads downstream stations. Smoothing cuts arrival variance. The intervention that pushes load downstream is registration automation, which removes a throttle in front of a pre-analytical station at 96% and assay machines at 98%.

The “robust” set does not match those constraints. Technical validation is already at 101% with a 16-hour queue, and cross-training takes four weeks, longer than the three-week peak. Moving two technical validators into accession correction is a standing capacity loss at the overfull station, not a temporary training dip, yet it is sold as robust. Evening medical validation is a real peak lever and release is short (median edge 2 hours to 5 hours), but the write-up never names that downstream station. Courier smoothing is reasonable. Automating registration is given high scenario coverage even though registration is the slack station in three of the four scenarios, and the write-up misses the actual quality path: recollection requests roughly double, the recollection-to-receive edge is 42 hours then 60 hours, and about 42% of peak recollection paths cancel. Outsourcing complex-panel overflow can unload assay and specialist review, but the stated harm, “delay the non-outsourced cases,” runs the wrong direction. The two largest capacity leaks, recollection and repeat assay, stay out of the core package. There is no case-class rule with a cap, so urgent protection and complex-panel protection are never designed as the conflict the brief requires.

The conditional policies are the weakest operational piece. The assay policy triggers on M2 failure and then routes routine work to M2. Its other trigger, sustained assay utilization above 90%, is already true across the peak (98%), so M1 dedication would stay on for the whole wave and reproduce the starvation result the brief warns about. A medical-validation median above 24 hours and a specialist median above 24 hours are late: the peak already sits near 22 hours and 36 hours, and an urgent order has an 18-hour contractual limit. Registration utilization above 85% is the peak average, so that switch is also on for the entire peak. Policies have no off conditions despite the request for on and off triggers. Early defect triage, the intervention aimed at the 60-hour recollection loop, appears only as a side action.

The table’s columns are present and its cells are mostly unearned labels. Nothing in the table is allowed to hurt urgent or routine work. Cross-training is marked as reducing rework and helping urgent flow while it consumes an over-capacity technical-validation pool. Registration automation is “high coverage.” Monitoring signals such as a specialist queue above 24 hours would be red for the whole observed peak, so they cannot time a switch.

Residual risk covers an M1 outage, staffing, and partner-lab traceability. The traceability point is a real self-inflicted exposure. Missing are the redesign’s own likely failures: technical validation going further past 100% because of cross-training, routine work locked onto M2 and generating repeats, and a regional partner being saturated in the same winter wave. The package is never checked against whether urgent tails can still clear 18 hours once assay (18-hour queue), technical validation (16 hours), and medical validation (22 hours) sit in series.

The dashboard mixes thresholds that fire continuously through the peak with registration queue thresholds (above 8 hours) the station does not exhibit. It omits the indicators that move first in this log: urgent-plus-complex share, urgent age before assay, batch-wait age, urgent bypass rate, repeat rate by machine and shift, recollection rate, and accession-correction queue. Queue length is listed as both a leading indicator and a lagging outcome. Weekly cycle time is too slow to police an 18-hour penalty.

Format compliance and a few directionally sound choices (evening medical coverage, courier smoothing, skepticism about global urgent-first rules) keep this above a failed submission. The causal reversals, the M2 routing contradiction, the unused frequency and performance figures, and triggers that cannot turn on and off make the redesign unsafe to implement as written.
