8.0

The delay decomposition is conceptually right and numerically reliable. Upstream time, branch queue, service, synchronization slack, fork–join elapsed time, downstream delay, final service, and total flow time are defined with case and branch indices, and the identity \(T_c=U_c+FJ_c+DQ_c+FS_c\) is the correct non-additive-safe split. The double-counting argument is exact: each branch’s queue + service + sync spans the same interval \([t^{fork}, J]\), so summing those triples yields \(3\,FJ\). All 12 case rows, the sync table, and the worked cases F02, F04, F07, and F11 match the timestamps. Critical-branch counts (A 2, B 8, C 2), means, maxima, the 132-minute extension above A’s 50-minute floor (B 120, C 12), and the frozen-downstream savings (A: 1.25 min/case; B queue: 7.71) are correct. The code’s tie-break, interval log, `format_dataframe`, and performance DFG, plus the comment that directly-follows performance does not recover max-join slack, meet the reproducibility requirements. Ranking B over A, despite A’s larger service time, is the right conclusion.

The score is held down by interpretive and completeness problems, not by arithmetic.

The phrase “only cases where A is critical (or near-critical)” misstates the fork–join rule the rest of the answer depends on. With positive slack, speeding A cannot move `join_ready`; near-critical cases have zero direct benefit. Only F01 and F11 benefit, and that part of the case list is right. F01’s saving is 5 of the 10 minutes removed, because B becomes binding at 08:55; that partial capture is implied but not stated.

The B-queue replay is one-sided. The mean saving rising from 7.71 to 10.17 is real under the stated model, and F11’s 15-minute cascade gain is real, but F06’s saving falls from 15 to 8 because the earlier join creates a new blocking wait behind F04. The prose then warns that cutting B’s queue may increase final-station congestion without reconciling that warning with an average that moves the other way or with this case-level masking. Section 3.3’s general “can mask” claim is therefore left disconnected from the counterfactual the answer actually computed.

The 6.08 / 8.58 split of downstream delay is reported too precisely. It exists only after fixing resource assignment and order, declaring the first job on each resource to have zero capacity wait, and holding residual lag constant when the schedule moves. That initialization rule is in a code comment, not next to the figures used in §4.3 and §5.2. Those figures are assumptions, and the recommendation that capacity is worth about 6 minutes while policy lag is worth about 8.6 treats them as a measurement. Section 7 does limit the claim; the earlier sections still over-weight it. The same model misses a concrete pooling fact in the log: F2 is idle at 11:30–11:40 while F11 is join-ready and waiting on F1, so part of the “20-minute capacity wait” may be assignment rather than lack of a server.

Section 5 does not cover the discussion list it was given. Flow-time tail, new bottlenecks, synchronization, and pilots are there. Throughput is absent, and final-station utilization is never computed even though both resources and a closed observation window are in the data. Calling A’s service “deterministic” and a hard floor overstates a constant in a 12-case, one-day sample. No sampling uncertainty is attached to 8/12.

So the fork–join accounting, case table, and main counterfactual arithmetic are strong enough to carry the answer well above the middle of the scale. The remaining gaps are misleading wording around noncritical speedups, an unreconciled congestion story, an under-labeled downstream split, and missing required redesign dimensions.
