6.5

The memo reaches the right family of decisions (hybrid E, not national A or blanket B) and covers every required section with mostly accurate baseline numbers, a real facts/interpretations/assumptions split, and coherent accounts of the five metric conflicts. It is not nearly flawless: several errors change the business case, the portfolio math, and the information value of the next step.

The executive recommendation treats the aggregate €23 visible-cost drop (EUR 184 to 161) as a proven simple-house saving and a reason to scale Track 1. That figure is portfolio-level, the pilot mix is harder than baseline, and estimated full cost rose to EUR 207 on incomplete complaint data. “Proven financial gains” on one-third of volume is therefore an interpretation stated as a fact. The same section assigns 55% to apartments, old buildings, and business (29+18+8), then the section 6 diagram puts rural into that same 55% box. Non-simple volume is 66%. Rural is omitted from the opening bifurcation (34+55=89).

Survey skipping is also mis-specified. The pilot skipped surveys for some medium-risk addresses, and the segment note says that rule was often wrong for apartments. The simple-case design change was merged validation/scheduling. The memo still makes “skip upfront survey” a proven Track 1 lever and says Alternative C would force unnecessary surveys “where skip rules succeeded.” That causal claim is not in the evidence. Bundled gains for simple houses (median −6 days, p90 −8 days, FTR +3pp, complaints −1pp) do support scaling something for that segment; they do not identify which component.

Fact and interpretation leak across the prose even though the table is structured. The complaint-type shift is a stated finding but is filed as an interpretation. Section 3.4 asserts that EUR 207 “substantially underestimates” true loss and that a second truck roll “costs significantly more” than a survey, after the table correctly marks the cost gap as an assumption. Resilience “may have” fallen; the memo says it was “destroyed.” Missing permit checks become “regulatory penalties” and “municipal” exposure. “Demoralizes staff,” revenue-recognition motives, and a drop from 93% to ~78% via 3-person pods are invented. Utilization at 93% was in two regions, then is treated as system-wide.

Process-mining concepts are partly conflated. Precision is not “behavior observed in compliant practice”; it is extra behavior the discovered model allows beyond the log. Billing-before-activation and repeat installs are observed alignment deviations (they support fitness and conformance failure). Using them as examples of low precision mixes replay, overgeneralization, and a normative model. Low generalization (0.56) and the working-together network around activation specialists are barely used. The Kingman citation is only a stylized M/M/1 congestion term, not evidence that these teams sit on that curve. SLA (13% to 16%, better for simple houses) is unused.

Value of information contradicts the action plan. M5 is ranked as the test that should choose solo versus shared ownership, while section 6 immediately installs 3-person pods. M7 is monitoring in the prompt; the memo upgrades it to hard ERP locks and ranks that control as the top information value. M1, the randomized stratum test that could falsify skip-by-segment, is sixth, below instruments that do not change the scale decision. Rollout gates are not decision-relevant: Track 2 FTR ≥85% does not validate a skip rule; p90 ≤25 and fully loaded cost ≤EUR 180/195 are unanchored; 0.0% billing complaints is an absolute block. Segment attributes are missing before week 3 and the regions are unrepresentative, yet Track 1 still goes national before replication.

The skeleton of E is defensible. The memo’s specific scale, savings, skip, pod, and threshold claims are ahead of the evidence.
