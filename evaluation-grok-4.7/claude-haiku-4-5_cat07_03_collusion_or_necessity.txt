5.5

The write-up has the right shape and the right top-line judgment, but it is not reliable as an audit analysis. Several headline figures are false, one of them is internally inconsistent, and those errors are copied into the prioritization plan and the report wording.

The central mistake is treating cross-person speed as a four-eyes breach. Create Purchase Order + Approve Purchase Order has 42 violating cases in total (1.8%). The 84 and 62 figures are fast handovers between different people (Carla→Gita, Dan→Hugo), not same-resource violations. Section 1 nevertheless labels them as violating cases, then also cites the 1.8% rate. Those cannot both be true: 84+62 is already several times 42. Manager_Hugo is not even among the resources listed for that violation. Section 3 repeats the error (“84 cases with four-eyes violation,” “62 cases with four-eyes violation”), and section 6 tells the reader the PO violations are concentrated in Carla/Gita and Dan/Hugo. That would send reviewers to the wrong population.

The speed ratio is also wrong, and it is repeated in the formal wording. 2.1 hours is 126 minutes, so 8 and 10 minutes are about 16× and 13× the baseline, not 40–50×. The 28× figure for 7.8 hours versus 17 minutes is roughly right, which makes the other multiple look careless rather than like a definitional difference. Section 6 then calls the 17-minute figure an average; the evidence gives a median, and only for a 16-case fast subset.

Other factual or logical slips:

- 260 events and 248 cases for Approver_Gita is about one event per case, which is low repetition, not “concentration.” The shared-account evidence is the separate fact that 23 emergency approvals use that account while `entered_by` names assistants. The report treats the 260/248 ratio itself as proof the account is shared.
- “2,348 procurement cases” is not in the evidence. Back-calculating from the rounded violation shares does not produce that number.
- Subcontracting counts are directed A→B→A frequencies. Calling them “both directions” and “29% of cases” converts a pattern count into an unsupported case share. The same move is used for Hugo/Dan (28%) and Lena/Kim (25%).
- The 88% and 79% emergency rates belong to the full Carla/Gita (126) and Dan/Hugo (104) subgroups, not to a demonstrated violation subset.
- Lena/Kim is the right cluster to rank first, but the denominators are mixed: 9 same-person bank/payment violations, 16 fast cross-person handovers, and 44 co-cases. A Lena→Kim handover complies with four-eyes if they are different people. The high-priority row is titled as 9 four-eyes cases while arguing from the 44-case and 16-case statistics.
- Priority labels clash: Dan/Hugo is “medium” with a “High” timeline, and the review question is posed as “or is it collusion?” Similar “undisclosed collusion” wording appears for Lena/Kim. That is short of a formal accusation, but it is more accusatory than the evidence and the stated constraints allow. Section 6 itself is appropriately non-accusatory.

What holds up: routine Aline/Elena and Boris/Felix are correctly left as monitoring-only; emergency handling is taken seriously for the medical and weekend-repair channels; batch `quarter_close_upload` timestamps, the legacy_erp resource mapping, the missing V-77x maintenance link, and role design for vendor-master work are all treated as real alternatives. Section 2 is the strongest part: two benign explanations per pattern, with concrete confirming and refuting evidence. Section 5 is also concrete and two-sided. The relative ranking—Lena/Kim first because of low emergency rate, high value, V-77x concentration, manual upload, and bank-detail plus payment—is the right qualitative call.

Section 4 does not meet the technical requirement. `filter_four_eyes_principle` is called with nonexistent keyword arguments and its result is treated as a pair table with `resource_a` / `resource_b`; the function returns a filtered event log, and in current PM4Py it keeps cases that respect four-eyes. Handover and subcontracting use nonexistent module paths and the wrong return type. `len([a, b]) == 2` is always true. Working-together discovery is missing entirely. Service-time columns are read from slices taken before insertion, under a column name the call does not create. The DECLARE object is invalid and never executed. The batch check looks for two events inside one case-minute, whereas the cited defect is the same minute across justification events from `quarter_close_upload`. The legacy_erp check searches for resources containing “Bot,” which is the opposite of the stated mapping failure (bot and human collapsed onto one human resource).

Section 6 also misses the 200–300 word constraint by a wide margin (roughly 700 words). It does distinguish exceptions from misconduct, names Lena/Kim as the main concern, recommends document follow-up, and states data limits. Those virtues do not repair the false counts and the false speed multiple inside the text an audit committee would actually read.
