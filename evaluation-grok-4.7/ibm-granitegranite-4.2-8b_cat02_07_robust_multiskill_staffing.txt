3.5

The write-up follows the nine required headings and does argue for robustness and fairness rather than base-case mean delay alone. That is not enough to carry it. The quantitative core misreads the evidence, contradicts itself, and does not support the recommendation.

The skill matrix is encoded incorrectly. Ben has no electrical skill; Chen’s electrical multiplier is 1.00, the best rate alongside Deepa. The answer treats Ben as an electrical specialist with speed 1.00 and gives Chen no electrical skill, including in the in-memory matrix. Emil, the fastest software resource (1.00), is left out of the software specialist set in favor of Chen (1.25) and Farah (1.40). Policy S then also drops Ben, a fastest mechanical resource (1.00), while keeping slower Deepa. Electrical and software bottlenecks, the U2 story, and the P1 reserve {Ada, Ben, Chen} are built on that swap. Chen is also the person removed in U3 and U5, so the software reserve fails exactly on the absence shocks, which the recommendation then waves through as still “On.”

Offered load is not auditable. Full-shift availability (about 450 minutes) is used as capacity inside each two-hour block, so loads of roughly 80–300 minutes are compared with a denominator several times too large and bottlenecks are dismissed. Block counts are inconsistent (10:00–12:00 electrical and ME shares are wrong; several block totals do not add). Complex-tail ranges are confused with resource multipliers: a 1.25 multiplier is not a 25% complex-tail uplift. Intake, breaks, overtime, and shift edges (Farah until 12:00, Deepa from 10:00, Emil from 12:00) are not turned into block-level load. Utilization is then given as about 1.3% per reserve resource in §2, 30–60% in §5, and about 80–90% in §4.

The SLA reading is the wrong one. “At least 90% complete within 120 minutes” is a case-completion service level, not “90% of base diagnostic work has been consumed.” P2 and P3 attainment never appear in the comparison, despite being required fields. Parallel versus sequential is muddled: Policy S assigns ME/ES branches to two resources and still calls that sequential with no 8-minute coordination. The objective adds a dimensionless miss rate to a P90 measured in minutes. Delay figures such as 15–30 minutes are asserted, not derived. Handover, setup, preemption, and coordination are described and then left out of the numbers.

The Python is not a reproducible scaffold. It does not parse (`P1_SLA = "On" for all...`, and the broken `soft_skilled intersection` line). Metrics are hardcoded constants, so they do not vary by scenario and do not use the load it computes. There is no event log with the required columns, and `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_service_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network` are not called. The prose claims those calls happen. Complex-tail probabilities, preemption, handover, and setup are not executed, and those omissions are not honestly listed. Monitoring also misses eligible-resource set, skill used, planned versus actual service, and a real queue-entry or assignment record.

§6 and §9 are the least damaged parts: starvation, preemption, coordination, validation pile-up, gaming, and a limited P1-protected pilot are relevant. They still inherit the wrong SLA definition and the unsupported utilization claims. The hybrid idea is a reasonable policy family, but this specific design and its dominance claim are not earned by the evidence or the arithmetic.
