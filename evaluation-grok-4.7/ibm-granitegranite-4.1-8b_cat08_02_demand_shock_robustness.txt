The write-up hits the required section skeleton, but its process reasoning is not reliable enough to use. Several recommendations are inverted relative to the logs, and peak-period figures are recycled as if they were scenario forecasts.

**Scenarios.** S1–S4 name the four required stress types, yet each is one-dimensional: mix, machine, absence, or courier, with no combined case-mix and disruption case. S1’s shares omit repeat-after-failure (35+30+30 leaves 5% unexplained). S2’s “20% throughput loss (runtime 15%)” is internally unclear and ignores that M2 is faster but fails more often. S3 invents a “Friday-night maintenance blackout” and stacks a full-week medical-validator outage on top of the given every-other-Friday specialist absence without separating those pools. S4’s “7h median” and the later “9h” pre-analytical queue are the observed peak (courier 7.5h, pre-analytical 9.5h), so this is not a new stress on top of the peak log.

**Bottleneck migration.** This is the weakest section. Queues and edges are mislabeled, and flow direction is often backwards.
- S1 treats the historical peak accession queue (14h), medical-validation queue (22h), and specialist queue (36h) as the result of a harsher mix, with no scaling. It also asserts, without DFG support, that urgent orders drive accession correction. The urgent bypass in the variant note is a batch-wait bypass (74% normal, 58% peak), not a medical-validation bypass.
- I1 and I3 are conflated. I1 does not send complex-panel work to M2.
- Batch wait is called “previously idle.” It already carries 760 normal and 1,180 peak cases, with sojourns of 9h/22h.
- Relieving medical validation is said to overload technical validation and create repeat assays. Repeats leave assay (normal 1,140; peak 1,260). Technical validation is upstream of medical validation and is already at 101% in the peak. Speeding medical validation does not generate assay failures.
- S2 labels the 5h/15h assay-to-technical-validation edge as “test preparation → assay,” then assigns technical-validation queues (3.5h/16h) to medical validation (actual queues 2.0h/22h). The claimed 30h M2 queue and 22h repeat loop are not in the data (repeat → assay is 10h/26h).
- I3 is then recommended for an M2 failure even while the text admits that dumping routine work onto M2 pushes delay and repeats. That is the conflict already stated for I3, not a reason to adopt it under S2.
- S3 reverses specialist utilization (“87% → 43%”). Losing a person raises remaining utilization and queue; it does not move the pool from the peak rate back to the normal rate. The 22h and 36h figures are again the baseline peak, not the absence case. I4 is misread as cover for specialist review. I4 trains technical validators for accession correction only. Specialist review is upstream of medical validation (technical validation → hold → medical validation). An evening medical shift does not “overflow” work into specialist review.
- S4 restates peak queues, conflates poor-sample recollection with accession correction, and turns I8 into “eighthour registration automation.” Aggressive triage would increase recollection and cancellations (peak recollection already cancels 370 versus 520 returns), not saturate pre-analytical check. The longest early loop, recollection → receive at 42h/60h, is never used.

Frequency evidence is almost unused. The logs show batch-wait share rising from about 4% to 13% of preparation exits, assay-repeat share from about 7% to 15%, technical-validation direct-to-release falling from about 74% to about 50%, and specialist-hold share rising from about 2% to about 8%. Those shifts are the migration. They are not calculated.

**Redesign.** I5 and I6 are directionally useful: medical-validation median queue moves 2h → 22h, and recollection requests rise while more of them cancel. The package still fails the robustness test.
- Peak assay utilization is 98%. The “10% spare on M1” assumption is false, and M1 is not the faster machine. Putting urgent plus complex-panel on M1 also tries to prioritize two types at once. Under S1 volumes that is on the order of 65% of orders onto one of two already-saturated machines.
- I4 pulls people out of the pool that is already over 100% utilization. The text claims this eases technical validation when medical validators are absent, then later says the same dual role harms technical validation. I5 staffs medical validation, so it does not replace the technical-validation hours I4 removes. The stated safeguard does not protect the station that is actually depleted.
- I8 targets registration (queues 0.7h/2.8h, utilization 68%/85%), a non-bottleneck, and the 9-hour error note is a limit on clerk overtime, not evidence of a current registration failure spike. Clerks are not described as pre-analytical capacity.
- I7 and I10, the candidates aimed at courier spikes and the worst queue (specialist 36h median, 48h hold edge), are absent. Temporary part-time specialists appear only as an after-the-fact mitigation.
- Normal-period urgent p90 is already 19h against an 18h penalty, while the median is 11h. The redesign never faces that tail. Routine p90 moving 68h → 120h against a 72h target is likewise untreated. That is average-case blindness in another form.

**Policies.** Triggers sit inside ordinary peak, and sometimes inside normal, operation, so they are not scenario switches. Batch-wait sojourn is already 9h median in the normal period, so P5’s 8h trigger is on before any crisis. Medical-validation median is already 22h at peak, so P3’s 20h trigger is on for the whole peak, not only absences. P2 uses the 5h assay-to-technical-validation median as an M2 cycle-time baseline, then routes routine work onto the degraded machine and pauses complex-panel submissions without a destination. P1 plus I3 both claim M1. P3’s “release routine results pending validation” is a clinical-control failure, not an operations buffer, and it is not tied to the actual skip path (technical validation → release), which is how most non-complex orders already avoid medical validation in the normal log.

**Trade-off table.** The columns are present, but the coverage legend contradicts the cells (two-scenario items are marked High while the note defines High as three). I3’s routine effect is described as M1 filling with routine work, which is the opposite of dedicating M1 to urgent and complex-panel. P2’s routine effect is “neutral” while the policy sends routine volume to a failing M2. Repeat reductions are credited to faster medical validation and to registration automation, neither of which changes assay-failure generation. “Next bottleneck” is repeatedly technical validation via “more handoffs,” which is not how these stations overload.

**Residuals and dashboard.** M1 single-threading and dual-role quality loss are genuine redesign-caused risks, and the Friday specialist gap is real. Calling them mitigated by monitoring overstates what a dashboard does. The dashboard then breaks the units. Batch-wait and assay delays are measured in hours (queues 4–22h, edges 5–26h) but alarmed at 10–20 minutes. Accession yellow starts at the normal median of 3h, with red at 6h versus a peak of 14h. Specialist red starts at 6h versus a normal queue of 5h and a peak of 36h. Rework yellow/red (5%/8%) sits on top of the normal rework rate of 7.5% and below the peak of 11.8%. Urgent breach yellow at 2% cannot be an early warning when normal p90 is already past 18h, and breach rate is a lagging outcome placed in the leading set. Courier and pre-analytical delays are also merged into one indicator.

The piece looks complete and names the right stations often enough to seem operational. Checked against the DFGs, queues, and variant notes, core policies point the wrong direction, thresholds would be permanently red, and bottleneck “migration” often moves upstream or onto the resource that just failed.
