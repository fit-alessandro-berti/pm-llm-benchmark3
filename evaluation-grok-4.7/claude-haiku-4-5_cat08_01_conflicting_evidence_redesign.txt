4.5

The write-up covers every required section and the right strategic tension: the frequent auto-approve-and-replace path looks fast and cheap only before complaints, reshipments, and rule breaks, and that gap is concentrated in high-value devices rather than accessories. The three redesigns are genuinely different mechanisms, each names a sacrifice, and the experiment section does try to catch downstream shift. Those merits do not survive the quantitative and process-mining errors that the recommendations are built on.

The diagnosis misreads the log. Median 3.2 hours is the dispatch-to-close edge, not time to dispatch; summing the intake–auto–approve–dispatch medians does not produce 3.2 either, and it conflicts with the segment fast-path medians of 8, 13, 20, and 28 hours. The 5,000-case directly-follows graph is then treated as weekly demand (“cases/week,” “5,000 c/week”) even though no observation window is given, so every capacity, hiring, and pilot-volume claim is dimensionally unsupported. Edge counts are treated as cases: 2,550 auto-to-fast-approval transitions are not 2,550 completed fast-path cases, and 820 “reopens” is the 510 complaint inflows plus the 310 manual outflows counted again. The 9.2% figure is incidence of complaint, not a repeat-complaint rate. Fitness 0.91 with 68% fitting traces is cited, but the gap between those two numbers is never interpreted, and low model precision is ignored. The 82% figure is a congestion threshold, not an observed utilization. “Mean time rises from 22 to 36+ hours” is not in the performance graph.

Several prompt results are converted into different facts. The evidence portal’s 30–45% reduction in repeated requests becomes a 30–45% auto-resolve rate. All 260 dispatch-to-complaint transitions become proven wrong-part cases. Abandonment becomes “320 of 1,110” and then 29%, from an invented denominator. Pre-dispatch delay of 6–18 hours is costed as 0.15 labor hours. Path “medians” are sums of mean edge times, including invented classifier and scoring durations.

The redesigns then contradict themselves and the rules. Redesign A forces every high-value claim down a serial manual-plus-fraud-plus-repair path. Rule R1 allows either manual review or fraud review and still permits fast approval afterward; review-then-replace is the obvious compliant alternative, and shifting fulfillment onto three quote technicians is never treated as the bottleneck that policy would create. A also says Premium low-risk cycle time is maintained at 8 hours while targeting 13, and calls 13-to-22 hours a 38% increase; that change is about 69%. The quality-gate failure mode calculates a deficit of hundreds of hours and then “mitigates” it with one hire. Redesign B both preserves the accessory fast path and classifies every Premium customer as high-risk, which would push the 8-hour / 6% complaint accessory segment into the slow path the evidence says not to universalize. Its 70/20/10 evidence split, and the cycle time weighted from it, are fabricated; the “good” path also drops the customer wait that the “borderline” path includes. Redesign C calls a fraud-cleared fast track an R1 exception and elsewhere allows fraud review to be skipped at the gate, which reopens the violation it claims to close. Parallelism is later admitted to wait on the evidence SLA, but the comparison table still gives C a large cycle-time and cost win.

The Pareto table states current-process dominance and cost, fitness, and utilization effects the log cannot support. Failure modes are the strongest analytical section because they notice gate overload, scorer error, digital abandonment, and synchronization delay, but they use the same weekly fiction and do not correct the table. The experiment plan has controls, stop rules, and a downstream-shift check, yet it runs for a year on invented baselines, ignores campaign spikes that the resource note flags, and locks in the same misread targets.
