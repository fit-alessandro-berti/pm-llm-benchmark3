4.5

The memo has the right outer shape and a defensible headline, but the process arithmetic and the rollout contradict that headline often enough that a bank could not rely on it.

The required sections, twelve-candidate matrix, three portfolios, four-module cap, one control-strengthening module, and the two-deep-integration limit are all present. Deprioritizing registration (A1) and the activation bot (A11), and pairing missing-document nudging (A5) with statement normalization (A6), is the right instinct. The rest of the reasoning does not hold that line.

Waiting time is treated as time the automation deletes. Entity-mismatch assistance is credited with removing the 31-hour wait, committee-pack assembly with removing the 26-hour wait, and the document checklist with removing the 18-hour wait before financial review. Those medians are mostly queueing and synchronization. Committee packaging waits on KYC status, normalized statements, collateral when required, and credit notes; auto-assembly does not remove that wait. The 31-hour figure is also called the longest wait. External valuation is 58 hours and customer signing is 44 hours.

The 82% figure is misread. Entity mismatch is on the critical path in 82% of the cases that contain it, about 1,750 applications, not in 82% of the book. Aggregate delay is dominated by signing, credit analysis, contract preparation, financial-document review, and external valuation. That comparison is never made, so the trade-off against A7 and A10 is incomplete.

Several causal claims run backwards. The resource note says clean inflow will overload the nine analysts unless normalization improves. The matrix, dependency section, and risk table treat A6 as the overload and then propose capping normalization throughput, which would recreate the bottleneck. A6 is the capacity release.

Portfolio 1’s effects ignore two of its own modules. It is said to save 3–5 minutes per case and to leave rework unchanged, even though it includes a 44-hour signing wait and a missing-document loop with a 52% repeat rate (3,300 forward, 2,800 back). The 0.2-hour figure is the wait before receipt, not time A1 removes. Handovers and resource demand are missing for that portfolio; control violations and handovers are missing for the bottleneck portfolio.

The compliance portfolio is not compliance-first. A3 and A12 appear in no portfolio. Four-eyes violations (5.8% of high-exposure cases at month end) and provisional signing (6.5%, 1.1% remediated) are untouched by A4, A5, A6, and A9. The four-eyes control is attached to A8, which is not selected, and the false-negative control is attached to A3, which is not selected. Auditability of suggestion, override, and final decision is a clause inside the A3 row, not a gate.

Phase order contradicts the dependency map. A6 is deployed before A5 after A6 is said to depend on complete documents. Contract generation goes live in months 1–6 while entity resolution, which the memo says blocks contracting, waits until months 7–12. The phase-2 gate of “under 20 hours before contract” is almost the current 22-hour median. Variant behavior is ignored: concurrent KYC and document review, collateral on 71% of equipment finance versus 18% of working capital, 14% contract rework on credit-line renewals, weekly high-exposure committee cadence, and afternoon slack in the 12-person back office.

The recommendation correctly refuses a frequency-first program. The effects, base rates, resource mechanism, and sequence are not accurate enough to support it.
