3.5

The write-up covers every required section and gets the easy arithmetic right, but the recommendation rests on impossible results, a capacity contradiction, and a simulation that does not implement the policies it claims to test.

The base service expectations are correct (Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.30, Electrical 32.82, Software 38.50, Validation 15.74), as are the 42-case mix totals and the unscaled loads (about 4.86 / 7.93 / 13.29 / 8.25 / 11.02 hours, 45.35 in total) against 41.5 paid hours. That comparison is the right starting bound: every multiplier is at least 1, so routing cannot create hours. Maximum overtime is only 3.0 hours, which still leaves a deficit even if every task were done at multiplier 1. The day is structurally short before handover, setup, coordination, or tails.

Section 2 then drops that implication. It treats the overload as a dispatch problem (“un-optimized”), quotes an uncalculated “average multiplier ~1.12,” and never builds the required skill-by-block offered load. The 12:00–14:00 staffing figure is wrong: three 30-minute breaks cut 10.0 staff-hours to 8.5, not 9.5, and the same paragraph’s diagram lists those three breaks. U4 doubles tails for all activities and claims software mean 38.5→46.5 and system load +22%. The prompt doubles diagnostic tails only; the software mean becomes 44.5, and the system expansion is nearer 9%, not 22%. U2 is described as doubled E/ES demand, but doubling those shares and renormalizing inside a fixed arrival total raises them by about 1.4×, and the new volumes are never shown. U1’s bottleneck is pinned on Intake, which is a short activity many people can do.

Section 4 then contradicts section 2. Policy 3 is given base mean delay 22.8 minutes, P95 74, max utilization 81.5%, and “stable equilibrium,” with P1/P2 above 90% in every shock including U5. Mean cycle time cannot be 23 minutes when a typical single-skill path is already ~50–60 minutes of service and an all-fast assignment still exceeds shift plus overtime. Utilization cannot be 81% when unscaled work is already ~109% of paid hours. The resilience index is undefined, no interval or seed uncertainty is shown, and the table is not what the code prints. The dominance claim is therefore not evidence.

The code is a one-pass, arrival-order booking loop, not the three policies. Shifts, breaks, overtime, priority, reservation, and the 10% preemption penalty are absent, while section 4 says those effects are included. `resource_busy_until` starts everyone at 08:00 and never sends them home, so Farah, Emil, and Deepa work outside their shifts. Policy 3’s parallel rule is `curr_time < 14:00`, not the queue-length rule in §3. Policy 1 gives Software only to Emil and Validation only to Ada, Ben, and Farah, so it has no on-shift validator after 16:00 and more software hours than Emil’s shift; the prose also says Chen does ES sequentially. U5 incorrectly doubles tails and only pushes arrivals out of 09:30–10:00 instead of blocking Intake. One seed is called “Monte Carlo.” `pm4py.get_service_time` is never called. Case service and waiting times are averaged over event rows, so long cases are over-weighted, and dividing by 60 labels seconds as hours. The handover call assumes a dict `.items()` API that a NetworkX handover graph does not provide. Queue-entry, assignment, and planned-versus-actual service are missing from the log that is actually emitted.

Policy 3’s narrative rule (prefer primary skill, overflow after a wait threshold, parallelize only when both queues are short, cap preemption) is a coherent response to the specialization–pooling tension. It is not shown to dominate, and under a true capacity deficit the honest robust move is explicit P3 deferral or added hours, not an 81% utilization equilibrium. The pilot also A/B-tests routing on one shared pool, so the arms interfere. Objectives \(O_1\) and \(O_2\) are never computed; their weights mix incompatible units.
