6.5

The memo reaches the right strategic posture: reject full national rollout and full rollback, keep the simple-house gains, stop survey-skipping and post-billing activation where the tail, rework, and complaints concentrate, and attach further measurement to any wider expansion. All six required sections exist, the evidence table is large and mostly well partitioned, and the five metric conflicts are explained in substance.

Those strengths are undercut by repeated overclaim and several analytical breaks. Billing-before-activation and skipped permits are treated as illegal, finable, “existential” regulatory breaches. The source only supports a designed process change plus conformance deviations. “Pre-1980,” a 2–3 day survey delay, and a second truck roll costing 3–4 times a survey are stated as facts and are not in the evidence. “Lean tenant” is the wrong word, and the quoted “Kingman” formula drops the variability and service-time terms.

Fitness is partly blamed on permissiveness. Permissiveness is the precision result. The pilot also loses generalization (0.70 to 0.56), which a flower-model story does not explain, and that tension is never addressed. Intended redesign behavior is lumped with unintended deviation. SLA breach, the rural segment, and activation-specialist ownership are thin or missing. Segment shares are used as solid facts while early-pilot attributes are incomplete.

Alternative B lists idle survey capacity as a rollback risk; restoring surveys would raise survey utilization, not leave it idle. Alternative D is narrowed into “keep harming the pilot for months,” which is weaker than the stated option. Pods are ordered within 48–72 hours, including redeployment that the evidence table itself treats as possibly blocked, while M5 is later described as the test that would decide whether pods work. Pausing second visits until reason codes exist would stall repairs before those codes are even collected.

Value of information is the weakest required piece. M7 is ranked first even though premature billing is already a frequent alignment finding and the memo has already decided to stop it, so that measurement would not change the decision. The randomized skip test, which could still change what is safe to scale, is ranked sixth. Several numeric gates (0% billing, ≤3% false negatives, ≤EUR 185, 75–82% utilization, 500 cases) are policy inventions presented as decision thresholds.

The recommendation is defensible. The memo is not yet careful enough with evidence, process-mining metrics, or the difference between a control and information that would change the choice.
