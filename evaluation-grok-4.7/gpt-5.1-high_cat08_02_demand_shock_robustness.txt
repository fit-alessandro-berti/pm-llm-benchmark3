The answer covers all seven required sections, uses the normal-versus-peak frequencies, queues, and variant effects in detail, and avoids a blanket “add capacity” recommendation. Scenario design, migration narratives, guardrails, and triggers are mostly coherent. The score is limited by a repeated process-order error and by thresholds that contradict the baseline data.

Specialist review is on the path Technical validation → Hold for specialist review → Medical validation. It feeds medical validation. The write-up treats an evening medical-validation shift (I5) as pushing cases into specialist review, then uses that reversed link to justify pairing I5 with complex-panel outsourcing. Clearing medical validation moves work to Release result; it does not load the specialist. The same reversal is repeated as a downstream risk of R3.

Scenario B mixes two mechanisms. The heading says assay capacity is relieved; the paragraph then describes throttling preparation, which reduces inflow. A real capacity recovery would empty the assay queue and surge technical validation. That later point is stated, but the earlier bullet labels the throttle itself as bottleneck migration.

Policy numbers are mis-anchored to the given baselines:
- Scenario A is set to turn off only when Urgent p90 is under 18 hours. Normal-period Urgent p90 is already 19 hours, so the rule has no exit back to the observed steady state.
- Dashboard “red” bands are placed on the peak failure queues (assay 18h, technical validation 16h, medical validation 22h, specialist 36h). Those are the broken state, so they do not lead.
- The “14–22h” figures called station queues in Scenario B are the performance edges Test preparation → Batch wait (14h) and Batch wait → Assay run (22h).
- I9 is described as moving Routine p90 “toward 120h,” which is the peak p90 already observed without that hold.

Scenario C says upstream stages “operate reasonably” near peak volume while pre-analytical, assay, and technical validation sit at 96%, 98%, and 101%. Early triage is credited with shrinking recollection loops even though Request recollection → Receive sample is already 42h/60h and is mostly an external return. Relaxing Routine batching during a compressed arrival lowers setup efficiency just as preparation is most loaded. Urgent and Complex-panel are given one shared machine-time cap, while the cited conflict is that Urgent-first dispatch starves Complex-panel inside that cap; no within-cap rule is defined. Repeat-after-failure, the longest p90 class and a double user of assay time, gets no real policy. Outsourcing complex panels is scored as leaving Routine untouched, although multi-assay complex work is a direct load on M1/M2 in an outage.

The package is evidence-heavy and usable, but these errors can mis-sequence staffing and latch emergency rules. That keeps it well short of nearly flawless.
