7.3

The answer meets the required structure and constraints, and its main conclusion is right: early slowness is mostly harder carryover work, late speed with rising errors is mostly queue pressure plus batching and logging artifacts, and raw late-shift error rates should not be used to punish workers. It is not nearly flawless. Several load-bearing claims are stronger than the tables, and parts of the proposed code estimate the wrong quantity.

The largest analytical error is treating “error rate later under low WIP” as an end-of-shift, low-WIP fatigue test. In the resource table, “later” means after the first 20 events, so it pools the mid-shift steady state with the tail. Flat senior and mid-level rates (2.1% vs 2.2%, 2.8% vs 2.9%) and the junior dip from 5.1% to 4.4% therefore do not show that fatigue “has almost zero impact” in the final hours. The junior comparison is also not complexity-adjusted, so it does not show that juniors have stopped being “overwhelmed” by early complex work. The right contrast is stated in the fatigue row’s test, then overclaimed in the narrative.

Case-mix is likewise over-attributed to late cherry-picking. Complexity and high-priority share fall monotonically from positions 1–5 through 76+ (0.72 to 0.43; 41% to 19%), including while the queue is still falling. That pattern fits clearance of carryover and a changing inflow at least as well as opportunistic selection in the last third of the shift. An Oaxaca split of processed events, as proposed in the table, cannot separate those two. Seniors’ 2.1% error in the first 20 events is also not their error rate on complex cases.

Automation is over-causal. More human corrections of Bot_Risk when queue length exceeds 70 does not show that the bot “likely misclassifies,” that classifications are flawed, or that automation quality degrades with WIP. The same pattern is compatible with a harder case mix, less human review time, or incorrect overrides. Quality-check deferral is a real clue, but the claim that it moves earlier errors into the 76+ `error_flag` bin is an assumption about timestamp attribution, not a result in the log.

The code does not implement the design in the prose. Model A uses resource fixed effects; Model B, the error model, does not, so it does not make each worker their own control. Cluster-robust groups are taken from the unfiltered frame and can misalign if rows are dropped. `discover_performance_dfg` returns edge times, not correction counts, so the low-queue versus high-queue “Risk Classification → Rework Correction” print does not measure overrides. `get_rework_cases_per_activity` counts repeated activities, not the 7-day `rework_flag`, yet the dashboard equates them. `discover_batches` is unpacked as pairs and then ignored; `is_batched` also misses deferred quality checks that are not sub-2-minute, sub-30-second repeats. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is described as if it were event duration.

Smaller defects include “nearly double” for 22.4 versus 12.7 minutes, “40% faster” for a 43% shorter duration, “anearly,” a lowercase sentence break, unused objects (`temporal_features`, `clusters`, `bot_cases`), and dashboard thresholds (30 minutes, 4%, 35%, 25%) that are not tied to the baseline. The early-shift last-two-hours contrast (4.2% errors, 12.7 minutes, falling queue) is the cleanest anti-fatigue evidence and is underused relative to the misread resource columns.
