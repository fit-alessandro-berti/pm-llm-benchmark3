4.5

The write-up identifies the R5/R6 conflict, keeps those policies apart, converts R12 into seconds with `zeta = 2.0`, and gets the business outcomes for T4, T5, T7, and T8 largely right. The formal models that the assignment asks for are not coherent, not executable, and not consistent with the verdict matrix.

The accepting-net code does not implement the backbone it describes. `add_arc_from_to` is called without the net, `@accepting_petri_net` is not a PM4Py API, there is no initial or final marking, and clarification is wired transition-to-transition, so the graph is not a Petri net. The three checks are self-loops on one token plus an early skip, which is conflict, not an AND-split/join, so `Classify Risk` does not require all three. `Close Onboarding` is never a transition. `Activate Vendor` loops on `approved` and `Release First Payment` can fire with no activation. Escalation and `Obtain Legal Opinion` are independent options, so Resolution A is not in the net. Nothing returns to a clarification entry, so the “repeatable loop” claim is false. No activity layer enforces the mandatory three-check join.

DECLARE semantics are inverted. `response` is eventual, not immediate; `chainresponse` and `altresponse` are binary, not ternary choices or two-step permissions. The `chainresponse` keys are 3-tuples and would not be accepted by `conformance_declare`. The `exactly_one` comprehension ignores `{"Reject Vendor", "Activate Vendor"}` and stores the dummy activity `primary_outcome`. `precedence(Manager Approval, Activate Vendor)` and `precedence(Compliance Approval, Activate Vendor)` each make that approval mandatory for every activation, so they contradict R2/R8 and reject both T1 and T2. `precedence(Release First Payment, Close Onboarding)` likewise rejects the rejection path. R4 is the wrong direction: `precedence(Sanctions Escalation, Obtain Legal Opinion)` does not require an opinion after escalation, and it forbids an opinion with no escalation. The matrix still marks T1, T2, and T3 as DECLARE passes and T6 as a DECLARE fail.

The log skeleton repeats that contradiction: both approvals are `always_before` activation, while `never_together` forbids them from co-occurring, and both rejection and payment are required before every close. Under ordinary log-skeleton semantics no accepting trace survives, and an empty `directly_follows` allow-list fails every consecutive pair. The matrix marks T1–T4 and T7 as skeleton passes anyway. `always_before(Obtain Legal Opinion, Activate Vendor)` also demands an opinion on every activation, which is stronger than Resolution A and disagrees with the DECLARE model. The prose orientation of `always_after` does not match the stored pairs.

Section 7 is the best operational layer: T4’s same-resource approval is real, missing resources stay inconclusive, and the R11 post-activation reading is careful. It still would not run as written (`pd` is undefined; `case_df.get("vendor_id")` is not a scalar). Under the chosen policy the R6 function returns `not evaluated` when the exception is disabled, while T6’s matrix cell says `policy-dependent`.

Temporal bounds are the part that is actually checked: 71h, 26h/84h, 61h, 17h/77h, and T7’s \(z \approx 3.67\) versus \(z \approx 1.94\) are right, and treating them as warnings matches R12. That does not repair the control-flow models. Sections 9 and 10 discuss vacuity, metadata, and check order well, while repeating the false claim that `response` means adjacency and recommending deployment of a net that cannot be aligned.
