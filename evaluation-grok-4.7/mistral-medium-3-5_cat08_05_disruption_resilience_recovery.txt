The memo covers every required section and gets several headline facts right, but it is not a reliable incident analysis. Core classifications contradict each other, several DFG counts are misread, and the recovery design can drop mandatory controls on high-risk products.

The timeline quotes the main conformance and exception figures correctly (fitness 0.96 / 0.71 / 0.82 / 0.90; traceability 0.8%, 9.5%, 5.4%, 2.2%; hospital fill 91% versus pharmacy fill 61%). It still misses the operational story those metrics support. Hospital median cycle time only moved from 8h to 10h, while pharmacy moved from 23h to 54h and wholesale from 36h to 96h, with p90 tails of 118h and 180h. Stabilization left pharmacy and wholesale far above baseline (medians 28h and 58h; p90s 74h and 120h) even after the hospital median returned to 8h. “Cycle times met targets” invents targets the case does not state. T3 is assigned backlog prioritization without a partial-recovery DFG; that activity is evidenced later, at 1,500 stabilization cases. Move-on-log deviations, move-on-model gaps, and the three temporal-profile deviations are essentially unused.

The adaptation table has repeated quantitative and causal errors. Phone proof of delivery is credited with 2,200 confirmations; 2,200 is the flow into spreadsheet invoice hold, while 2,600 cases entered phone proof and 400 did not continue. Dispatch without scan is equated with the phase-level 9.5% traceability-exception rate, although that rate is about 532 cases and the dispatch-without-scan inflows are 1,800 + 1,450. Temporary release is tied to the T3 cold-chain miss of 2.3%; the manual phase, where that control actually appears, had a 4.8% cold-chain gap and a 2.9% controlled-substance dual-control gap. Audit remediation is credited with “reducing exceptions to 2.2%,” which treats a still-elevated period rate as if after-the-fact repair were real-time control. Backlog prioritization is credited with the T3–T4 cycle-time improvement despite covering a small share of stabilization volume. Skip-authorization is limited to pharmacy and wholesale ambient orders even though it is a sibling branch of emergency triage, not a customer-segment path.

Classification of resilience versus concealed noncompliance is internally inconsistent. The table marks the hospital fast lane as Modify because it bypassed authorization, then Section 3 calls it true resilience “while using” manual authorization, and Section 7 says to retain it. The DFG shows the opposite structure: fast lane, manual authorization, and skip-authorization are alternative branches. Of 1,000 fast-lane cases, 920 go straight to the inventory snapshot. Snapshot use is Modify with a 24-hour limit in the table, then flattened into a risky shortcut in Section 3, erasing the early-window role the case states. Temporary release is “true resilience” in Section 3 after the table says it missed cold-chain documentation. That also leaves the important abduction unexamined: if pharmacists checked every controlled-substance order, the rise from 0.2% to 2.9% missing dual control means either those orders leaked through the 1,800 packs sent straight to unscanned dispatch, or the manual check did not create the dual-control record. Coverage was about 1,650 of 3,600 manual packs. K and L never receive a classification. Spreadsheet invoice hold, an explicit move-on-log deviation, is never analyzed.

The recovery model includes a trigger, tiers, evidence capture, backlog order, and a remediation step, but the control logic is unsafe. “Hospital-critical + controlled/cold-chain” does not say whether product risk applies to every customer. Tier 3 wholesale orders get a snapshot only, with no authorization, release, scan, temperature, or custody minimum, so a wholesale controlled, cold-chain, or recall-sensitive order can ship under weaker rules than an ambient hospital order. Recall-sensitive product is never mentioned. Pharmacy-standard work is deferrable once backlog exceeds 4h, below the normal 23h median. The sketch is not explained, collapses pick, pack, inventory, and dispatch, and does not connect the backlog back into release. It also allows operational end before a compliance gate. The case’s partial-recovery failure mode—intake restored while sync and scanning remain untrustworthy—is not an operating mode; a binary “WMS down” diagram would recreate T3. Logging deviations on another central spreadsheet repeats the workaround that became spreadsheet invoice hold.

The indicators would not reliably detect this incident. A hospital median under 12h would have stayed green throughout (observed medians were 10h, 9h, and 8h) while the p90 and the other segments deteriorated. Backlog age is filed under traceability. The fairness target, under 20% of Tier 2/3 orders deferred, is about the deferral intensity already seen while pharmacy fill fell to 61%; fill rate, cancellation, and cycle time by segment are not the fairness measures. A lagging “complete audit trail” can be satisfied by reconstructed scans and later remediation, which collapses the distinction the case requires. Several targets simply sit near the weak stabilization results rather than baseline (rework under 5% versus 3.5%; cancellations under 3% versus 2.1%).

Recommendations invent precise benefits: traceability under 2%, cold-chain exceptions under 1%, and rework under 4%. The last is worse than the 3.5% baseline. A standing list for the “top 80% of customers/products” is not bounded away from controlled substances, quantity limits, or license expiry. Extending a pharmacist sample check is not the same control as a contemporaneous temperature record. Mobile pick/pack apps are a poor primary control for a cyber outage that may remove the same identity and network services; the stated residual risk covers training, not that common-mode failure. The playbook that would bind the other actions is ranked last. Missing from the priority list are a hard no-ship rule for controlled, cold-chain, and recall-sensitive goods without contemporaneous custody evidence, a ban on invoice close ahead of minimum evidence, a snapshot-age limit, a partial-recovery re-entry test, and a rule against declaring recovery while the remediation queue is open.

The closing trade-off correctly says some slowdown must be accepted and that skipped authorization and unscanned dispatch should be refused. It then retains the fast lane as if it had been compliant, and it reopens those refusals if someone judges supply-loss risk larger than noncompliance risk. For this wholesaler, that escape hatch is wide enough to authorize the same concealed noncompliance in the next emergency. The hidden post-recovery workload is not part of the decision even though stabilization still shows 2,300 invoice closes entering audit remediation and 1,200 proof-of-delivery remediations.
