The base delay decomposition is coherent and numerically accurate: upstream time, branch queues and services, max-based `join_ready`, critical-branch labels, downstream queue, final service, and total flow all check out for every case, including the worked examples. The double-counting argument is right, and the ranking correctly treats B as dominant because of critical frequency and queue-plus-service, not because of mean service (A’s service is longer). Limits of inference are appropriately cautious.

The counterfactual section breaks the same max logic. Reducing A by 20% does not move F01’s join from 09:00 to 08:50; B still ends at 08:55, so the join reduction is 5 minutes, not 10, and that 5 is fully absorbed by the 8-minute downstream wait under the answer’s own masking rule. Halving B’s queue is likewise overstated where the next branch binds: F05 falls only to A’s 10:00 (10 minutes, not 12.5), and F10 falls only to A’s 11:15 (5 minutes, not 10). The claimed 100-minute aggregate and the conclusion that F05 still yields a net flow-time gain are therefore wrong; only F12 clearly retains a net gain. “High-flow” is also the wrong description of the A-critical cases (93 and 110 minutes).

The Python encodes the max correctly but would not run: `format_dataframe` is called with `case_id_key`, `discover_performance_dfg` is given an unsupported `start_timestamp_key` and then treated as a DataFrame, and `get_service_time` is treated as a DataFrame. Per-case time savings are computed and never printed, and join reductions are not converted into flow-time savings.
