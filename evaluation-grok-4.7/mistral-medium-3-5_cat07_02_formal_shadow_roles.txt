The write-up follows the required six-part outline and gets the broad cast right: Nora is automation, Fatima owns pricing, Hana owns approval, Erik is a low-volume critical specialist, Giorgio is a commercial shadow, and Mateo is a cross-functional broker. Benefits and risks of shadow work are both stated, and several cited figures match the log (substitution counts, 38→74 minutes, 21 preliminary approvals with 13 rechecks, similarities, Erik’s 96 security reviews). That skeleton is not enough. The analysis still commits the frequency-clustering mistake it was written to avoid, misstates authority, and the PM4Py section is not coherent.

Role structure. Temps R11 and R12 are placed in the same role as R01 and R02. Their medians are 0.18–0.19, they have no certifications, they never configure, implement, check quality, reopen, or update the knowledge base, and 17 later reopens were traced to their entitlement notes. The table’s own “naïve clustering” cell gives those reasons and then ignores them. Front-office evidence cites a median range of 0.11–0.34; 0.11 is R14, who is not in that role. Member medians are 0.18–0.34. Giorgio’s peripheral cell says “A1 Close.” A1 is Register (90 events); Close is A12 (130). His profile is broad and shallow, his critical-case count is the highest in the log (76), and the Week 5 approvals were a one-week cover, 13 of 21 later rechecked. Routing is the job; the cover is not. Quality medians of 0.52 and 0.48 are called medium–high. Chen (0.71, 42 critical) and Dina (0.48, 9 critical) stay one undifferentiated role aside from a caveat.

Core versus peripheral, and shadow roles. Ambiguity. Giorgio and Mateo are framed fairly, and the recheck evidence is the right test of Mateo’s authority. Dina’s second interpretation says she has “higher complexity than Dina,” which is incoherent. Six security events do not make a junior security specialist; the live question is whether she can be staffed like Chen. Code. `pm4py.discover_organizational_roles` is a public PM4Py function. The script says it is not and never calls it, so there is no role-discovery result to join to `case_complexity` or `temporary_substitution`. The import `pm4py.discovery.organizational_mining` is not the library layout. Similarity is assumed to be a square DataFrame; the SNA helpers return graphs or pair dictionaries, and those objects are never inspected. Cluster labels are written onto a resource `groupby` by position, so they are not aligned to matrix order. `AgglomerativeClustering(..., affinity='precomputed')` uses a parameter current scikit-learn does not accept. Case complexity is aggregated on events, so busy cases outweigh cases. Arbitrary cluster ids are then mapped to formal titles, which is the over-reading the comments warn against. `temporary_substitution` aggregated with `max` flags whoever touched a flagged event, not who was absent and not who was competent to cover.

Operational use. The two cautions, the reopen follow-up, and the limit on Giorgio’s cover are usable. They sit next to conflicting advice: train Dina as a security backup after warning that she must not be read as a security specialist, and train Mateo toward preliminary approval on the strength of a week of rechecked substitutions. Staffing from these mined roles would still flatten temporary and experienced front office and would treat a triage broker as an approver.
