The write-up keeps the required ten-part skeleton and the right rough chronology (March labels, May supplier shock, June capacity, October season, December mixed recovery). It is not reliable as a drift diagnosis. Core numbers are wrong, several “observations” are not in the aggregates, and multiple explicit deliverables are missing or non-runnable.

## 1. Drift timeline
May is filed as population/case-mix drift. The supplier event is incomplete bundles, and the sharp co-movement is rework (`rework_variant_pct` 13→24, `Assess→Request Info` 12→24 per 100 cases). The share jump (18%→43%) is real, so the interval is mixed: composition plus control-flow. A single mix label drops the mechanism the later sections need.

July lists “queue redistribution” as an observed change with strength 70. The brief states that the monthly aggregate does not show a high-complexity versus standard wait split. That row treats a hypothesis as a measurement. The priority rule, if it did anything visible here, would be performance drift (reorder of waiting), not control-flow drift.

October’s onset is labeled Recurring while the phenomenon is the annual renewal season. Seasonal and recurring are separate onset types in the required distinction. December is narrowed to mixed behavioral drift even though volume, complexity, and supplier share all fall at the same time as the overtime and quality campaign.

September is absent. After the 2026-09-10 supplier recovery note, supplier share 38→24, rework 27→20, and median cycle 45.0→41.5 before any December intervention. That is a distinct partial-recovery interval. Pre-May gradual rises in complexity and supplier share are also flattened into the March and May rows.

## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation
About 160 words, short of the required 180–280. The qualitative point is right: remapping `Eligibility Screen` and dropping duplicate `Open Case` closes most of the raw fitness hole (about 0.93→0.55 raw versus about 0.90 mapped) and still does not prove the work unchanged. The residual mapped slide (0.93→0.86 by July) is never quantified, so the part that might be real conformance loss is blurred into the logging gap.

Duplicate `Open Case` is about 4% of March cases (26/640) against a label switch on about 500/640. The text treats duplicates and renames as joint causes of “nearly all” misalignment and says missing resources limit alignment checks. Standard alignment fitness is activity-sequence conformance. Resource missingness hits organizational mining and resource-conditioned performance. Post-May is correctly refused as pure logging, but the discriminating facts are unused: activity-label count locks at 11 from March, duplicate and missing-resource rates stay in a narrow band, while rework and cycle time move.

## 3. Quantitative drift decomposition
The change table has five metrics. Six were required, and the omitted ones are the diagnostic ones (supplier share, raw fitness, mapped fitness, p90, missing resource, duplicate rate).

Against the stated February baseline, several cells do not match the series:
- High-complexity, May versus February: 25−20 = +5 points, not +7.
- High-complexity, December versus October: 34−41 = −7 points, not −11 (that is November→December).
- Fast-variant, October versus February: 38−60 = −22 points, not −25 (that is November).
- Rework, May versus February: 24−11 = +13 points, not +12 (January’s base).
- Median cycle, February→March: 31.2−30.8 = +0.4 h, not +1.3 h.

Baselines switch between January, February, October, and November under February/October headers.

The DFG “Jaccard” figures (0.18, 0.25, 0.40) are not Jaccard and do not fit the fragment. After normalization, January versus March differs by 1 point on three of seven edges (L1 = 3 on a per-100 scale). March is the month that should look almost unchanged. A distance of 0.18 there inverts the logging-versus-behavior result. Senior Assessment is in the supplied fragment; calling it an omitted edge misstates the caveat. Omitted edges are the paths not in those seven columns.

Cycle-time shares (case mix ~30%, rework ~40%, queueing 35–45%, plus a +0.6 h service delta) are not a partition and are not identified from the aggregates. Median cycle rises by about 13 h from February to July while recorded `Assess` service rises by about 0.6 h. That supports “service is small; waiting and extra passes dominate.” It does not support those percentages. May, post-15 June, and October are different increments and are collapsed into one “from May onward” split.

Change points: one for logging (March), one for behavior (July), two for performance (May, June). Two per family were required. The clearest control-flow breaks are May (`Assess→Request Info` 12→24) and October (`Assess→Senior Assessment` 7→22 in the normalized table, 9→22 from July). July is the weakest behavioral candidate because the aggregate does not show the queue split. The multiple-change-point conclusion is right and under-argued.

## 4. Competing mechanism hypotheses
Eight rows and the required mechanisms are present, and some “evidence against” cells are the best causal restraint in the answer (pre-May Z complexity, queue rise before the priority rule, pre-season delays, December rework dip). Those restraints are then contradicted elsewhere.

“High Supplier Z rework May–August” and “Queue gap widened post-July” are stated as evidence. Neither split exists in the tables. Mapped fitness did not “recover” in March; it barely fell (0.93→0.90) while raw fitness collapsed. Rank is undefined, so logging as rank 1 reads as a global explanation. The interaction row stays generic: it never names staffing load inflated by Z rework loops, or a priority rule reallocating a queue that got shorter on capacity in June.

## 5. Discrimination
The six requested cuts are listed. Several pattern cells swap the three drift types. Uniform delay after the staffing date is a capacity signature, not population drift. Population drift would be stable within-segment DFGs with changing weights. Pre/post-migration behavior is described as “rework paths match adjusted event labels”; after a pure rename, mapped rework rates should stay flat, which is what January versus March shows (12 and 12). “Temporal fold” is not a process-mining artifact. Log skeletons, DECLARE constraints, alignments, and variant shares are largely unused. Within-supplier and within-complexity rework rates — the actual discriminators — are not specified as the comparison.

## 6. Segmentation plan
One-way cuts with 10% case floors are reasonable at 600–800 cases per month, but there is no collapsing rule for supplier × complexity × month, which is where cells get small. “Priority-rule timing” with a “20% of cases” support rule is a calendar split of the whole log, not a 20% segment. “Effort levels” are undefined.

The primary population is high-complexity Supplier Z. That is the most confounded cell: Z was already more complex, Z’s bundles broke in May, and the July rule targets high complexity. Logging drift, standard-case queue harm, and non-Z control behavior sit outside it. The same table already flags supplier–complexity overlap, then chooses that overlap as the reference population.

## 7. Monitoring architecture
Ten monitors, varied thresholds, and the main families are mostly covered. There is no service-time monitor, so queueing and work-content cannot be separated in production. Variant-distribution monitoring is only indirect. Duplicate-`Open Case` rate and distinct label count — the March signatures — are absent.

Thresholds are not tied to the observed series. Missingness >5% is already the post-migration plateau (about 4.8–6.5%), so the alert stays on from March and cannot see further movement. “Median queue > historical peak” stays silent through the entire climb until a new record. “Rolling 1 month” is used as the reference window, so the baseline swallows the drift it should detect; a seasonal check needs a lagged seasonal reference, not a one-month roll.

The seasonality and multiple-testing note does not specify a procedure. Separate segment baselines do not control the false-discovery rate across monitors, segments, and months.

## 8. Python / PM4Py
Part A does not embed either table. It reads missing CSVs, so it is not self-contained. Validation is a null check and a column-name check. Baseline change is one absolute difference on case counts. Distance uses `scipy.spatial.distance.jaccard` on two scalars over only `Register→Eligibility` and `Eligibility→CollectDocs`, the edges that barely move; Jaccard on positive rates is either an error or zero, and it cannot reproduce 0.18 / 0.25 / 0.40. Fitness “recovery” is a raw gap, which is fine, but the change-point rule thresholds `cases_change` diffs at 0.1 cases, which flags essentially every month, and it never watches raw fitness, so March’s collapse is invisible. Caveats are two generic print lines.

Part B names `format_dataframe`, `filter_time_range`, `discover_dfg`, `get_variants`, and `discover_temporal_profile`, which matches the simplified API at the function-name level. It does not compute monthly DFGs, does not discover a log skeleton or a DECLARE model, and does not copy the frame and remap `Eligibility Screen` → `Validate Eligibility` while leaving the original log intact. `discover_dfg` returns `(dfg, starts, ends)`; the comparison treats that tuple as a DFG.

## 9. Provisional attribution
About 160 words, short of 250–400. March-as-logging and “later drift is real” are the strongest claims and are directionally right, with the duplicate-event share overstated. Supplier Z as a co-cause of the May rework jump is plausible, not established: the share tripling is not explained by incomplete bundles, and no within-Z rework rate is observed. Calling the July redistribution “good evidence” contradicts the aggregate. Confidence is only “strongest” versus “plausible,” with no levels. The revision condition is “better individual-level data,” not a falsifier (flat within-Z rework, equal within-class waits after 1 July, senior-assessment rise only where recording was previously inconsistent).

Two planted ambiguities are missed. `Request Info` is an allowed path, so a rework surge need not move fitness much; the mapped-fitness dip needs its own account. Senior Assessment was historically recorded inconsistently, and the October rate jumps from 7–9 to 22 per 100 cases as the high-complexity share jumps. That edge is both a seasonal mix candidate and a semantic-recording candidate.

## 10. Decision rules
Seven IF / THEN / BECAUSE rules are present. Rule 1’s rationale (“missing logs … were likely adjusted”) misstates a rename-plus-duplicate artifact. Rule 5’s 5% missingness trigger is already true for every month from March onward, and missing resources do not by themselves distort activity conformance. Rule 6 sends rework peaks to supplier or staffing together, which erases the distinction the monitors were supposed to make. Rule 7’s trigger (“queue waiting increases precede service events”) is true whenever a queue exists, and the action (add assessor capacity) is the wrong response if the queue is rework from bad bundles or a zero-sum priority rule. None of the rules separate seasonal recurrence from a new break.

## Bottom line
The narrative outline would be a passable draft. The graded product manufactures a DFG distance that hides March’s stability, mis-adds the monthly deltas, promotes unobserved queue splits to findings, chooses the most confounded slice as the primary population, and ships Python that does not embed the data or implement the distance it claims. Those are central failures on quantitative reasoning, causal restraint, and monitoring design, not polish issues.
