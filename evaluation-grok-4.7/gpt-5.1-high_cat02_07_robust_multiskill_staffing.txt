5.5

The write-up has the required nine-part structure, labeled assumptions, two real objectives, three operational policies, and a full policy-by-scenario table. Service-mixture means and daily case-type counts are mostly right, and the hybrid-plus-surge recommendation is a coherent qualitative stance. The quantitative spine does not hold, and the reproducible model contradicts the recommendation.

Farah works 08:00–12:00 with no break (4 hours, 240 minutes). Sections 1–2 still give every resource 450 minutes and “6 × 7.5 = 45 hours.” That overstates her base-time capacity by 210 minutes in every skill pool (Intake 450 vs 240, Software 321 vs ~171, and the same error for M/E/Validation). The headline “45.3 hours of demand versus 45 hours, so utilisation is about 90%” does not follow: 45.3/45 is already about 101% before multipliers, handovers, and skill mismatch, yet the same section later treats 54 hours versus 45–48 as the load picture. Those claims cannot be true together.

U3 software utilisation is miscalculated on the author’s own figures: 495 / (1131 − 360) ≈ 0.64, not “~0.73.” U2 is worse. Doubling the E and ES shares and renormalising, which is what the code does, raises midday electrical task share by roughly a fifth and leaves software share about flat. The text still says electrical and software minutes “roughly double.”

Daily skill totals hide the coverage hole the shift table implies. After 16:00 only Deepa can do mechanical work; after 18:00 only Emil is on duty, and Emil cannot do mechanical work. Evening software and electrical work also collapse onto Emil. “Mechanical is not capacity-limiting because ρ_M ≈ 0.30” is an all-day, fully dedicated comparison, not a time-block result. No block-by-skill load table is actually computed, and there is no critical-path bound of Intake → ME/ES → Validation against the 120-minute P1 clock, with multipliers, the 7- and 5-minute penalties, and the 8-minute parallel coordination. That comparison is the main parallel-versus-sequential decision.

Section 4’s SLA and delay bands are not derived from Section 2. The code makes the gap explicit. `utilisation_estimates` credits each qualified resource’s full shift to every skill at once, so base diagnostic utilisation stays near 0.5 and U4 barely moves. `rough_delay_metric` then reports short delays and P1 SLA stuck near 0.95 whenever utilisation is under 0.85, including Chen’s absence. Policy multipliers give B and C the same P1 shortfall factor and give B the better mean delay, while Sections 4–5 say C has the better P1 SLA. U5’s outage never changes load or capacity; it is only a severity constant. The claim that C stays at or above 90% P1 SLA in every scenario conflicts with the table’s U5 band of 0.86–0.93. Complex-tail draws are not “about the 90–95th percentile” (software’s tail mass is 20%). The 90% individual-utilisation fairness rule is breached by the recommended policy even in the base row, without an infeasibility analysis.

The event log does not implement the three policies, shifts, queues, setup, parallel branches, preemption, or U1–U5. `queue:entry_time` is set equal to the start, so waiting is not identified. `format_dataframe` is called without `start_timestamp_key`, so PM4Py will not treat the log as an interval log and case service versus waiting will be mislabeled. `discover_handover_of_work_network` is unpacked as a pair, and `get_service_time(...).head()` assumes a table; stage-specific queue metrics are never computed. Handover, setup, branching, and preemption are not explicit mechanisms in the policy scores; they sit inside unexplained constants such as 0.9 and 1.2.

Format, policy prose, and the monitoring/pilot discussion are ahead of the arithmetic. The capacity identity, shock magnitudes, time-block bottlenecks, and code-to-table consistency are not reliable enough to audit or to defend the ranking.
