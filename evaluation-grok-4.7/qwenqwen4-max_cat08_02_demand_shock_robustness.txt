7.5

The final answer meets every structural requirement and is strong on mix-normalized demand, authority-versus-capacity bottlenecks, gated policies, downstream overload, and redesign-induced risk. It is not nearly flawless: several prominent quantitative claims conflict with the given logs.

The load-bearing batch-wait claim is inconsistent with the frequency DFG. Peak Batch wait has 1,180 entries in total, yet 42% of 2,871 Urgent cases is about 1,200 Urgent entries alone. “58% bypass” does not mean those 1,200 cases traversed Batch wait, and Routine is the class described as tolerating batching. The 14h + 22h = 36h “path” also adds medians. That figure is then reused as a measured leak in the redesign and the dashboard invariant.

The autoverification lever is arithmetically wrong. Sixty percent of 1,350 direct-release cases per week is about 810 cases, roughly 30% of peak technical-validation volume, not ~20%. The jump from 101% to ~81% utilization depends on that 20% figure. Those same TV→Release cases already skip medical validation, so R3 does not “immediately” overload medical validation; any effect is indirect, via unblocking the medical-validation-bound subset.

Smaller grounding errors remain. Hold volume is about 5.6× weekly (203 versus 36), not 5.9×. The stated 4-week cross-training attribute belongs to technical validators, not to training correction specialists and clerks into technical validation. The per-class service targets are asserted without a capacity bridge showing the package can hit them.

The scenario set, migration logic, rejection of I1/I2/I3/I4/I9 as written, trigger hysteresis, and leading-versus-lagging monitoring are otherwise well grounded and non-average-seeking.
