The required seven-part structure is complete, and the redesigns are tied to real artifacts (fast-path complaint rates, evidence loops, R1/R2, the 82% manual-review threshold, the portal, and the 6–18 hour gate) rather than generic Lean language. Trade-offs, failure modes, bottleneck shifts, and a staged pilot with downstream checks are all present. That is not enough to offset several material evidence and logic errors.

The diagnosis misstates the central segment facts. Premium high-value median time is 20 hours on the fast path and 96 hours on the reviewed/repair path, with a 22% fast-path complaint rate; the write-up assigns 96 hours to the fast path and then contrasts it with “20 hours on the fast path.” Standard high-value is then said to “mirror” this at an 18% complaint rate and a 120-hour median, mixing the fast-path complaint rate with the reviewed-path duration. The 2,300-case edge is Fast approval → Dispatch replacement, not the Intake → auto-screen → Fast approval path (auto-screen → Fast approval is 2,550). Fitness of 0.91 and the explicit R2 exception for premium low-risk accessories also make “the fast path systematically bypasses compliance” too strong.

Redesigns A and C are not materially different. Both are segment-gated fast approval plus the same prompted pre-dispatch gate. C’s title says post-dispatch interception while the mechanism is pre-dispatch, and it treats the existing Dispatch → close mean of 5.0 hours as the duration of a new quality check. A claims segment rules will limit manual inflow, then later warns of the prompted 15–35% inflow increase. The slow quality path’s actual premium problem—Manual review → Repair quote, driven largely by part-price waits—is never redesigned, nor is R3 (reopens must pass manual review; 120 go to the non-scalable escalation board).

The comparison table violates the instruction not to invent unsupported precision: roughly 7–8% complaint and rework rates, 3–8% cost effects, 40–70 hour medians, and EUR 228–684 per gated case. That euro figure wrongly multiplies elapsed 6–18 hours by the inspector wage. The “9% cheaper after rework” finding is the fast path’s remaining cost advantage after complaints, reshipments, and credits, not savings a redesign can book. Experiment thresholds such as a 2% wrong-replacement rate and a 60% reduction invent a baseline the log does not contain. Directional effects in the table are also often unreadable, so the Pareto comparison does not reliably show the trade-off.
