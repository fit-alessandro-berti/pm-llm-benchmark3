The probe matrix, Model S/C language readings, and the third process tree are largely correct, and the required eleven-section structure, disaggregated metric table, negative-suite stance, and non-token dissent are all present. The score is held down by metric-mechanism errors and a few concrete slips in a review whose main job is metric pathology.

The behavior analysis holds up. Under standard process-tree semantics, Model S is the parallel of four `X(activity, tau)` blocks, so it really does admit the six cited skips and early-Expedite traces, including B3 and B4, and it really does reject B6 and B8 because `Correct Dossier` and loops are absent. Model C is exactly eight full sequences: it accepts B1 and B7, rejects B2 (Auto Approval only on Credit-then-Technical, never the reverse), rejects B5 (no Supplier + Expedite + Auto branch), and its only correction branch redoes both checks under Manager Approval. Those calls are the right ones, and separating language membership from alignment near-misses is the right criterion. The third tree `*(+(Credit Check, Technical Review), Correct Dossier)` followed by independent optional Supplier/Expedite and an XOR approval is a real improvement: mandatories are not optionalized, Expedite cannot precede the checks, naked rechecks are structurally impossible, labels are not duplicated, and B5/B2/full-recheck-plus-Auto become generable without Model C’s branch list. Defining `*(A,B)` as `A(BA)*` avoids a silent parser ambiguity. Not inventing numeric scores is also correct.

The metric section is weaker than the control-flow section. Optionalizing Credit Check and Technical Review does not inflate alignment fitness on a log that already contains those events; fitness stays high because observed traces remain replayable. A strict superset of the same log would score similarly. The pathology is that fitness is blind to the extra skips, not that the taus produce the 0.992. Token-based generalization is likewise mis-explained. It penalizes rarely visited markings. Model C’s 0.412 is the enumerated-branch effect, which the answer gets right elsewhere, but Model S’s 0.907 is not “the same reason” its precision is 0.584. A compact tree that still required both checks would also revisit few markings often. High training precision for Model C is also not mainly an artifact: if the language is essentially those eight traces, precision on that positive log should be high. What fails is the inference from that number to policy precision, which the answer does state, then oversells as inflation.

Smaller faults: Adv1 contains one `Correct Dossier`, not a multi-correction trace, yet the audit labels it “multi-correct.” The post-hoc rule that internal orders must not contain Supplier Review is stronger than P3’s “required only for external,” which does not by itself forbid the activity internally. Section 9 asks for simplicity on training, holdout, and negative logs even though arc-degree simplicity is a property of the net alone, and alignment-cost sensitivity is only a comment. “Not accidental side-effects” and “accidental semantic flaw” pull against each other. The dissent is within the length band and actually attacks force-both recheck, but it credits Model S’s metrics with already showing the value of a permissive skeleton plus external checks, which S does not have.

Governance use-case split, retirement of both originals for enforcement, and deployment gates (negative suite, soundness, owner sign-off on force-both and unconditional Supplier optionality) still follow from the evidence. The deduction is for misstating why the headline numbers move, not for the model choice.
