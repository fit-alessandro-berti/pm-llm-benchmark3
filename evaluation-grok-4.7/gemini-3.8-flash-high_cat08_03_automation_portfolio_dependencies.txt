The portfolio direction is coherent: defer registration, reject activation, pair specialist and analyst relief with a real control module, and keep Year-1 scope to four modules. The write-up cites frequencies, waits, DFG edges, pools, and the control findings, and it covers every required section. Those merits do not survive the quantitative and causal claims built on top of them.

The central argument against automating registration is mechanistically wrong. Application arrivals are exogenous. Speeding a non-bottleneck with afternoon slack does not raise injection rate, inflate downstream WIP, or lengthen cycle time under Little’s Law. The opportunity-cost case for skipping A1 stands; the claim that A1 actively worsens queues and four-eyes breaches does not. Drum-Buffer-Rope is invoked without a release rule.

Waiting-time arithmetic is invalid. Median waits are summed across optional, concurrent, and variant-specific activities, then turned into “~45h / ~115h / ~50h” and “−10% to −50%” end-to-end cycle time. No baseline cycle time exists. Entity-mismatch delay applies to 1,750 cases, on the critical path in 82% of those, yet it is treated as a system-wide saving. Concurrency is drawn and then ignored in the totals.

Resource math contradicts itself. Analyst demand of ~29,700 hours versus an assumed 9 × 1,750 = 15,750 hours is an unsupported denominator; observed volumes could not have been processed at ~190% of that capacity. On that same model, removing normalization still leaves analysts near 150% loaded, so “crisis solved” and “no overtime” are false. The 6,500-hour / 3.7 FTE saving also conflicts with the later 75-minute-to-10-minute residual check. The same hours test is never applied to specialists: 1,750 × 55 minutes is only ~30% of 3 FTE (about 46% of the two seniors). A 31-hour median wait at that utilization is a key-person, batching, or external-delay problem, not an aggregate capacity deficit, so cutting handle time does not justify 31h → 4h or 8h.

Control claims are mis-assigned. Four-eyes is a credit-analysis versus committee-packaging segregation failure. The matrix locates it on A8. Portfolio 3 and the recommended set {A4, A5, A6, A9} exclude A8, yet both promise 5.8% → 0%. A9 can gate KYC-before-signing and bind contract terms to the decision; it does not enforce that segregation. Absolute eradication (0.0%, ≥99%, >99.8% recall) is repeated as a gate.

Dependencies are stated and then dropped. A5 is said to require A2’s product and exposure rules, and A2 is omitted. A6 is said to need clean documents and still assumes high straight-through rates despite the stated PDF failure mode. The compliance map places the KYC hit pre-classifier after entity resolution and labels the whole KYC stream as the 3 specialist FTEs; hit screening belongs to Compliance ops at KYC time. The diagram sends every case through committee, but only 3,650 of 7,600 contract preparations directly follow a committee decision, so structured “committee terms” do not cover most contract volume.

The integration constraint is restated incorrectly. Two deep core-banking integrations are allowed in the first six months, not as a Year-1 cap. Section 2 assigns both slots to A6 and A9 inside six months; the rollout places A9 in months 7–9 and calls that an annual limit. A6-to-credit-engine and A1/A3-to-core are asserted as core-banking depth without evidence. A4’s limited coverage is acknowledged in the matrix and then treated as near-complete queue removal. Targets conflict: 55 minutes to under 10 versus under 15; contract rework to <0.5% versus <1.5%; a Month-6 gate of 1,500 liberated hours in one month, above the implied steady-state run rate.

The required matrix is in A1–A12 order, not a ranking, and gross activity hours are presented as labor savings for recommenders and assistants. Handover effects stay vague. The final trade-off answers A1 at length and barely accounts for leaving the 58-hour valuation wait and the 44-hour signing wait in place, which the earlier analysis treated as dominant.
