6.5

The write-up is a serious competing-risks analysis with the right skeleton: time zero at `Submit`, horizon 72, resolution separated from withdrawal, rejection, transfer, and open cases, and estimands mapped to decisions before estimators are chosen. Several cumulative-incidence figures check out (overall resolution by 72 hours about 30.4% if transfers compete and 31.3% if they are censored; cohort A/B about 29.2%/31.6%; P1/P2/P3 about 25%/43.1%/20%; 4/13 late resolutions; mean resolved duration 49.8 hours; the four already-late opens S09, S11, S25, S26). The P1 reversal, the 1−Kaplan–Meier fallacy, immortal time for `Escalate`, and the refusal to treat transfer as harmless dropout are real strengths. It is not numerically or internally reliable enough to score higher.

The sensitivity matrix misstates active survival at 72 hours. With transfers as competing events, survival after the last event at or before 72 (S08 at 68 hours, then S30 at 70) is about 42.2%, not 38.0%. With transfers censored it is about 48.3%, not 43.5%. Those printed figures are survival after S20’s resolution at 74 hours, which is past the horizon. The code’s `time <= 72` cutoff would not produce them, so sections 6 and 8 disagree. Scenario 3’s 47.4% breach rate is not recoverable from the stated definition. The code builds `s3_codes` and then never uses it; a code of 3 would also be invisible to an estimator that only updates on codes 1 and 2, so those cases would never leave the risk set. “AutoCheck-first 33.3%” matches the crude 4/12 count, not the cumulative incidence (about 37%), and “transfers = 37.5%” is the crude 3/8 eventual share, not the incidence by 72 hours (about 28%). Those quantities are placed in a CIF table.

Definitions wobble as soon as they are applied. Estimand 1 is formally \(1-F_{\text{Resolve}}(72)\), but S06 and S08 are then reassigned between failure, competing exit, and censoring inside the same estimand. Estimand 4 is that CIF identity, not another breach probability. \(F(\infty)\) is written as if eventual resolution were identified, although censored mass remains after the last event. S03 is placed in the second `Review` at hour 72; only total duration is observed, and the gap is 0, so the stage at 72 is unknown. `escalation_timing` is listed as extracted from prefixes; the prefixes give order, not clock time. The Mermaid model omits observed transitions (`Screen→AutoCheck`, `AutoCheck→Review`) and calls `AutoCheck→Resolve` instant. A semi-Markov stage model is recommended as the secondary analysis even though sojourn times are not in the extract.

Section 7 undoes the leakage argument. Landmark alerts are given invented risks (about 15%, 30%, above 55%, 70%, and 80%). The claim that, at 48 hours, rework or escalation implies a breach probability above 70% cannot be computed: escalation and rework times are unknown, and using the final flags is the immortal-time leak section 5 forbids. P3’s 20% resolution incidence is real, but it is driven heavily by early rejection and withdrawal; “priority starvation” and an automated scheduler are not in the data. No interval or even a qualitative sampling-error statement accompanies subgroup percentages from 8–17 cases, so 29.2% versus 31.6%, and 30.4% versus 31.3%, are presented as operational contrasts when they are small relative to noise. The gap>24 hour sensitivity is vacuous at 72 hours: every such open case is already older than 72.

The implementation does keep opens in the risk set, separates two transfer encodings, and stratifies by cohort and priority. The PM4Py call as written passes a list to a string parser and then assumes integer case ids; variant counting is only loosely a control-flow feature. The hand-rolled Aalen–Johansen recursion itself is the right estimator and is more careful than the prose numbers attached to it.
