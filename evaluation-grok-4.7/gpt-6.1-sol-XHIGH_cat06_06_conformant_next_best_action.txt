The answer is a strong, control-first analysis: A and B are correctly excluded, second review is not replaced by rescreening or self-certification, P-901 is treated as a real spillover, and the approval and breach ranges are not naively multiplied. It is not nearly flawless.

The main logical gap is timing consistency. C is timed as 40–90 minutes only after unquantified post-validation analyst work, while D’s 75–155 minutes is a from-now envelope that assumes that work is already finished and that S4 reviews as slowly as S2. Those envelopes are then used to call C the shortest route. The sequential trace (R17, then S2) also sits poorly with “secure S2” immediately: interrupting P-901 before the handoff can impose the 0.12–0.28 breach increase during time S2 cannot yet use. The minute-50 “latest start” arithmetic ignores that same predecessor.

Rule 6 requires a second officer before approval or execution, not before rejection. Action F’s 10–20 minutes only makes sense on that reading, but “required disposition reviews” never says whether rejection can occur without S2/S4. That ambiguity matters because it is the difference between a fast conditional control and a slow one.

Smaller defects: the continuation column is mostly durations; the primary recommendation adds optional parallel screening that is not the ranked plan; and the 35-minute reranking trigger omits the need for predecessor work to be ready. Benefit and harm cells repeatedly say the expectation is unknown, so the ranking is only partly connected to the stated loss ranges.
