4.1

The write-up covers every required section and frames the right tension—specialization versus pooling, SLA tails versus fairness—but its quantitative core is not reliable enough to support the recommendation.

The offered-load analysis reverses the operating picture. Mixture means are computed as ordinary midpoint plus probability times complex midpoint, omitting the ordinary weight `(1-p)`. Skill demand counts only the single-skill case types, so ME and ES branches are missing from Mechanical, Electrical, and Software load. The same shift minutes are then treated as dedicated capacity for every skill a person can perform, which triple-counts Ada, Chen, Deepa, and the others. Break and shift arithmetic is wrong in several places (Ada’s break is 30 minutes, not 45; Deepa’s shift is 8 hours, not 7; Emil’s break is not removed). Emil is qualified for Validation at 1.00 and is dropped from that capacity. There is no time-block balance, so the afternoon cliff and the post-18:00 mechanical coverage gap never appear. The claim that the base day has no bottleneck does not follow from the data; a shared-capacity account is tight even before multipliers, handovers, and coordination.

Those errors carry into the scenario claims. U1 cannot turn Intake into a bottleneck under the author’s own spare-capacity numbers. U2 is described as doubling all 17 arrivals from 12:00–16:00, while the code only doubles the E and ES shares and renormalizes. U3 is treated as a software-only loss even though Chen is also the fast Electrical resource through the middle of the day. U5’s 09:30–10:00 Intake outage is never quantified.

The comparison table is not an estimate from the model. It is a full policy-by-scenario grid of narrow numeric ranges, while the text says a simulation “would refine” them. Within that grid, Hybrid’s base failure mode is “reserved idle” and its maximum utilization is simultaneously attributed to Farah. The two stated objectives—weighted SLA violations plus p95, and minimum fairness across shocks—are never scored. Hybrid is then declared dominant. Reserving Farah for P1 is also a weak design relative to the skill matrix: she is the slowest diagnostic resource and works only 08:00–12:00.

The Python does not execute the required comparison. Service, skill, and demand tables stay as dicts and are then used with DataFrame indexing, `.loc`, `.at`, and `.iterrows`. Scenario edits mutate global skill and complex-tail data, so later policies inherit Chen’s absence and doubled tail probabilities. The loop is not a discrete-event simulation: each case is advance-booked to completion in arrival order, so priority is not a queue discipline. Parallel branches are flagged and then run sequentially. Preemption uses `datetime.now()` against a 2023 timestamp. Setup is charged from the same case’s previous activity and stacked on the cross-team penalty, which is not the resource-level rule. Hybrid eligibility includes unqualified resources. Overtime and the U5 outage are absent. Delay is first-start to last-complete, so wait before Intake is outside the SLA clock. The reported p95 is an average of per-case quantiles. Utilization sums full case duration onto each activity row. One seed is used, with no repeated-seed summary. The PM4Py calls are named, but `start_timestamp_key` is not passed, service versus waiting outputs are not labeled, and stage-level queue metrics are not separated.

Section 6 and the pilot plan are the strongest parts, and they still slip: Emil is proposed as an added validator although he already has the skill, preemption “at most once” is offered as a new control although it is already a process rule, and the pilot treats a shared-resource 20% split as a clean control while allowing an 80% P1 pass bar against a 90% target. The limits section does not walk back the bottleneck conclusion or the unsupported table.

Format compliance and qualitative coverage keep this above a failed attempt. The recommendation is not something an operator should trust.
