**3.5**

The write-up keeps the required six-part shape and does not flatly accuse anyone of fraud, but its load-bearing facts are wrong often enough that the prioritization and the report wording are not usable.

Four-eyes is treated as a property of different-person handovers (Carla–Gita, Dan–Hugo, Lena–Kim). The control is same-person execution of the two activities. The same-person override/justification cases (17) and the “may be role design” vendor-check/bank-change cases (31) are dropped, while co-working pairs are labeled high-severity breaches. Fast approvals add Buyer_Aline, whose 2.4h median sits on the 2.1h global median, and omit the 17-minute Lena–Kim bank-to-payment path and the 9-minute same-person justifications. Vendor concentration groups Marc–Iris (medium, ordinary) with Lena–Kim (72% family V-77x). Lena and Marc are staff, not vendors. Shared-account and `legacy_erp` bot/human mapping are collapsed into one Gita story; the 12 identical `quarter_close_upload` justification timestamps are later attached to Lena–Kim.

Benign explanations invent observations: no other approvers present, retrospective justification not required, `emergency_flag=False` for most Gita–Carla loops (the subgroup is 88% emergency), low price variance, an ERP migration schedule, and error-free manual entries. The actual rule is same-day override only if a different person justifies within three working days. Support and refutation tests are mostly missing; several “Evidence:” lines are stated as findings.

Priority is inverted. Carla–Gita is called high because of vendor concentration, which that subgroup does not have. Dan–Hugo (104 cases, 79% emergency, 10-minute approvals, 21% late justification) is absent from every tier, then cited in the report as a non-emergency example. Routine Aline–Elena and Boris–Felix volumes (about 500 cases, ~2.5h, ~4–5% emergency) are medium reviews, which treats ordinary co-working as risk. Bot_Match→AP_Kim (210; loop frequency 52) is both high priority and, mislabeled as Bot_PO→AP_Kim, low priority.

The PM4Py sketches would not run and would not answer the question. `filter_four_eyes_principle` is given an `activities` list instead of two activities and only one pair. Working-together is omitted. Subcontracting never sets n=2. `filter_event_attribute_values` and `filter_paths_performance` use nonexistent parameters. `min_value=8*60` keeps slower paths, and the “1 hour” filter is 60 seconds if the unit is seconds. Log-skeleton arguments are reversed. There is no DECLARE or skeleton rule for a different resource justifying within three working days, no service/waiting-time comparison, and source-system filters are not used to recompute violations.

Section 5 mostly lists reducers, one of them backwards: a justification minutes after approval is more concerning unless it is the known batch upload. “Contracts with Lena and Marc” repeats the employee/vendor mix-up. Bot failures do not explain 8–10 minute human PO approvals. Increase-concern tests (non-emergency same-person PO approval, bank change and payment by one person outside the V-77x ticket population, payment to a new account before a linked maintenance ticket, `entered_by` equal to `org:resource`) are absent.

The report is about 270 words and avoids a fraud finding, but its headline statistics are misread columns: 88% is Carla–Gita’s emergency-flag rate, not her share of emergency justifications; 16% is Lena–Kim’s emergency-flag rate, not her share of emergency approvals. It names Carla–Gita as most concerning while the attributes that resist an emergency explanation sit on Lena–Kim (low emergency, 68% high amount, 83% `manual_upload`, V-77x, fast bank-to-payment). It then explains Gita’s identical timestamps by the shared account, which is a different flag from the quarter-close spreadsheet batch.
