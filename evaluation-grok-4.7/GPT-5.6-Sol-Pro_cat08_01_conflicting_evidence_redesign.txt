The diagnosis is strong process-mining work: it refuses to add conditional medians (0.5 + 0.4 + 1.8 + 3.2), refuses to treat DFG counts as variants, separates token fitness 0.91 from 68% fitting traces, and keeps the 31% versus 9% cost gap and the segment complaint splits observational. The three redesigns are genuinely different mechanisms, each with a named sacrifice, and the pilot design correctly treats R1–R3 as non-randomizable constraints while watching right-censoring past the 180-hour post-dispatch tail.

The main failure is inside Redesign A. R2 only waives evidence-before-dispatch for Premium low-risk accessories; it does not authorize unrestricted fast approval, and it does not waive R1 when the claim is above EUR 400. The redesign nonetheless gives that whole segment a rapid auto-approval branch “using the explicit R2 exception,” then later forbids final fast approval for every claim above EUR 400 unless manual or fraud review has occurred. Those rules conflict, and the same collision hits Standard low-risk claims above EUR 400, which are told both to fast-approve after an evidence event and not to fast-approve at all. Mid-risk devices, which the process explicitly handles and which the outcome table never isolates (the five shares already sum to 100%), are never routed.

Stage 1’s go bar is not tied to one baseline. A 25–35% relative complaint reduction is a low hurdle against the 18% Standard high-value fast path and a high hurdle against the 6% reviewed path, while cycle time is compared only with already compliant reviewed cases. Redesign C also blurs a measurement rule (do not treat first close as success) with a process delay (hold close until the 70–180 hour complaint window has “matured”). Smaller slips: the portal effect is promoted from “could” to “estimated,” the 40–60 hour example is not in the evidence, and “approximately more than 10%” is not an operable threshold. A and C both consume the same two inspectors without a contention rule.
