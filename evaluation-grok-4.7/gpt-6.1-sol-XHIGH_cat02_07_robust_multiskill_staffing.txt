7.6

The capacity accounting is unusually careful and mostly right, but the decision-facing comparison is narrower and less clean than the write-up implies. Under a harsh reading this is strong analytical work with several real gaps, not a near-flawless answer.

The auditable load math holds. Uniform-mixture means (6.94, 26.30, 32.82, 38.50, 15.74), branch volumes (18.09 / 24.30 / 12.85), block loads, the 2,490-minute roster, the 2,241-minute 90% budget, the 109% / 121% ratios, the endpoint bounds near 2,065 and 3,376, and the U1–U5 raw totals are coherent. Multipliers are all at least 1, so under the uniform means the raw total is a lower bound on labor, and maximum overtime still misses it. The one-day cohort draining into empty later days is correctly treated as disguising instability. Assumptions are labeled, the two objectives are real and distinct, and the recommendation does not pretend the 90% SLAs are met.

The main analytical failure is that this capacity uncertainty never enters the policy comparison. The same section shows that the fairness constraint is feasible near the lower endpoints and badly infeasible at the uniform means, yet every SLA, delay, and staffing number used to pick a policy is generated only at those uniform means. The “add at least 533 minutes” result is an optimistic fluid bound inside that one assumption, not a result that survives the range the answer itself treats as decision-relevant. U2 has the same problem: weight-renormalization barely changes total load (about 2,729 versus 2,721), while the absolute E/ES reading, which the answer calculates at about 3,188, is never dispatched. Bottleneck migration under that shock is therefore untested.

Policy A is a weak specialist strawman. It forces fork/join and the coordination penalty even when the branches cannot overlap, locks Ada and Ben out of work they do at multiplier 1.00, and, with Chen absent, has Deepa stop Validation before 16:00 rather than add Electrical. Pooling then wins partly by construction. Policy C confounds an admission cap, a 10-minute parallel hurdle, scarce-skill charges, and a conservative preemption rule. The answer notices the confounding and still recommends B, whose parallel test takes any myopic elapsed-time gain and ignores queue opportunity cost—the trade-off Section 2 says can waste scarce resources. The later “guarded pooling” triggers are unsimulated, so the policy actually recommended is not the policy in the table.

Smaller defects: calling the 14+80+35 path an “optimistic” sum is the wrong word for upper endpoints of the complex tails; the no-multiplier, no-handover reading is optimistic, the durations are not. Reported p90s are means of seed-wise quantiles, and utilization is day-0 only; both are disclosed, but the table is still easy to over-read. In the simulator, preemption frees a resource without binding it to the preempting case, in-progress work is shift-locked by `pin`, and the Farah “215.6 versus 216” comparison assigns her every morning Software minute at 1.40 even though that is a bound, not a required schedule. PM4Py handling is a genuine strength: interval semantics, negative case waiting under overlap, segment-versus-operation service, and handover-network misuse are stated correctly.

The arithmetic and the refusal to certify an infeasible roster are good enough to stay well above a merely adequate analysis. The untested service bounds, the mild-only U2, and the stacked policy design are enough to keep it well below a nearly flawless one.
