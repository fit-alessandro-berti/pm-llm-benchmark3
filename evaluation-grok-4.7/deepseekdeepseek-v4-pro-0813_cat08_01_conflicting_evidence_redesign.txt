7.4

The final write-up meets the required structure and is well grounded in the given directly-follows counts, timings, conformance rules, segment outcomes, and resource constraints. The three redesigns are genuinely different, trade-offs are explicit, and the fast path is not treated as universally good or bad. Those strengths keep it well above a merely adequate answer.

It is not nearly flawless. The main process-mining weakness is treating path-outcome gaps as if they were treatment effects: the 22% versus 5% and 14.1% versus 3.3% complaint contrasts are used as expected results of routing and of the pre-dispatch gate, without allowing for case-mix confounding. A second concrete error is using the 12.5% rework-repair rate, which applies to repaired devices, as a benefit of a gate that sits only before replacement dispatch. The experiment plan misreads the fraud/risk → fast-approval p90 of 38 hours as the activity’s current wait, and it sets a “p90 does not worsen beyond 120 hours” go line when the only high-value baselines given are medians. Redesign A also conflicts with itself: R1 is satisfied by manual review or fraud review, but a later rule sends high-value cases specifically into the two-person fraud queue. The comparison table then names unclear/reopened claims as a best fit for that routing design, even though the same answer says A does not fix the evidence loop that already produces the 140-hour tail. Post-close reopens and the 140 fast-approval → complaint edges are diagnosed more weakly than the dispatch-complaint tail, so some conformance and rework mass is left outside the redesigns.
