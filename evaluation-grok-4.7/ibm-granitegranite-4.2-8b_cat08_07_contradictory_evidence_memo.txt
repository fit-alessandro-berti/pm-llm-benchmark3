The recommended direction is sound: do not scale Redesign R nationally, do not roll it back wholesale, and instead keep coordinator ownership where simple low-risk houses improved, stop survey-skipping and billing-before-activation, restore permit control for older buildings, and measure before any wider rollout. All six required sections exist, the main rates and model scores are mostly cited correctly, and the five metric conflicts are at least addressed.

The final recommendation is still not reliable enough for an executive decision. Simple-house results are repeatedly stated as a “median time of 6 days” and “complaints 1pp”; the evidence is a 6.0-day median reduction and a 1pp complaint decline. “30% medium-risk share” is attached to simple houses and is not a real segment. Apartment median duration improved by 2 days even while p90 worsened by 12; the memo treats those cases as median failures. Segment “second visit +5–7pp” is not in the evidence and matches the complaint changes instead. The survey-skip rule applied to medium-risk addresses, yet the memo credits it with the simple-house gains, which the temporal profile ties to merged validation and scheduling.

Process-mining interpretation contradicts itself. Low precision (0.51) with high fitness means the discovered model is too permissive; calling that overfitting is the wrong mechanism, and it is then linked too directly to alignment moves. One directly-follows edge is reversed: appointment scheduling to installation visit rose from 54% to 64%, not the opposite. A process-mining interpretation is also placed in the facts column.

Option C is misstated as still deploying survey skipping; C suspends skipping and post-billing activation. Coordinator scope flips between “keep it in the pilot” and “simple houses only,” while business and rural segments, the merged scheduling step, 93% coordinator load, and absence backup never become explicit actions. Case-mix confounding and 30-day complaint censoring in a 6-week pilot are not used. Thresholds are arbitrary, leave a 15–20% gap, and the cost gate disappears between the memo and the final recommendation. The value ranking treats almost every measurement as relevant only to the skip rule, so the absence test is undervalued relative to scaling coordinators, and a 4-week window cannot support 30- or 60-day outcomes. The memo also runs well past the 700-word limit and repeats garbled figures.
