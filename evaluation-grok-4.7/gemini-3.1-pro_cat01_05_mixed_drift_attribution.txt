5.5

The write-up keeps the right broad sequence — March label/provenance break, May rework, later queue growth, October mix shift, December confound — and it hits the required headings, column sets, seven rules, and the two-part code split. It is not reliable enough to act on. Several required quantitative objects are wrong or unidentified, a few causal claims are stronger than the aggregates allow, and the monitoring design would miss the drift it just described.

## 1. Drift timeline
Six intervals meet the minimum and the onset vocabulary is present, but the timeline is one mechanism per date and skips the contrasts that actually separate mechanisms. Mapped fitness is not “stable” in March: it moves from 0.93 to 0.90 and keeps falling to 0.86 in July. About 140 of 640 March cases still emit `Validate Eligibility`, so the migration is partial. June is dated as the performance onset even though p90 is already 84 in May, before the 15 June staffing cut; June is only a partial-capacity month. July ties the `Assess → Request Info` peak to the priority rule. That rule reorders the queue; it does not create document rework. October is labeled pure population drift, but senior-assessment intensity changes, not just mix: January has 4 senior assessments per 100 cases at 21% high complexity, while October has 22 per 100 at 41% high complexity. Historical recording of senior assessment was already inconsistent, so this spike is also a routing or logging candidate. August (supplier share down, p90 still up) and November (worst volume, complexity, rework, and p90) are absent. Resource/organizational drift never appears as a dimension. Strengths of 85–95 are high relative to those confounds.

## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation
Length is inside 180–280 words, and the main claim is right: the raw-versus-mapped gap is a semantic/provenance failure, while post-May rework and cycle time survive label repair. The section still overreaches. Duplicate `Open Case` events in the snippet are a prefix (`Open Case → Register`, 26 events, about 4% of March cases), not demonstrated false loops or parallelisms. “Physical delays” and “directly correlating” treat co-movement of monthly totals as case-level causation. The residual mapped-fitness decline, which is the evidence that conformance drift remains after the mapping, is not stated.

## 3. Quantitative drift decomposition
Most January-relative percentages check out. March raw fitness does not: \((0.55-0.92)/0.92 = -40.2\%\), not \(-39.1\%\) (that figure matches September’s 0.56). The stated MAE versus January is correct only for March (\(3/7 \approx 0.43\)). The same measure gives May \(40/7 \approx 5.71\), July \(59/7 \approx 8.43\), September \(4.00\), October \(64/7 \approx 9.14\), and December \(36/7 \approx 5.14\), not 5.43, 8.29, 3.71, 9.29, and 5.00. The code later implements the correct MAE, so the prose and the reproducible block disagree. The omitted-edge note is also inconsistent: zeros were not included in the seven-edge average; including shared zeros would shrink MAE, while excluded edges that actually changed would enlarge it. The decision edge and the senior-assessment edge are compositional, so October’s distance double-counts one reroute.

The cycle-time split (\(>60/25/10/<5\)) is a manufactured partition. A single extra 0.6 h of assess service against a median rise of about 13 h by July–August supports a small direct service share only for one assessment touch; repeated assessments are ignored. May’s median rise (31.5 → 36.5) occurs before the staffing cut, so “queueing \(>60\%\), driven by June staffing” assigns the early slice to a mechanism that has not started. Case mix at “~10%” is not identified: within-class cycle times are not in the tables. Logging “two change points” are both 1 March. The global-versus-local conclusion is the strongest paragraph in the section and matches the staggered series.

## 4. Competing mechanism hypotheses
All eight required hypotheses and the required columns are present, and supplier, staffing, and December confounding are handled with some care. The table then breaks its own evidence logic. Seasonal drift has “evidence against: None,” despite the pre-October complexity climb (21% → 29% by August), the September drop as supplier share falls, the November peak beyond October, and no year-over-year series. Non-random resource missingness is listed against the missingness hypothesis; concentrated missingness on migrated activities is support for systematic measurement failure. The priority row’s “support” is the aggregate rise that the brief itself says cannot reveal redistribution. Interaction’s “hard to isolate” is a limitation of the design, not evidence against interaction. Rank 1 is a certain local explanation scored above the interaction hypothesis that better accounts for performance.

## 5. Behavioral, population, and logging discrimination
The required segments appear, but the staffing row is N/A in every pattern column, and the priority row never states the signature that would discriminate it: within-class control flow stable, high-complexity wait down, standard wait up. Senior assessment’s logging pattern is N/A despite the brief’s statement that this activity was recorded inconsistently. Several “population” cells restate the segment’s size instead of a pattern in the named artifact. Supplier Z’s pre-existing complexity is noted, which is necessary, and then left unused in the May attribution.

## 6. Segmentation plan
Supplier, complexity, epoch, and variant are reasonable axes, and the support floors are in a plausible range for a 600–800 case month. The variant row’s bias cell describes rare-path loss rather than conditioning on an endogenous path. The recommended primary population — standard, non-supplier-Z — does not isolate the priority rule. That rule’s burden falls on standard cases, and dropping high complexity removes the renewal regime that produces the worst month. Non-Z is also a different region/customer mix. No resource, team, or region cut is specified, so the staffing and supplier-geography mechanisms cannot be checked inside this plan.

## 7. Monitoring architecture
Eleven monitors, distinct owners, and non-identical rules satisfy the coverage ask. Calibration does not. Mapped fitness never falls below 0.86, so “fitness \(< 0.85\)” would stay silent through the real conformance dip; the monitor also does not say whether the statistic is raw or mapped. Missing-resource \(> 2\%\) is true in every month from March onward (4.8–6.5%), so the alert is permanent after the known migration. “EMD \(>\) threshold” and “significant deviation” are unnamed thresholds. Benjamini–Hochberg is invoked for rules that have no p-values. Seasonality is applied to volume and case mix only; cycle time, rework, senior assessment, and the DFG baseline are left on a January–February reference that will refire every renewal season. Duplicate `Open Case` rate and within-class waiting are not monitored.

## 8. Reproducible Python and PM4Py plan
Part A embeds a reduced monthly table (no supplier, complexity, median, fast-path, missing-resource, or duplicate-open rates) and validates only non-nullness. The change-point rule flags every month from March through December, because mapped-minus-raw fitness stays above 0.3 after the migration (0.35 in March, 0.32 in December). That is a level detector and contradicts the multi-change-point argument. Baseline-relative change is computed for p90 only.

Part B names the requested wrappers, copies the frame before relabeling, and can be left unexecuted. `discover_monthly_dfg` does not bucket by month. `compare_mappings` relabels one column and returns; it does not compare logs and does not drop duplicated `Open Case`. A dataframe `.replace` path is not a comparison of raw versus normalized behavior.

## 9. Provisional attribution
Word count is in range, and the confidence tiers plus revision triggers are the right shape. The placements are too certain for unidentified aggregates. May’s supplier-share jump (18% → 43%) is not the same fact as worse bundles; Z was already more complex, so aggregate rework can rise from mix alone. “High confidence” that incomplete bundles caused the structural rework is exactly the test the revision sentence says has not been run. October senior-assessment routing is not implied by the complexity jump alone, for the rate ratio above. “Queue waiting exploded (seen in the p90 spike)” equates a tail of total cycle time with a queue. The performance trough is November, not June–July. Autumn rework returns to 29% in November while supplier Z is 27%, not the mid-40s, so the late rework hump is not the May supplier signature. “Non-linear” degradation is asserted. The revision conditions are necessary and still too narrow: a senior-assessment recording change, a supplier-attribution change, or a different prior-year renewal curve would also force a rewrite.

## 10. Monitoring decision rules
Seven rules use the requested IF / THEN / BECAUSE shape, and rules 1, 2, and 5 match real mechanism splits. Rule 3 treats a stable assess service time plus a higher p90 as staffing; the same pair is also what May rework and an arrival surge produce. Rule 6’s 2% missing-resource trigger is below the post-migration base rate, so it pages continuously. Rule 7 sends assessors to refresher training when rework stays high after a supplier “recovery,” although the brief says backlog remained and the observed December action was overtime plus a document-quality campaign. Residual mix, residual backlog, and appropriate requests for missing documents fit the same observable.

The account a careful reader can keep is the staggered, mechanism-specific timeline and the warning not to read March’s raw fitness collapse as a process breakdown. The distances, the cycle-time percentages, the October and May certainty, the primary segment rationale, and the alert thresholds need to be recomputed before this diagnosis or this monitoring design is used.
