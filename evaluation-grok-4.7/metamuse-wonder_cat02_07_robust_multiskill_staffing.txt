6.5

The prose is a serious capacity-and-policy analysis with the required structure, labeled assumptions, two real objectives (tail/SLA and fairness/robustness), auditable base-load arithmetic, and a recommendation that does not chase base-case mean delay. It is not nearly flawless. Several numerical and implementation failures break internal consistency, and the Python scaffold does not implement the policies the narrative ranks.

The service expectations in §1 check out (Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.30, Electrical 32.82, Software 38.50, Validation 15.74). Day totals are also right: about 18.1 / 24.3 / 12.9 diagnostic branch-cases, ~2,720 base person-minutes versus 2,490 shift-minus-break minutes (2,670 with full overtime). The block table (444/480, 586/600, 455/510, 651/570, 392/210, 192/120) is reproducible, and the caveats that these ratios ignore multipliers, skill mismatch, penalties, and carry-over are the right ones. Parallel versus sequential critical-path gaps (~18 min for ME, ~25 min for ES before multipliers) are correctly derived. U1 scaling and U4 tail means (29.6 / 37.1 / 44.5) are correct. Bottleneck migration—morning Software, 14:00–16:00 volume, evening eligibility (Emil cannot do Mechanical; only Emil after 18:00)—follows from the roster.

The U2 software claim does not. With the author’s own fixed-volume renormalization in 14:00–16:00 (E 60, ES 24, total 142), Electrical-involved cases move from 6.2 to ~7.3, but Software-involved cases move from 3.0 to 10×42/142 ≈ 2.96, not “about 3.5.” Software load is essentially flat; the text says it rises. Absolute doubling of E/ES counts would raise Software cases to ~4.2, which is also not 3.5. The electrical conclusion survives; the software half of that sentence is an arithmetic error, and it is the number used to describe bottleneck migration. U3 overtime capacity is also slightly wrong: an absent Chen cannot contribute 30 overtime minutes, so 2,040 + 5×30 = 2,190, not 2,220.

§4 is the weakest analytical section. The prompt allows ranges, and the ±25% / ±40% bands are stated, but the minute and SLA figures are not computed from the offered-load identity or from the code. They are then described as estimates “under A1–A7 and the Section 8 scaffold logic.” That claim is false. The scaffold never produces those cells. The bands also overlap so heavily (base P1 SLA 50–70% versus 60–78%; means overlap in every row) that “P-C wins on P1, p90, fairness, and resilience” is not identified by the table. Calling P-B “typically 5–10% faster on mean” treats overlapping ranges as a measured effect. A further definition error: P1’s 120-minute clock cannot be repaired by next-day resumption, yet the SLA columns are defined as if that assumption applied to P1. P2/P3 can depend on an unsupplied next-day roster; P1 cannot.

§3’s dispatch rules are concrete enough to audit (tiers, overflow waits, once-only preemption, parallel threshold, validation holdback). The reserve is undersized relative to the author’s own demand: 14:00–16:00 already expects ~1.8 P1 arrivals, and an ME/ES P1 may need two resources, but the rule protects one Tier-1 slot. §5 is appropriately non-dominant and honest that U3/U5 are capacity failures, yet the trigger story and “materially better tail” language are stronger than §4’s distinguishability. §6 and §7 are the most reliable parts: gaming, P3 starvation, Farah’s 08:00–12:00 limit, preemption budget, validation displacement, and the case-level versus stage-level PM4Py warning are all on target. Required log attributes and the named PM4Py calls are present. “Mathematical/Mechanical” is a stray typo, not a design flaw.

§8 meets the surface checklist and labels case-level service/waiting correctly, but the mechanisms that are supposed to separate the policies are mostly absent or wrong, and not all of those omissions are declared.

- Cases are booked FCFS, in full, at arrival. Priority, deadlines, aging, and the P1 reservation never change sequence or resource choice, so the simulator cannot support the §4 P1-SLA ranking.
- P-A’s preference lists do not matter: every eligible resource remains a candidate and the code still picks minimum predicted finish. Strict specialization is not implemented.
- The cross-team penalty compares the current activity with `prev_act`, which the caller sets to that same activity before the call. The +5 minute term never fires.
- The preemption term is attached to the P1 case, not the victim, and is not added to flow time. That inverts the stated rule.
- `avail_from` never stops at shift end, so after-hours work is unlimited, then still counted in mean delay and SLA. The 30-minute overtime cap is not applied.
- The priority-mix line subtracts 4 points from P2 in every scenario, including base, so P3 is inflated whenever U1 is not active.
- U5 sets the arrival clock to 10:00, which deletes outage wait from the SLA clock instead of delaying only Intake.
- P-B/P-C can add the 8-minute coordination penalty even if both branches land on one resource.
- The Tier-3 `pass` branch is a no-op; the +20 penalty always applies, including in the scenarios the `pass` pretends to exempt.
- Three seeds are averaged with no dispersion, on a P1 sample of only a few cases per day.

The closing “OMITTED” line discloses interrupt-resume preemption, breaks inside a task, next-day restart, P-D, and overtime workflow. It does not disclose that priority dispatch, specialization, shift caps, the cross-team penalty, and the U5 clock are wrong. A scaffold may omit mechanisms; it may not claim to represent policies and scenario metrics it does not compute.

§9 is sound: ranges are not distributions, uniforms are an assumption, penalty occupancy is unidentified, and the shadow-then-stepped-wedge pilot with a P1 override and stopping rules matches the risk. That does not repair the quantitative gap between the load math, the comparison table, and the code.

Net: strong problem framing and mostly correct static load analysis, undermined by one clear U2 arithmetic error, an untraceable policy table that overclaims its link to the simulator, and a reproducible program whose policy contrast is largely illusory.
