6.5

The required six-part structure is complete, the hard constraints are respected, and the main conclusion is right: early slowness is largely case-mix, later speed with rising errors is largely queue pressure, and learning, fatigue, circadian effects, batching, and bot corrections are conditional rather than sole causes. Interventions 3, 5, and 7 are preferred, and punishment on raw late-shift errors is rejected.

That reading is undercut by concrete misuses of the tables. Sequence-bin figures (complexity 0.72 and 41% high-priority; 0.61 and 34%) are treated as every shift’s first hour and as the Day-shift starter’s mix. The shift table contradicts this: first-hour high-complexity share is 48%, 31%, 35%, and 56%. Early shift’s last two hours also have the lowest error rate (4.2%), so the claim that Early staff would be punished for higher errors is not supported. The circadian “evidence against” cites Early shift’s last-two-hour mean of 12.7 minutes, which is midday, not the early-morning hour. “60–70% driven by upstream conditions” is invented. Night staff are called the most fatigued without evidence, and Night-shift 15.2 minutes is labeled as night-staff service time.

The methods are similarly loose. The “complexity adjustment” snippet divides by activity-family mean and never uses complexity. `service_time` is used although it is not in the log and is never derived. Filtering case duration does not remove event-level batch outliers. The position effect is said to shrink “per the WIP×complexity table,” which contains no position dimension. Micro-breaks are called one of the strongest evidence-based options even though the same answer says that window is confounded by WIP.

The dashboard has twelve indicators and covers service time, WIP, rework, errors, complexity, batching, handover, and load fairness, but it omits waiting time, which was explicitly required. Arrow-stripped ranges (“18.8  13.6”) also leave several comparisons unclear.
