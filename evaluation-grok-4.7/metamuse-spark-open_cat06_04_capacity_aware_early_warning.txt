The answer meets the required structure and the core qualitative tests: I-03 is refused because the swarm is past its effective stage, I-05 is suppressed as an unchanged refresh while I-07 is re-opened on the new log, and spillover is carried through eligibility, ranking, scenarios, fairness, and monitoring. Segment calibration, observational-versus-causal risk, and label feedback are treated explicitly, with concrete safeguards rather than slogans.

Decision-theoretic coherence still breaks in several places. The stated rule makes a case swarm-worthy only when midpoint benefit exceeds expected spillover, and applies a 0.75 haircut to Strategic effect estimates, but the selected portfolio does not follow those rules. I-01 is chosen despite a negative base marginal value (about €1,685 versus roughly €2,400 spillover). I-04 is called uniquely robust only by skipping the Strategic haircut that would put its low-bound result near or below zero after spillover. Section 3 then says the robust action is one or two swarms, so the official three-case decision and the later analysis disagree.

Operationally, naming I-02 as the standby for a slot held until 15:00 is too late: at 12:00 it has 3.0 hours to the deadline. Arithmetic is also not clean. I-03’s upper bound is €1,200 − €850 = +€350, not −€250. Several “net after spillover” ranges mix base and optimistic assumptions, the optimistic breach band “0.67–0.93” is not derived, and the adverse “0.46” parenthetical is ambiguous. Expected benefit is sometimes reduced by the full false-alarm burden as if every selected case were certainly a false alarm. The claim that 50 randomized decisions a month would tighten effects to ±0.05 is not statistically credible.

Coverage is strong; the allocation, timing, and audit trail are not reliable enough for a higher score.
