7.5

The diagnosis is unusually well grounded. Frequency counts, edge medians and p90s, fitness 0.91 versus 68% fitting traces, the three alignment deviations, the 31% versus 9% cost collapse, complaint rates of 9.2% / 14.1% / 3.3%, segment gaps, the 26% repeat-evidence rate, the 82% review cliff, and the idle-inspector fact are almost all reproduced correctly. The answer keeps the fast path 9% cheaper after rework, separates Premium low-risk (where slowing the case is a bad trade) from high-value (where the complaint gap is 12–17 points), and refuses a fake ROI. The three redesigns hit different levers — authorization before fast approval, a one-pass evidence lane, and a parts-master release gate — and each states a sacrifice. The experiment order (parts gate first, evidence lane second, rule change only inside freed capacity), the 180-hour episode window, and the demand to watch receiving queues are real process-mining reasoning.

Those strengths do not make the reasoning tight.

The summed fast-path medians are about 5.9 hours (0.5 + 0.4 + 1.8 + 3.2). Calling that sketch a match for the Standard low-risk fast median of 13 hours is false; it is only close to the Premium low-risk figure of 8 hours. Summing medians is also not a case duration.

Compliance is over-mapped. “Dispatch replacement without risk review” is not, as written, an R1 breach: R1 allows Manual eligibility review or Fraud/risk review, and it is triggered by value above EUR 400, not by the high-value label. “Customer complaint/reopen after close” is a move-on-log; it becomes an R3 breach only if the reopen then closes or is denied without manual review. Grouping all three deviations as direct conflicts with R1–R3 blurs that. The later claim that most high-value fast approvals lack an R1 token upgrades “frequent” to an unmeasured majority. Calling all 2,550 auto-screen fast approvals an illegal highway does the same thing: Premium low-risk dispatch is an explicit R2 exception, and the nonconforming share is unknown.

The capacity sentence confuses the control limit with the overload band. Spare hours below 82% are correctly called unknown, and then the 15–35% inflow shock is judged “large” relative to the 18 points between 82% and 100%. That band is the region after the queue has already broken. A relative inflow change and a utilization-point gap are not the same unit.

Redesign A’s mechanism borrows the 5–6% reviewed/repair complaint rates (and the 3.3% full-repair rate) for a path that still ends in Dispatch replacement. The performance profile ties the long complaint tail on that activity to wrong part selection, which A deliberately leaves in place. Fraud review can enforce eligibility; it does not select the SKU. The table makes this looser by quoting entry waits of median 8–15 hours and p90 28–60 hours. Those upper ends are Manual eligibility review → Repair quote, the edge A says clear defects should avoid. Section 2’s own figures are median 8 or 13 hours and p90 28 or 44 hours.

Failure mode A then says that once manual utilization crosses 82%, Premium low-risk cases stretch toward the 24-hour credit line. Under A those cases stay on the unlimited auto-screen and never enter that queue. Delay reaches them only if the new rules misroute them. That coupling is not stated.

Section 7 says the log flatters fast approval because Quality/compliance close happens before the complaint edges. In the frequency graph, complaint is a direct alternative successor: 140 from Fast approval and 260 from Dispatch replacement. After-close reopen is a separate conformance deviation, not the structure of those edges. The 180-hour episode window is still the right remedy. The abandonment threshold is not: “320 of 390” is a log-wide count split, and a subset pilot cannot be scored against a count.

The piece is therefore a strong, segment-aware redesign with several local inference errors. It is not nearly flawless.
