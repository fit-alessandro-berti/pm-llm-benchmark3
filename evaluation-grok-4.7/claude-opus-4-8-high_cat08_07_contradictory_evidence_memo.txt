7.4

The memo is a coherent, decision-first product: it selects a segment-gated hybrid, refuses both national scale and a full rollback, separates fact / interpretation / assumption in a real table, and covers every required conflict, alternative, and measurement. The recommendation would still be directionally usable. It is not nearly flawless. Several statements are wrong or internally inconsistent, and those errors sit on the claims the decision rests on.

The clearest factual break is calling simple low-risk houses “the majority” while citing a 34% pilot share in the same breath, then repeating “the majority” when rejecting rollback. Thirty-four percent is the largest slice, not a majority. That inflates how much volume the favorable result covers. “Manual activation and second visits roughly doubled” is also wrong as a pair: manual activation went from 7% to 15%, second visits from 11% to 18%.

The median-versus-tail explanation is too coarse. Medians improved in four of five segments (simple houses −6 days, apartments −2, business −3, rural −1); only old buildings worsened at the median (+1). The conflict is not “the easy majority got faster and the hard minority got slower.” Apartments themselves improved at the median and deteriorated at p90 (+12 days). The write-up misses that within-segment split.

Precision is defined incorrectly. Precision of a discovered model is extra behavior relative to the log, not “behavior we never intend.” The sentence that the model “must allow a large space of variants to stay fit” mixes observed variants (fitness) with unobserved combinations (precision loss). Generalization falling from 0.70 to 0.56 is never used.

The graduation rule is logically inconsistent. “First-time-right within ~1pp of baseline” is a two-sided band, so the simple-house result that is already +3pp would fail the bar the memo uses to justify scaling that segment. Complaints are correctly framed as “not higher.” The same section also says post-billing activation is paused “everywhere,” then limited to “any non-trivial case,” while the alternative-E write-up keeps billing-after-activation only for risky cases. Those are three different policies.

Mechanism attribution is stronger than the design. The pilot bundled coordinator ownership, the validate/schedule merge, survey skipping, and post-billing activation. Handover reduction is directly observed; the simple-house speed gain is not identified as coming from the two pieces labeled “clearly good” and “proven.” Coordinator utilization of 93% was measured while coordinators owned the full book, yet the staffing and absence-backup action treats that rate as if it still applies after the model is cut back to about a third of volume. Survey-team utilization falling from 78% to 52% while the second-visit team rises from 66% to 92% never enters the action plan, so reinstating surveys has no capacity consequence.

Value-of-information ranking overclaims and mixes sequence with value. Full downstream cost is ranked first because “cost sign determines go/no-go,” but the memo’s own bar treats tail, first-time-right, complaints, and billing-before-activation as binding even if cost is favorable. Putting rule validation above a randomized skip trial because the trial “depends on” the validation is an implementation sequence, not a ranking of how much the decision would change. The evidence table ties uncertainty in the complaint and first-time-right rates to cost data stopping after week 4. Those rates are reported as measured; the incomplete object is the all-in cost estimate.

Coverage gaps that the process evidence made available: SLA breach (13% to 16%, better for simple houses), NPS (+21 to +17), the installation-to-activation slowdown for apartments, the failed-visit-to-second-visit delay in high-utilization regions, activation specialists’ unclear ownership after billing start, and distinct business-dispute and rural-rescheduling mechanisms. Business (8%) and rural (11%) are folded into a house-versus-apartment/old-building gate.

Section 4 also steps out of the memo and cites the assignment constraints as a reason alternative A is unacceptable. The strategic stance itself — do not scale the bundle, do not discard the simple-house gain, stop billing-before-activation and missed permits, buy better measurement before a wider rule — is defensible. The grade is held down by the majority error, the threshold bug, the bundled-cause overclaim, the precision slip, and the cost-decides-everything ranking.
