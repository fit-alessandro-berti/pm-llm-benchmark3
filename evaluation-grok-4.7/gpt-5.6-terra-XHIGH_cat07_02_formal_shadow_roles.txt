The role analysis is strong, numerically careful, and aligned with the required structure. It separates activity frequency from authority, uses complexity and substitution evidence, and treats Giorgio, Mateo, and Erik as overlays with both benefits and risks. The main conclusions hold.

The evidence cell for Erik overstates his position. His median complexity, 0.86, is the highest, and 55 of 166 cases are critical, but the critical-case counts are higher for Mateo (76) and Hana (62). “Critical-case exposure is highest” therefore blurs rate, median severity, and volume.

The PM4Py section is coherent as an illustration and correctly uses `format_dataframe` (`case_id`) and `Role.originator_importance`, but it would not run cleanly against current PM4Py. Handover, working-together, and activity-similarity results are NetworkX graphs, while `edge_frame` assumes pair-to-weight dictionaries. Passing `activity_key` / `resource_key` into the high-level SNA wrappers is also unreliable. `substitution_activity_counts` is built and never joined to the role table, so the required substitution join stays at a coarse case share. Filtering `escalation_level` for the string “critical” at event level conflates that field with the case-complexity buckets and can create artificial handovers when only some events remain.

Smaller gaps: Nora’s “exception logging and handoff metadata” is not in the matrix. Amira–Giorgio similarity of 0.84 is never used, despite being direct evidence for Giorgio’s front-office base. Tenure, certifications, and Lina’s late shift are largely unused, so the shift-coverage claim misses that late simple closures sit with the temporary agent tied to the entitlement-note reopens. Hana–Mateo merging is presented as a likely frequency-cluster error even though their reported similarity is only 0.54.

Those defects keep the answer short of nearly flawless, without undermining the staffing, authority, and shadow-role argument.
