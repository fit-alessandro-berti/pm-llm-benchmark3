5.7

The memo reaches the right strategic conclusion — hybrid Option E, with no national scale and no full rollback — and it covers every required section, including fitness/precision, alignments, DFG shifts, resource networks, and segment splits. It is not a reliable decision document. Several claims a manager would act on are wrong, internally contradicted, or invented.

The executive memo misstates the choice set. It calls D an “uncontrolled” experiment; D is a controlled regional experiment, and section 4 later describes it correctly. It says C loses the handover benefit; C keeps coordinator ownership, which is the mechanism tied to fewer handovers, and section 4 admits C captures that benefit. It says national scale would “likely” cause a 16 percentage-point complaint increase. The observed change is 6.1% to 9.8% (+3.7 pp), the worst segment change is +7 pp, and the pilot mix is already skewed toward apartments and older buildings. That figure is not in the evidence.

Process-mining details are mishandled where the task specifically requires them. The manual-activation edge runs Manual activation → Billing start (6% to 16%). The evidence table reverses it to billing followed by manual activation. Low precision (0.51) means the model allows too many paths; calling that “overfitting” contradicts the same paragraph’s permissive-state-space account and the mining team’s reading. The permit-check diagnostic is a move-on-model: an expected permit check is absent for older apartments. Treating it as an allowed-but-unobserved path reverses the conformance problem. Section 3.2 also explains apartment and old-building tails partly via “failed field survey,” which sits poorly with a rule that skips the survey.

The rollout gates do not match the numbers they cite. A p95 ceiling of 46 days is described as slightly better than the baseline of 45 days. A complaint ceiling of 7.5% is tied to “baseline −1 pp,” but 6.1 − 1 is 5.1, not 7.5. Simple-house NPS “lift” and a forecast of +2 to +4 NPS points are not in the data; only overall NPS is given, and it fell from +21 to +17. Holding non-pilot segments at an 82% first-time-right rate treats the overall baseline as if it were each segment’s baseline. Phase-2 savings of EUR 5–10 per case and “1–2” extra coordinators are unsupported precision. Rural cases (11% of the pilot) are never assigned a path, while the write-up alternates among 34%, 47%, and business-included populations.

Value of information is ordered and mostly relevant, but the ranking undercuts the decision. M6 is placed above a randomized skip test and treated as near-sufficient, even though skipped cases have no field-survey outcome to validate against unless new surveys are done. M5, the test of ownership versus shared backup when a coordinator is absent, is deferred while ownership is scaled immediately. Designed post-billing activation is also recast as load-driven shortcutting, and mechanisms such as asbestos are added without evidence.

Fact, interpretation, and assumption are separated in the table, and the five metric conflicts are structurally explained with a real median-versus-tail and centralization-versus-resilience account. Those strengths keep the paper above a failing grade. The executive numbers, the mislabeling of C and D, the reversed conformance edges, and the self-contradictory halt rules keep it well below a strong one.
