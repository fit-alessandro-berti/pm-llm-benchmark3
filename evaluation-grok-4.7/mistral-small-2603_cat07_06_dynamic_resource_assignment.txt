The answer covers every required section and gets a minority of case-level decisions right, but it is not a deployable or internally consistent policy. Hard rules are inverted or ignored, the algorithm does not implement the scoring scheme, and the recommended queue contradicts both the scenario and the answer’s own constraints.

The training rule is reversed: the scenario requires at least 20% of normal/low-priority assignments to support documented training priorities when SLA risk is acceptable; the answer makes 20% a maximum. It also invents a hard cap of 8 open cases. Six of the ten resources are already at or above 8 (R1 9, R2 12, R4 14, R5 8, R7 10, R9 11), so that rule would bar most of the roster, including people the later table assigns. “Trainees must be supervised” is applied to every activity; the scenario requires supervision for high-priority work below skill 2, and for Security only at skill 1–2 under R7 or R8. **Algorithm.** Eligibility is computed once for the whole queue. Skill, overtime, and urgency are not decided per case-resource pair, so one urgent case can open every high-overtime resource for every case. `should_keep_continuity`, `calculate_assignment_score`, and `meets_skill_requirements` are undefined. The selection loop `break`s after the first accepted pair, so the scorer assigns one case and the fallback dumps the rest onto first-available without skill, supervision, continuity, or overtime checks. Already-assigned cases are not removed. The training gate treats `training_priority != "none"` as a training assignment, which is true for 9 of 10 people, and caps it at `0.2 * len(all cases)` rather than enforcing a floor on normal/low work. Workload scoring uses the inverse of hours remaining, which prefers the people with the least time left. SLA points (0–100) swamp training (0–10), so the score cannot meet a training quota. Tie-breaking ends in random choice, which is a poor fit for an auditable policy. Supervision pairs and the quality-batch rule are absent.
