3.5

The write-up follows the required nine-section outline and gets the unweighted midpoint service times right, but its capacity conclusion is internally contradictory, several bottleneck claims fight the shift and skill tables, and the scenario scores are not produced by the supplied code.

The service-time midpoints are auditable and correct: Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.3, Electrical 32.8, Software 38.5, Validation 15.7. The daily arrival total of 42 is correct. After that, the load arithmetic breaks. The component loads printed in §2 already sum to about 2,700 base minutes (roughly 290 intake + 470 mechanical + 750 electrical + 540 software + 660 validation). The same section then calls total pure service “~1,300 minutes” and systemic utilization “~60%.” Those two statements cannot both be true. Net staffed time is also wrong: five 8-hour shifts plus Farah’s 4 hours, minus five 30-minute breaks, is 41.5 hours (2,490 minutes), not “roughly 36 hours.” Multipliers are never applied, so resource-minutes are never distinguished from base minutes. With best-case multipliers near 1.0, offered work already meets or exceeds staffed minutes before handover, setup, coordination, breaks, and shift misalignment. The “manageable 60%” premise is the opposite of what the section’s own components imply, and every later SLA and utilization figure sits on that premise.

There is no time-block by skill account, which is the analysis the staffing pattern requires. Farah (08:00–12:00) and Emil (12:00–20:00) do not overlap, and only Chen, Emil, and Farah can do software. That makes Chen the only software bridge across noon. §2 never computes this. Bottlenecks are then mislabeled. U1’s morning intake load stays small relative to four or five intake-capable people; the morning pinch is diagnostic coverage before Emil arrives, especially software. U2 doubles electrical as well as ES software; “software grows linearly” overstates a four-hour surge and skips Deepa, the on-shift electrical multiplier of 1.00. U3 treats Chen’s absence as an electrical problem and names Ada (1.35) and Deepa as the absorbers, omitting Emil on electrical and the fact that 08:00–12:00 software collapses to Farah alone at 1.40. U4 has no revised expectations, and “preemption cascades” conflicts with the one-preemption cap. U5 is asserted rather than sized. Case-mix weights are rounded (software cases stated as ~14 versus an arrival-weighted ~13; electrical understated), and the separate 5-minute team-change and 5-minute skill-switch penalties never enter the load.

The tail objective is not what its prose says. \(\min(P_k, 0.90)\) summed over three priorities weights a P1 miss the same as a P3 miss, and any policy that merely clears 90% on all three ties. It is not a joint probability and it does not “penalize P1 heavily.” The fairness objective is a min-max utilization slogan plus an unformalized variance term; scenario robustness is not in either formula. SLA clocks, overnight gaps after 20:00–20:30, and the 30-minute overtime cap are never defined, so “elapsed minutes” is ambiguous for afternoon P1 and P2.

Policy rules are only partly operational. Policy A’s “multiplier ≤ 1.10” rule leaves Emil as the only software resource and excludes Farah from validation at 1.20, while the text also says Farah does validation. Morning software under A has no eligible resource, and that infeasibility is not stated. Policy B names SERPT but describes earliest expected finish given a resource speed, and the score omits setup and handover. Policy C calls Deepa a generalist shock absorber; Deepa has no software skill. Holding her idle once utilization exceeds 85% tightens the very capacity constraint the rule is reacting to. The fallback then disables ME/ES parallelism whenever utilization stays above 90% for 60 minutes. That lengthens P1 critical paths in the regime where the 120-minute clock is already failing, which reverses Policy C’s own “go parallel if P1 is at risk” rule. The 10% remaining-time preemption penalty is not in any dispatch rule.

§4 does not cover the required comparison. U4 is absent; Policy B is missing for U2 and U3; Policy A is missing for U5; handover/setup burden is not a column; P2 and P3 are fused; delay is Low/Medium/High with no minute scale and no interval. Percentages such as base P1 SLA 98% and U2 Policy A P1 SLA 50% are not derived from the capacity math and contradict the ~60% utilization story once the component loads are added up. The footnote calls them capacity approximations. A daily load ratio does not identify a P90.

The recommendation therefore overclaims. Policy C is not shown against B on U2, U3, or U4, so “most robust across all tested uncertainties” is not supported by the table. The 90% utilization trigger is the base-scenario fairness line; under U5 it would be on almost continuously, making the fallback the real policy. No section scores the two stated objectives.

§7 omits most required attributes: queue entry, assignment decision, eligible set, planned versus actual service, setup, policy version, and SLA clock. It assigns `concept:name` to lifecycle transitions, which would erase the activity sequence, while the code does the opposite. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is described as if it separated preemption and stage queues; those outputs are case-level and need their own stage metrics. Throughput by two-hour block is labeled concept drift. Bottleneck migration and service-time change are not given a concrete PM4Py procedure. `get_service_time` is never mentioned.

The code fails the reproducibility contract. Skill multipliers and service ranges are encoded; shifts, breaks, the demand table, and U1–U5 are not. Only a Policy C sketch is run, on 50 i.i.d. cases with flat type and priority probabilities, one seed, and independent clocks, so resources are never busy and no queue forms. Intake is always Ada, validation always Ben, mechanical always Deepa, electrical and software always Chen — not the §3 eligibility rule. A flat 5 minutes is added for multi-branch cases and a flat 7 minutes before validation, whether or not the resource changed; team-change, skill-family setup, the 8-minute coordination rule, the 10% preemption penalty, and suspend/resume are absent even though the comment says preemption is tracked. The log has no `start_timestamp`. `pm4py.get_service_time` is not called. No policy-by-scenario table is printed. §9 then says the preemption cost “is modeled” as 10%. It is not.

The pilot also misreads the demand table: 12:00–16:00 is the heaviest part of the day and the U2 window, not off-peak. Hard-routing P1 to Ada, Ben, and Chen sends software P1s to two people who cannot do software and bypasses Emil. The limits section correctly notes micro-bursts and quality risk, but it does not flag the 2× load contradiction, the missing overnight clock, or the fact that the published SLA table cannot be regenerated from the code.
