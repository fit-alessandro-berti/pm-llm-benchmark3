6.5

The write-up is structurally complete and often shows the right kind of counterfactual discipline, but several clock, mechanism, and calibration errors are large enough that it is not close to reliable.

The K-337 baseline is wrong by a full day. Register at Mon 11:00 through notify at the following Wed 09:10 is about 214 hours, not 238. The Monday-morning counterfactual end near 165–170 hours is internally plausible, so the repeated “about 70 hour” saving is not: the base reduction is about 45–50 hours. That inflated delta is then reused in the capacity discussion, the ranking, and the synthesis. The Friday-evening absolute range of 103–110 hours does match the clock; the headline comparison does not.

The same section reverses the scaled mechanism. Prohibiting the second information request removes Friday supplier waits. It does not shift those requests into the weekend or create a Monday document-check surge. The capacity table gets the direction right—document and assessment demand fall, while Friday/Monday compliance and signature demand rise—and the prose contradicts the table. “Assessment is the binding constraint in all four cases” is also too broad. In K-104 the long compliance wait dominates, and in K-337 the external loops do.

K-219 freezes an endogenous timestamp. Across the four logs, the manager signs within roughly 0.5–2 hours when the package is ready during the working day, and the next morning when it is ready at night. If D2 finishes overnight and A2 starts around 14:00, a same-afternoon signature is the pattern-consistent case, with a gain on the order of 12–16 hours. The answer instead makes an unchanged Wednesday 08:30 decision the base, worth 0–2 hours, and puts a Tuesday-evening signature in a “best” band of 38–42 hours. Those three claims cannot be true together: a 14:00 assessment start plus a Tuesday-evening signature lands nearer 33–38 hours. The worst band of 60–75 hours also cannot contain the stated 36-hour external response plus a new document check and assessment.

K-104 is more careful, but the worst case treats Wednesday 18:00 as a manager appointment. In the factual trace that time is 20 minutes after compliance finished at 17:40. Holding it fixed makes the gain vanish by construction. The preliminary C1 screen is also placed on Monday without reconciling it to the stated 20-hour compliance queue. Probabilities such as 70%, 20–35%, and 90% are presented as if they were measured.

Section 1 is weaker than it looks. The two partial-order operators are typographically identical, so precedence and overlap cannot be read from the legend. Bracketed rule notes recover some lines, but R5 still blurs “may overlap” with a required order. The intervention classification itself is sound: K-219 is routing, K-411 is concurrency, and K-337 changes the admissible decision, not merely its duration.

What keeps the score from falling further is the reasoning the format was built to elicit. Mandatory compliance and four-eyes separation are preserved; K-337 is correctly treated as a policy change that must record the discrepancy; earlier readiness is repeatedly separated from earlier completion, especially for K-411; queue snapshots are treated as forecasts; and the pilots measure spillover, logging, and assessment-ready time rather than cycle time alone. The rank order does not simply follow the largest clock saving. The remaining defect is that Section 7 calls K-411 the most robust intervention while the robustness table ranks K-104 first.

Overall, the control-flow judgment is good and the interference table is mostly serious, but the numeric backbone and one system-level mechanism are not trustworthy.
