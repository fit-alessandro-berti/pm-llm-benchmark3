The diagnosis is unusually careful and most of the quantitative anchors match the artifacts: the 3,600/1,400 intake split, 1,430 evidence inflows versus the separate 26% repeat-request statistic, 2,790 fast-approval inflows, 14.1% versus 3.3% complaint rates, the 31%/9% cost narrowing, fitness 0.91 with 68% fitting traces, the 3,625 terminal count against 5,000 starts, segment shares and medians, and the resource constraints. Edge times are treated as conditional directly-follows durations, fitness is correctly refused as a compliance rate, and the fast-versus-reviewed gap is treated as confounded by selection. Trade-offs are segment-specific, sacrifices are explicit, and dominance is left conditional. That is the right standard for this evidence.

The score is held down by concrete process-mining and design errors.

The opening volume claim attributes the 2,300 Fast approval → Dispatch replacement transitions to the 2,550 Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval transitions. Fast approval is a shared activity with a second inflow of 240 from Fraud/risk review. Directly-follows counts do not support that subset claim. The same section’s later warning that the graph is not a case-level account does not repair the attribution.

Redesign A then targets “46% of dispatches reached through Fast approval.” 2,300/5,000 is 46% of started cases. Of observed dispatch inflows, 2,300 of 2,365, about 97%, already come from Fast approval. The sentence inverts where dispatch volume sits, and the following “35% high-value” figure uses a different denominator.

The three redesigns are directionally different, but they are not cleanly separated. A and B both add pre-dispatch part validation, evidence-completeness gating, and human confirmation for high-value replacements. B’s stated mechanism is a case owner coordinating evidence and technical work, which is also C’s core mechanism. C’s WIP limits, protected board capacity, and post-close loop are distinct; the ownership language still makes B and C overlap.

Experiment 3 calls the Premium and Standard reviewed/repair medians of 96 and 120 hours a “first-disposition p90” benchmark. Those figures are end-to-end medians, not first-disposition percentiles. The 20–30% reduction target is therefore attached to the wrong metric.

The first pilot is aimed at complete low-risk accessories, with a go bar that Premium low-risk median time stay within about two hours of the current 8-hour median. That population is where the complaint gap is 3–4 points and wrong-replacement exposure is EUR 95. The sharp contradiction is Premium and Standard high-value devices, at 22% versus 5% and 18% versus 6%, with EUR 410 part-error exposure and R1/R2 risk. The same design says the pre-dispatch gate adds 6–18 hours and can push Premium low-risk cases through the 24-hour credit threshold. A pilot that must preserve an 8-hour median within two hours does not test that gate, nor the high-value compliance failure the redesign is built to stop. The complaint rule is also internally loose: “no worse than about 2 percentage points” and “a 25–40% relative reduction” are not one threshold.

Smaller faults accumulate. “Average a 140-hour median” conflates mean and median. Part-price delay is treated as technician idleness; the artifact only says the quote wait is mostly price confirmation. Expanded fraud triggers in A are not stress-tested against the two-specialist Monday and campaign bottleneck. The 350 Fast approval → close transitions and the fraud-review inflow/outflow gap of about 220 are unused, despite the answer correctly noticing that the graph does not balance globally.

The synthesis, conformance caution, and refusal to invent ROI are strong. The mis-attributed flows, the dispatch-share error, the median/p90 swap, the overlapping mechanisms, and a first pilot that misses the segment and control it claims to test keep it well short of nearly flawless.
