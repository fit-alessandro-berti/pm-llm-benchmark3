6.5

The write-up covers every required heading and stays appropriately cautious about a full rollout, East versus North/South, tails, fairness, and learning. It is not nearly flawless. Several claims conflict with the given volumes, routing rules, or each other.

The mechanism map is a topic list, not a map from each restructuring action to a log measure. “~73%” utilization is reasonable; saying a 15% relative increase pushes the pool “to 85%+” is not (about 84%). Calling the wait curve exponential is loose: the usual queueing relationship blows up like \(1/(1-\rho)\), and pooling several servers at the same utilization shortens waits. That benefit is never separated from extra demand (more tech referrals, difficult quality checks, triage). The sharpest quantitative risk is missed: legal/risk volume 9→12 per 100 cases on resources already at 91–93% implies offered load above 1 if capacity is unchanged, so management’s 9.6h→10.5h wait is not credible without more capacity or much shorter cases. The adverse scenario then reverses the staff warning: it has legal/risk staff pulled into tech triage, whereas the stated risk is that the technical pool absorbs legal/risk triage and hard quality checks.

The optimistic forecast has median cycle time falling by about a day while rework stays flat, even though management’s own waits lengthen on the specialist and approval paths. The fairness illustration is arithmetically wrong: East is only about 25% of volume, so North/South slower by 1 day and East faster by 2 days makes the volume-weighted average worse, not a hidden success. The recommendation’s warning that a new shared queue will “prioritize easy wins” contradicts the rule that high-complexity and specialty cases are forced into that queue.

The pilot does not test the proposal. Moving only East into a shared service while North and South keep embedded techs creates a queue of one, so pooling cannot appear. The same design then sets a North/South “shared resource starvation” guardrail that those teams cannot hit if they keep their specialists. The high-complexity 90th-percentile stop of 25 days is compared with East’s overall 90th percentile of 22.8 days. Right-censoring and started-versus-completed cohorts are ignored despite multi-week cycle times. Shared approval and the legal queue are barely in the pilot.

The indicator table is real and split into leading versus lagging, but it omits the measures that match the input: handover arcs per 100 cases, median wait by arc, specialty, and customer segment. The PM4Py sketch names the right families of functions, then runs them only on high-complexity East cases, never filters `required_specialty` or `customer_segment`, and never compares edge weights, quantiles, or variant shares before and after. The SLA rate assumes an undocumented `outcome` value of `SLA_Miss`.
