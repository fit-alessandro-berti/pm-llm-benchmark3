5.5

The write-up has the right skeleton: March is mainly semantic/provenance drift, later rework and delay are real, and the change points are mechanism-specific. The numbers, several causal links, and the PM4Py plan are not reliable enough to carry that story.

**Timeline.** April is grouped into the supplier interval, but Supplier Z’s break is 2026-05-05; April rework is still 13% and median cycle time is 32.0h. May’s supplier step is sudden, not a gradual April–June ramp. June’s complexity rise to 27% is a pre-existing mix trend, not a staffing effect. July `Assess→Senior Assessment` at 9 per 100 continues 4→5→7→9; the jump is October (22). The priority rule reorders the queue; it does not by itself create senior-assessment frequency. December duplicate `Open Case` at 4% matches March and September, so it is not an intervention or logging recovery. The worst cycle time is November (49.0h / 121h), which is absorbed into October. Mixed onsets (supplier plus staffing, season plus priority plus residual rework, December load decline plus overtime) are collapsed into one label each. Evidence scores of 90–95 sit on those confounded intervals.

**Raw versus mapped.** The fitness gap is interpreted correctly as label and duplicate distortion, with a real residual afterward. The 98→96 `Eligibility→CollectDocs` move is then used as behavioral proof, and May’s `Assess→RequestInfo` doubling (12→24; March is still 12) is attached to the migration. Missing resources are treated as random noise; they are systematic on migrated activities. Priority “redistribution” is cited as an observed post-May pattern, while the monthly aggregate cannot show it.

**Quantities.** Baseline-relative figures against January are wrong in five cells: high-complexity in October is +95.2%, not +119%; Supplier Z in July is +214.3%, not +178.6%; rework is +100% in May, +141.7% in July, and +116.7% in October, not +125%, +158.3%, and +141.7%. The heading says month-to-month; the table is baseline-relative. Duplicate `Open Case` is all `n/a` because the January base is 0, which drops the cleanest March step (0→4–6 points).

The DFG “EMD” values do not match the stated measure. One-dimensional Wasserstein on the seven rates sorts the numbers and discards edge identity; that distance is about 0.43, 5.7, 8.4, 4.0, 9.1, and 5.1 for March, May, July, September, October, and December, not 0.04, 0.12, 0.18, 0.10, 0.20, and 0.08. Aligned L1 ranks September closer to January than December (28 vs 36); the table reverses that pair. The Part A code calls `wasserstein_distance` on those vectors and would not print this table.

The cycle-time shares sum to 100 in every column and are not derived. Assess service time moves from about 4.8h to about 5.4h, roughly 5% of the July cycle-time gap (+12.7h), not 10–15%. Queueing’s share is set to fall from ~40% in May to ~20% in July and ~15% in October, which is the period when four assessors are gone and volume later rises. Extra assessments belong with rework, not with service slowdown.

Logging’s second change point, 2026-12-05, has no schema, missingness, or duplicate break. The first performance break is May (+4.5h median, +13h p90), before the 2026-06-15 staffing cut. July is a weak behavioral point if behavior means control flow; October’s senior-assessment rate is the sharper routing/recording break. The multi-change-point conclusion itself is right.

**Hypotheses.** The evidence column is mostly a confidence score. “Evidence against: None” for a pure March migration skips the partial relabel (about 140 of 640 March cases still `Validate Eligibility`) and mapped fitness of 0.90 versus 0.92–0.93. Missing-resource growth is listed as support for staffing-driven queueing; that missingness is a migration measurement pattern. Supplier recovery is listed as evidence against seasonality. The interaction row puts the cycle-time peak in October; it is November. There is no competing hypothesis that senior assessment was recorded inconsistently and became visible in the renewal season. October intensity is not mix alone: about 4 senior assessments per 100 cases at 21% complexity in January versus 22 per 100 at 41% complexity in October.

**Discrimination and segments.** Queue time, service time, and throughput are filed as behavioral drift. In the pre/post-migration row, the label change and duplicate `Open Case` are placed in the behavioral column. Supplier-Z logging is described as resource missingness concentrated in Supplier Z; the evidence places it in migrated activities. Segment keys are monthly rates (`supplier_z_pct`, `high_complexity_pct`) rather than case attributes. Priority is both a segment and “not in the raw log.” The stated primary population, non-Z standard cases from January–September, still contains the migration, spillover congestion, staffing loss, and the priority rule, and it removes the supplier, complexity, and renewal strata where the drift is visible. That is a control series, not a stable primary population.

**Monitoring.** Coverage is broad and thresholds are not universal. Several rules are poorly posed. Raw fitness `<0.85` and missingness `>3%` stay on for the entire post-March plateau unless the reference is rebased. A three-month rolling reference that moves with the series dampens the sudden breaks. Benjamini–Hochberg is named for threshold rules that do not produce p-values, on highly dependent monitors. STL seasonality with one year cannot separate recurrence from a new shock confounded with supplier, staffing, priority, and the December campaign. The priority alert fires when high-complexity wait is shorter than standard wait, which is the rule working. Two consecutive months of confirmation delays detection of the sudden March and May shocks.

**Code.** Part A’s `pct_change() > 0.2` rule never flags median cycle time: the largest month-to-month rise is about 14% in May. It therefore misses May–August performance drift and does not return the narrative’s June 15 or July 1 points. Part B misses the required calls `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `pm4py.filter_time_range`, `pm4py.discover_dfg`, `pm4py.get_variants`, and `pm4py.discover_temporal_profile`. `discover_dfg(log)` treats the algorithm module as callable; the public call is `pm4py.discover_dfg` or `.apply`. Variants are imported from a pandas path that does not match `pm4py.get_variants`. Temporal profile is replaced by a sojourn helper, `get_sojourn_time_from_event_attributes`, that is not the temporal-profile API. Token replay is called as `token_replay.apply(log, label_mapping)`; replay requires a net and markings, and no net is discovered. The mapping copy does not drop duplicate `Open Case` events. Alpha miner, converter, and constants are imported and unused. Log skeletons and DECLARE are omitted.

**Attribution and rules.** March at high confidence is the strongest claim, with a small residual mapped gap left understated. Supplier dominance for all of May–September at ~90 is too strong: Supplier Z’s share falls into August while median cycle time rises from 40.8h to 45.0h, which fits capacity loss. Seasonality at ~95 as the primary driver of the cycle-time peak overstates a late increment on top of a level already reached by August, from a single year, with the December decline also produced by lower volume and active interventions. Rule 2 treats any large rework rise as supplier disruption. Rule 4 treats a complexity jump as renewal season. Rule 7 looks for a large p90 rise with a small median rise. A priority rule that slows the standard majority should move the median, and in these aggregates median and p90 rise together; that signature fits a minority of very slow cases better than queue reordering.

The usable residue is the artifact-specific timeline and the raw-versus-mapped split. The arithmetic, the distance table, the queueing shares, the July and December attributions, and the executable PM4Py plan would need to be redone before the monitoring design could be used.
