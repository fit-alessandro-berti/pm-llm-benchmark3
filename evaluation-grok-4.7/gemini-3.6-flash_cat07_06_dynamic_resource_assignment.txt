5.5

The write-up covers every required section and keeps most hard gates (approval sign-off, batching eligibility, the four-assignment cap, and continuity on C007 and C016) in view. It is not a reliable policy: the scorer does not implement the rules it states, several recommended rows contradict the matrix and the evidence, and the audit code would not measure the indicators it names.

The utility model is internally inconsistent. \(S_{\text{SLA}}\) depends only on the case, so it is a constant in \(\arg\max_r\) and cannot change who is chosen, yet the comparison later says urgency overrides the other weights. Multiplying continuity by \(1+\text{complexity}\) pushes that term to about 1.88 while the other terms stay in \([0,1]\), so continuity swamps skill and fairness and supervised pairs are never charged for the supervisor’s time. The overtime predicate allows a high-overtime resource only when every resource is high-overtime, which is a different rule from “urgent and no safer qualified alternative.” The 20% training quota, the 12-hour training gate, the 1.5× cross-team rule, and within-batch updates of open cases and hours are stated and then omitted. \(\text{SpecialistLoadRatio}\) is undefined. The soft rule that compares 3.3h with 2.7h is always true on the given medians, so it always transfers a skill-1 owner, which contradicts both constraint 5 and the assignments that retain R3 and R1.

Those breaks show up in the queue. C001 gives an urgent, 0.88-complexity security review with 3h left to a skill-1 owner. The same evidence the policy cites says a weak owner adds about 3.3h of service time, which already exceeds the remaining SLA; calling the cost “minor supervisor overhead,” and marking it as training, is wrong on both counts (R3’s documented priority is negotiation, and \(S_{\text{Train}}\) would be 0). C015 has R5 execute security review even though her security score is 0, which the matrix marks not allowed and which rule 6 limits to levels 1–2 under R7 or R8. R10’s “approval training” does not match a security training priority. C012 is explicitly a trainee case and is given to a skill-3 specialist. C003 treats two prior reassignments as a reason to reassign again, despite the stated 1.8× rework / 1.4× appeal effect past two handovers. R3 is given C001, C003, and C007 plus C009 mentoring inside 6.0h; R6 is given two urgent cases plus a sign-off inside 6.5h; R10 sits on the cap of 4. Fairness is claimed from a static snapshot.

The baseline comparison names the right dimensions and the right qualitative direction, and it does not pretend there is a unique optimum. It never runs first-available or specialist-only on C001–C016, and “strongest SLA protection” and “high fairness” are not earned by this batch.

The worker note is readable and non-punitive. It overstates the mechanism: high load does not hard-pause intake except through the cap and the overtime screen, and team leads are not generally notified.

The ten audit indicators are all listed. The handover ratio is not computed inside the case. Reassignment \(\le 2\) is treated as a rule the scenario never set. The bottleneck formula takes the mean of a function name. Skill, overtime, and training checks require joins the log columns do not contain.

The PM4Py sketch calls the right family of functions and then audits the wrong objects. `format_dataframe` is given non-existent parameters (`case_id_column`, `activity_column`, `resource_column`). `filter_case_performance` filters duration, not reassignment count, and the result is unused. The mismatch filter compares a nonexistent `required_skill_level` with 2 instead of joining the resource’s skill to `required_skill`. Overtime is tested with `workload_snapshot == 'high_overtime_risk'`. Training share looks for an `assignment_reason` token the policy never writes. SLA breaches are counted per event. Networks, roles, batches, and the performance DFG are computed and ignored, and nothing feeds them back into handover costs, weights, or role constraints.
