4.2

The write-up has the right skeleton (time origin at `Submit`, a 72-hour horizon, competing terminals, transfer scenarios, immortal time, Aalen–Johansen versus Kaplan–Meier), but the estimands, the case-level accounting, and the code contradict each other. Those are the core of the task, so the document is not a reliable analysis.

Section 1 defines E1 as one minus the cumulative incidence of resolution by 72 hours, and E2 as still having no terminal disposition at 72 hours. The worked examples do not implement those definitions. S03, resolved at 76 hours, is described as an E1 event after 72 hours; for a 72-hour cumulative incidence it is still unresolved at 72 and only enters the incidence after the horizon. S09, open at 92 hours, is called right-censored at 72 under both estimands. Follow-up to 92 hours makes the state at 72 known: no resolution and no terminal disposition. Censoring at 72 would fit only an administrative stop at the horizon, which cannot be applied to S09 while S03 is kept as a post-horizon event. S06, withdrawn at 27 hours, is called a competing event under both estimands. Under E2 that withdrawal is the terminal-disposition event itself. S08’s transfer-as-censoring option is labeled non-informative with no discussion, despite every transfer in the table (S08, S21, S32) being escalated P1. The section also never says which estimand governs breach reporting versus open workload.

Section 2 undoes the prose. The E1 indicator is 1 only for resolutions after 72 hours, so early resolutions, open cases already past 72, withdrawals, and rejections are all coded 0. That is not \(1-F_{\text{resolve}}(72)\). The E2 indicator is 0 for every status, including observed terminals. Open cases share competing-risk code 0 with success. The open-row censoring rule (censor at `duration_or_age_h`) conflicts with the claim that S09 is censored at 72. The three transfer labels are present, but transfer-as-breach does not separate S21 at 26 hours from S08 at 68 hours and S32 at 96 hours.

Section 3 cites real open and transferred IDs, then misstates the bias. Resolved-only analysis keeps S03 and S13; it drops the unresolved tail (S09, S11, S25, S26), plus rejections S05, S14, and S23, which are omitted from the drop list. Open and still before 72 hours are S10 (47 h), S15, S24, S29, and S31; S10 is missing. Cohort “imbalance” is reduced to earlier `AutoCheck` use. That is the policy change, not the non-random assignment, priority mix (P1 is 3/15 in A versus 5/17 in B), or incomplete rollout.

Section 4’s method list is mostly standard, but restricted mean time to 72 hours is not the expected time to resolution, and “open at cutoff” is not an absorbing process state. Landmarking at 72 conditions on already missing the SLA; a landmark before 72 is the operational prediction design. Fully stratified Aalen–Johansen by cohort and priority on 32 cases leaves sparse cells, which is not a justification tied to this sample. Cause-specific Cox coefficients are also not a direct comparison of intensities across causes.

Section 5 correctly flags ever-escalation and rework as immortal-time risks if used at baseline. It then treats `last_event_gap_h` as a legitimate time-varying covariate. In these data that field is a single end-of-observation gap, and it is 0 on every terminal row, so it largely restates open status and age. The state diagram misses paths that are in the prefixes: `AutoCheck→Screen` (the Cohort B pattern), `Screen→Reject/Withdraw/Escalate/Resolve/AutoCheck`, `AutoCheck→Escalate`, `Escalate→Review`, and `Rework→Resolve`.

Section 6 names three transfer treatments and a withdrawal/rejection censoring alternative, but it never specifies an informative-censoring analysis. The claim that counting transfers as breaches could reverse a Cohort B advantage is asserted with no counts. The escalation “reversal” is about leakage, not about the sensitivity factors requested.

Section 7 avoids calling the curves causal, which is right, but a 48-hour alert threshold is invented, early rejection is never separated from a timeliness breach, and “fairness” is only cross-cohort calibration. No estimand is tied to a decision.

Section 8 does not run. `last_event_gap_h` has 31 values and is shifted (S23 receives 4; S32 is absent). `rework_count` is all zeros, wiping S07, S13, and S27. The import `pm4py.objects.log.importer.pm4py` is not a fallback. `e1_event` marks only late resolutions, then the print labels that mean “unresolved at 72 h.” `e2_event` is unused. Time is clipped at 72 without a risk-set or competing-event coding, so early resolutions become non-events and opens past 72 become censored at 72. The transfer “sensitivity” is a mean of incomparable indicators, equal to minus the late-resolution share. `parse_event_log_string` is fed `case:a>b` under the default comma separator, and `get_variants` keys are not prefix strings, so the variant map is empty. No Kaplan–Meier or cumulative-incidence table is built.

Section 9 is the cleanest part: timestamps, post-transfer outcomes, calendar origin and cutoff, workload, assignment, and rollout compliance are the right gaps. It still does not rank them or note that the supplied gap and ever-escalated fields cannot be reconstructed as baseline covariates.
