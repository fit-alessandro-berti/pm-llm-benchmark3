6.4

The arithmetic in the pre/post tables and the two-period contrasts is largely accurate, the required sections and columns are present, median and p90 are not collapsed, and the headline that a single “reduces cycle time by X%” claim is indefensible is the right one. The answer still overstates what these data identify, and several of its own caveats are not carried into the estimates, the confidence levels, or the rollout design.

The identification section names the right family of problems—staggered timing, forbidden TWFE comparisons, intensity versus availability, selection, and a shared fraud desk—then treats a clean-cohort difference-in-differences as if it absorbed the shocks that actually break it. The May complexity increase is closer to a common shock. The April routing premium and the staffing changes are aimed at the adopting branches and timed with adoption. Differencing against Delta and Epsilon does not remove those. The justification paragraph says the design accommodates the organizational shocks; the threat table later admits the opposite. Those two passages cannot both be the primary strategy.

Identifiability is graded inconsistently. Availability effects on rework and escalation are marked identifiable under parallel trends, while the same contrast for median and p90 is only partial, even though staffing, routing, case mix, and fraud-desk referral hit all of those outcomes. The fast-path dose effect is marked non-identified, then reported as −0.130 hours per percentage point, attenuated to −0.062 with controls, and converted into a causal interval of −2 to −5 hours. That interval is not a sensitivity analysis to the biases that would manufacture a benefit, especially extra fraud-desk load on the controls. The −2.19 to −4.17 “covariate-adjusted” band is not derived in the text, and the supplied code does not estimate it. Section 5 was required to keep arithmetic separate from causal language; the bounded conclusion explicitly says automation “causally reduced” median cycle time.

The process reading misses the comparison the design most needs. Fast-path share rises from about 4–6% to 30–50%, so the branch-month median can move because the median case changes path, not because the same work got faster or capacity increased. That mix shift is named and then left unquantified. The July–August median decline and the widening p90-to-median ratio are presented as adoption signatures even though never-treated branches also improved in those months and their tail-to-median ratios widened too. Alpha versus Beta is pooled despite opposite raw median changes and opposite staffing shocks. Pre-period “parallel trends” are three noisy months, selection on history before January 2026 is invisible, and Gamma’s similar contrast does not “strongly refute” regression to the mean. Baseline fast-path shares of 4–6% before availability, and rework, escalation, and complexity series that stay constant for months at a time, are never treated as measurement limits.

The recommendation is directionally sensible and then operationally inconsistent. Epsilon-then-Delta is a chosen sequence, not a stepped wedge. A 15% rework stop rule sits below Epsilon’s observed 16.2%. “No significant CSAT decline” treats an underpowered, selected survey as a safety proof. Case-level randomization does not “cleanly isolate” the algorithm when both arms share specialist and fraud-desk queues, and the 8-week experiment is judged at month 3. The code is reproducible as a descriptive TWFE exercise with a real cluster-robust variance formula and appropriate warnings, but it never estimates the event study the answer calls primary.
