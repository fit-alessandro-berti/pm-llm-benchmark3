The answer meets the required structure and is strong on the main analytical demands: prerequisites and four-eyes are kept, queue times are treated as forecasts, earlier readiness is separated from earlier completion, and suppressing the second information request is correctly treated as a decision-policy change rather than a pure time cut. The 100-case discussion covers compliance reservations, D1/D2 substitution, rework, assessment congestion, weekends, and false concurrency.

It is not nearly flawless. The formal partial order both requires a request-more-info loop and forbids the next loop, and it places final compliance after every rework loop even when the case is not high-risk or high-value. The summary table and the derivations disagree: K-104’s worst case is about +1 hour in the table and about +2 hours later; K-411’s table allows only 0 to −3 hours, while the derivation allows a worsening of about +2 hours. For K-337, mapping a Monday or Tuesday compliance start to a −24 to −48 hour gain is arithmetically wrong; the −48 hour case is the Friday path, and a Tuesday compliance review can leave the Wednesday decision unchanged. Manager signing is also inconsistent: Tuesday-night signing is allowed for K-219, but Friday-evening signing is not considered for K-337 even though same-day signing occurs in the factual record. The pilot’s “80% power” claim is false for a 2-hour effect with a standard deviation of about 6 hours at 30–50 cases per arm, and a 5-point rework difference is similarly underpowered at 40–60 per arm.
