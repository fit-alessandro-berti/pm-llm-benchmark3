The answer is analytically strong and mostly numerically exact, but it is not nearly flawless. Estimands are defined before estimators, competing events are separated from right censoring and from already-observed breaches, and the concrete case accounting holds up.

Checked claims that are correct include the status split (13/4/3/3/9), on-time versus late resolutions (9 and 4; cohort conditional lateness 2/6 and 2/7), priority mix (3 vs 5 P1; 7 P2 and 5 P3 in each cohort), and the finite-extract bounds (18–23, 9–14, and 16–23 of 32; cohort non-resolution ranges 60.0–73.3% and 52.9–70.6%). The primary Aalen–Johansen figures also reproduce: pooled resolve / non-resolve / still active / restricted mean active time are about 30.4%, 69.6%, 38.0%, and 50.4 hours; cohort A is 29.2%, 70.8%, 33.9%, 49.6; cohort B is 31.6%, 68.4%, 41.6%, 51.1. Withdrawal, rejection, and transfer incidences are about 14.0%, 10.1%, and 7.5%. The (72, 96] resolve increment is about 14.3% (37.8% of those still active), with three cases at risk just before 96. Priority-standardized non-resolution is 68.75% and 67.5%. Transfer-as-censor gives about 68.7% and 43.5%; the optimistic proxy gives 62.1% pooled and about 62.3% versus 62.1% by cohort; the net resolve-only calculation gives 63.9%. AutoCheck counts (2/15 and 13/17), the S03 versus S27 rework distinction, and elapsed times 47−19=28 and 79−48=31 are right. S08, S21, and S32 are all escalated P1 cases. The identity \(B_R(72)=A(72)+F_W(72)+F_{reject}(72)+F_X(72)\) is used correctly, and the Aalen–Johansen value 38% sits inside the active-case bound 28–44%, as it should.

The main weaknesses are in uncertainty presentation and a few labels. Headline cohort percentages are given to 0.1 point while nine cases remain at risk at 72 hours and several priority cells have only 3–7 cases. The text correctly says this cannot rank cohorts, but it never puts a standard error, bootstrap interval, or even a risk-set-based range next to 70.8% versus 68.4%. That gap is smaller than ordinary sampling noise, so the table is sharper than the evidence. In the transfer-as-censor row, 43.5% is called “no substantive disposition.” That product-limit quantity is the extrapolated probability of avoiding resolve, withdrawal, and rejection after removing transfers as censoring; the real-world internal event-free probability is the primary 38.0%. The row states the exchangeability assumption, but the label is easier to misread than the net-resolution warning elsewhere. The PM4Py branch is only an `ImportError` fallback: a present but incompatible `get_variants` return type can raise rather than degrade, and `tuple(key).split` handling is version-fragile. Notation also slips (`Jc` versus `J` / `d3=J`).

Those are limited faults. The answer does not treat withdrawal or transfer as ordinary non-informative censoring, does not drop open cases, does not use Escalate or final gap as baseline covariates, and does not turn the cohort contrast into a causal policy effect. The code implements the stated estimands, keeps every case, and matches the prose calculations.
