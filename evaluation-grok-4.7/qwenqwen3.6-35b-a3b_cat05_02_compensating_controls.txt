The case analysis is often careful and non-mechanical, but it is not reliable enough on recoverability, pair independence, or the required executable checks.

The strongest judgments are AP-202 (failed: override, conflicted supervisor, late ratification, unrecovered loss), AP-203 and AP-207 (ED-1 met, including AP-207’s post-release but pre-settlement approval), AP-205 (stopped before completion), AP-208 (multiple ED-1 failures and no timely correction), and AP-201 (same-user release without an emergency permission, only partly offset by independent pre-settlement review). The preventive/detective/hybrid treatment of manager action before settlement is coherent, and the monitoring section separates alerts from proof.

AP-209 is misclassified. Distinct users, a full AV-2 pass, a timely clean audit, a modest amount, and an established vendor do not support OBJECTIVE_FAILED, high residual risk, or high confidence. Missing manager approval is not a breach of FEP-1 on a nominally separated payment, and the repeated u12/u13 pairing is explicitly unresolved without organizational-design evidence. INDETERMINATE or OBJECTIVE_PARTIALLY_MET fits the facts. AP-204’s substantive failure is defensible because a fabricated receipt passed both actors, but “recall available” and “recall likely possible” are wrong: the audit is on May 8, far outside the usual 24-hour recall window. AP-206’s token defect is also stated too certainly; the facts leave logging delay versus a real ED-1 breach open. m21’s independence is assumed without support.

The code does not run. `axis=1` falls outside `apply`, and a `format_dataframe` result is treated as an event log with `.get_attribute_values` and `.get_dataframe`. Blocked releases are not actually identified. Manager “timing” is inferred from activity names, bank-change risk is proxied by a homemade vendor-risk label (falsely flagging AP-204), and emergency eligibility is reduced to token presence, which wrongly clears AP-206 and AP-208.
