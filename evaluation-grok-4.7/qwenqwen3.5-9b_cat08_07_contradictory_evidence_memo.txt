5.5

The recommendation is directionally defensible: do not scale nationally, do not fully roll back, restrict the redesign to simple low-risk houses, suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation, and measure before any wider rollout. The six required sections exist, the memo stays within the word limit, and it does not treat one metric as decisive. That is the ceiling.

The supporting analysis is not reliable enough for an executive decision.

The memo misstates segment results that drive the choice. Apartment median duration improved by 2 days; only old buildings worsened by 1 day. Lumping both as “suffer (median +1 day, complaints +7%)” uses the old-building figures for both and writes percentage points as percents. Apartment complaints are +5pp, not +7. The evidence table then calls 29% a survey-skip “failure rate.” That figure is the pilot share of medium-risk apartments. “34% of simple houses” is the same kind of error: simple houses are 34% of pilot cases.

The evidence table does not keep facts, interpretations, and assumptions apart. Manual activation rising from 7% to 15% is a reported fact, not an interpretation. Survey skipping is a design fact. Exclusion of complaint cost after week 4 is a coverage fact, yet it is the table’s only “assumption.” Real assumptions—case-mix confounding, whether EUR 207 is directionally trustworthy, and whether the skip rule caused apartment harm—are never labeled. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings, segment attributes are missing before week 3, and the full-cost estimate is incomplete; the memo still reads baseline-to-pilot gaps as effects of the redesign.

The fitness/precision explanation conflates the two metrics. Low precision means the discovered model allows many traces that did not occur. Billing before a successful activation test is observed log behavior; it is an alignment and DFG finding, not what precision measures. High fitness with low precision is better read as a permissive model over highly variable pilot behavior, which is the interpretation the process-mining team already offered.

Alternative C is misread. C already suspends survey skipping and post-billing activation. The memo says C fails to address those issues. A–E are not evaluated with advantages, risks, and conditions for each. Temporal-profile deviations, DFG edge shifts, and the resource shift from survey teams (78% to 52%) to second-visit teams (66% to 92%) are largely unused despite being central evidence.

The value-of-information ranking names topics but not how each result would change the decision. M1, the randomized test of skipping, is ranked only “medium” and treated as overlapping M6, although it is the causal test of the rule the recommendation suspends. M4 is ranked high and then dropped from what to measure next. M2 and M8 are discounted for weak reasons. The rollout gate—p95 within 5% of 45 days and complaints under 7% within 8 weeks—is arbitrary, not segment-specific, and ignores utilization, permit conformance, and first-time-right. Scaling coordinator ownership for simple houses also ignores 93% utilization and absence stalls, which the memo itself flags as a resilience problem.

The headline action is reasonable. The numbers, categories, and causal claims underneath it are not clean enough to score higher.
