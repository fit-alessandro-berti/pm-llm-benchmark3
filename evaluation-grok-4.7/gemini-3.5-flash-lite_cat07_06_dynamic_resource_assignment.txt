4.5

The write-up has the required seven sections and names the right objectives, but it is not a coherent, auditable policy. The recommended queue contradicts the hard rules the answer itself declares, several case rationales are factually wrong, and the scoring procedure cannot produce the table.

The overtime rule is stated as non-negotiable, then broken. C003 (normal, 18h), C010 (normal, 20h), and C014 (normal, 25h) all go to high-overtime resources even though safer legal alternatives exist: Celine for diagnosis and rework, and Farid or Eva for approval. C003 is described as an “urgent need” although its priority is normal. C010’s main reason says Bruno has low overtime risk; the trade-off cell and the matrix say high. David, already at 14 open cases and 3.0 hours, receives two new cases. The pseudocode would have excluded all three assignments, so the table is not an output of the stated algorithm.

Training labels are systematically incorrect. A match requires the activity to be the resource’s documented training priority on a normal/low case with acceptable SLA risk. Only C009 (Amal, diagnosis, skill 1, 22h) meets that. C004, C006, and C012 are marked as training while the text admits the activity is a different skill. C010 treats a skill-2 approver as a trainee; Bruno’s training priority is quality, and skill 1 may only prepare an approval under supervision. C015 assigns the work to Hugo, then calls it training for Eva, whose security skill is 0 and who therefore cannot perform a Security Review. Of nine normal/low cases, one real training assignment is about 11%, below the 20% rule. The notes on C010, C012, and C015 are not implemented as supervised pairs.

Several continuity claims are internally inconsistent. C002 “bypasses the current owner” and keeps that same owner. C005 and C015 describe a handover while assigning the case to the person who already owns it. C001 is called an optimal safety match with no trade-off, despite high complexity, high handover cost, and 3h remaining against the stated median 2.7h waiting penalty for a high-complexity owner change. That handover can itself consume the SLA, and the answer never weighs a supervised alternative.

The scoring function is not operational. Weights are undefined. The SLA term depends only on the case, so it is a constant and does not change the chosen resource. Continuity is a crude +1 only in an ambiguous “high complexity/handover” situation, and weak-skill continuity is not penalized despite the stated 3.3h service-time effect. Overtime is both a hard exclusion and a −∞ penalty. There is no resilience or bottleneck term, no batching rule, no hours-remaining gate, no sort by urgency before load is updated, no tie-break, and no exception path when the filter is empty. Section 1 correctly covers high-priority work at skill below 2, but the pseudocode applies that gate only to urgent cases, so priority “high” can still reach a skill-1 resource.

The comparison awards the proposed policy minimized SLA risk, optimized fairness, active development, and high resilience on every soft dimension. Those claims are not earned by this queue, and the baselines are not actually run on C001–C016. The worker text says high-overtime people are shielded from non-urgent intake, which this batch does not do.

The audit list covers the requested indicator families, including a 20% training share and overtime exceptions. The sketch does not compute them. It ignores the log fields that would make an audit possible, mislabels reassignment filtering as rework, and would fail as written: `format_dataframe` has no `resource_key`, a handover network is not a dict with `["edges"]`, and `discover_working_together_network` does not unpack into two values. `discover_batches`, `get_service_time`, and `filter_case_performance` are unused, and nothing in the code learns or calibrates the policy.
