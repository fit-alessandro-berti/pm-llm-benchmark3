The answer meets the required structure and stays numerically faithful to the process data: all twelve candidates are ranked with the requested columns, frequency is not the ranking key, three portfolios are differentiated, effects are tied to specific activities and directly-follows counts, and the rollout respects the four-module budget and the two deep integrations in the first six months. A11 is correctly kept out, and human approval, audit trails, and exception capacity are treated as remaining obligations.

The main failure is the cycle-time claim. Calling A2, A4, A5, and A7 the best end-to-end portfolio overstates a variant-specific result. Entity mismatch covers 1,750 cases and collateral valuation about 2,350, while customer signing (7,300 cases, 44h), credit analysis (8,900, 28h), contract preparation (7,600, 22h), and normalization (5,200, 20h) remain largely untouched. The 3,900 committee cases versus 7,600 contracts are never reconciled, so straight-through versus committee paths are blurred. Committee packaging is also said to wait on normalized statements, yet A6 is omitted from the “synchronization” portfolio even though A5 is expected to increase clean inflow to the nine analysts—the exact overload the resource evidence warns about. WIP caps do not remove that bottleneck.

Control coverage is thinner than the findings require. Phase 1 deploys A2 and A5; A2 is routing quality, not a direct response to four-eyes violations (5.8%), KYC-before-signing, or contract-term mismatches (8.5%, 14% on renewals). Those stay manual, while A4 arrives only in months 3–6. Concurrency between KYC and document review is named once and not turned into a design rule, and the weekly high-exposure committee cadence and afternoon back-office slack are unused. The recommendation is coherent and well controlled, but the central process-level trade-off is not fully defended.
