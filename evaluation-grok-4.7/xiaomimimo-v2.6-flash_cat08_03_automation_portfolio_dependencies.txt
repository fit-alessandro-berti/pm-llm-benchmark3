8.2

The final answer is a strong, constraint-aware portfolio recommendation, but it is not nearly flawless. It hits every required section, refuses a frequency-first ranking, keeps A11 and A8 behind upstream controls, and treats compliance as a retained human duty. The deductions below are for unsupported precision, one material control-design gap, and a few overclaims.

What holds up. All twelve candidates are ranked on critical path, joins, scarce pools, rework, and control findings rather than volume. A1 is correctly kept out of year one: back-office demand is small relative to 12 FTE, utilization drops after 15:00, and `Register → Classify` (11,680) would move the queue into an 8% misroute. The dependency map covers data quality, KYC-before-signing, four-eyes, the 380-case `Resolve entity mismatch → Contract preparation` bypass, the 3-FTE specialist bottleneck, the external 58-hour join, and the two-deep-integration cap. Q, B, and C each state what they optimize and sacrifice, and R (A9, A4, A5, A6) is a defensible hybrid: A9 in wave 1 meets the control-module rule, A9 and A4 are the only deep integrations inside six months, and resource shifts (analyst WIP, legal-ops redeployment, afternoon back-office slack, RM co-design) are explicit. Effects cite activities, variants, and DFG edges, and the risk table covers audit trail, false negatives, drift, exceptions, and four-eyes.

What should cost points. The matrix was required to stay qualitative. The effects and gates do not: waits are cut to “~5–10 h” or “~2–4 h,” rework to “~2–3%” or “<3%,” and gates use ≤2%, <20%, ≤30%, and ≥70% with no basis in the data. Those are unsupported forecasts, not ratings. More serious, A9 is the wave-1 control module whose core rule is “contract terms match the committee decision,” yet `Committee decision → Contract preparation` is only 3,650 against 7,600 contract preparations. About half of contracts have no committee predecessor in the DFG (renewals and delegated authority are the obvious variant, and renewals already show 14% remediation). The answer never splits the rule for that path, so the flagship control can false-fail a large share of volume. Smaller faults: “200 cases signed but never activated” treats a 7,300 vs 7,100 snapshot gap as confirmed leakage; A4’s AML/KYC write-back is assumed to be one of the two core-banking integrations; and rank 1 is A9 (“High” process benefit) over A4 (“Very high”) while the ranking intro says the order is process evidence.
