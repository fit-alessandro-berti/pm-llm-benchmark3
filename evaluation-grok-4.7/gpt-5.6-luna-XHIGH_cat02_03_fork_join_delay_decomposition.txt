7.5

The delay decomposition is correctly defined and internally additive. Upstream time, branch queue, service, join-ready as the latest branch end, fork-join elapsed time as \(\max_i(Q_{ci}+S_{ci})\), downstream gap, final service, and total flow time are the right quantities, and \(T_c=U_c+L_c+D_c+F_c\) is the right non-overlapping case identity. The double-counting argument is also right: each branch path \(Q+S+W\) equals \(L\), so the three paths sum to \(3L\), and adding \(\sum W\) on top of \(L\) counts the same calendar interval again. The F02 illustration (sync waits 10+0+35, \(L=60\)) is correct.

Every case row I recomputed matches the table, including the less obvious cells: F04’s sync waits (7, 17, 0), F08’s C path of 55, F11’s A-critical join at 11:30, and the means \(U=19.58\), \(L=61\), \(D=14.67\), \(F=20\), \(T=115.25\). The worked examples for F02, F04, F07, and F11 are exact. Critical-branch counts (A 2, B 8, C 2), service and queue summaries, and conditional critical-path lengths are exact. The ranking correctly refuses to treat A’s 50-minute service as dominance and uses critical frequency, queue-plus-service, and the 75-minute tail. Calling A and C frequency-tied, then separating A’s stable floor from C’s queue spikes, is defensible.

The three counterfactual tables are numerically right, including effects a naive half-queue or 20% cut would miss. A’s 20% service cut saves only 15 minutes because only F01 (5, then B binds) and F11 (10) move. Halving B’s queue saves 92.5, with F10 correctly cut to 5 once A binds. The extra C-service check, 4.4 on F04 and 5 rather than 6 on F08, is also right. Zero-benefit cases are identified, and holding \(D\) fixed is labeled as an isolation assumption rather than a predicted operating result. Section 7 correctly limits causality, hidden preemption, capacity, and the queue-versus-policy reading of \(D\). The Python implements the table, the max join, deterministic A-before-B-before-C tie breaks, all three counterfactuals, and a guarded `format_dataframe` plus performance DFG, with a comment that directly-follows performance does not recover synchronization.

The score is held down by a real inconsistency and a missed use of supplied evidence. Section 5 says that cutting B’s queue can increase A and C synchronization waits because those branches “finish earlier relative to B.” Under the paper’s own definitions, A and C end times stay fixed and the join moves earlier, so their waits fall. F02 is enough to show it: A’s wait goes from 10 to 0 and C’s from 35 to 25. The prose also treats F10 as the only partial B-queue gain. F05 is partial too: half of its 25-minute queue is 12.5, but A’s path of 50 caps the saving at 10. The table has 10; the text does not.

More importantly, `final_resource` is barely used. F1 and F2 are often idle during `final_start - join_ready`. At F01’s 09:00 join both servers are free; F02 waits until 09:40 while F2 is idle; F03, F07, F11, and F12 likewise wait on a resource that is already free. Both servers are busy at the join in only a minority of cases, such as F05 and, briefly, F09. That directly weakens the claim that downstream congestion is masking branch gains and the 176-minute \(D=0\) ceiling as a capacity opportunity. Section 5 does say to validate the gap before adding capacity, but the timestamps already support that test. The throughput claim is also ahead of a 12-case log that section 7 itself says cannot support a throughput law. Finally, a directly-follows DFG on concurrent A/B/C completions invents sequence; the code calls it as required but does not state that limitation.
