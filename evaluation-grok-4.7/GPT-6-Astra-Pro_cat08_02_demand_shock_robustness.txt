8.2

The answer is a strong, constraint-aware redesign with unusually careful evidence handling, but it is not nearly flawless. Several moderate gaps and smaller logical slips keep it well below a top score.

What holds up. All seven required sections are present and actually used. The four mandatory disruption types are separated from total volume, and S5 adds a compound case. The mix decomposition is arithmetically right: about 2,300 to 3,300 orders per week (+43.5%), Routine essentially flat, Urgent about +131%, Complex-panel about +121%, and Urgent plus Complex-panel about 2.27×, correctly labeled as an order-count proxy rather than a workload multiplier. The repeat-edge shares, 6.7% to 14.8%, match the listed Assay-run outs and are explicitly distinguished from the 7.5% to 11.8% rework rates. Normal Urgent p90 of 19 hours is correctly treated as already over the 18-hour contract, so the normal median is not used as the target. DFG medians are not summed. Utilization above 100% is not treated as a literal busy fraction. Downstream overload is discussed for every retained intervention. I3, I4, I9, and I10 are modified or rejected for the right reasons: M2 is not made the Routine dump, technical validation at 101% blocks unconditional cross-loan, release is not shown to be the bottleneck, and 12 hours of transport is not subtracted from the 48-hour hold edge. Anti-starvation scheduling, conditional activation, and redesign-caused waiting are all explicit.

Moderate problems. The Urgent slack band is amber only at 0–4 hours and red at or below zero, while the same answer reports peak median queues of 16–36 hours. That band fires too late to be a leading control for an 18-hour deadline. The two-hour triage target has the same tension with a 9.5-hour pre-analytical queue and 96% utilization. It is labeled provisional, but the proposed operating rule is still mis-scaled to the evidence. In S1, the bottleneck story follows process order from intake forward. The queue data show a different pattern: medical validation inflates from 2 hours to 22 hours, specialist review from 5 to 36, and courier from 1 to 7.5, versus roughly 4–5× in the middle stations. The decisive comparison is never stated: the peak medical-validation queue alone exceeds the entire Urgent allowance, so upstream acceleration cannot save Urgent cases that still require that review. The migration text is directionally sensible, but it underplays where delay actually explodes.

Smaller faults. Peak technical validation, 7,240 in from Assay run versus 8,080 out, is real, and the window-censoring caveat is sound. Offering flattened multi-assay behavior as an explanation for more outs than ins is weak: repeated assays would tend to raise both sides. The larger Assay-run conservation gap is not measured—normal inflow about 18,260 versus outflow about 17,030, and peak about 10,250 versus 8,500—even though that gap is more relevant to capacity. The resource note states that M2 has a higher failure rate; the trade-off table then treats M2 mainly as an association still needing investigation, blurring a given fact with the night-shift cluster. The I4 rule also applies a three-week average utilization of 101% to a next-shift donor guard. Open cases are visible in the DFG—roughly 11% normal and 13% peak never reach END—but are not used to test the censoring claim. Repeat-after-failure remains thinner in the trade-off table than its double assay consumption warrants. Burden ratings cluster at medium–high and do little discriminating work.

No material arithmetic invention or constraint violation turned up. The deductions are for a late warning design, an under-ranked bottleneck, and a few loose uses of the log, not for a wrong redesign.
