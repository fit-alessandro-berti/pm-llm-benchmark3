The answer is a serious, well-structured portfolio recommendation that follows the required sections, stays inside the four-module budget, refuses a frequency-led A1 lead, and keeps A11 behind control readiness. It is not nearly flawless. Several process-level claims are loose, and the final trade-off argues against the easy alternative more than against the real one.

The matrix is usable and mostly faithful to the evidence. A1 is correctly kept low on process benefit despite high direct labor saving. A11 is correctly last. A4 is treated as a critical-path exception, not a volume play. Ratings stay qualitative. The dependency section covers data quality, KYC-before-signing, scarce specialist escalation, the external valuer, and the two-deep-integration limit. The three required portfolios are distinct, and the phased plan actually obeys the six-month integration cap by keeping A4 sidecar and A9 in shadow until month 7.

The main reasoning failure is the recommended set A4, A5, A6, A9. Dropping A7 leaves the longest synchronization delay in the log, the 58-hour external valuation on 2,350 cases and on 71% of equipment-finance files. Dropping A10 also leaves the 44-hour customer-signing wait and its 21% reminder loop. Section 3 names the sacrifice as A1 and A7, then defends it with a reason that only fits A1: registration is short. That does not explain why a partial entity-mismatch assistant on 1,750 cases outranks valuation triage, or why contract generation outranks the second-longest wait. Section 7 repeats the A1 argument and still does not make the A4/A9 versus A7 comparison.

Waiting time is mishandled throughout section 4. Median wait before an activity is an upstream queue, not the activity’s own service time. A5/A6 can cut repeat loops and free analyst hours; they do not directly “target” the 18-hour wait before financial-document review or a vague “credit-analysis preparation” wait. The same section speaks as if medium-feasibility assistants will materially move those queues. Phase 0 notes that KYC and financial-document review are concurrent while the log stores them in sequence, then the effect claims still read the recorded waits as additive cycle time.

Control coverage is overstated. Four-eyes failures sit between credit analysis and committee packaging in 5.8% of high-exposure cases, especially at month-end. None of A4, A5, A6, or A9 performs that segregation. A contract-rule check occurs after packaging, so it can detect a violation only late. Calling A9 a hard KYC-before-signing gate also misses the stated bypass: provisional signing already occurs in 6.5% of cases. The 380 cases on Resolve entity mismatch → Contract preparation are a second control-sensitive skip and are never examined. The compliance-first portfolio’s claim that it can enforce four-eyes is stronger than the modules support.

Two smaller priority errors remain. A6 is ranked first with “very high” first-year suitability even though feasibility is only medium, low-quality PDFs are an explicit failure mode, and the text itself says A6 works best after A5. A5 and A6 are also labeled the two deep core-banking integrations, while portal nudging and statement extraction are weaker core-banking candidates than contract generation or KYC/entity resolution. The weekly high-exposure committee cadence is named once and then left out of the cycle-time ceiling for every portfolio that still feeds committee packaging.

The recommendation is directionally defensible: flow and control before registration volume, analysts protected by normalization, activation not automated on dirty controls, audit logs and escalation limits specified. The score stays at 7.5 because the decisive exclusion of the 58-hour sync point is not earned, two long waits are under-reported as sacrifices, and several control and waiting-time claims go beyond what the DFG, variants, and module scopes can support.
