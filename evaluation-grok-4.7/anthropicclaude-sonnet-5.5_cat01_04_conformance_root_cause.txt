7.5

The analysis is strong on abductive structure, but it is not nearly flawless. It misreads a source fact, then builds on that misreading, and several headline numbers contradict the same answer’s own arithmetic.

The clearest error is the 2026-07-10 memo. The chronology says the memo reminded reviewers not to bypass compliance checks and **did not** mention a known increase in violations. Section 1 (E18), the noncompliance row, and section 7 all quote a “known increase in violations” as if it were in the memo, and treat it as support for employee noncompliance. That is a fabricated citation, not a weak inference. Reliability 35 does not repair it.

The conformance arithmetic is otherwise careful and mostly right: April–May is 600/600 fitting against the tree, not 93%; June–July raw fit is 0%, not 32%; label map alone is 90/650 (13.8%), not 58%; map plus dropping `Queue Routed` is 535/650 (82.3%) against a reported 81%; full restoration of the 70 reversals ceilings near 93%; August–September after the same repairs ceilings at 345/620 (55.6%), not 66%. Enhanced share (30% / 26% / 43.5%), missing-check rates (14.7% then 11.1%), sign-off 210/620, and events per case (5.43 and 6.12 versus reported 5.4 and 6.3) all check out. The path-specific reversals, the vanished `Request Info` loops, the conjunctive label/`Queue Routed` effect, and the overpermissive optional sign-off are the right discriminators.

Those findings are then undermined in the conclusion. Section 7 says staged repairs lift fitness “from 32% to 89%” and that the optional model recovers “about twenty points,” after E5 has shown 32%, 58%, and 66% cannot be reproduced. On the variant table the August–September optional-sign-off gain is roughly 30 points (about 56% to about 90% if every reversal is repaired), not 20. The missing-resource claim is also invalid: `Queue Routed` is about 14% of June–July events, so a 5.9% null-resource rate can still be entirely a subset of that technical event. It does not show that null resources “are not simply the technical events.”

Presentation makes the quantitative story hard to use. Transition markers are gone, so “32→58→81” becomes “325881,” “20→35” becomes “Manual Override (2035)” in the conclusion, and “5.4→6.3→6.8” becomes “5.46.36.8.” A reader can take 2035 as a case count. The counterfactual row for the label × drop factorial says both single repairs fit “only 30 hybrids”; dropping `Queue Routed` alone fits 30, but the label map alone fits 90.

Format and coverage meet the spec: eight headings, the required columns, at least twelve ledger rows across the requested evidence types, six ranked hypotheses with a separate operational-risk judgment, a 180–280 word causal write-up, eleven counterfactuals, four phases that isolate provenance, repair attribution, model comparison, and residual audit, and six governance rules tied to the failure mode. The PM4Py block uses the required calls, encodes both trees, expands the variant tables, compares raw and normalized logs, and states the EUR 50,000 overgeneralization caveat. The flower baseline and the order-fraction sensitivity are useful. `parse_event_log_string(..., sep=",")` is only assumed, and alignment-tuple parsing is defensive rather than guaranteed, but the pipeline is logically coherent.

What keeps this above a mid score is the discrimination itself: logging and technical events are separated from model obsolescence, fitness is not treated as validation, employees are not blamed for log moves, and unchanged approval and appeal rates are correctly treated as low-power. The memo inversion and the reuse of numbers the answer itself refutes are what keep it well short of 9.
