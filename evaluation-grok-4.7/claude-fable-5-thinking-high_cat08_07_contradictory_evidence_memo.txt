7.4

The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision and keeps facts, interpretations, and assumptions mostly distinct, but several overclaims, one muddled causal sentence, and uneven use of the process-mining evidence keep it short of nearly flawless.

The recommendation is the right family of choice. Option E, with survey-skipping and post-billing activation held back and measurement before any wider scale, respects the simple-house gains without nationalizing the worse tail, rework, and billing-before-activation pattern. Rollback is rejected for a segment-specific reason, not because some metrics fell. The evidence table is the strongest part: twelve items, with directly observed numbers kept apart from causal readings and open assumptions. The five conflict explanations are mostly right about what each pair of metrics actually measures. Alternatives A–E each get advantages, risks, and conditions, and the value-of-information ranking is tied to decisions rather than to generic data quality.

The strict deductions are these.

The median-versus-tail account invents an “easy 60% / hard 40%” split and treats the conflict as mostly between segments. Apartments themselves improved on the median (−2 days) while their p90 worsened (+12 days), so the same split occurs inside segments. That invented split is a real analytical error.

“Some ‘improvement’ may be unmeasured variance rather than design” does not follow from precision falling from 0.79 to 0.51. Low precision means the discovered model is permissive. It does not turn the measured 14.2→10.8 median into unmeasured noise.

Confidence is uneven. Simple houses “improved on every metric” and the gain is “verified,” yet cost, NPS, manual activation, and second visits are not reported for that segment, attributes are incomplete before week 3, and the same answer later allows a novelty effect. “The other two-thirds deteriorated” is then illustrated with aggregate p95, first-time-right, complaints, and second visits, which are not the other two-thirds’ metrics. Manual activation went from 7% to 15%, which is more than doubled. Pilot mix is correctly called biased against R, then simple houses are still treated as about a third of national volume.

Temporal profile is the weakest required artifact. Installation-visit → activation-test worsening for apartments is essentially unused. Billing-start → complaint as a new long-tail time relation is only partly covered via edge share. Generalization (0.70→0.56) never appears. Activation-specialist centralization and unclear ownership after billing start are thin. Billing before activation was an intentional redesign for acceptable signal, not only a control failure; outcomes justify stopping it, but the memo sometimes treats designed paths as pure indiscipline.

Internal lines do not match. Immediate action suspends survey skipping for apartments and old buildings; the final action also forces surveys back onto business and rural cases, whose stated problems are manual-activation disputes and harder rescheduling. “End-to-end” R for simple houses conflicts with stopping post-billing activation in every segment. M6 is ranked above M1 even though the rollout gate requires a randomized result, so M6 alone cannot open that gate. M7 is ranked fourth while described as not a decision-changer. Coordinator ownership is extended broadly while the absence test (M5) is deferred to a second wave. The 85% utilization cap, 2pp / 1pp / precision-0.70 gates, and 8-week window are stated as if calibrated; a 1pp complaint difference is a hard thing to see in a short stratified test, and that feasibility gap is never discussed. Moving survey staff onto second visits assumes those crews are interchangeable.

Section coverage, trade-off clarity, and refusal to scale or roll back wholesale are strong. The remaining issues are repeated small inaccuracies plus a few moderate reasoning faults, which under a strict reading hold the score at 7.4.
