3.5

The memo is only a short, non-decision-grade paragraph, and the later sections are nested under it rather than delivered as the six required top-level sections. It correctly avoids national scale and a full rollback, but the recommendation is not well defended.

The evidence table collapses items into three blob cells and misclassifies them. It inverts precision (the pilot model’s precision fell from 0.79 to 0.51; it did not become “higher”) and treats “complaint handling improvements” as an assumption when complaints rose from 6.1% to 9.8%. Coverage, confounding, cost truncation, sparse timestamps, alignments, DFG changes, temporal profiles, and resource or social networks are largely absent. Cross-references such as “Fact 3” and “Fact 6” do not match the table.

Metric conflicts are incomplete. Fitness versus precision is never explained. The median-versus-tail account wrongly limits the median gain to the 34% simple-house share, even though most other segments also improved at the median while tails worsened. Cost, handover, resilience, and segment points are directionally relevant but use the wrong fact labels, omit magnitudes, and skip the region mix and incomplete-cost caveat.

Alternatives A–E lack real advantages and conditions of reasonableness. E, the option most consistent with the segment split, is dismissed mainly as “fragmentation,” and D’s risks are not stated. The value-of-information ranking contradicts its own High/Medium labels, has M1 “validating” M2 and M3, and claims M1/M3/M6 resolve downstream cost, which is M4’s job. It does not say how results would move the choice among A–E.

The final call pauses only national rollout. It leaves survey skipping and billing-before-activation in place, defers even the already supported simple-house gain, and sets crude gates, including an unsupported 85% utilization cutoff and a requirement that every segment beat baseline.
