The core diagnosis is right and the required structure is complete: March is treated as a semantic/provenance break, later rework and queue growth are not collapsed into logging drift, and the timeline separates supplier, staffing, priority, seasonality, and the December confound. The baseline-relative table and the L1/L2 figures on the seven supplied edges are arithmetically correct. That is not enough for a high score. Several quantitative claims are invented or internally inconsistent, causal language outruns the aggregates, and parts of the code and monitors would not work on this evidence.

The repeated “22–26 per 100” senior-assessment rate is not in the data. October is 22; November has no DFG row. That figure is used in the timeline, the seasonal hypothesis, and the attribution. It is manufactured precision. The cycle-time split has the same defect. An 8–12 hour penalty per reworked case is unsupported, and it does not produce the stated ~3.0 hour population burden: a +17.5 point rework increase at 8–12 hours is about 1.4–2.1 hours, not 2.5–4.0. Non-Assess service times are declared stable without evidence. Kingman’s approximation is invoked with no utilization, headcount, or arrival process. The four shares are not an identified partition; their midpoint happens to sit near +17.85 hours, while the narrative premises do not generate those shares. Queueing is plausibly the largest piece because Assess service rose only about 0.6 hour, but the percentages are not estimated.

DFG rigor is thinner than the distance table looks. L1/L2 are transparent and January is the correct baseline, since February is not supplied. The claim that omitted edges are ≤2% and stable is incompatible with the fragment. January Collect Documents inflow is about 98+11 against a Collect Documents→Assess rate of 95; in October the gap is about 95+25 versus 90. October’s request-info, senior-assessment, and decision rates sum to 121, versus about 99 in January, so edge mass overlaps and the overlap changes. L1 therefore double-counts dependent moves and misses a growing residual exactly where drift is largest. October is also a weak logging change point: label cardinality stays at 11, and Assess→Senior Assessment already exists in January at 4 per 100. The senior rate rises much faster than complexity share (about 7 per 100 at 26% complexity in September versus 22 at 41% in October). That disproportion is the interesting clue and is never measured; 94% confidence that mix “caused” the surge is too high, especially given historically inconsistent senior recording.

Priority is over-claimed. The brief states that the monthly aggregate cannot show redistribution. P90/median only moves from about 2.19 at baseline to about 2.33 in June, before the rule, and about 2.44 in August. Section 4 is appropriately skeptical; sections 1 and 9 still say the rule redistributed delay. July rework is a continuation of May–June, not a new control-flow break. June is called gradual, but the departure is 15 June, so the June bucket mixes two regimes. September 10 and December 5 have the same mid-month problem and are not treated as mixed bins.

Smaller factual slips accumulate. “Missing execution timestamps” is not in the evidence. September rework is 20% and December is 21%, so rework does not “drop further” after the quality campaign. Supplier-Z share falling does not “mask” October volume; total intake rises. Hypothesis 6 compares cycle-time levels with earlier increments and calls that super-additivity. Mapped fitness still slips from about 0.91 to 0.86 by July and tracks operational stress; the final attribution treats March as if mapping fully restored conformance.

The control stratum (standard complexity and not Supplier Z) is a good attribution slice and a poor primary monitoring population, because it is designed to exclude the supplier, complexity, and priority mechanisms that dominate the loss. Several minimum-support rules fail in baseline months, including Supplier Z, high complexity, and senior-only paths, with no pooling rule.

Monitoring covers the requested families and correctly rejects one global threshold. Hierarchical gating is sound. Several rules are not calibrated to this series. A mapped-versus-raw gap above 0.10 is true in every month after February, so the conformance alert never clears. Duplicate rate above 1% and missing-resource rate above 2% are likewise permanently on after the migration if interpreted as change detectors. P90/P50 above 2.5 never occurs, including November at about 2.47, so the starvation rule would not have fired during the observed crisis. Benjamini–Hochberg is named for threshold rules that do not produce comparable p-values.

The code would reproduce the valid aggregate math, and copying the log before relabeling is right. Duplicate removal is not. March shows Open Case→Register, and 26 such edges match the 4% duplicate rate; the code only drops consecutive Open Case→Open Case pairs, so it would leave the observed artifact in place. The consecutive check also depends on row order rather than timestamp order. Attaching `org:resource` after `format_dataframe` is index-fragile. The change-point rules are simple and, unlike the prose, correctly flag May cycle time; they still miss the May supplier-share break and the prose/code lists do not agree.

March logging versus May rework versus later queue growth is the correct skeleton. The grade stops at 7.0 because the answer then assigns false precision, states unresolvable mechanisms as findings, and specifies normalization and alerts that contradict its own evidence.
