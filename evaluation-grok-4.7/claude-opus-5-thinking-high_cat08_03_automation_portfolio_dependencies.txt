The portfolio logic is genuinely strong: frequency is rejected with a population waiting-exposure view, A1 is held back because it feeds the analyst pool, A11 is refused while KYC and contract controls are weak, and the three portfolios state real sacrifices. Those choices track the log, the resource network, and the control findings. The write-up is still not reliable enough to score higher, because several of its own quantitative claims contradict each other or the same data.

The binding IT constraint is told three different ways. Sections 2, 3, and 7 call A6 and A4 medium-depth integrations that leave core-banking capacity in reserve, and treat A1 and A9 as the scarce deep slots. Section 6 then assigns A6 and A4 as deep slot 1 and deep slot 2. A first-year plan cannot both consume the two-deep-integration limit and keep it unused.

The headline effect does not survive the answer’s own caveats. “About 190h for a plain case” adds median waits, including committee packaging and committee decision, after the text has shown that only 3,650 of 7,600 contract preparations follow a committee decision. KYC and financial-document review are described as concurrent, then their queue reductions are added into the “35–45h / 20–25%” claim anyway. That figure is the political justification in the final recommendation, and it is not a critical-path result.

The same utilization test is applied selectively. Back-office work of about 4,800 hours on 12 FTE is correctly treated as slack. Specialist mismatch work is then given as about 1,600 hours, “~33% of specialist capacity,” and still described as a pool that cannot absorb escalations. A one-third-loaded 3-FTE pool does not, by itself, explain a 31-hour wait; the two-senior handover concentration might, but that mechanism fights the later requirement of mandatory specialist confirmation on every case. Analyst demand is totaled near 30,000 hours against 9 FTE and never reconciled, so the step from “A6 frees about 22%” to “the 28-hour credit-analysis queue falls to 15–20 hours” is asserted rather than shown. Faster normalization also raises arrivals into credit analysis; the answer uses that Little’s-law point against A1 and then assumes it away for A6.

A7 is recommended in year 1 while Section 2 says safe “valuation not required” triage depends on classification quality that A2 is supposed to supply. A2 is deferred, and the risk table never covers a skipped valuation caused by the existing 8% misroute rate. Parallelizing valuation before the credit decision also creates scrap requests for cases that will be declined; that cost is absent.

Directly-follows gaps are over-read. The 380 mismatch-to-contract edges are called the mechanism of 6.5% provisional signing, without a count reconciliation (about 475 signing cases at 6.5%). The 370 valuation cases that do not directly reach committee packaging are called leakage, although many valued files can be the non-committee variant already identified elsewhere.

Elsewhere the precision is invented: normalization queues “to 4–8h,” straight-through “~85%,” field errors “under ~1%,” “15–25%” of PDFs, e-sign “above ~70%.” Those are not measurements. Four-eyes is also nudged off its actual pairing when A6 is said to separate acceptance of extracted figures from packaging, rather than credit analysis from committee packaging.

The qualitative ranking, exception design, audit trail, and refusal to automate activation early are the parts that hold. The score stays at 7.0 because the rollout’s key constraint, the signature cycle-time number, and the capacity story used to justify A4 and A6 are internally inconsistent.
