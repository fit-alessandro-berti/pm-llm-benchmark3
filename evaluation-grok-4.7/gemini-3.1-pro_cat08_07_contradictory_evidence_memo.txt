6.5

The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision and hits every required section, but it is not close to flawless. Causal language is repeatedly stronger than the evidence, several process-mining claims are wrong or incomplete, and the measurement ranking conflicts with the recommendation’s own gates.

The executive memo correctly refuses both national scale of Redesign R and a full rollback, and it uses the simple-house gains versus apartment and old-building losses as the decision pivot. It stays within the length limit and names the speed-versus-resilience trade-off. It overstates the record. Handovers were not “eliminated”; cases with three or more fell from 34% to 12%. Coordinator utilization of 93% is reported for two pilot regions, not as a pilot-wide fact. “Segment-level data proves” ignores incomplete segment and building-risk attributes before week 3. Composition bias—more apartments and older buildings in the pilot—is absent from the decision logic even though it can inflate aggregate tail, complaint, and cost figures. Business customers and rural installs, together about 19% of the pilot and both worse on first-time-right and complaints, disappear from the action.

The evidence table does keep facts, interpretations, and assumptions in separate columns and includes eight items. Several facts are blurred. Permit-check deviations are tied to the 18% “old building” segment, while the alignment result is specifically older apartment buildings. The resilience finding is treated as an open assumption after the log already reports stalls when coordinators are absent. Known limits—sparse start timestamps, cost data cut off after week 4, and incomplete attributes—are thinly represented. Interpretations such as “leading directly” and “incentivized” assert a single mechanism the log does not isolate.

The metric-conflict section covers all five contrasts, but the fitness explanation is technically wrong. Fitness of 0.94 is not a perfect fit, and high fitness is not explained by a “flower model.” The stated mining interpretation is that many cases follow the simplified coordinator path, while low precision comes from too many allowed combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start. Generalization also fell, from 0.70 to 0.56, and is never used. The median story over-credits simple houses: they are 34% of the pilot, and apartments, business, and rural cases also have better medians. “Guarantees” unprepared installs and “true total cost” overclaim a non-random pilot and an incomplete estimate that is only EUR 9 higher. Temporal-profile deviations are essentially ignored: faster scheduling for simple houses, slower installation-to-activation for apartments, the new billing-to-complaint tail, and longer failed-visit-to-second-visit delays where coordinator load is high. Resource shifts are also missing, notably field-survey utilization falling from 78% to 52% and the second-visit team rising from 66% to 92%.

Alternatives A–E each have advantages, risks, and conditions. The conditions are thinner than the evidence. A is not reasonable merely if rework is cheap; tail duration, SLA, manual activation, and permit gaps would still matter. C does not necessarily forfeit simple-house speed if merged scheduling remains. E’s operational cost of a split process is fairly stated, but transportability and the ability to classify “simple, low-risk” at intake are not conditions, despite incomplete risk data.

Value-of-information reasoning is the weakest section. M4 cannot “definitively” settle viability, because the decision also turns on tail, SLA, compliance-like activation, and segment mix, and a 60-day total that is not segmented would not by itself confirm the simple-house scale-up. M7 is framed as rule enforcement rather than as information that could change the choice. M1 is ranked too low on the claim that the pilot already showed the skip rule failed. The pilot was not randomized, regions differ in building mix, and attributes are incomplete; a stratified randomized test is one of the few measures that could overturn the decision to keep skipping off. M5 and M6 are the rollout gates in the final section but rank sixth and fourth. M8 is dismissed as lagging even though the unresolved trade-off is appointment speed versus working service.

The final recommendation is directionally consistent with alternative E, but it does not close. It scales coordinator ownership for simple houses while utilization is already extreme and before absence resilience is tested. It does not reinstate permit checks as an immediate control. It leaves business and rural cases unassigned. “Network-wide” is ambiguous for a four-region pilot. The “>90% accuracy” rule for broader survey skipping is arbitrary and is not a decision threshold: accuracy without base rate, false-skip cost, or precision and recall would not justify expansion. M6 and M5 are required for broader rollout but are absent from what to measure next.

The piece is usable as a decision draft. Under a strict reading it loses substantial credit for overclaiming, a real process-mining error, neglected temporal and resource evidence, and a measurement plan that does not match its own decision.
