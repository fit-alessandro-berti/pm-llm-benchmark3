3.7

The write-up keeps the required nine-section shape and shows some real process-mining vocabulary, but the estimands, case facts, estimator choice, and code disagree with each other. An operator who followed it would misclassify breaches.

**Estimands are not well defined.** The quantity named “probability of not being resolved by 72 hours” is then specified as 1 only when `duration_or_age_h > 72` or the case is still open. Under that rule a withdrawal or rejection before 72 hours scores 0, even though the case was never resolved. That same subsection then says S08 (transferred at 68 hours, not open) contributes 1, which contradicts the stated formula. S05 is described as withdrawn at 27 hours; S05 is rejected at 9 hours, and S06 is the withdrawal at 27 hours. The cumulative-incidence examples repeat the error by calling S05 “resolved before 72 hours.” An open case at 92 hours is treated as both an observed breach and a censored contribution to incidence at 72 hours. The claim that “still no terminal disposition at 72 hours” is the same estimand is false: that quantity excludes cases that already withdrew, were rejected, or were transferred, whereas non-resolution includes them. Right censoring before 72 hours (S15 at 35 hours, S31 at 23 hours), competing exits, and already observed breaches (S09, S11, S25, S26, and S32 transferred at 96 hours) stay blurred. Transfers before 72 hours and the transfer after 72 hours are not separated.

**Encoding and sensitivity do not implement the prose.** Section 2 calls “treat transfer as resolved” conservative for the SLA; section 6 calls the same choice optimistic. The three sensitivity rows never change how withdrawal and rejection are handled, and each row changes transfer coding, gap handling, and cohort adjustment together, so a reversal would not be attributable. There are no counts or bounds on a fully enumerated set of 32 cases. A one-at-a-time bound is available: only five cases are open with age under 72 hours, so several 72-hour probabilities can be bounded without a model. “May decrease significantly” is not such a bound. Two of three transfers occur before 72 hours; S32 does not.

**Naive-analysis critique uses the wrong mechanism.** Completed resolutions include late resolutions such as S03 at 76 hours and S13 at 110 hours. Those cases are not dropped by a resolved-case analysis. Cohort B does not have more escalations (6 of 17 versus 6 of 15 in A). Early competing exits are not treated as successes by ordinary completed-case analysis; they are dropped or, under a different mistake, recoded. The below-72 versus above-72 open-case contrast is the soundest part of that section and is still only qualitative.

**The analysis plan recommends a mismatched primary estimator.** Kaplan–Meier with competing events censored estimates cause-specific survival, and \(1-\widehat S(t)\) lies above the cumulative incidence of resolution. Recommending it as primary for a real-world non-resolution or SLA-breach probability conflicts with that limitation. Restricted mean survival time up to a horizon is \(E[\min(T,\tau)]\), not mean hours beyond 72. Landmarking is dismissed even though the task turns on activities that appear after start. A continuous-time multi-state model is called primary although intermediate timestamps are absent, so stage sojourn hazards are not identified. Strata of cohort by priority are mostly size 3–8, which is not confronted.

**Leakage labels are unsafe.** Full-prefix `path_pattern` is marked a baseline feature with no leakage, although it contains the terminal activity. Final `current_stage` for a resolved case is `Resolve`. `rework_count` and `review_count` summarize the whole observed path and are not baseline covariates. The escalation warning is right, but “S04 escalated at 29 hours” invents a time: 29 hours is the time to `Resolve`. Every terminal row has `last_event_gap_h = 0`, so the gap cannot discriminate terminal outcomes; the text still treats it as a straightforward covariate for informative censoring. The state diagram misses the cohort B pattern `Submit→AutoCheck→Screen`, misses `AutoCheck→Escalate`, and misses `Rework→Resolve` on S27.

**The code does not estimate either stated estimand.** Capping every time at 72 hours and leaving `event = 1` for resolutions at 76, 88, 110, and 74 hours codes late resolutions as events at the horizon, which reverses their role in \(S(72)\). The conservative transfer branch sets `event = 0` and leaves the transfer time unchanged, so the Kaplan–Meier sensitivity is identical to censoring at transfer. `competing_risk` stays 0 for both resolution and open cases, so an Aalen–Johansen fit cannot separate the event of interest from censoring. `lifelines` is used with no fallback, and `AalenJohansenFitter` is not a usable lifelines estimator here. Printed `event_table` output is not a 72-hour risk. The PM4Py block fabricates one shared timestamp, drops `Submit`, stores a list in `concept:name` instead of exploding events, and calls `dataframe_utils.convert_to_event_log`, which is not the converter that was imported. `review_count` is a string count, not a control-flow result. The illustrative “5% of P2 cohort B cases” is not produced by the data.

Section 9 does ask for timestamps, post-transfer outcomes, cutoff and start dates, workload, assignment, and rollout compliance. That coverage cannot repair the estimand contradictions or the non-executable sensitivity.
