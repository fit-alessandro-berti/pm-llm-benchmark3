The high-level story is directionally right. It is not reliable enough to score higher.

The central narrative mislabels sequence position as shift. “Early shift (positions 1–15),” “Mid shift,” and “Late shift (positions 51–76+)” collapse two different designs in the data. Early’s last two hours end at a queue of 42 with a 4.2% error rate, while Day ends at 75 with 7.5%. Position 76+ and queue 88 are aggregates, not a universal end-of-shift state. That conflation undermines the requested separation of time-on-shift, clock time, and shift workload.

Several factual readings are wrong or overstated. Queue 46 is medium under the given bands, not “low queue pressure.” The cleanest evidence against fatigue is missed: later error rates under low WIP stay flat or improve (juniors 5.1% to 4.4%; seniors 2.1% to 2.2%). The late 7.9% error spike is therefore weak evidence for fatigue because it co-occurs with queue 88. Circadian support is also misassigned: Night’s 56% first-hour complexity is case mix, and Day’s late-afternoon peak is not a circadian trough. Overlapping shifts, the natural way to separate clock time from time-on-task, are not used. Experience × WIP is underused even though seniors at high WIP (4.9%) and juniors (12.4%) are central to “errors rise only in some conditions.”

Intervention 2 invents an “early error risk” problem. Early errors are relatively low despite high complexity; moving complex work later risks putting it into the high-WIP cell, where high complexity already has a 10.9% error rate. Capping all shift lengths likewise assumes a fatigue effect the low-WIP split does not establish. Some monitoring measures are surveys or audits rather than event-log indicators.
