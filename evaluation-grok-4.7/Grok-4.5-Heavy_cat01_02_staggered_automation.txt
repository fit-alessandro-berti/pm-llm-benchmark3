The answer is a serious causal analysis with the right policy conclusion and broad coverage, but it is not numerically reliable enough for a high score. Several derived results contradict the answer’s own inputs, and those contradictions are then reused as evidence.

The raw adopter pre/post levels are mostly accurate: Alpha’s overall median change is about −0.4 h, Beta’s about +1.9 h, Gamma’s about −3.4 h, and the stated never-treated Jan–Mar to Jul–Aug shifts (median +3.6 h, p90 +14.7 h, rework +2.5 pp, escalation +2.0 pp, CSAT −0.06) check out. The late-window contrast does not. Beta’s Jul–Aug median averages 46.75 against a Jan–Mar mean of 45.9, a change of about +0.9 h. The reported −0.9 h is a sign reversal. That error supports the later claim that late recovery was stronger for the adopters. Epsilon’s May–Jun to Jul–Aug median drop is about 5.2 h, close to Alpha’s 5.9 h and larger than Beta’s true late change.

The difference-in-differences assembly is also inconsistent. Adopter “overall” changes use Apr–Aug, while the never-treated benchmark uses Jul–Aug. Subtracting the stated +3.6 h never-treated median change from Gamma’s −3.4 h gives about −7 h, yet the text reports a Gamma relative effect of −3.5 to −4 h. The −4.5 to −5.5 h Alpha/Beta range does not follow from the Beta and never-treated figures printed immediately above it. Matched-window arithmetic is closer to roughly −3 to −6 h, so the published interval is shifted toward a larger benefit. “Excluding May–June attenuates relative median gains” is not shown and is false for Alpha: those are Alpha’s worst median months, so dropping them increases Alpha’s improvement. The −2 to −6 h and −7 to +5 h bounds are judgmental labels, not calculated sensitivity results.

There is a further internal contradiction. The unadjusted 4–5 h figure is promoted to a conditionally causal claim, while the text says that after branch and complexity adjustment the intensity coefficient on the median is near zero or modestly negative. No adjusted coefficient is reported, and the code never prints one. If the use gradient collapses under minimal adjustment, an unadjusted 4–5 h availability effect cannot be the headline conditional result. Section 7’s confidence of 35 and range of −2 to −6 h already contradicted that ladder.

Section 1 misstates the complexity shock as about +5 pp in every branch. May is a common +5 pp step, but by June Alpha and Epsilon are up about 8 pp from baseline and Beta only about 3 pp. Beta’s complexity also fell in the first treated month, while FTE rose and median and p90 worsened. That immediate adoption-month pattern is not examined. The stable 4–6% automated-fast-path share before availability, including at never-treated branches, is labeled “background” without being treated as a measurement problem for the intensity estimand.

The estimands and threat list are substantively good: availability is separated from use, median from p90, and interference, selection, nonresponse, aggregation, and small clusters are all present. Weaker points are real. The population column repeatedly mixes the target population with the comparison group. E5 combines two different interventions. Adjustment for arrivals and FTE changes the availability estimand into a direct effect holding post-rollout load and staffing fixed, but that bad-control problem is not separated from ordinary confounding. Fraud-desk bias is described as understating branch benefits; contaminating never-treated branches through a shared desk would instead inflate the branch difference-in-differences. Escalation is given higher confidence than median cycle time despite the common March +2 pp break and further untreated increases.

The preferred design is named as a never-treated event study, but the explanation is about 80 words against a required 150–250, and the code estimates covariate-adjusted two-way fixed effects instead. Relative time is constructed and then unused. The rollout recommendation correctly selects embedded evaluation and names guardrails, holdout, and case-level data, but it is slightly under the required length, calls the historical pre-period “clean,” and does not stop the routing bundle. With one new treated branch, branch-level assignment still fails to solve the small-cluster problem; within-branch encouragement or team phase-in would identify fast-path substitution with many cases. The code also compares datetimes with strings, which is fragile to a TypeError, and it warns appropriately about five clusters without estimating the design it recommends.

The final ladder’s “not supported” bullet is the right answer to leadership’s X% request. The “conditionally causal” bullet is too sharp for the arithmetic and the adjustment result above it.
