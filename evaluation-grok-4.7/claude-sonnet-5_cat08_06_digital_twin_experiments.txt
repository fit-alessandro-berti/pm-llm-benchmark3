7.3

The design is a serious discrete-event twin: non-stationary arrivals, shared resources, calendars, latent objections, architect clustering, a full D1–D10 plus stress matrix, and a PM4Py analysis path kept apart from the simulator. It is not nearly flawless. Several specification errors would change simulated cycle times or intervention rankings if implemented as written.

The control-flow reading is wrong in a way that matters. In the given tree, `+` contains only Planning review and the optional Fire and Environmental reviews. Public notice, the inspection loop, decision drafting, and legal check are sequential under `->`. Section 1’s `waiting_for` set and Section 3’s “AND-join” put public notice and inspection inside that parallel block and call this consistent with `+()`. The excerpt shows the opposite order: notice after fire, inspection after the notice interval, drafting after inspection. The code comment then describes `+` correctly, so the specification contradicts itself. Implemented from Section 3, inspection would overlap planning and durations would come out too short.

Council dependence is also the wrong mechanism. Complex variance is calendar-gated: cases wait for a meeting date, then a batch is discharged. The design only turns legal capacity nearly off during council weeks. That produces the opposite queue pattern. Holidays are applied to the public-notice countdown without stating whether the 14-day minimum is calendar days or business days.

Warm-up guidance conflicts with itself. It says initialization must cover a full seasonal cycle because no steady state exists, then discards about 3–4 months. Welch’s method looks for a flat transient; a process with 1.7× spring and autumn peaks never flattens. A 24-month aggregate also dilutes D4, whose effect exists only in peak months, while the decision table seasons utilization but not cycle time or SLA. Starting empty, with no calendar origin tied to holidays and council dates, leaves the warm-up window season-specific.

Tail handling works against the metrics used for decisions. Assumptions cap rework at 3; Section 5 flags paths above 5 and removes them from aggregates. Complex-variance p90 (260 days) is exactly those long paths. Capping them biases the tail downward. Importance sampling of rework length is named without likelihood-ratio weights, so those tail estimates would be biased. Common random numbers are proposed without a stream map for policies that delete activities (D2) or loops (D1), which is where CRN usually breaks.

The estimation plan has a concrete numerical slip: top-20 architects account for 31% of cases, so the rest account for 69% of cases. “Remaining 80% of architects” is not supported by anything given. Service-time text also wobbles. Section 2 treats the published medians as priors because many starts are missing; Section 3 treats those same medians as representative service times while also calling them contaminated. The supplied table is already an inferred split, not a raw sojourn time.

Replay validation is conformance (token replay or alignments on a Petri net), which the write-up itself says is not a timing test. In this setting, event-log replay means holding historical arrivals and routing fixed and comparing simulated waits and resource load. A final 2–3 month holdout checks one season, not whether the annual 1.7× pattern extrapolates.

Operational gaps that the source data flagged and the twin mostly drops: the Monday portal-support clerk (completeness capacity falls on the 2.2× arrival day), inspection no-shows, the scheduler as its own 20-minute pool, district travel in the baseline rather than only inside the D3 bundler, and the share of Simple renovations under EUR 50,000 with unknown violation history. D3 is specified as a new policy without stating how much informal district batching is already in the baseline, so the incremental gain is unidentified. D9 adds detection work with no staff time. Planner affinity is visible in the excerpt (the same planner reviews and drafts) and is unmodeled, which matters for D6. Planning rework does not say whether fire, environment, and notice must be repeated. Baseline queue discipline is “FIFO-with-priority” with no baseline priority rule. Shift overrun is unspecified even though planning, environment, and legal p90 service times exceed one shift.

The skeleton matches the requested PM4Py conventions: a pandas frame, `format_dataframe` with the three standard columns, `parse_process_tree` plus an explicit syntax limitation, variants, DFG, performance DFG, and case-arrival average. Elapsed gaps are correctly left unsplit. `Case` still has no `requires_fire`, `requires_environment`, or `requires_public_notice` fields, while routing and the objection flag depend on them. Applicant response and the statutory notice timer, which the prose treats as non-resource delays, have no engine objects. `has_prior_violation` defaulting to false would make D2 skip every low-value simple case.

Decision rules are appropriately non-optimizing and cover median, tail, SLA, district and architect fairness, utilization, rework, backlog, and stress rank-stability. Thresholds such as 10%, 15%, and 75–90% utilization are declared judgment calls. That discipline does not repair the structural miss on what runs in parallel, or the warm-up and tail procedures that feed those same metrics.
