The required seven-part structure is complete, and several process conclusions are sound: weekly volumes (2,300 to 3,300), the flat routine count, the +131% urgent and +120% complex shifts, medical-validation exposure rising from 23.0% to 41.4% (4,240/18,400 and 4,100/9,900), the 12.3% peak pre-analytical defect rate, normal direct release at 73.6% (11,700/15,890), and the warning not to strip technical validation at 101% utilization. Case types are not treated as one queue, strict urgent-first and blanket capacity are rejected, and redesign-caused risks are named.

The quantitative layer is not reliable enough to support the policies.

Denominators change without notice. Specialist review is given as 8.4% of technical-validation outputs; 610/8,080 outputs is 7.6%, while 8.4% is 610/7,240 entries. The normal 1.8% uses outputs. Peak direct release is stated as 55.9%, which is 4,050/7,240, after the normal figure correctly used outputs; on that same basis the peak rate is 50.1% (4,050/8,080). Section 2.3 then says 47.2% of peak cases need medical validation, which is 3,420/7,240, contradicting the 41.4% case share in Scenario A. The 4,100 medical arrivals and 610 specialist holds are also additive in the narrative even though 560 holds already sit inside the 4,100.

One observed peak snapshot is reused as the causal result of four different shocks. The 1,260 repeats, 14.8% rerun share, 780 recollections, 14 h accession queue, and 48 h specialist edge are historical peak facts. They are not measurements of an M1 outage, a 40–50% validator absence, or a courier collapse. Scenario B’s “6.2% to >15%” first-pass failure uses repeats divided by cases (1,140/18,400); repeats divided by assay completions are about 6.7%.

Several operating claims fight the capacity data. A 45-minute urgent assay cap cannot guarantee the 18-hour release SLA while register-to-receive is already 8 h in peak, technical validation queues 16 h, and medical validation queues 22 h; urgent p90 is 33 h. Metering routine intake at 150 cases/hour is several times pre-analytical throughput: about 463 checked cases/day at 96% utilization implies on the order of 20–40 cases/hour, so the throttle would flood the station it is meant to protect. “>12% into manual accession correction” is wrong; correction is 420/9,730, about 4.3%. The 12.3% figure is correction plus recollection. Comparing a 12-hour partner transport leg with a 36–48 h local specialist queue treats different parts of cycle time as substitutes. Cutting the pre-analytical queue from 9.5 h to under 2.5 h, and routine cycle time to under 50 h, is asserted at 96–98% utilization with no capacity model. “Complex singletons” misstates multi-assay panels.

Cross-training preparation techs and clerks into accession, from pools already at 89% and 85% with errors rising after 9-hour shifts, never identifies the station that move overloads. The trade-off row lists technical validation, the pool this variant deliberately avoids. A universal 2-hour visual triage is not reconciled with a 9.5-hour queue at 96% utilization. Policy B derates M2 while M1 is down, and routing urgent work to M2 is then the only remaining machine. Dashboard bands disagree with themselves and with Section 4: the summary red intake ratio is >1.5, the table uses >35%, and Policy D trips on 30% of daily volume inside two hours. Lagging targets (urgent breach under 0.1%, complex p90 under 80 h) are tighter than the normal period itself, where urgent p90 is already 19 h and complex p90 is 118 h.
