7.3

The write-up is complete, arithmetically careful on the embedded tables, and correctly treats March as a semantic/provenance break while keeping later rework, queueing, priority, and seasonality as separate mechanisms. It is not nearly flawless. Several timing and attribution claims are factually or causally off, and those errors sit on the question the task is about: which mechanism produced which part of the drift.

The baseline deltas and L1 distances check out. Versus the January–February means, the reported gaps for fitness, rework, fast-path share, cycle time, complexity, Supplier Z, and missing resources are right. Fragment L1 versus January is 3, 40, 59, 28, 64, and 36 for March, May, July, September, October, and December. March really is a label/provenance step: raw fitness 0.93 to 0.55, mapped fitness about 0.90, normalized edge movement totaling 3 per 100 cases, cycle time flat through April. The recovery share for March is about 93% of the loss versus the 0.925 baseline. Section 2 stays inside the word limit and correctly refuses to read that recovery as proof the business process was unchanged.

The timeline labeling is wrong in a way that changes the story. The row headed “2026-09 to 2026-10 (partial reversal)” uses August-to-September numbers: Supplier Z 38 to 24, rework 27 to 20, fast path 42 to 50, median 45.0 to 41.5, request-info back to 20 per 100. September to October moves the other way (rework 20 to 26, fast path 50 to 38, median 41.5 to 47.8). The staffing row has the same off-by-one habit: “2026-06 to 2026-08” is described as median 36.5 to 45.0 and p90 84 to 110, but 36.5 and 84 are May. June-to-August alone is 40.8 to 45.0. Hypothesis 8 then says July–August has the worst p90 at 104–110. November’s p90 is 121 and October’s is 116.

That boundary error feeds a real mis-dating of performance drift. Median cycle time first jumps in May, from 32.0 to 36.5 (+4.5 h), and p90 from 71 to 84 (+13 h), before the 15 June staffing cut. May already accounts for about 5.3 h of the 13.1 h July-versus-baseline median gap. Section 3.4 still names the performance change points as June, October, and December, and the July decomposition assigns roughly 50–60% (7–8 h) to queueing tied to that staffing cut. Flat service time and rising wait are also what rework-driven load produces with an unchanged roster. The 0.6 h service increase is observed; the 7–8 h queueing share is not, because the prompt only says reconstructed wait rose “much more.” The shares are labeled as an ordering, but the band is tighter than the aggregates can support. Rework counts are occurrences per 100 cases (about 29 in July, matching the rework-variant share), so a pure extra-loop effect hits a minority of cases and the median more weakly than the mean; the median’s 13 h rise is evidence of a broad slowdown, which the decomposition asserts without making that distinction.

Supplier co-movement is over-smoothed. Z’s case volume roughly triples in May (about 18% of 655 to about 43% of 680). That is its own population shift, not just worse bundles. A constant Z-versus-rest rework gap large enough to fit January and May under-predicts November: Supplier Z is back near 27% while rework variants are 29, the July peak, alongside high-complexity at 45%. “Rise and fall together, bracketed by disruption and recovery” fits May through September and fails October–November. Complexity is mentioned as a confounder; the November residual is the actual falsifier and is not used. December rework at 21 versus a ~12 baseline is also a weak adaptation signal while high-complexity is still 34%.

Smaller faults accumulate. “Robust z” is one January-to-February delta, floored at 5% of the baseline level, not a median/MAD robust z. On median cycle time that floor is about 1.56 h, so a month-to-month move must be about 4.7 h to flag: the rule would catch October and December and miss the May–July ramp the prose calls a change point. “Every L2 cell above ~150 in normal months” is false for the monitoring population they recommend; January–February high-complexity cells are about 130 and 121 cases, and weekly slices of that cell sit under their own support rule of 40. The priority rule reorders the queue; it does not itself “push cases to seniors.” Benjamini–Hochberg at q=0.1 across a deliberately correlated weekly battery overstates how much dependence the procedure handles.

The rest of the design is strong: separate monitors for labels, ordering, DFG/variants, rework, temporal profile, class-specific wait, service, seasonal case mix, resource missingness, mapped conformance, outcomes, and backlog; seasonal reference windows; persistence before alerting; and decision rules that route March-like gaps to mapping, supplier-local rework to the supplier, uniform wait with flat service to capacity, and in-band renewal mix to the seasonal plan. Part A is reproducible and its caveats about omitted edges are right. Part B uses the PM4Py entry points the task asks for and copies the log before relabeling. Provisional attribution is appropriately hedged on priority, senior-assessment recording, and December, and then too confident that October–November cycle time is “dominated” by season and that June–August queueing is the staffing signature.

The arithmetic and the monitoring architecture would carry a higher score. The mislabeled recovery window, the May performance onset absorbed into the June staffing story, and the unused November supplier–rework split are central, not cosmetic.
