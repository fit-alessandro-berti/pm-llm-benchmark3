The diagnosis is strongest where source semantics and lifecycle integrity diverge from raw timestamp equality: G1 is tied to fixture capacity and second-scale PLC recording, G4 is correctly separated from physical multi-item batching, G6 is correctly read as bulk logging that pushes sojourn into waiting and service to zero, and G7 is correctly treated as 15-minute censoring. Sections 6 and 7 are prioritized, concrete, and appropriately conservative. Those parts are not enough to carry the paper.

The service-time direction for G2 is reversed. With a shared 10:00 start and completions at 10:08, 10:14, 10:21, and 10:27, measured durations are 8, 14, 21, and 27 minutes. If one person works serially after “begin group,” the later completions absorb earlier work; the earliest completion is the least inflated. The text says the early cases are inflated and include others’ later work. That correction would adjust the wrong cases. The same section gets the analogous G3 direction right (28 down to 6 minutes), which makes the G2 reversal a calculation error rather than a definitional ambiguity.

G2’s second label, “sequential batching,” also conflicts with the signature matrix. The matrix requires sequential batching to have unequal starts, adjacency, and no overlap. G2 has one shared start, no start-to-prior-end adjacency, and overlap on every pair. Serial work after a group button is a latent physical hypothesis for a start-batched log, which section 5 states, but the diagnosis table promotes it to the observed batch class.

G5 is under-quantified. All six interval pairs overlap, and from 13:12 to 13:20 all four cases are open, while `analyst_pool` has at most three analysts. Pairwise “partial overlap” does not state that peak occupancy. That fact rules out one case per analyst and is the strongest reason multitasking, bad timestamps, or identity collapse must be in the explanation. The code never computes peak concurrency, only pair rate and sum-of-service over wall span.

The PM4Py block is not a reliable comparison. `discover_batches` takes a parameter dictionary (`merge_distance`, `min_batch_size`); the sweep passes those names as keywords and then falls back to positional integers. Both paths should fail closed, after which every cluster is reported as unmatched. The parser is also speculative about batch-object shape. The prose then assigns categories the failed call did not produce, and two of those assignments are the ones the taxonomy most easily gets wrong: G6 has equal starts and equal ends, so the usual type order labels it simultaneous before end-batching; classic discovery has no concurrent type, and at a 60-second merge distance G5’s staggered starts and ends need not form a batch at all. G6 versus G1 versus G7 is exactly the case where an uncorrected PM4Py label is misleading, and the write-up still predicts “end batch” for G6.

Smaller consistency problems add up. Shrinking a merge tolerance does not split timestamps that are already equal; it only stops near-equals from merging. G7’s `recorded_at` values step 14:16 through 14:20 in `sequence_no`, which is direct evidence of serial insertion behind identical quarter-hour bounds and is barely used. G3’s own counterevidence says the staggered starts may be real, which fits serial work plus a folder submit better than “concurrent work,” but concurrent is still second. G8 is given a batch label that G4’s own caveat correctly withholds from an equally strict one-piece handoff chain. The granularity indicator is computed and then omitted from the printed table. Throughput and conformance bias are mostly left at the G6 clump.

Assumptions A1–A2, the G6 mechanism, the censoring treatment, and the collection redesign are sound. The reversed G2 bias, the G5 capacity gap, and a batch-discovery section that would not actually run or discipline the labels keep this below a strong score.
