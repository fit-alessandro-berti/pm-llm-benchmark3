**5.0**

The write-up covers all nine required sections and states two real objectives, three operational policies, and a P1-safe pilot. The decision-relevant arithmetic and the claimed comparison do not hold up.

Capacity in §2 misreads the skill and shift table. Farah (Electrical 1.40, on until 12:00) is left out of 08:00–12:00 electrical capacity, so base 08:00–10:00 electrical is shown as ~209 minutes and ~61% instead of about 295 minutes and ~43%. The same omission produces the U1 “~103%” morning electrical bottleneck; with Farah included that block is nearer 70% under the in-block load assumption. Emil (Electrical 1.30, 12:00–20:00, break 16:00–16:30) is omitted from electrical capacity in 12:00–16:00 and from the entire 18:00–20:00 row, which is reported as zero capacity. He is included for software in those afternoon blocks, and his break is applied to 16:00–18:00 software (~90) but not electrical (~212). Intake at 10:00–12:00 drops Farah while 14:00–16:00 intake treats Emil’s 1.10 multiplier as 1.00. Validation at 12:00–14:00 is 450 against an addition of the same rows that is about 490. Those errors change who is scarce: 18:00–20:00 still has Intake, Electrical, Software, and Validation from Emil; mechanical work is the skill with no one left. Calling every P1 arrival after 18:00 structurally infeasible (§2, §9) overstates the evidence.

Skill columns are also treated as simultaneously available. The same person-minute is counted in every skill that person can do, so the table is an upper bound on each skill, not a joint plan. That contention is material for Emil at 16:00–18:00, where software load alone is about his whole post-break shift.

U2’s “100–110%” electrical result does not follow the stated rule. Doubling E and ES shares and renormalizing, with block arrivals held fixed, leaves 12:00–16:00 electrical well under the corrected capacity. The code uses that renormalization; the prose reports a saturated system. U5 deletes arrivals during 09:30–10:00 instead of holding them with Intake stopped.

§4’s means, p90s, SLA rates, and “±” replication errors are presented as five-seed simulator output. They are not printed from the code, and the code cannot produce that policy ranking. Cases are booked in arrival order onto future `free_at` slots, so later work cannot use gaps before an earlier case’s downstream activity. Shifts and breaks do not move start times. `p1_reserved` is never consumed, and the 20-minute overflow threshold never appears, so Hybrid resource choice collapses to the strict specialty list. Parallel branches still add handover and cross-team delay between each other. The IT outage, preemption, restart penalty, overtime, and same-resource setup are absent; only some of those are named as omissions. The recommendation then says Hybrid meets the §1 fairness constraint, while the same table shows `util_std` of 0.16–0.21 against a stated cap of 0.15.

The parallel-versus-sequential point is the right tension, but the P1 budget is never checked. A sequential ES path plus handovers already sits near 120 minutes with no queue; that calculation is what would justify Hybrid’s parallel rule.

§7–§8 name the right log fields except an explicit queue-entry time. The code calls `format_dataframe`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network`, not `get_service_time`. It then groups `case:waiting_time` and `case:service_time` by activity and labels the result as activity waiting and service. Those fields are case totals repeated on each event, which is the case-versus-stage mix-up the task said to avoid.

§6 and the pilot are usable. The grade stays at 5 because the bottleneck map, the scenario table, and the robust ranking rest on capacity mistakes and on metrics the supplied program does not compute.
