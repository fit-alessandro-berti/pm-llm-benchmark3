6.2

The case classifications are mostly non-mechanical and directionally sound, but the answer is not close to flawless. Timing semantics, the sufficiency test, several factual claims, and the required code all have material defects.

Section 1 identifies five relevant threats, yet it never consistently states prevent, detect, correct, or fail for each of AV-2, MG-4, RA-7, and ED-1. ED-1 is missing from collusion. AV-2’s explicit inability to show who controlled a compromised account is blurred into a generic fraud discussion. “MG-4 prevents unilateral release if performed before settlement” is wrong: release has already occurred; the live question is whether settlement can still be stopped.

Section 2 lists the requested dimensions and gives a valid tail-risk reason that equal expected loss is not enough. It does not define a test. Nothing states when a package is fully equivalent, partially equivalent, merely mitigating, or ineffective. Dimension 1 also says settlement ends correction by recall, while dimension 5 correctly preserves a 24-hour recall window.

Section 3’s labels are the strongest part: AP-202 and AP-208 failed, AP-205 was stopped before completion, AP-203 and AP-207 can meet the objective despite one operator, AP-204 can fail despite two user IDs, and AP-206 and AP-209 are not mechanical passes or fails. The cells undermine that reasoning. Pre-settlement manager action is marked detective for AP-201 and AP-207, contradicting section 4. AP-201’s corrective capacity is called low even though m04 acted before the 10:00 settlement, and “none critical” ignores unknown review depth. AP-203 and AP-207 say defects were “caught” when none was found. AP-206 lists the late token as a preventive control and puts token timing in the corrective-capacity cell; m11 also approved before AV-2 completed, which is unmentioned. AP-208’s audit is “5 days” late against a four-day fact. AP-209’s corrective capacity is low despite an audit still inside a plausible recall window. AP-204 as OBJECTIVE_FAILED is defensible on the fabricated receipt, but high confidence plus a collusion narrative conflicts with the denial and missing organization design.

Section 4’s hybrid reading of manager action after release and before settlement is right. The AP-208 illustration is not: there was no manager action in that interval, and settlement was about an hour after release, not immediate.

Section 5 asserts stolen credentials and a closed-cell collusion that the facts leave unresolved. For AP-204 it does not say which control breaks the chain; the bad payment was not stopped, and the “independent releaser” counterfactual turns into rotation and sampling recommendations. AP-206’s residual risk is overstated as negligible. AP-208 again describes barrier collapse rather than a break in the chain.

Section 6 covers every requested monitoring topic and correctly separates alerts from proof. The rules are still blunt: any same-user payment above EUR 10,000 is queued for review with no compliant ED-1 exception, and shared-workstation detection “within minutes” misses a 30-day pairing pattern.

Section 7 builds the log in memory and labels the printout as evidence, but it does not do the required job. Standard `filter_four_eyes_principle` keeps cases in which the two activities have different resources; the code treats that return value as the violation set and states this with no version caveat, despite being told to explain unguaranteed PM4Py behavior. The facts table is only amount, bank change, and a raw emergency bit. It does not flag emergency eligibility, AV coverage, manager timing, audit timing, or repeated pairs, even though those timestamps are pivoted and then unused. `utc=True` is omitted, and the `==--` headers are broken. As written, the helper can invert nominal four-eyes evidence and still miss the judgment flags.
