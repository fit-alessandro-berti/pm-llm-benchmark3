7.2

The answer is structurally complete and often quantitatively careful, but it is not nearly flawless: several identification claims are directionally wrong, a few reported figures do not match the panel, and the claim ladder disagrees with the answer’s own confidence ratings.

The main pre/post means and pooled Delta–Epsilon difference-in-differences check out (Alpha/Beta/Gamma median DiDs about −5.6, −3.4, and −3.8 hours; escalation DiDs about +1.1, +0.7, and +0.9 pp; the month-by-month Alpha median path −3.6, −6.6, −4.0, −5.8, −8.1). Leave-one-control-out median bounds of roughly −2.6 to −6.4 hours are also real. Availability versus use, staggered selection, SUTVA through the fraud desk, treatment-version heterogeneity, and the refusal of a single “reduces cycle time by X%” statement are handled at a high level. The primary design correctly avoids already-treated controls and static two-way fixed effects. The code meets the requested descriptive specification, including clustered standard errors, small-cluster warnings, and explicit non-causal comments.

The serious problems are interpretive. In §5.4 the adopter–control median gap narrows sharply (Alpha’s gap versus the control mean falls from about 12 hours in January to about 3 hours in August). The text says that gap widens and that this is the opposite of regression to the mean. Units selected for long cycle times regress by closing that gap, and there is already an April effect, so the anti-RTM argument is overstated as well as factually reversed. In §4 and §5.3, growth in the event-time DiD is blamed on July–August easing “seen in all branches.” Those DiDs already subtract contemporaneous control changes: from April to August Alpha’s median fell about 5 hours while the control mean fell about 0.5 hours. Common seasonality does not explain that divergence. “Tracks intensity” is also too strong. Intensity rises steadily; the DiDs do not (Beta’s largest median DiD is June, then it shrinks).

Smaller numerical and wording errors accumulate. Delta +6.0 h and Epsilon +4.4 h versus January–March are April–August changes, not April–June. The Beta dose slope of −0.07 h per percentage point does not follow from that branch’s window DiD divided by excess fast-path share (closer to −0.10), and §5.3’s −0.18 for Alpha conflicts with §7’s −0.16. “All branches” did not gain 5–8 complexity points; Beta’s net move is about +3. “Adopter p90 rose ~10 h” omits Gamma’s absolute decline. E3 points at “E3’s median analogue.” Event time “4–5” does not exist if April is time 0. Unrecorded fraud-desk waits are later treated as excluded from cycle time; the data only say they are not separately recorded, so the hidden-tail story may have the wrong measurement direction.

§7 and §10 are internally inconsistent. Median evidence is rated stronger (confidence 60, central −4% to −9%) but labeled only “Suggestive,” while the weaker p90/rework findings (confidence 35 and 30) are labeled “Conditionally causal,” with a 5–8 hour tail range that drops the −4.8 hour leave-one-out bound. The 0–13% median interval is a judgment layered on a calculated range of about −6% to −13%, and it does not clearly carry the fraud-desk spillover that the answer elsewhere says flatters automation. Escalation confidence 75 is high for five smoothed clusters, and the leadership sentence merges specialist review with the fraud desk.

The rollout choice is the right one, and case-level randomization plus a staggered second branch would improve identification. The decision rule then ignores that design: scale-up hinges on an unpowered one-branch median DiD of −3%, within-branch queue interference is unmentioned, and routing, staffing, and local practice are collected rather than held fixed. “No sign of customer harm” is stronger than the unidentified CSAT result. The “11+ pre-months” parenthesis attaches that count to January–August history, which is eight months.
