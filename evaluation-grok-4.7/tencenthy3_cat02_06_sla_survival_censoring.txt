6.4

The write-up is well structured and meets the required nine-part format. Estimands are defined before estimators, mapped to decisions, and illustrated with S03, S09, S06, and S08. Competing risks, transfer scenarios, leakage from `Escalate`/`Rework`, and non-randomized cohort assignment are treated seriously, and the code keeps open cases and does not invent event times.

The evidence handling is not reliable enough for a high score. Cohort B has two transfers (S21, S32), not three; that false count is repeated in §§3 and 6 and drives the claim that B’s SLA conclusion could reverse. “3 of the 4 observed breaches beyond 72” does not match the table: nine cases have time >72, split 4/5, and the four open cases past 72 are split 2/2. The priority-mix remark (“easier P2/P3”) also runs against the data: B has more P1 cases (5 vs 3).

The risk-set discussion is conceptually wrong. An open case at 104h (S26) belongs in the resolution risk set at 72h; censoring it at 104 does not hide that it was unresolved at 72. What would understate breach risk is dropping it or coding competing terminals as ordinary censoring. The state diagram also omits observed transitions `Screen→AutoCheck` (S01, S15, S17) and `Rework→Resolve` (S27), and not every transfer passed through `Review`.

The code does not carry the plan. `competing` and `worst` are encoded identically, Estimand 2 is never summarized, `at_risk_72` drops late resolutions that were still at risk at 72, and `fillna(False).mean()` treats pre-horizon censoring as non-breach—the bias the prose warns against. Control-flow features are mostly pandas string checks rather than results of the PM4Py call.
