The answer covers all seven required sections and is operationally strong, but repeated evidence errors and an unresolved policy conflict keep it well short of nearly flawless.

**Factual problems**
- Hold volume is described as doubling “in a period half as long.” The windows are 8 weeks and 3 weeks. Counts move from 290 to 610 (about 2.1×). The weekly rate moves from about 36 to about 203 (about 5.6×). Both “double” and “half as long” misstate the comparison.
- Cancellations after recollection are said to rise “on half the volume.” Peak volume is 9,900 cases against 18,400, and weekly throughput is higher in the peak window. The rise is real; the volume base used to dramatize it is wrong.
- Several DFG comparisons use raw counts across unequal windows (batch-wait 760 vs 1,180; holds 290 vs 610). Direction is often right, but the rate change is understated. That sits poorly beside otherwise careful rates: repeat-assay exits 6.7% (1,140/17,030) and 14.8% (1,260/8,500), recollection 4.2% and 8.0%, technical-validation routing about 25% and 42%.
- S2 places a “30–50%” assay-capacity loss in the evidence column. Relative M1/M2 throughput is not in the data. M2 is faster and less reliable, so the goodput effect of losing it is unknown.
- Release is called a station with no queue problem and the safest downstream. The queue table has no release pool. The only support is the short Medical validation → Release edge (2h / 5h), which is not cited for that claim.

**Design logic**
- The observed peak already exceeds essentially every ON trigger: Urgent+Complex share 49% > 40%, assay median queue 18h > 12h, repeat-assay rate 14.8% > 10%, medical queue 22h > 12h, correction queue 14h > 8h, recollection about 8% > 6%. P1, P2, P3, and P5 would all stay ON for the whole reference peak. The switches do not separate a winter wave from worse states inside it.
- Those simultaneous policies conflict. R3/P1 move Routine toward larger batches with a wait cap and keep Complex-panel flowing. P2, also ON, stops large Routine batches. P3, also ON, stops upstream expediting of Complex-panel work that cannot be validated. An operations team cannot run all three.
- A 50–60% cap on priority pre-emptions is loose against a 29% Urgent arrival share and can still starve Routine and Complex-panel work. P1 then raises that cap in the mix-shift the cap was meant to control. Complex-panel orders also consume multiple assays, so a cap on starts does not bound assay time.
- P2’s small-batch rule, in an assay-capacity crisis, increases setups. The source note says larger batches improve setup efficiency. That self-inflicted capacity loss is not analyzed.
- S1 names Pre-analytical check and Preparation as the first bottleneck. In the peak snapshot, Technical validation is at 101%, assay median queue is 18h, medical queue is 22h, and specialist queue is 36h with a 48h edge. The upstream-to-downstream story is plausible. It is not the severity order in the metrics.

**Smaller issues**
- Repeat-after-failure (a case type, 8–9% of mix, two assay consumptions) is blurred with the Repeat assay edge (6.7% then 14.8% of assay exits).
- P5’s “recollection rate” is not tied to the specific edge used in S5, so the 6% and 8% bands become ambiguous once correction-originated recollections (70 and 110) are included.
- P4’s second intake point can overload Pre-analytical check, already at 96% with a 9.5h peak queue. That downstream effect is thin.
- The 6h batch cap, the 4h P1 cap, and the 24–72h lead-time claim are unanchored. Queue time is already part of cycle time, so queue age is only partly leading.
- An opening paragraph sits outside the seven required sections.

What holds the score in the 7s is the scenario design, real use of migration and the conflict hints, rejection of strict I1, rigid I3, and I9, conditional treatment of I2, I4, I8, and I10 with downstream risks, redesign-caused vulnerabilities (triage and urgency-label gaming), a complete trade-off table, and correct use of tails (Urgent median 17h vs p90 33h against an 18h penalty). The structure is strong. The evidence arithmetic and the policy package are not clean enough for a higher score.
