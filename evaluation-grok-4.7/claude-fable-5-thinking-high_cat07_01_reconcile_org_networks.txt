7.4

The write-up meets the required structure and is stronger than a typical organizational reading: the three SNA metrics are defined correctly, centrality is not treated as inherently good or bad, bots and vendors stay in view, removal is refused without a replacement path, and the risk section stays on the right side of “opportunity” versus “wrongdoing.” The composite in §2 is also properly non-exclusive. It is not nearly flawless. Several validity errors sit on the exact question the analysis is supposed to settle.

The serious flaw is an internal contradiction about whether Mira stays on a case. §1 treats the subcontracting counts (126 medical, 98 legal) as delegate-and-resume and as evidence that she “retains functional ownership” and decides the next step. H5 then says 960 events over 905 cases means she is “almost never twice in the same case.” Those claims cannot both be true. About 55 surplus events cannot support 224 specialist return-loops, and 224 loops is not “almost never.” The same accounting problem hits the handover table: listed inflows to Mira sum to about 1580 and listed outflows to about 1640, against 960 events. Under a directly-follows handover definition, which §1 correctly states, those edge weights cannot add up. The data-quality section flags vocabulary and possible tool-logging, then still treats the edge weights and the resume story as solid. That underweights H5 and overstates how cleanly subcontracting proves ownership.

The working-together follow-up test has the sign backward. Short routing events already count as full case co-membership, so deleting them cannot raise her weighted degree. Conditioning on `exception_flag` can change the rate; filtering out her touches cannot “confirm” that peripherality was dilution. “Sub-minute” is also unsupported: her median service time is 4.2 minutes.

The absence result is narrowed without warrant. The source result is that median cycle time for ordinary low-value claims did not move. §1, H2, §3, and the executive summary all generalize that to ordinary claims. Separately, the summary says nearly every difficult claim passes through her. The given figures are 64% of reopens and 71% of complaints, and her own exception share does not imply coverage of nearly all exception cases. “She is a broker” also settles a role the body correctly leaves composite.

Smaller misses accumulate. Adjuster co-case rates are 6–8%; 4–5% are the team-lead pairs, so “4–8% with each adjuster” is loose. “Essentially every downstream human role” overshoots a hub that, in the top edges, does not hand to the team leads, the temps, or the vendors. Junior versus senior “rework” equates similar handover counts with unfair load and flattens the table’s own reassignment-versus-rework note. “By definition” is too strong for brokerage: low embeddedness is typical of this liaison pattern, not definitional. A few H1/H4 predictions (a written policy exists; field audit of writer IDs) are not future-log predictions. `lifecycle:transition` is never tied to whether the published service times are start-to-complete durations, which is the validity condition for the PM4Py service-time tests proposed in §4.

§5 is the most usable part: backup coverage, explicit routing criteria, sampled review, and logging cleanup are separated from what should wait. The +65% arithmetic and the n=1 caveat on the absence window are handled carefully. Those strengths keep the answer well above a merely competent one; the unresolved event-count contradictions and the reversed co-occurrence test keep it out of the high band.
