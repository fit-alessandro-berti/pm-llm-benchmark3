4.0

The write-up keeps the right overall shape and avoids the worst shortcut (coding rejected, withdrawn, or open cases as non-default). It still misses the design the problem is built around: activation selects who can have a default label, label time is not the same thing as the 90-day horizon, and an as-of cut has to be enforced in both the study design and the code. Several sections contradict each other, and the implementation skeleton does not run.

## 1. Target definition
The primary estimand is in the right family: 90-day default given activation, with rejected files left out of the risk set. The rest of the definition does not hold together. The stated population is only matured activated accounts, while later sections treat immature activated accounts as censored and still in scope. Those are different estimands. Complete-case restriction estimates default only among accounts whose outcome feed has already arrived, which over-weights older, pre-policy cases.

The prediction point is the final Credit Review outcome, before Contract Signed. Activation is still in the future at that time, so conditioning on Activated conditions on a post-prediction event. Accepted cases that never sign, never activate, or are withdrawn after approval are silently dropped. That selection is not named.

The alternative target is not credible, and the two glosses contradict each other. Coding rejects as non-default is not the same claim as “they would have defaulted at the approved rate.” Neither is a well-defined multi-state outcome, a censored time-to-default, or an explicitly assumptive policy counterfactual. The business question (an early warning from the first events of every application) and this late, activation-conditioned target are never reconciled.

## 2. Cohort construction
The counts sum to 48,000, and rejected, withdrawn, transferred, open, and immature cases are at least separated from the 21,600 labeled rows. The maturity rule is wrong. The 3,600 are described as activated accounts whose 90-day window has not elapsed by 15 February 2026. The problem states that this bucket also includes accounts whose horizon has elapsed but whose feed has not arrived (typically 7–35 days later). That error carries into the censoring section.

Open applications are marked as censored default outcomes even though their default clock has not started. Permanently missing feeds on partner-originated activated accounts never appear in the flow, even though those accounts are inside the primary population. Linkable versus unlinked transfers are collapsed to one “partially missing” row. Inclusion and exclusion rules do not define an as-of risk set; they restate the extraction window.

## 3. Censoring and missing labels
The five mechanisms are not kept distinct. Open cases and immature activated accounts are both called ordinary right censoring, with the claim that both “survived” past the prediction point but not through a 90-day default horizon. Open, rejected, and withdrawn cases were never exposed. Their default outcome is undefined under the observed policy, not censored survival.

Delayed labels are not given a strategy. A late feed can still report a default that already occurred inside 90 days; that is ascertainment lag, not a censored event time. Permanently missing partner feeds are not separated from administrative immaturity. Transfers are simultaneously a competing event, an inheritable label, and a censored outcome, with no primary rule. Inheriting a partner label also changes product and policy; linkage does not fix that.

IPW “if generalizing to all applicants” answers a different estimand from the one selected as primary, and it cannot create a default outcome for people who were never activated. For the conditional estimand, the live problems are administrative censoring, delayed feeds, and non-ignorable permanent missingness among activated accounts. None of those receives an estimator. “Survival or classification” is not a choice: complete-case classification drops the immature tail and lines up with the policy-change and migration eras.

The sensitivity analysis perturbs open and transferred cases, which are outside the primary risk set, so it does not stress the estimand being estimated. Linkage rate is treated as a test of MAR. The rate is observed; whether unlinked transfers have the same outcome distribution is not. Covariate balance between linked and unlinked cases is only a partial test. Outcome MAR for unlinked transfers and for permanently missing partner feeds is untestable here. That last point is the one that should have been stated.

## 4. Prefix extraction
Fixed-length prefixes and elapsed-time prefixes are named, then defined in a way that does not match the prediction point. Taking the first \(k\) events after a time filter is an early landmark. Cutting at the final Credit Review is a late landmark. Document loops sit between them, so these are not two views of one prefix.

Credit Review repeats. The skeleton never says whether the cut is the first review, the accepting review, or the last review before Contract Signed. Padding short cases with a synthetic “Case Terminated” activity invents an end-of-case signal that was not observed at prediction time. Collapsing same-activity events inside one hour will also erase genuine document rework, which is later used as a feature. Renaming across the 15 October 2025 migration is acknowledged; duplicate-event identity versus real loops is not.

## 5. Process-aware features
Static channel, region, and amount, a policy-era flag, elapsed time, event count, and a document-loop flag are legitimate prefix-time ideas, and extraction-time tenure is correctly rejected. “Number of unique variants seen” is not a prefix feature; a prefix is one variant. Queue state is missing: waiting time and queue load need an as-of history of other cases, with future cases excluded. Resource identifiers are left as raw IDs. There is no as-of rule for a risk score that is revised on later events, and no migration-era feature beside the policy flag.

## 6. Leakage audit
The table covers the required fields and correctly drops `collection_status` and future contract, activation, and collections events. Several decisions are unresolved. `final_limit` and `decision_reason` are “repair” or “drop/repair,” while section 1 has already placed the call after the final credit decision, where the offered limit may be known and changes the question to default given the limit set. `documents_total` is both “count only events before the timestamp” and “drop.” `default_90d` is “keep” without stating that it is a label, not a feature, and that it may be used only when `label_available_at` is on or before the training as-of date. A label that arrives during the horizon is look-ahead in a backtest even if the column is not used as an input. Resource experience is “drop/repair” rather than one rule: historical tenure at event time, otherwise exclude.

## 7. Validation design
The split is temporal and ordered around the policy change and the migration, which is the right instinct. It is not a valid backtest of this label. Cases that start from 15 October 2025 through 31 December 2025, and many that start in the validation window, cannot have a matured 90-day outcome plus a 7–35 day feed by 15 February 2026. The test partition is exactly the immature region, so discrimination and calibration on that slice mix missing labels with migration shift. A usable scheme would freeze features at a past as-of date, require `label_available_at` before that date, and leave a gap of at least 90 days plus the reporting delay after activation. One pre-policy train / post-policy validate / post-migration test cut also cannot separate those three shifts, and there is no refit, rolling origin, or “labels as known on that date” rule.

A random case split is misleading because it leaks later policy and activity names into training and scores cases whose labels would not yet exist. The stated reason (overfit to pre-policy data) is the failure mode of the proposed temporal train set, not of a random split. Subgroup performance is absent (channel, region, amount, partner-originated, prefix length). “Brier score requires recalibration for censored data” mixes two operations. Under censoring, a proper binary score needs something like inverse-probability-of-censoring weights; recalibration is a separate post-hoc map. C-index versus AUC is left open because survival versus binary classification was left open. Fast activations are the ones that mature inside a late window, so metrics on the accidentally labeled subset are themselves selected.

## 8. Limitations and decision-use boundary
The useful boundary is present: scores are associations for accounts that activate, not default risk for files the lender declines. The deployment paragraph then breaks. Tightening approval is described as making the training cohort riskier and the served cohort riskier. Tightening changes who activates; the served activated book is the selected subset, and future labels are generated only for that subset. That feedback loop is the deployment effect that matters, and the direction is wrong as written.

The other two shifts (linkage falling, another policy change) are real data risks, but they are not clearly consequences of deploying this score. Missing effects include underwriters changing document loops and overrides when shown a score, collection treatment changing the 90-day outcome the model is scored on, and a pre-decision use of a post-decision model. No uncertainty method is specified, so “with uncertainty quantification” is not earned.

## 9. Implementation skeleton
The block is short of a concrete pipeline and would not execute.

- `pm4py.format_dataframe` is called with `activity=` and `time=`. The real arguments are `activity_key` and `timestamp_key`.
- `pm4py.get_prefixes_from_log` is never called. Prefixing is a per-case filter.
- Cohort status reads `status` and `label_available_at` from a frame that only has start and end timestamps. Those columns are not in the event schema; activation, rejection, withdrawal, transfer, and “still open” have to be derived from activities as of the cutoff.
- The cut passed into the prefix is `end_time`, the last event on the case. That includes Contract Signed, Account Activated, collections, and Default Recorded, which is the leakage the audit is supposed to block.
- `audit_leakage(df, "end_time")` looks up `end_time` on the event table, where it does not exist.
- `print(casesorts)` is an undefined name.
- Withdrawn, transferred, and open cases fall through to one “Censored” bucket.
- `TimeSeriesSplit` is imported and unused. It is not the temporal case-start design.
- `discover_dfg` appears only as a comment, on a full log that still contains post-outcome events and both policy regimes.
- Nothing marks where a censored likelihood, competing-risk model, or IPCW evaluation would attach. Nothing drops `default_90d`, `collection_status`, `final_limit`, or `decision_reason` from the feature table. `numpy` is unused. Only one timestamp column is parsed.

## Bottom line
The answer recognizes selective labels and writes a temporal split and a leakage table. Those points are outweighed by treating non-activated cases as censored default times, ignoring feed delay, placing the test window inside the immature period, leaving the estimand and the prediction-time cut inconsistent, and shipping a skeleton that both crashes and leaks post-outcome events. That is a failed design with some recoverable pieces, not a near-miss.
