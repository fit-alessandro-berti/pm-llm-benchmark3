The diagnosis is the strongest part: it uses the frequency DFG (Auto → Fast approval 2550; Fast approval → Dispatch 2300; Request missing evidence → Manual 890; 320 abandonments), performance times, fitness 0.91 versus 68% fitting traces, alignment deviations, the 14.1% versus 3.3% reopen split, the 31% to 9% cost collapse, segment outcomes, and the 82% manual-utilization cliff. The contradiction is stated in segment terms rather than as a single average. That is real process-mining use, not a generic Lean list.

The score is held down by mechanism and experiment errors that misread the same evidence.

Redesign A still allows Fast approval after review, then claims that taking high-value volume off Fast approval directly cuts the 14.1% rate. That rate is for all Fast-approval cases. The long complaint tail is tied to wrong part selection on Dispatch → complaint (mean 96 h, p90 180 h), so a post-review replacement can keep the failure mode. The pre-dispatch gate is the relevant control, and it is only loosely attached. A also folds in a portal and a preferred repair path, so it is partly B and C rather than a clean alternative.

Redesign B says parallelism collapses the 34 h Request → Manual edge. That edge is mostly customer evidence wait (median 26 h, p90 88 h); screening in parallel does not remove it. The flow then routes high-value cases to Manual or Fraud, while the sacrifice section says high-value Fast volume falls only indirectly and R1 exposure remains. Those cannot both be true.

Redesign C applies the Premium low-risk 24-hour service-credit rule to every Premium claim. Premium high-value reviewed/repair median is 96 h; the prompt never states a 24-hour credit there. It also treats the Repair quote → Customer approval mean of 30 h as technician overload. The prompt says that delay is mostly part-price confirmation, which extra repair volume does not fix and which the redesign never changes. Two inspectors being “underused” does not show they can absorb a shift of the 35% high-value share onto repair. The 3.3% versus 14.1% and 5–6% versus 18–22% gaps are observational; defect mix and selection into repair are not treated as confounders.

The comparison table’s quality column uses ↑ for improvement while cycle time uses ↑ for worse, so the signs are not readable. Marking A “not dominated” also sits badly next to the correct caveat that dominance cannot be calculated.

The pilot is the clearest logic failure. A’s claimed benefit is high-value diversion, R1, and the part-selection gate. Stage 1 pilots low-risk accessories, where fast-path complaints are already 6–8% and the fast path is what A mostly keeps. Go criteria then allow sustained manual utilization of 85–88%, above the stated 82% queue cliff. Control cohorts and 30–60-day reopen, reshipment, and credit tracking are the right way to detect downstream shift, but they are attached to a pilot that does not test the risky trade-off. Mid-risk devices and the R3 gap (120 reopen cases go to Escalation rather than Manual) stay unspecified in all three designs.
