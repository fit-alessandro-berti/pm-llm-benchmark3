The diagnosis is the strongest part of the answer. It correctly rejects an average-speed reading, uses the segment split to show that the fast path is reasonable for low-risk accessories and poor for high-value devices, and anchors that claim in the 31%-versus-9% cost gap, the 14.1%-versus-3.3% reopen gap, fitness 0.91 with 68% fitting traces, the named alignment deviations, the 34h/p90 88h evidence return, and the 96h/p90 180h dispatch-to-complaint tail. The three redesigns are genuinely different mechanisms, each has an explicit sacrifice, and the experiment plan is unusually careful about cohorts, holdouts, post-close reopens, and rework-adjusted cost.

The lead redesign is not compliant with the rule it cites. R2 exempts only Premium low-risk accessories from evidence completeness before dispatch. Redesign A puts both accessory segments, 43% of volume, on an untouched fast dispatch lane and calls that lane explicitly compliant. The table then says routing enforces R1 and R2. R1 can be enforced this way for claims above EUR 400; R2 is not enforced for Standard accessories, and routing high-value cases to review does not by itself confirm evidence completeness before dispatch.

The quality mechanism is also over-assigned. The 22%-to-5% Premium high-value gap belongs to the fast path versus the reviewed/repair path. Redesign A only stops the auto-screen from issuing Fast approval. Manual review and fraud review can still fast-approve and dispatch, which is an existing edge (240 fraud-review cases already do this). Accepting 96–120h medians later in the section implies a shift onto repair, but the flow never closes the post-review fast-approval door. That same shift ignores the 12.5%-versus-4.0% repair-rework gap on high-value devices, so a move toward repair can displace one quality cost with another.

Several smaller evidence errors accumulate. Part-price waiting is a cause of delay at repair quote, but the answer attaches it to Repair quote → Customer approval. The 260 dispatch-to-complaint cases and the whole 31%-to-9% collapse are treated as wrong-part events; the log only links that long tail to wrong parts, and 140 complaints leave Fast approval directly. The 15–35% manual-inflow range is the estimate for a rules update, while disabling auto fast-approval for the high-value slice could move substantially more volume. “Mid-risk” is given an amber lane even though the five reported segments already sum to 100% and the residual 22% is the unclear-evidence segment. Fast approval → close (350) is labeled an anomaly without evidence that those cases required fulfillment.

The Pareto column mishandles its own term. Current is marked dominated, conditional on the redesigns working, while the prose correctly says exact dominance cannot be established. Redesign C is called dominated by A “on compliance” and complementary in the same cell. Weaker on one dimension is not dominance, and complementary options are not dominated.

Failure modes and the pilot sequence are specific and mostly well grounded. Overlapping the high-value routing pilot with the high-value dispatch gate still risks confounding the shared complaint outcome, although the within-gate holdout partially addresses it.
