The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision, covers every required section, and correctly refuses both national rollout and a full rollback. That strategic core is sound, but the answer is not nearly flawless.

The headline claim that low-risk median duration fell 42% is unsupported. Overall median fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days (about 24%). The simple-house change is −6.0 days, and no segment baseline is given, so 42% uses the wrong denominator. In the same parenthesis, the aggregate handover and visible-cost changes are attributed to low-risk cases, which the evidence does not show.

The operating recommendation contradicts itself. The memo and the immediate action keep post-billing activation for simple houses, while “what to pause” stops it for every segment until conformance monitoring. Those are different decisions.

The rollout thresholds required in the final section are unreadable: the comparison operators are missing, so “p95 … Baseline p95” does not state a gate. The 95% rule-accuracy and 85% utilization cutoffs are also unexplained.

Fact, interpretation, and assumption are split in the table, but the split is imperfect. The memo treats skipping and sequencing as having caused the tail, despite region mix, a non-random pilot, and building-risk attributes missing before week 3. One assumption treats the incomplete EUR 207 cost estimate as accurate, while the value-of-information section treats that same cost as decision-changing.

The five metric conflicts are explained in the right direction. Fitness versus precision is mostly right, but low precision is equated too quickly with an unstable process, and the drop in generalization (0.70 to 0.56) is ignored. Composition bias is not separated from within-segment harm.

Several process-mining results that bear on the decision are thin or absent: repeated installation without a survey, the new billing-to-complaint tail, failed-visit delay where coordinator load is high, survey utilization falling while second-visit utilization rises to 92%, and the simpler handover network versus the more centralized working-together network.

M6 is ranked above M1 for the wrong reason. Rule accuracy does not establish whether skipping surveys is safe; the randomized skip test is what would change that policy. Measurements that could reverse the immediate simple-house scale decision are also ranked as if scaling first were already settled, including full cost, conformance, and coordinator absence.

Option E remains a reasonable choice, and alternatives A–E are evaluated with real risks. The execution errors, causal overclaim, and incomplete use of the mining evidence keep it well short of excellent.
