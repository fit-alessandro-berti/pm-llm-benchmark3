4.5

The write-up follows the requested section order and names the main calendar events, but it is not a reliable drift diagnosis. Several central quantitative claims are wrong or incoherent, the fitness-gap logic is inverted, missing-resource rates are repeatedly given the wrong cause, and the Python would not reproduce the prose.

**Timeline and attribution.** March is correctly treated as the semantic break, and May, June, July, October, and December are the right epochs to discuss. That skeleton is then undermined by specific errors. `missing_resource_pct` peaks at 6.5 in July, not 6.2 in June, and the brief states that missingness sits on migrated activities; using it as evidence of the June staffing cut is a causal mistake repeated in the timeline, hypotheses, attribution, and rule 3. Median cycle time in March is 31.2 versus 31.5 in January, so the reported `+0.3` has the wrong sign. The large control-flow move is `Assess→Senior Assessment` from 4 per 100 cases in January to 22 in October; July only reaches 9, after 7 in May, so tying that edge to the July priority rule mis-times it. Median cycle time, p90, volume, complexity, and rework all peak in November (49.0 h, 121 h, 790 cases, 45%, 29%), not October. September is omitted: supplier-Z share, rework, and cycle time all improve after the 10 September recovery while staffing is still unrepaired, which is the strongest aggregate check on a pure staffing story. October–November rework rises again while supplier-Z share stays near 24–27% and complexity jumps, so “supplier backlog explains autumn rework” does not fit the series. Confidence of 85–95% on confounded, case-aggregated mechanisms is stronger than the design supports. The second logging change point (supplier documents misclassified as `Validate Eligibility`) is not in the evidence.

**Fitness and DFG.** Mapped fitness near 0.90 in March means the old model still fits after the label map and duplicate removal. The raw-versus-mapped gap stays large through December because `distinct_activity_labels` stays at 11 and duplicate `Open Case` stays about 4–6%. That gap is the persisting logging discrepancy. The mild real conformance signal is the mapped series itself, 0.93 in February to 0.86 in July. Section 2 says the persisting gap shows behavioral drift, which reverses those two facts. It is also under the required 180–280 words (about 150), and it treats missing resources as creators of spurious control-flow paths. Missing resources break organizational and resource analyses; duplicate `Open Case` events are what disturb sequence fitness. Queueing is mislabeled as service-time degradation even though Assess service time only moves from about 4.8 h to about 5.4 h. Because the normative model already allows `Request Info`, doubled rework need not collapse fitness; the small mapped-fitness dip is what that model predicts. The normalized March fragment is almost the January fragment, so a March DFG distance cannot be blamed on `Eligibility Screen` or `Open Case` after those corrections. The reported distances (0.02, 0.18, 0.25, 0.30) are roughly unweighted Euclidean distance on rates divided by 100, not the baseline-weighted distance the text claims, and October is about 0.27 rather than 0.30 if the six edges used are scored that way. The decision edge, which falls from 83 to 72 as rework rises, is omitted. Calling absent edges “0 if absent in the baseline” does not state a coherent partial-DFG assumption.

**Cycle-time split.** For October the text assigns roughly +10–15 h to case mix, +5–10 h to rework, +0.6 h to service, and +15–20 h to queueing. Those pieces sum to about double the actual median increase of +16.3 h from January (31.5 to 47.8). The same +16 h is also described as the queueing component alone. The July +12.7 h versus January stacks supplier rework, complexity, staffing, and the priority rule; it is not an estimate of the staffing effect (June versus May is +4.3 h). No complexity premium or hours-per-rework-loop is stated, so the hour ranges are not identified.

**Discrimination, segments, monitors.** Cross-sectional patterns are written as if they were drift: more senior assessment on high-complexity cases is the designed process, and a within-class change over time is what would indicate drift. The priority rule is placed in the population-drift cell. The primary monitoring population is low-complexity, non-supplier-Z cases, which removes the two strata where supplier rework and renewal mix actually appear. A rolling three-month median reference absorbs a level shift within a quarter, so it will go quiet on the persistent March and May changes. The mapped-fitness alert at 0.85 never fires on this year (the low is 0.86). The row labeled queueing monitors total `median_cycle_h`. Rule 3 treats missing resources as staffing shortages; rule 7 correctly treats migrated-activity missingness as a migration artifact.

**Code.** Part A embeds 4 of the monthly metrics and drops the decision column. `baseline_relative = iloc[0]` is not a baseline calculation. The change-point expression flags essentially every month from March onward because raw fitness stays about 0.35 below January, and it does not match its own comment. Part B never imports `pm4py`. `get_variants` has no `variant_threshold`. `discover_temporal_profile` is not computed for one activity name. Token-based replay is called without a net, marking, or discovered model. `.map()` on a partial label dictionary turns every unmapped activity into missing values. `f"{month}-31"` is an invalid end date for several months. Log skeletons and DECLARE models are absent. The printed distances from this code cannot match section 3.

The outline is the right monitoring story. The numbers, the fitness interpretation, the September/November timing, and the executable checks do not hold.
