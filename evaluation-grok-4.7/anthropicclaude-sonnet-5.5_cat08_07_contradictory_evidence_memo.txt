7.5

The memo is a real decision document: it picks a reversible, segment-limited path, refuses both blanket scale-up and blanket rollback, and ties gates to the tail, rework, and billing-before-activation problems. The six required sections are all present, the evidence table actually separates fact, interpretation, and assumption, and the metric-conflict section uses fitness versus precision, alignments, the temporal profile, DFG shifts, and the handover versus working-together networks. That is the right shape of answer.

It is not nearly flawless.

The opening evidence paragraph states that simple houses “saw a median of 6 days, p90 of 8 days” and “complaints of 1pp.” The source gives changes of −6.0 days, −8.0 days, and −1pp, not levels. Overall median duration is 10.8 days in the pilot, so a segment median of 6 days is a different claim, and it is not supported. The same sign loss reappears in the table (“6 d median, 8 d p90,” “FTR 4 to 11pp”) and in the weighted check (“about 2.8 days” versus “the observed 3.4,” while p90 keeps its plus). The arithmetic behind that check is right (−2.8 versus −3.4; +6.7 versus +7.5), but the write-up drops the direction on the median. Comparison arrows are also missing throughout (“31.038.5,” “4562,” “0.870.94”), so many core contrasts are hard to read.

Two causal sentences overreach. Skip-rule errors, missing permits, and early billing are said to have “no offsetting benefit outside simple houses,” but median duration still improved for apartments (−2 days), business (−3), and rural (−1). Only old buildings worsened at the median. Later, the overall median gain is described as “dominated by simple houses,” which are 34% of pilot cases, not a majority. Most of the median improvement is shared with other segments; what simple houses uniquely dominate is the joint improvement in median, tail, first-time-right, and complaints.

Process-mining language slips in the place it matters. The memo says precision of 0.51 means “the new process permits many loosely controlled combinations,” and the table says “the new process is followed.” Fitness and precision describe the discovered model against the log. High fitness is not conformance to the intended redesign, and low precision means the model allows behavior the log does not show. Section 3 explains that distinction correctly, then the memo and table blur it again.

The value-of-information ranking puts retrospective rule validation (M6) above the randomized skip test (M1) without noting selection bias: cases that still received a field survey are not a valid test set for addresses the rule skipped. M6 can easily give false confidence. Section 6 also reorders the work (M6, M7, M3, M4, M1) relative to the stated value ranking without making the clock-time versus decision-value distinction in that section. “About one quarter” of forgone savings, a 1,500-case floor, 98% permit compliance, and a 1% billing-before-activation cap are policy choices presented as if they were measured thresholds.

The recommendation itself — Alternative E, pause survey-skipping and post-billing activation, keep the simple-house gain, gate any wider rollout — is defensible and matches the evidence. Wording undercuts it: “do not scale R nationally” and then “scale R for simple low-risk houses, nationally” say opposite things unless the reader already accepts the author’s narrower definition of R.
