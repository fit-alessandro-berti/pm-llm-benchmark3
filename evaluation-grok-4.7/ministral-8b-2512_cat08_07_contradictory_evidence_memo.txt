The memo lands in the right decision neighborhood and covers every required section, but it is not reliable enough for an executive decision. Several errors are decision-critical, not cosmetic.

The recommendation contradicts itself. The opening sentence keeps Redesign R for simple low-risk houses and rolls survey skipping, post-billing activation, and coordinator-only ownership back only for apartments and old buildings. The immediate-action list, and Section 6, then strip survey skipping and post-billing activation from simple houses as well. Those are different operating models. A reader cannot tell what management is being asked to deploy.

That contradiction is also an identification error. Simple houses improved on median, tail, first-time-right, and complaints under the full bundle. The memo claims to preserve those wins while removing two of the bundle’s distinctive changes, without evidence that coordinator ownership or the merged task produced the gains. Component effects are not identified in the pilot.

The 60% volume figure is wrong and repeated. Simple low-risk houses are 34% of the pilot, which the memo itself states earlier. There is no 60% “simple” pool in the data.

The fitness/precision explanation is conceptually wrong. Low precision means the discovered model allows many traces that were not observed. Billing start before a successful activation test is a frequent move-on-log: behavior in the log that the model does not expect. Treating that deviation as an example of low precision inverts the diagnostic. Generalization’s drop from 0.70 to 0.56 is ignored. The facts cell “pilot model allows invalid sequences” puts that misreading in the fact column.

The evidence table also sign-flips the simple-house result (“+6d median” instead of a 6-day reduction) and calls the 34%→12% change a handover reduction rather than the share of cases with 3+ handovers. Apartments are treated as pure deterioration even though their median still improved by 2 days while the tail, first-time-right, and complaints worsened. The median/tail split is within segments, not only between them.

First-order caveats are missing: pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings, attributes are incomplete before week 3, complaint cost stops after week 4, start timestamps are sparse, and the windows are 10 versus 6 weeks. Aggregate worsening cannot be read as a clean treatment effect. The causal line that centralization “led to” skipped surveys reverses the design: the skip rule was intentional, and lower field-survey utilization is the result.

Alternative C is misstated. Its risk is described as skipped surveys, which C already suspends. Value-of-information ranks are partly sensible (M1, M3, M4), but M5 is ranked near the bottom even though the memo’s own resilience concern is exactly what M5 tests. Implementation times, the EUR 200 cap, the 8% and 10% triggers, and “baseline +5 days” are invented and not labeled as judgments. “Regulatory/compliance exposure” overstates an intentional activation-timing change and missing permit checks.

The structure, the rejection of national scale and of a full rollback, and the segment-aware stance are sound. The operating decision, the volumes, and the process-mining mechanism are not.
