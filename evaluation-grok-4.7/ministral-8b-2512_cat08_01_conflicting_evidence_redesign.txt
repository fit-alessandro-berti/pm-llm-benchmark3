The write-up follows the required seven-part structure and does recognize the central conflict: the high-volume auto-approval path is fast and cheaper before rework, while complaint, conformance, and segment results cut the other way. That is not enough to carry it. Several load-bearing figures are invented or misread, and those errors drive the diagnosis, the redesign effects, and the pilot thresholds.

Evidence problems are severe. Alignment diagnostics do not say that 23% of fast approvals dispatch without complete evidence; that rate is not in the log. The “31% of reopened claims violate R3” figure is the pre-rework cost gap (fast approval is 31% cheaper before complaints and 9% cheaper after), reassigned to a different metric. Reopen outflows are 310 to manual eligibility and 120 to the escalation board, so 31% is not a reopen rate. Fraud/risk inflow is not “400+ cases per month”: the listed directly-follows counts into fraud/risk are 400, 360, and 220, and the 5,000 cases have no time window. “80 complaints out of 260 manual-to-repair-quote cases (3.1%)” is wrong twice: 80/260 is about 31%, and the 80 complaints sit on all repair quotes, including the 310 that arrive from fraud/risk. The stated 3.3% repair-path complaint rate is a different statistic. Summing the 0.4h and 1.8h medians is not a cycle time and contradicts the segment medians of 8h, 13h, 20h, and 28h. “5–10x slower” overstates the reviewed-versus-fast ratios, which are about 4–6x. Premium customers are not described as abandoning after 24 hours; that threshold is service-credit exposure on premium low-risk accessories.

Mechanism errors compound this. A pre-dispatch gate is aimed at wrong replacement selection (about EUR 95 or EUR 410, and the long dispatch-to-complaint tail). It is not a control for repair rework, which is 12.5% of repaired high-value devices and 4% of repaired low-risk devices after repair and quality check. The claimed 60–80% catch rate appears nowhere and is then repeated as a table result. Low precision is attributed to outdated segment rules; the stated reason is that the discovered model allows low-frequency auto-approval, risk, and repair combinations that policy forbids. “Without risk review” is treated as an R1 breach, but R1 is satisfied by either manual eligibility or fraud/risk review before fast approval on claims above EUR 400. R2 also has an explicit exception for premium low-risk accessories, which the redesigns override without discussion. Observational gaps, especially 22% versus 5% for premium high-value, are treated as the effect a redesign would produce.

The three redesigns are only partly distinct. A is a real risk-tier policy, but the 24-hour repair-quote SLA sits next to a median that is already 21 hours and does not remove the part-price wait. That same manual-to-repair-quote edge is already too slow for premium cases, yet premium high-value work is pushed toward it. B changes the router to an uncalibrated model with 10%, 30%, and 15% cutoffs, against a 9.2% base complaint rate and a known risk that rule changes raise manual inflow 15–35%. Two fraud specialists cannot absorb “parallel” review by working shifts. C is mostly a harsher version of A: nearly all standard volume goes through manual review and fraud review unless the claim is under an unexplained EUR 100 cutoff, while premium high-value is forced onto repair even when a reviewed replacement would comply. “Hire one reviewer” and “no more bottlenecks” do not fit a seven-reviewer queue that degrades above 82% utilization or a two-person fraud desk already peaking on Mondays and after campaigns. The escalation board is one 45-minute daily slot and does not scale to C’s premium complaint path.

The comparison table keeps dominance conditional, which matches the evidence limit, but the cells stay vague and carry the fabricated 60–80% quality gain. The pilot plan mixes incompatible bars: pass if median cycle time stays under 24 hours against an 8-hour baseline, but stop if cycle time rises more than 30%; match a 6% complaint rate while also requiring a 20% reduction; compare a “<10%” abandonment rate with 320 cases. Phase 2 costs of EUR 150, EUR 180, and EUR 200 per claim are not in the data. Phase 3 uses the 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate as the baseline for unclear-defect cases. Downstream checks mention complaints, quality-check failures, and abandonment, but not R1–R3 conformance, post-rework cost, or the 82% utilization cliff as the test that savings were not pushed into another queue.
