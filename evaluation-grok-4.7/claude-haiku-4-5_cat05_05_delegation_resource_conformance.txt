4.5

The write-up covers every required section and gets several plain violations right, but it contradicts itself on decisive facts, misses designed authority traps, and does not actually run the required PM4Py checks. Under a strict reading it is not a reliable event-time judgment.

The worst error is CHG-501. Asha’s leave is 2026-08-10 through 2026-08-14, so the 2026-08-11 09:00 approval falls inside it. The matrix states that leave window, then the case row says she was not absent and was “working normally before leave,” and also says leave did not start until the evening of 2026-08-10. Those claims cannot be true together, and the resulting UNAUTHORIZED label is the wrong outcome. The real residual issue is evidence timing: the signed PDF is 2026-08-09 18:00, while the QMS row appears only on 2026-08-12 09:15 during migration. Section 2 never answers the required question of whether a later database row can prove an earlier delegation.

Weekdays are systematically wrong. With 2026-08-15 and 2026-08-16 as the weekend, 2026-08-11 is Tuesday, 2026-08-12 Wednesday, 2026-08-13 Thursday, and 2026-08-14 Friday. The text calls 2026-08-11 Monday and 2026-08-14 Thursday, and the CHG-510 bullet calls 2026-08-11 Monday and 2026-08-12 Wednesday in the same sentence. Section 2 then says the CHG-505 review on 2026-08-14 is within two business days; Section 3 says it is day 3 and therefore late. The lateness direction happens to survive a correct Tuesday-to-Friday count, but the reasoning does not.

Delegation scope and sick-leave identity are missed where they change the result. D-QM-01 is production-equipment approval authority only. Ben’s retrospective reviews of CHG-505 and CHG-510 are cooling-system events, so they are not within that scope; the text treats both reviews as valid QM-delegate acts. Ben’s certified sick leave runs from 2026-08-13 12:00 through 2026-08-14 23:59. CHG-506 at 13:00 on the 13th is discussed, but the CHG-505 review at 16:00 on the 14th is inside the same interval and is ignored. That is the same account-use problem, on the act that is supposed to cure the emergency.

The actor matrix inverts principal and delegate. Asha is shown as holding the D-QM-01 and D-QM-02 “delegated role,” Diego as holding D-IC-03, and Farid as holding D-IC-04. Those people are the principals. Clara, the purported D-IC-04 delegate, is given no delegated role. Eva is described as having “held QM only from 2026-08-12.” She never held QM; she moved from Independent Verifier to Quality Engineer. The same Eva row calls CHG-509 a scope mismatch with production equipment. D-QM-02 is training-document only, and CHG-509 is a training document, so the scope matches. Section 3 later says it matches. The matrix and the case table disagree.

Other case judgments are uneven. CHG-502, CHG-507’s approval, CHG-508 in the case table, and CHG-510’s self-approval plus prohibited subdelegation are sound. CHG-507 overstates Grace Li’s verification: RR2 separates implementer from verifier and does not give the verifier a QM-style role test. CHG-509’s cell is internally argued (“Wait—re-read”), treats approver-equals-verifier as an RR2 problem, and offers VALID_SUBSTITUTION, SUSPICIOUS_PATTERN, and AUTHORIZED at once. RR2 is implementer versus verifier; Clara implemented and the stronger session points to Eva. CHG-504 and CHG-506 are locked to AUTHORIZED and UNAUTHORIZED despite the problem’s rule that ambiguous identity has no single canonical answer. Section 4 is more careful than Section 3, which makes the case table overconfident. Section 7 then undoes CHG-508 by calling the normalized `b.koch` / `u_bk` pair a data-quality alias failure. Normalization reveals a true same-human RR1 breach; it does not invent one. CHG-501 is likewise misplaced in the data-quality bucket on the false absence theory.

Formal RR statements are partly unreadable because the comparison operators are blank, so “Drafter(c) Approver(c)” and the interval inequalities do not state a rule. The business-day definition itself (Monday–Friday, no holiday in this window, weekends excluded) is fine and then misapplied.

The code does not meet the mandatory PM4Py checklist. `format_dataframe`, `filter_four_eyes_principle`, `discover_handover_of_work_network`, `discover_working_together_network`, and `filter_activity_done_different_resources` are imported and never called. Manual substitutes are not those checks. It is also unlikely to run: timezone-aware CSV timestamps are compared with naive `datetime` bounds. Delegation validity never tests principal absence. Sick leave is stored under `b.koch` after aliases have been normalized to `u_bk`, and absence is tested on the actor rather than the principal. D-IC-03 is tied to the string `CI-44` and compared with case ids `CHG-505` and `CHG-510`, so the valid IC delegation is rejected. `emergency == 'true'` fails if pandas infers booleans. The printed findings are hardcoded and repeat the prose errors. Service-account handling is at least explicit: `svc_qms` stays unattributed in the checks and CHG-503 versus CHG-509 is left manual. That is the right caution, but it is not a four-eyes or handover-network analysis.

Section 4 is the strongest part. Repeated Clara–Ben pairing is treated as an indicator, with benign workload explanations, adverse rubber-stamping explanations, and evidence that would separate them. Section 6 covers delegation recording, aliases, absence, authentication, emergency clocks, rotation, and service accounts in useful operational detail. Network monitoring and evidential retention are thinner than the other requested controls. The three named investigations are plausible, but they omit the 2026-08-14 review under Ben’s credentials and the cooling-system scope failure, so the priority list follows the same blind spots.

The net result is a complete-looking report whose central authority conclusions are not stable enough to use. Clear same-person breaches and the illegal subdelegation are identified; event-time absence, delegation scope, sick-leave coverage, and the required PM4Py tests are not.
