6.5

The capacity arithmetic and the recommendation’s direction are strong, but the policy comparison is not internally reliable, and the simulation does not faithfully implement the policies it is supposed to test.

The service-time means are right: Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.30, Electrical 32.82, Software 38.50, Validation 15.74, and the doubled-tail U4 diagnostics 29.60 / 37.14 / 44.50. Daily activity counts (18.09 / 24.30 / 12.85) and the multiplier-1.0 lower bound of about 2,721 resource-minutes against 2,490 regular and 2,670 overtime minutes are also right, including 2,721/2,670 = 101.9% and the 90% gap of about 480 minutes (about 533 extra minutes to sit at 90%). U1’s morning volume (16 → 27.2; P1 about 2.05 → 5.66), the mix-only U2 renormalization, Chen’s 450-minute removal, and the U5 ratio near 157% of post-absence overtime capacity all check. Assumptions are labeled, both objectives exist, routing is not sold as a cure for missing capacity, and sections 6–7 and 9 are concrete.

That core is undermined by how the comparison is built.

The block “work/capacity” ratios treat all downstream work of arrivals in a block as load on that same block. Early ratios therefore overstate immediate congestion, and 16:00–20:00 ratios of 187% and 160% mix a real staffing cliff with work that cannot be performed then. The daily total is the valid figure; the block percentages are not utilizations. The table is also slightly inconsistent: rounded Mechanical and Electrical cells sum to 475 and 796, while the total row shows 476 and 798, and skill totals sum to 2,722 rather than 2,721.

U2 is only the mix-preserving reading. Doubling E/ES counts and then renormalizing would raise afternoon volume. Both readings fit the wording; only one is analyzed, and under that reading U2 barely changes total work. The 14:00–16:00 claim that electrical work exceeds Chen and Deepa’s 210 minutes is true for that pair, but Ada and Emil are also electrical-capable then, so this is a speed and contention problem, not a hard electrical infeasibility.

Section 4’s bands are not derived from section 2 or from the simulator, and they conflict with Policy 1. Strict intake is only Ada and Farah. After Ada’s 16:00–16:30 overtime, nobody eligible remains until 08:00 the next day, so most of the expected nine arrivals after 16:00 miss a two-hour P1 target and an eight-hour P2 target by construction. Those cases are more than 10% of volume, so a strict base p90 of 450–900 minutes is too low; many of the worst cases wait 12–16 hours before intake starts. The same blackout applies to strict validation after Deepa leaves. Finite delays also depend on an empty next day. A repeating day is unstable at roughly 109% before multipliers and handovers, yet the delay table reads like a steady-state comparison. Handover load is never bounded, although two resource changes per case would be on the order of several hundred receiver-minutes under the stated reservation rule.

Policy 1 is a weak specialization design, and the code makes it weaker than the prose. Cross-pool priority for Ada, Chen, and Deepa is unspecified in section 3. The code always prefers the first named pool member, so Ada takes intake and mechanical whenever she can fit the task and Chen is preferred for both electrical and software. That is not FIFO within fixed queues. It manufactures Ada/Chen overload and leaves Farah idle. The dominant failure mode, the late intake and validation blackout, is not stated.

Prose and code diverge elsewhere. Pooling is specified to parallelize when two resources are available; the code always marks ME/ES parallel and still charges the 8-minute coordination penalty if one resource ends up doing both branches. Hybrid P1 parallelization ignores the stated availability test. Hybrid P3 aging after 240 minutes only moves the rank to 1.5, so every P2 still outranks that P3, despite the starvation rule. Section 3 preempts hybrid P1 work after 5 minutes; section 5 uses 15 minutes or 45 minutes of slack. Pooling’s tie-break is cumulative assigned minutes, not normalized utilization. Electrical and Software are collapsed into one family, so a Chen or Emil switch between those skills incurs no setup, while the team-transfer rule is a separate 5-minute penalty. Parallel branches each hand over from the intake resource, so the same resource can be charged twice and the mechanical-versus-electrical transfer is not applied on the branch edge.

The repeated-seed design does not create common service times. Arrivals share a seed, but service draws occur when tasks are created and that order depends on the policy. “Common random numbers” in section 9 are not what section 8 does. Mean delay is completed cases only, which rewards policies that leave hard cases unfinished; section 4 does not say that. `J_B` is a max over scenarios, so U5 almost certainly dominates the fairness terms, which the recommendation does not discuss.

The PM4Py ending is likely to throw before demonstrating the required analysis. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` in standard PM4Py does not accept `service_time_column`, `waiting_time_column`, or `sojourn_time_column`; those columns are fixed as `@@service_time`, `@@waiting_time`, and `@@sojourn_time`. The later groupby depends on the custom names. The case-level versus stage-level distinction in the text is correct, but the printed stage metric is assignment time, whereas section 7 defines stage delay as actual start minus queue entry. Several consequential simplifications, including the family collapse, non-common service draws, and forced parallelism, are not listed in the code’s omissions.

The hybrid recommendation is still the most defensible of the three as specified: it protects a small P1 volume, avoids some unnecessary branching, and does not pretend U5 is feasible. It is not yet a result of the reproducible experiment, and a less artificial strict baseline could narrow or reorder the base-case gap.
