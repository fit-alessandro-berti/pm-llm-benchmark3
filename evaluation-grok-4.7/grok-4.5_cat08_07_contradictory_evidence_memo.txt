The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision and covers every required section, but it is not nearly flawless. Several decision-critical ambiguities and one process-mining conceptual error keep it well below excellent.

The recommendation correctly rejects national scale and blanket rollback, preserves the simple-house gain, and ties rollout to tail, rework, cost, and activation-order gates. The evidence table really does separate facts, interpretations, and assumptions. The five metric conflicts are each given a mechanism, and M1–M8 are ranked by how they would change the next decision. Segment results, alignments, DFG shifts, and the handover versus working-together networks are used, not merely named.

The decision is internally inconsistent. The memo pauses survey-skip and billing-before-activation only outside simple low-risk houses. Section 6 prohibits billing before a successful activation test on every segment and does not scale the skip rule at all. Those are different operating designs. The memo also never says whether simple houses keep early billing, which matters because that change can itself shorten recorded cycle time and move failures past the measured endpoint. Calling the median and visible-cost moves “real operational wins,” then later treating visible cost as partly off-book, leaves the same ambiguity.

Simple-house gains were observed under the full bundle. Scaling only coordinator ownership plus merged validate/schedule treats separability as established while the table correctly lists it as untested. Immediate qualification also depends on a building-risk attribute the pilot did not capture cleanly and on a predictive rule the memo itself does not trust.

The fitness/precision account is technically blurred. High fitness means the discovered model replays the log; low precision means the model also allows unseen behavior. Alignments showing frequent billing-before-activation, repeat installs, missing permits, and post-billing complaints are observed conformance deviations, not proof of imprecision. The write-up equates those observed deviations with low precision and then extends precision to “operating reality.” Generalization falling from 0.70 to 0.56 is tabulated and never interpreted. The temporal finding that failed-visit to second-visit delay worsened where coordinator utilization was high is omitted, as are the unequal windows (10 weeks / 24,000 cases versus 6 weeks / 9,200). Survey utilization at 52% is overstated as idle capacity. The 85% utilization cap and the six-week holdout are reasonable management choices, but they are presented as decision criteria without being marked as judgments.

Value-of-information is directionally sound, especially M4, M6, M3, and M1, yet M5 is ranked last while the immediate action scales single-owner coordination, the design most exposed to the resilience failure. No measurement isolates merged scheduling plus ownership after early billing and survey-skip are removed, which is the actual policy being deployed.

The strategic direction is sound. The memo a decision-maker would execute still contradicts the final control set, over-attributes the median gain, and mishandles precision.
