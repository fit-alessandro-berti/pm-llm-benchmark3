3.8

The write-up covers every required section and names the right family of methods, but the estimands, the event coding, and the data claims do not hold together. Those are the core of the task, so the result stays well below competent.

The headline estimand is not well-defined. E1 sets \(S(72)=P(T>72)\) with \(T\) equal to time to “any internal resolution,” then removes withdrawal at 27 hours as a competing event and censors transfer at 68 hours. Under that probability, a withdrawal at 27 hours has already settled internal status before 72 hours; it is not a non-event that merely leaves the risk set. E2 writes \(F(72)=P(T>72,\ \text{Resolve before right censoring})\). Conditioning on the observation scheme makes this a property of the extract, not a case-process probability. A resolution at 76 hours is called a breach event at hour 76. For a 72-hour SLA the breach is already known at 72; 76 is the later resolution time. An open case at 92 hours is described as both right-censored and already breached, without separating the binary “unresolved at 72” indicator from a censored time-to-resolution contribution.

The encoding table repeats that mix-up. Timely resolutions are coded `event = 0` and not censored, so they are neither events nor censorings. Late resolutions are events at the resolution time, which is after 72. Open cases at or above 72 hours stay censored, so a known passage beyond the horizon is thrown away for the binary breach question. Scenario C labels every transfer an “automatic late breach,” including S21 at 26 hours and S08 at 68 hours, and would place the event at the transfer time. That moves a 72-hour threshold failure onto the clock before hour 72. The lifelines sketch then does `e1_event.fillna(0)`. The only 1s are resolutions at 74, 76, 88, and 110, so the printed \(S(72)\) is identically 1. Competing withdrawals and rejections are censored in that fit even though the table says they are not censored.

Section 3 misreads the extract. It says no open case is still under 72 hours. S10 (47), S15 (35), S24 (67), S29 (51), and S31 (23) are open and below the SLA. That is the contrast the task asked for, and it is mislabeled left truncation. Left truncation would be delayed entry into the risk set. The same paragraph lists only S11 and S26 as already past 72 hours and omits S09 (92) and S25 (79). “Completed-case analysis counts withdrawals as successes” describes a different mistake from dropping non-resolved cases. Cohort B is said to add more escalations; both cohorts have six `yes` values (6/15 vs 6/17). All three transfers are P1, not “mostly” P1, and only S32 has already passed 72 hours at transfer, so the claim that most would have breached internally is stronger than the rows support.

The analysis plan’s preference for Aalen–Johansen over a pooled Kaplan–Meier is directionally right, and stratification by priority and cohort matches the design. The plan still treats sparse 32-case strata as the primary reporting cut, recommends time-varying Cox adjustment without interior event times, and defines “restricted mean time below SLA” as \(\int_0^{72} S(t)\,dt\). That integral is mean unresolved time. The later rule “if it is below target, add reviewers” treats a smaller value as worse speed, which fights the formula. Landmarking “the probability of ever being resolved” is not identified from a Kaplan–Meier curve.

The feature discussion correctly keeps priority and cohort at baseline and flags ever-escalated and ever-reworked as leakage if they are fixed at `Submit`. It then treats `last_event_gap_h` as an ordinary time-varying covariate. In this table the gap is 0 on every terminal row and positive on every open row, so it restates observation status. The state diagram misses the cohort-B signature `Submit → AutoCheck → Screen`, misses `AutoCheck → Resolve`, `Screen → Reject`, `Screen → Escalate`, and `Rework → Resolve`, and adds `Submit → Reject`, which no prefix shows. Immortal time is named, but not tied to the actual requirement that a case must remain open long enough to reach `Escalate`.

Sensitivity section SA1’s “±10 pp” is not computed. SA3’s inverse-probability weights use gap, which separates open from terminal rows perfectly, so the weighted analysis is not a censoring model. The reversible claim that “P1 always has lower breach probability” is not a result in this extract: the eight P1 rows are dominated by transfers and opens. Section 7 sets a 48-hour flag at probability 0.3 and a Hosmer–Lemeshow check without saying that case-level scores from this sample are not effects of escalating or of the cohort policy.

The code builds the 32-row table accurately, keeps open cases, and attempts a PM4Py log without inventing timestamps. It does not do what section 8 requires. `encode` defaults to transfer scenario A and is never run for B or C. E2 is never coded. The executed “analysis” is a count group-by, not a risk-set or cumulative-incidence comparison. The variant call uses `pm4py.statistics.variants.log.get_variants` via a package alias rather than `pm4py.get_variants` or `get_prefixes_from_log`. A second Python block follows the required single code block. `esc_before_review` is read off the full prefix, including activities after case start.

Section 9 does list the missing pieces that would change the inference: event timestamps, post-transfer outcomes, cutoff and start dates, workload, assignment, and rollout compliance. That inventory cannot repair the estimand, the backwards event indicator, or the false statement about open cases under 72 hours.
