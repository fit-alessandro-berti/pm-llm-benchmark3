The core timeline is the right one: April is a schema and prefix-pipeline incident, June is a routing-mix change with stable within-variant breach rates, August is a real parts-delay regime change, September adds Partner Z, and October’s 0.16 breach rate is censored. That keeps the answer out of the “one drift label for every month” failure. It is still well short of a strict pass on triangulation, falsification, and the required monitoring sketch.

## 1. Drift diagnosis table
The six rows exist and the confidence levels are mostly in the right place. April is correctly isolated as a pipeline failure, June is correctly kept out of concept drift, and July–August V3 deterioration is correctly separated from Partner Z.

The boundaries are loose. Rural distance and device age were never model covariates, so putting them under covariate drift misstates the mechanism: they are omitted factors under a population shift, and they can look like concept drift in P(breach | observed features). June’s V2 jump is a real mix shift; October is mostly continuation plus censoring, not a new covariate event. Process drift stops at September while the cited parts edge of 155 h is October’s, and that edge keeps worsening after September (148 h, then 155 h), independent of labels. Concept drift starts in the table in August, but the matured V3 rate already moved from 0.35 in June to 0.46 in July when the supplier slippage began. Label delay is right for October and rightly treats August’s 93% maturity as too high to invent the 0.64 V3 rate; September’s 82% maturity and slower Partner Z labels get almost no evidence in that row.

## 2. Competing explanations and timeline
January–March, April, May, June, and October are directionally right, and the four episodes are not collapsed into one outage. Several claims are stronger than the evidence.

April is called purely synthetic. The variant breach rates staying near the Q1 baseline is the actual falsifier of an April concept shift, and it is never used. The migration also built prefixes at the wrong event or not at all; the write-up reduces that to broken feature extraction. “Label patch” for the 6 May activity-mapping fix confuses outcome labels with activity names. May’s residual is real (unknown activity 2%, missing queue feature 3%, ECE 0.050), but the unrecomputed cache is specifically an April feature-backfill hole, while 1–5 May is the leftover live window.

June correctly notes stable V1/V2/V3 breach rates. It skips the calibration signature: mean predicted risk stayed 0.20 while the observed breach rate fell to 0.15 and ECE rose to 0.060. Discrimination mostly returned; the model did not follow the safer mix.

July and August are one bullet. They are different magnitudes: July still had an aggregate breach rate of 0.19, V2 near 50%, and a parts edge of 65 h; August had V3 at 35% and 0.64, an edge of 130 h, mean prediction 0.19 against an observed rate of 0.28, and AUROC 0.68. A variant-weighted August rate is about 0.31 versus an observed 0.28, so censoring likely understates August rather than creating it. That decomposition is absent.

September is headed as a population shift. Supplier delay, V3 conditional risk near 0.61, mix, Partner Z, and 82% maturity are simultaneous. The 0.45 versus 0.27 Partner Z gap is reported as amplification without the problem’s own caveat that Partner Z labels mature more slowly. October correctly calls 0.16 a backlog artifact and notes longer paths are hit harder. It does not say the apparent flip to over-prediction (0.23 versus 0.16) is the same artifact, or that the parts edge and Partner Z share are still valid, non-label facts.

The direction of error is the cleanest separator in the table and is never used: April over-predicts (0.28 versus 0.19), June over-predicts mildly (0.20 versus 0.15), August–September under-predict (0.19 versus 0.28, 0.21 versus 0.31), October appears to over-predict only because labels are immature.

## 3. Diagnostic tests
All seven requested test names appear. The two-hypothesis rule is only partly met, and one design is biased.

The maturity-curve idea is right, and fixed-vintage backtesting is named. The procedure then says to score October only on cases that resolved quickly. Under a backlog that hits long paths, those cases are enriched for non-breaches, so that subset shows a low breach rate whether October truly improved or not. A fixed-vintage check holds the start cohort fixed and moves the horizon, or uses a survival estimate; it does not condition on early resolution. The stated expectation (“the true rate projects higher once delay is adjusted”) does not follow from that sample.

Label normalization states only the pipeline hypothesis, and “instantly recover to March” ignores prefix rebuild and the unrecomputed cache. If a real April change existed, repaired features would not restore March AUROC; that alternative is missing. Stratified calibration names August V3 under-prediction and a Partner Z gap, but not the censoring alternative: the August gap should remain on matured V3 labels, while a pure backlog would shrink once the same follow-up window is enforced. No stratified AUROC result is given. The parts-edge versus remote-edge check restates evidence already in the prompt and calls the rise exponential. A global capacity shock would also move dispatch and repair edges; a label artifact would move breach metrics while edge times stayed put. Neither contrast is stated.

P(breach | variant) is not P(breach | same path). June versus August at variant level is useful and already in the matured-rate table. The missing falsifier is: if identical diagnosis-time prefixes keep the same breach rate and only the mix inside V3 changes, August is still process mix, not concept drift. Partner Z reweighting on variant shares is the right transport sketch and omits differential maturity, so a residual gap can be slow labels rather than rural distance or device age. The lineage check is aimed at April and May alike, even though the unrecomputed “events before diagnosis” cache is an April backfill defect, and it is written as confirmation rather than a result that could fail.

## 4. Recalibration-versus-retraining decision
The staging is proportionate: repair April features, do not fit on October outcomes, use a temporary V3 risk adjustment while the supplier regime lasts, and add Partner Z features before a full retrain. That is the right order.

Training eligibility is incomplete. January–March and June are reasonable mature, concept-stable months. July is listed as safe even though the supplier miss starts on 10 July and V3’s matured breach rate is already 0.46. August (93%) and September (82%, slower Partner Z labels) are neither safe nor unsafe. September is not usable for ordinary supervised training until the cohort is matured or censoring is modeled; August is usable for a persisted-supplier calibrator only with an explicit transience decision. “Block October from all metrics” is too wide: outcome metrics are unsafe, and the parts-edge series is not. The V3 calibrator is not tied to matured August–September cases.

A separate V1/V2/V3 ensemble is called highly justified because of “entirely distinct feature importances.” No importance evidence is in the material. The score is issued immediately after diagnosis, before Dispatch, Remote Fix, or Order Part, so the realized variant is not automatically a feature. Variant-specific recalibration is justified if the planned route is known at diagnosis; three models are not established.

## 5. Monitoring architecture
Label maturity gating retraining, and suppressing a false breach-rate drop, matches the October failure. Data-quality gating matches April. Process-edge, population, and stratified predictive monitors are present in compressed form.

An intervention and deployment monitor is absent. The month breaks are exactly dated changes: the 1 April rename, the 6 May mapping patch, the 1 June routing policy, the 10 July supplier failure, the 1 September partner onboarding, and the October outcome-API backlog. Without that log, a real policy shift and a bad release look the same. Predictive alerts are not explicitly dependent on green maturity and green lineage. Process and population alerts are not barred from automatic retraining.

The code sketch calls pandas, normalizes `Diagnostic Assessment Completed` before extraction, uses `format_dataframe`, `get_variants`, `discover_performance_dfg`, and `extract_temporal_features_dataframe`, and does not invent a drift detector. It does not use `filter_time_range` or `discover_dfg`. `event_month` is computed and then unused, so there is no event-calendar summary. That cohort is the one that shows the April rename on events from cases that started in March. The duplicate rule compares `activity` with `shift()` after a global sort, so the first event of a case can be dropped because it matches the previous case, and exact duplicate keys are never defined. `format_dataframe` renames `case_id`, `activity`, and `timestamp` to the standard names, after which the later column references do not exist. The performance DFG value is stored as a median; the usual aggregate is a mean, and a missing edge is stored as 0. Open October traces also bias the parts edge downward, because unfinished Order Part → Part Received pairs never enter the DFG. The summary cannot audit the incident it is for.

## 6. Risks of acting on the wrong diagnosis
Four failure modes are present, and the October and fairness mitigations point the right way. The pipeline item is titled as calibrating around a bug and then described as retraining on `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY` and missing queue features. The calibration-specific failure is different: an April isotonic map that pulls 0.28 down toward 0.19 will under-predict as soon as the features are repaired. The 95% terminal-state rule still fails when the unlabeled tail is exactly the long V3 paths. The supplier mitigation does not say what to undo when lead times recover, which is the cost of baking August into a permanent retrain. The Partner Z mitigation correctly prefers features and reweighting over a fairness threshold, and it still treats the higher breach rate as inherent rather than maturity-confounded.

The answer would be a 6.4 because the month-level story is the one a reviewer should reach, while the calibration-sign evidence is unused, September maturity is underweighted, two required test and monitor conditions are missed, the quick-resolution test is biased, and the process-evidence code does not run as specified.
