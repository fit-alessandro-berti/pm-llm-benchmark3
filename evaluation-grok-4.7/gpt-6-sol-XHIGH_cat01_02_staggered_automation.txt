The answer is a strong, cautious causal-policy assessment: arithmetic checks out, median and p90 are kept separate, staggered adoption is not collapsed into a single two-way fixed-effects effect, and the rejected “reduces cycle time by X%” claim is the right conclusion. It is not nearly flawless.

The main quantitative results are correct. Equal-month Alpha/Beta changes (about −0.4 h / +1.86 h median, about +9.7 h p90), Delta/Epsilon contrasts (about −4.49 h median and −6.32 h p90), Gamma’s windowed contrasts, March–April and June–July checks, and the ±5 h / ±10 h / ±2 pp scenario arithmetic all match the panel. Selection, the March fraud-desk jump, May complexity, staffing, routing, survey nonresponse, and branch-month quantiles are handled in the right places. The claim ladder matches the evidence.

Points come off for identification and sensitivity, not for arithmetic. Section 2 marks every estimand “not identifiable,” including availability contrasts that are identified only under explicit parallel-trends and no-interference assumptions. That conflates “assumptions untested” with “no identifying functional,” and it sits awkwardly beside the later “conditionally causal” bullet. The fast-path-use estimand is replaced by a case-level triage-assignment contrast. That substitution is conceptually sound, but the requested use/intensity contrast is never formally defined, even as an ill-posed one.

The sensitivity ranges are symmetric around descriptive difference-in-differences points. The leading threats—regression to the mean from selecting weak branches, and fraud-desk congestion that can worsen controls—both push those contrasts toward apparent benefit. A symmetric ±5 h band therefore overstates how beneficial the median effect can plausibly be. Calling those illustrative bands “plausible ranges” in section 7 upgrades an arbitrary drift allowance into something the data do not justify.

The intensity write-up is endpoint-driven. Alpha 48.3→43.3 h and Beta 47.3→45.0 h from April to August omit May–June, when fast-path share was already rising and medians and p90s worsened, and they omit the June–August easing also visible at Delta and Epsilon. The dose-response caveat is present, but the displayed signature is still the favorable endpoints. Beta’s April result is also underused: median and p90 worsened despite availability, +1 FTE, and a slightly easier recorded mix.

The code does what the prompt lists, labels the regressions as descriptive, clusters by branch, and warns about five clusters and aggregated quantiles. It still estimates the naïve two-way fixed-effects specification the narrative rejects, does not compute the cohort contrasts that carry section 5, and builds event-time dummies that dump Gamma’s early leads into the zero category. Those dummies are printed rather than fit, so this is a specification trap rather than a reported false estimate.

The rollout choice—embedded randomized phase-in at Delta and Epsilon, ITT first, spillover measurement, p90 and open-case guardrails—is the right class of decision. The 5-hour, 1-point, 0.1-CSAT, and 3-hour rules have no reference contrast, no noise or repeated-look standard, and no minimum number of queues. Historical treatment is branch availability; queue-level assignment is assumed to be feasible without establishing that. “Common rise and fall in demand” is also too strong for a period in which routing differentially loaded Alpha and Beta.

No format miss, no fabricated regression coefficient, and no unsafe single-number causal claim. The remaining gaps are real methodological ones, so this stays short of the top band.
