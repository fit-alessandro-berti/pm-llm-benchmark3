4.5

The memo covers the required topics and correctly rejects a pure frequency ranking, but it is not a usable recommendation. The portfolio, the dependency claims, and the rollout describe three different programs, and the quantitative case is internally impossible.

The decisive failure is that the year-one decision does not hold together. Section 3 defines Portfolio B as A4, A6, A9, and A12, and defers A1 and A2. Section 6 then implements A1, A2, A12, and A6 in the first six months and adds A4, A3, and A9 in the second half, with A10 and A11 also starting inside the year. That is seven to nine modules against a hard cap of four. Section 7 recommends B, then justifies the IT constraint with deep integrations for A1 and A6, even though A1 is outside B. Section 8 repeats the seven-module plan and labels it Portfolio B. A reader cannot tell which set is actually proposed.

The same break appears in the prerequisites. The memo states that A9 needs clean KYC from A3, resolved entities from A4, normalized financials from A6, and control discipline from A12. It also states that A4 needs A1 and A2, and that A6 without A2 leaves misrouted work on the analysts. Portfolio B includes A9 and A4 while omitting A1, A2, and A3. That directly undercuts the rule against downstream automation while upstream controls are still unreliable. A9 is also assigned “Very High” legal risk and only “Medium” first-year suitability, then selected ahead of candidates the matrix itself rates more suitable.

Resource identity and arithmetic are unreliable. The three FTE are compliance specialists, with dependency concentrated on two seniors. The memo treats those three people as the whole KYC operations pool and caps them at roughly 5,000–6,000 checks. Relationship-manager headcount is invented. Frequencies are treated as monthly, which makes the stated FTE impossible: normalization alone is given as about 6,500 analyst-hours against about 1,440 hours for nine analysts, before financial-document review and credit analysis. The same paragraph calls 1,440 hours the capacity remaining after meetings. Later, baseline analyst utilization is given as 90%+. Those statements cannot be true together. The 6.5% provisional-signing rate on about 7,300 signings is about 475 cases, not 70. The figure 650 is reused for the contract-remediation directly-follows edge, provisional signing, and cases saved. Rework is “an 8–12% reduction” in one section and a fall from about 15% to 7–8% in another.

Process-level weighting is skewed. Median wait multiplied by volume puts customer signing, the credit-analysis queue, contract preparation, financial-document review, and external valuation well ahead of entity mismatch. Mismatch is severe when it occurs and is on the critical path in 82% of those cases, but it is a conditional branch of roughly 1,750 cases, not the dominant end-to-end delay. A synchronization portfolio that leaves the 58-hour valuation wait and the 44-hour signing wait untouched, and barely uses the committee-packaging AND-join, is aimed at the most dramatic wait rather than the largest delay mass. Concurrency of KYC and financial-document review is effectively ignored, while a 180-hour cycle time and a 25-hour reduction are asserted without a path calculation. Collateral mix, the weekly high-exposure committee cadence, and the missing-document loop (3,300 out, 2,800 back, 52% repeated) do not materially affect the choice.

Smaller but cumulative defects: A1 is tied to 12,000 cases rather than registration at 11,880; A6 is “medium / no core update” in the integration table and a deep core integration in the rollout; the matrix has no rank order, and its bottleneck column means “creates a downstream queue” for some rows and “relieves a queue” for others; the quick-win portfolio is built to violate the control rule and the A11 prerequisite, so it is a weak alternative; four-eyes exposure is discussed and then left outside the recommended modules; Phase 3 starts the activation bot before contract-rule controls are live. The required output also continues past the seven specified sections.

Section 5 is the strongest part: false negatives, rule drift, override logs, and segregation of duties are treated as operating controls. The qualitative warning that faster registration can flood analysts unless normalization is improved matches the resource evidence. Those points are not enough to offset an irreconcilable portfolio, a broken dependency chain, and a numeric argument that contradicts the staffing data.
