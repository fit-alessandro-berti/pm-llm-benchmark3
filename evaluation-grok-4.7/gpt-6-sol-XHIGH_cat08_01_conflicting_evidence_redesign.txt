The diagnosis is evidence-heavy and correctly refuses to treat the fast path as uniformly good or the reviewed path as uniformly better. Transition counts, edge times, fitness versus perfectly fitting traces, R1–R3, the 31% to 9% cost erosion, the 14.1% versus 3.3% reopen contrast, segment outcomes, and the 82% utilization threshold are used accurately, and the three redesigns hit different mechanisms: routing policy, evidence acquisition, and pre-dispatch verification. Sacrifices, bottleneck migration, and downstream follow-up are explicit, and dominance is not faked.

It still is not nearly flawless. The 890 Request-missing-evidence → Manual-eligibility-review transitions are called “returns,” but that edge is the main successor of an evidence request, mixing first-pass handoffs with true repeats; only the separate 26% figure supports rework. The same slip is repeated for the 26-hour / 88-hour edge, and the portal’s 30–45% repeat reduction is then treated as if it directly attacks that duration. Mid-risk devices are named in the process context and then disappear, so the binary low-risk-fast / high-value-reviewed design has an unstated hole. Redesign A promises a “prompt” technical decision while the known part-price wait is left to Redesign B. The experiment never resolves the collision between immediate, non-reversible R1 enforcement and the 82% reviewer stop-rule if the rule itself creates the overload. Within-segment fast versus reviewed rates are still observational, but they are used as the design basis with less caution than the aggregate contrast. The “dominated? No” cells are intelligible only after the footnote redefines them.
