The write-up has the required nine sections and a defensible qualitative story (evening staffing cliff, specialization versus pooling, P1 reservation, P3 starvation). The quantitative core does not support the conclusions.

Offered load is never computed by skill and block, and speed multipliers, breaks, handovers, setup, coordination, and the 10% restart penalty never enter the figures. Gross work from the given ranges is already about 2,700 resource-minutes against roughly 2,490 minutes of shift capacity after breaks, before multipliers. Base load is at or over capacity, so steady-state delays and 88–98% SLA claims are not available from this data. Daily-average utilization also hides the 16:00–20:00 collapse to two resources, then one.

The comparison table is not policy-by-scenario and omits several required fields. Its 85/95/98% P1 SLAs contradict the code, which applies the same fungible utilization to every policy and then shifts SLA by unexplained constants. That formula stays finite above 100% utilization, so it is not a queueing result. In code, U2’s doubled E/ES demand is never applied, the U5 intake outage is a no-op, the U5 P1 uplift is not limited to 08:00–12:00, and P1 mix is unused.

The log is not an interval log (`start_timestamp` is absent); one resource holds every case, so the handover network cannot show handover burden. Case service versus waiting time is not separated from stage or queue time. Monitoring omits most required attributes (queue entry, eligible set, skill, planned versus actual service, pause/resume, setup, shock flag). The pilot assigns policies to different shifts, so policy is confounded with the time-of-day mix the demand table itself establishes.
