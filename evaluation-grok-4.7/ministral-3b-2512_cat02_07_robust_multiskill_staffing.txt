3.1

The write-up keeps the nine required headings and names two objectives, three policies, and the uncertainty set, but the analysis that should support those headings is numerically and conceptually unreliable. Later conclusions inherit those errors, and the mandatory comparison table and Python section do not do the work the task asks for.

Demand is misread. The 08:00–12:00 intake total is given as 7+9+7 = 23, which pulls in the 12:00–14:00 block. “20 ME cases” in 12:00–16:00 treats a 20% mix as a case count; expected ME volume in that window is on the order of a few cases, and all-day ME volume is well under ten. Capacity arithmetic does not hold: an 8-hour shift is treated as about 100 minutes via “8h × 120 min,” and Farah is described as an 8-hour resource limited to about 20 minutes. Her shift is 08:00–12:00. Chen’s shift is given as 12:00–16:00; it is 08:00–16:00. Breaks, the 30-minute overtime cap, and afternoon coverage collapse (after 18:00 only Emil is on duty, and Emil has no mechanical skill) never enter the load account.

Skill and control-flow rules are misstated. Emil is used as a cross-skilled ME resource; the matrix gives Emil electrical and software only. ME cross-skill sits with Ada, Deepa, and Farah. Parallel versus sequential is reversed: a cross-skilled resource is what makes sequential execution on one person possible, while parallel execution needs two resources and then the 8-minute coordination delay. The 10% figure is a restart penalty on remaining active time, with at most one preemption per case. The policies replace that with unrelated “>10% complete” and “<50% complete” gates. Speed multipliers are almost unused, so Farah at 1.40 is treated as a natural specialist rather than the slowest person on every diagnostic skill, and only until noon.

Offered load is never built by skill and block. A midpoint-uniform reading of the ranges, before multipliers and penalties, puts electrical well above mechanical and software, intake near 7 minutes per case, and validation as a large shared load. The write-up instead names intake handover and Farah’s ME work as the base bottlenecks. U1’s “+25% mechanical from the P1 share” does not follow from an 8-point P1 mix shift plus a 1.7 arrival multiplier. U3’s “Farah’s ME/E load doubles because Chen is absent” ignores that Farah has already left and that Chen’s work is electrical/software. U4 is missing from the comparison. Handover (7 minutes), team change (5), setup (5), and coordination (8) are never added into minutes of load.

The objectives do not match the decision. Objective 1 is a weighted tardiness expression with an example weight, while the stated targets are 90% of cases inside 120 minutes, 8 hours, and 24 hours. Objective 2 adds maximum utilization to queue-length variance and then equates the prompt’s 90% utilization fragility line with “queue capacity.” Neither formulation specifies minimization, priority weights, or how tail probability is estimated.

The three policies are not operable as written. Strict specialization assigns mechanical work to Farah, electrical to Chen, and software to Emil, dropping faster or later-covering qualified staff. All three policies “reject” P1 cases after a busy-time threshold, which conflicts with a completion SLA. Preemption triggers contradict the penalty rule and contradict each other. Reservation is “Farah for ME, Chen for E” with no reserved minutes, no time-of-day coverage, and a rule that preempts when the reserved resource is idle. Handover minimization and an auditable priority score are absent.

Section 4 is a scenario table, not a policy-by-scenario table. U4 and P2 SLA are missing. Fairness and resilience have no defined scale. The minute and utilization bands are not traceable to a capacity model, and utilizations of 105–115% sit beside a recommendation that claims no resource above 95%. Section 5 then cites base P1 attainment of 70–85% as the reason to prefer the hybrid, which is the base-case reasoning the task warns against. The stated switches (pooling above 98% utilization; specialization if P1 SLA stays under 60%) would fire inside the same stress scenarios the hybrid is supposed to survive, and moving toward stricter specialization when SLA is already failing is unsupported.

Secondary effects list the requested topics, but the mechanisms are off: SERT gaming is described as delaying P1 work; pooling is said to ensure that no resource is overloaded; validation is named without relating it to who can validate, at what multiplier, after 16:00. Monitoring omits eligible-resource set, assignment score, pause/resume, policy version, shock flag, and SLA clock. The PM4Py discussion does not separate waiting from service or show how drift, handover, service-time change, and bottleneck migration would be tested.

The Python block does not meet the reproducibility checklist. Tables are read from CSV paths and only partially sketched, rather than encoded in memory. One stub path is labeled hybrid; the other policies, U1–U5, seeds, handover, setup, parallel/sequential choice, and preemption are comments. The constructed interval has equal start and end timestamps and undefined variables. `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_service_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network` are not called. The XES import API is used as if it wrote a log. No comparison table is printed. The scaffold does not identify each omitted mechanism.

Section 9 correctly notes that ranges do not identify a distribution, then proposes “simulate 1000 cases” as a pilot and an unspecified historical backtest. There is no shadow mode, P1 guardrail, switchback, or stopping rule that would distinguish policies without an unsafe full rollout.

The heading skeleton shows the right agenda. The evidence, definitions, scenario table, and code do not support a robust staffing recommendation.
