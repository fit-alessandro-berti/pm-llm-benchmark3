The write-up meets the required section structure and uses many real figures correctly (14.1% vs 3.3% complaints, 31% vs 9% cost, 68% fitting traces, R1/R2, 22% vs 5% and 18% vs 6% by segment, 26% repeat evidence, 96-hour dispatch-to-complaint edge, 140-hour unclear-defect median, 15–35% manual inflow, 6–18-hour gate). The three redesigns are distinct, sacrifices are stated, and the pilot includes a control and a downstream-shift check.

The reasoning is not reliable enough to score higher. The diagnosis reverses the auto-screen problem: outdated, non-segment-aware rules are too permissive (tightening them is what raises manual inflow 15–35%), yet the text says they force low-risk cases into manual review. Redesign A then contradicts itself—Premium low-risk may stay on fast approval, while the target statement keeps the fast path only for low-amount Standard claims and sends all Premium cases to manual review. It also treats a part-selection gate as proof of R2 evidence completeness; those are different controls. “Slight” delay understates a 6–18-hour add-on against an 8-hour Premium low-risk median and a 24-hour service-credit cliff.

Redesign B sets 12-hour combined review and 48-hour express-repair targets without a mechanism that beats the measured waits (repair-quote median 15 hours and p90 60, often too slow for Premium; customer approval median 21 hours and p90 80; part-price confirmation; two inspectors). One listed failure—that dropping fast replacement will drive high-value customers away—conflicts with the 22% vs 5% complaint gap. Redesign C is a generic scorer with arbitrary 30/70 bands, labels risk “Premium,” and reserves 20% of a review pool already stressed above 82% utilization. The comparison table asserts current-process dominance and several unsupported hour deltas, and several cells do not state a clear direction.
