The write-up follows the eight-section skeleton and reaches the right neighborhood: June–July fitness loss is mostly logging, the August policy outran the model, and staff noncompliance is not the lead explanation. Under a strict reading it still fails several explicit requirements and contradicts the supplied alignment table.

The evidence ledger has twelve rows and the right columns, but it never uses the directly-follows counts, the 40-case UI audit, or the omitted high-value attribute. Several attributions are loose: rising missing-resource rates are treated as support for an obsolete model, and stable appeals are treated as evidence against both noncompliance and model obsolescence.

The ranking does not match the staged deltas. Label mapping moves fitness from 32% to 58%, dropping `Queue Routed` moves it to 81%, and source-sequence ordering only then reaches 89%. Technical-event insertion is therefore about as large as the rename and much larger than the timestamp defect, yet it is ranked fifth at 55 while timestamping is third at 80. Stable appeals are listed as contradicting employee noncompliance and as contradicting an obsolete model. Unchanged outcomes do neither. The table also never separates “cause of the measured drop” from “remaining operational concern,” and several “discriminating tests” are repairs already reported in the alignment summary.

The causal graph misnames `recorded_labels`, sends recorded labels into the normative model, sends measured fitness into business outcomes, and omits technical events entirely. The explanation is about 145 words, short of the required 180–280. It claims the label map plus `Queue Routed` removal “proves” the drop was semantic and ordering rather than behavioral, and it treats the whole collapse as one measurement artifact. Model obsolescence is a different mechanism: the process really gained a sign-off and the tree was not updated.

The counterfactual table predicts that label mapping alone restores fitness above 80%. The given result is 58%. Adding sign-off to the model is also predicted to create a `Compliance Check -> Supervisor Sign-off` directly-follows edge; a model edit does not change the log’s directly-follows graph. Two of the eight rows are not hypotheses from the required set.

The four-phase program does not isolate one mechanism per phase. Label remapping and source ordering are not separate ablations, fitness and precision are named but not tied to a real method, and `pm4py.discover_variants`, `pm4py.alignment`, `pm4py.filter_events`, and `pm4py.log_files` are not the methods the reproduction section itself requires. The overpermissive-model risk is mentioned only in passing.

The code block is the clearest failure. It never calls `pm4py.parse_event_log_string`, never builds an August–September log, never runs alignment fitness or precision, and prints hardcoded 0.32 / 0.81 / 0.66 / 0.86 instead of computing them. `convert_to_petri_net` is treated as returning a single net. The candidate tree uses a nonexistent `Y` operator and places sign-off as an alternative to `Decision`, so it cannot accept the observed sign-off-then-decision traces or justify the 86% figure pasted beside it. `discover_dfg` is called on comma-separated strings. The EUR 50,000 caveat is printed, which is the one required point this section actually meets. The script is not executable.

The conclusion is inside the word range and gives IT, process owners, and compliance distinct actions, plus a warning not to claim systematic noncompliance. It still calls the preprocessed August–September 66% figure “raw,” folds the smaller ordering gain into “label mapping and technical-event removal,” and treats missing sign-off as a high-value compliance failure even though value was never joined to the traces. It again reads stable appeals as evidence that the process works and that checks may still be happening. The recommended model update does not carry the control-flow overgeneralization warning into the action.

The six governance rules cover the six requested topics and are the cleanest section. The semantic-contract rule still misses the actual failure mode, a stable activity name or alias when `Classify` became `Risk Screen`, and largely restates the technical-event rule.

Directionally competent, but factually inconsistent with the alignment ladder, causally mis-specified, and non-compliant on the reproduction code.
