The memo reaches the right strategic posture: reject national rollout of Redesign R, reject a full rollback, and keep the simple-house gains while restoring survey, permit, and activation-before-billing controls. All six required sections are present, most source statistics are transcribed correctly, and the evidence table actually separates columns. That is not enough to carry the analysis. Several claims a leadership team would act on are unsupported or internally inconsistent.

The headline economics are invented. Visible cost fell by EUR 23 on the average case, not “per simple case,” and nothing in the data yields “EUR ~1.5M” in annual savings. Pilot segment shares are then treated as national volume (“34% of national volume,” “47% of volume”) even though the pilot regions were richer in apartments and older buildings. Those two errors make the value case in the memo look quantified when it is not.

Causal attribution is similarly over-tight. The survey-skip rule was aimed at medium-risk addresses and is described as often wrong for apartments; the temporal gain for simple houses is on address validation to appointment scheduling. The memo credits accurate survey-skipping for the simple-house win and assigns the entire EUR 23 to skipped surveys. Post-billing activation, merged tasks, handover cuts, mix shift, and a shorter window are left out of that arithmetic. “Doubled” for manual activation (7% to 15%) and “significantly higher than EUR 207” are one-sided tightenings of incomplete figures. Legal non-compliance, churn, and coordinator morale are added without evidence. NPS (+21 to +17) and the SLA split (worse overall, better for simple houses) never appear, despite being direct inputs to a hybrid choice. Business and rural cases, 19% of the pilot and also worse on quality, drop out of the executive framing.

The fitness/precision account starts from the right definitions and then slips. Low precision means the discovered model allows too many unobserved combinations; it does not by itself show that a workflow engine lost its controls. Observed billing-before-activation and missing permits are alignment findings, and they are real, but they are not the definition of precision. Generalization (0.70 to 0.56) is ignored. The tail story leans on a general “above 90% utilization” queueing claim, while simple-house p90 improved by 8 days, which points to segment-specific rework and permit delay rather than one shared bottleneck.

Value of information is organized but not reliable. Segment and building-risk attributes are incomplete before week 3 of a 6-week pilot; that gap sits under the entire hybrid split and is never named. M1, the design that would unconfound survey skipping, is ranked near the bottom. M7 is described as if monitoring itself were a hard system block. M5 is ranked as a test of whether pods are warranted, then the recommendation immediately imposes 3-person pods. The false-negative gate is 5% in the ranking and 3.0% in the final table. Several rollout bars (p95 ≤ 35 days, complaints ≤ 5.5%, FTR ≥ 83%) are stricter than baseline and are overall metrics for a segment expansion decision.

The recommended direction is defensible. The memo as written is not yet a document management should use without correcting the fabricated savings, the national-mix error, the survey-skip causal story, and the contradictory measurement plan.
