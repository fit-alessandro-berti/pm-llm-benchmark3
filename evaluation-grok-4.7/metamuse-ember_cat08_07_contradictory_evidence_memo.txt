6.5

The memo is complete and strategically coherent, but it is not nearly flawless. Several decision-critical claims are inaccurate, internally inconsistent, or stronger than the evidence.

The required six-part structure is present. Alternative E is a defensible choice, national scaling and full rollback are correctly rejected, and the metric-conflict section explains fitness versus precision, median versus tail, handover count versus resilience, visible versus downstream cost, and segment heterogeneity with real process-mining content. The evidence table mostly keeps facts, interpretations, and assumptions apart, and it includes the right caveats on mix, missing attributes, sparse start times, and incomplete cost.

The final decision is not stable across sections. Section 1 suspends survey skipping for higher-risk segments, which implies simple houses keep it. The scaling paragraph then limits simple houses to coordinator ownership and merged validate/schedule. The pause paragraph adds a simple-house exception for post-billing activation while also requiring zero tolerance for billing before successful activation. Those are the same sequence, so the exception cancels itself. A reader cannot tell which variant management is being asked to deploy.

Component causality is overclaimed. Simple-house gains are package results. The pilot does not identify coordinator ownership or the merged task as the cause, nor show that those gains survive after survey skipping and post-billing activation are removed. Calling those two elements “proven” overstates identification.

The rework sentence is factually wrong. Manual activation rose from 7% to 15%, but second visits rose from 11% to 18%, which is not roughly a doubling. “Apartments and old buildings drive the tail” is also stated as fact without a contribution decomposition; business and rural p90s worsened too, and the pilot mix was already more apartment- and old-building-heavy.

The rollout gate is infeasible and ambiguous. A 6–8 week window cannot produce the required 60-day post-billing cost result. Comparison operators are missing on cost, complaints, first-time-right, and utilization, so “is baseline,” “are 6.5%,” and “sustained 85%” do not specify a pass rule. “Close to baseline” and “effectively zero” are not operational thresholds. A complaint bar of 6.5% would also accept a worse result than the 6.1% baseline.

Value-of-information ranking does not follow the stated criterion. Irreversible customer and compliance harm would put conformance monitoring and the survey-skip experiment above a cost estimate that cannot by itself authorize scaling. The recommendation then sequences rule validation before the randomized skip test, reversing its own rank order. Coordinator-absence testing is ranked last even though single-coordinator ownership is what the memo proposes to scale.

These are decision-specification and reasoning errors, not formatting nits. The analysis is strong enough to stay above a failing grade, but the contradictions and overclaims keep it well below an excellent one.
