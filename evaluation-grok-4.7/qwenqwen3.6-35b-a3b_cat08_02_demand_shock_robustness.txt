5.5

The write-up has the required seven sections, four scenario types, a complete trade-off table, conditional policies, and a leading-versus-lagging split. It correctly avoids blanket capacity adds and blanket urgent-first dispatch, and it is right that cross-training technical validators into accession correction would worsen the station already at 101% utilization. Those merits do not offset several process-logic errors.

The bottleneck story is inverted in the place it matters most. Technical validation is upstream of medical validation. An evening medical shift does not “saturate technical validation,” and relieving medical validation does not move the constraint backward onto tech. In the peak log the medical queue is already 22h and the specialist queue 36h, and medical validators are required for Urgent and Complex-panel cases. With Urgent’s penalty at 18h, median 17h and p90 33h, that downstream queue is the binding customer constraint. Treating 101% utilization as automatically tighter than a 22h or 36h queue misreads the evidence. The same reversal reappears in the I5 row, the package failure mode, and the residual-risk claim that extra valid flow piles up at pre-analytical and preparation rather than at assay and technical validation.

Process-mining use is thin. The frequency DFG shows mechanisms the redesign never measures: batch-wait share rising from about 4% to about 13%, assay repeats from about 7% to about 15% of assay outcomes, technical-validation outflow to medical validation rising from about 25% to about 42%, and urgent batch bypass falling from 74% to 58%. I6 is credited with cutting overall rework from 11.8% to under 5%. Pre-analytical rejection cannot do that. Peak rework is driven at least as much by assay repeats, which cluster on M2 and repeat-after-failure cases, as by recollection.

The trade-off signs are not reliable. Larger batches are marked positive for Complex-panel cases even though only Urgent cases bypass batch wait, and Batch wait → Assay run is already 9h / 22h. I5 is marked negative for Routine because of a “resource shift,” but the intervention adds a temporary medical shift and most Routine cases release at technical validation. I6’s stated next bottleneck, “pre-analytical flow increases,” is the opposite of what an early reject does. I7 names the same station it smooths, so two recommended interventions never identify a downstream station they can overload.

Scenario policies are mis-calibrated to the given peak. Urgent mix above 25%, recollection above 10%, and accession queue above 12h are already true in the observed peak (Urgent 29%, accession median 14h, recollection already near 8–9%). Those rules would stay on for the whole peak, so they are not scenario switches. P1 then puts Urgent and Complex-panel cases in one fast lane, which is the simultaneous prioritization the case mix cannot support: complex work needs multiple assays and specialist review and will consume the lane Urgent needs for an 18h release. P2 stops M2, the faster machine, and sends the whole book to a pool already at 98% without weighing extra repeats against lost runtime.

The dashboard threshold for urgent pipeline age, over 10h, sits below the normal Urgent median of 11h, so it would alarm in the stable regime. Daily leading indicators also omit the signals the peak actually moves: case-mix share, assay queue, medical-validation queue, and specialist queue.

The result is a coherent memo with the right section contract and some sound local choices, undermined by a reversed flow constraint, over-claimed rework effects, and triggers and table cells that contradict the logs.
