6.5

The design correctly refuses the biased 21,600-row binary problem and keeps rejects, withdrawals, transfers, and open cases out of a fake “non-default” class. The nine required sections are present, the cohort counts partition all 48,000 cases, and the leakage table drops the obvious post-decision fields. It is still not a statistically tight study: the estimand, the metrics, the maturity rules, and the skeleton do not describe the same analysis.

The primary estimand is not identified as written. \(F_1(90\mid x)=P(A_i<\infty,\,D_i\le 90,\,C_i>A_i\mid X_i)\) has no finite horizon on activation. With follow-up ending on 15 February 2026, “eventually activated, then default within 90 days” depends on tail behavior after the extraction date. The “90” is time since activation, not time since prediction, so this is not a cause-specific cumulative incidence on a declared clock. \(C_i>A_i\) is also redundant if \(C_i\) exists only when activation does not occur. Calling 90-day non-default an absorbing competing event is the wrong survival object: survival past a fixed horizon is administrative end of follow-up, not a second event time racing default.

Prediction time is inconsistent for elapsed-time prefixes. Setting it at start plus 7 days, while dropping events at the earlier decision, scores cases whose rejection, withdrawal, transfer, or acceptance is already known. Fixed-length pre-decision prefixes are the coherent early-warning rule; the elapsed-time rule is not.

The flow table adds to 48,000 but does not do the taxonomy it was asked for. The 3,600 immature activated accounts remain one mixed bin of “censored or missing,” so ordinary administrative censoring, the 7–35 day feed lag, and permanently missing partner feeds are not separated. Unlinked transfers, about 40% of 2,900, are the selectively unobserved group and are not counted as such. Data-quality exclusions are announced and then absent from the flow.

Censoring strategy is directionally right and then blurs estimands. Treating transfer as censored, or reweighting the 60% linkage, does not estimate the same platform-process risk as the primary competing-event analysis; independent censoring at transfer is a counterfactual assumption and should be labeled as one. Tipping-point analysis for permanent missingness is appropriate. Assumptions are only split into partially testable and untestable; calendar censoring and the stated label delay are directly checkable, and conditional independent censoring for the primary estimator is never stated.

Prefix and feature design has smaller but real leaks. Historical default or activation rates “from the training period only” still use later training outcomes at earlier prediction times. Variant vocabularies are not fit as-of or on train folds. Queue state, waiting time, and workload are missing despite resource/queue features being required. `risk_score_asof` from pre-cutoff rows fails if the warehouse has already overwritten every row with the latest score, which is the failure mode the audit itself names. `policy_epoch` and `migration_epoch` have no variance inside training, so they cannot be learned and cannot explain the shift.

Validation metrics score a different target from section 1. IPCW AUC and Brier “at 90 days after activation,” and calibration against Aalen–Johansen on that clock, are defined only for activated accounts and match the alternative conditional estimand. The composite needs a horizon from prediction or start, a competing-risk score, and a censoring model that does not treat informative partner missingness as ordinary censoring. The proposed test slice, starts on or after 15 October 2025 evaluated at 15 February 2026, is exactly the slice whose 90-day outcomes plus feed lag mostly cannot have matured. A random split is rightly rejected, but this temporal split confounds policy, migration, and label incompleteness and never states a maturity cutoff.

Section 8 is the strongest part, but it still understates the decision meaning of the chosen score. \(P(\text{activate and default})\) ranks likely rejects as low risk even when \(P(\text{default}\mid\text{activation})\) is high, so it is a poor underwriting early-warning probability. The section also does not limit any claim about post-October performance.

The skeleton does not implement the paper design. `get_prefixes_from_log` is called on the full post-outcome log, discarded, and hidden in `try/except: pass`, while features come from a separate `groupby().head(5)`. Label maturity as of a cutoff is not built; `extract_date` only appears in a `permanent_missing` flag that requires `label_available_at` to be present and already past, which misses a feed that never arrives. `Censored` is then a hard class in `y`, so a model fit on that label learns the extraction regime, especially in the late test split. Sorting ties by `org:resource` contradicts the stated original-row-order rule. `len(dfg[0])` counts DFG edges, not nodes. The closing comment points at a person-period hazard model that this label construction would not feed.
