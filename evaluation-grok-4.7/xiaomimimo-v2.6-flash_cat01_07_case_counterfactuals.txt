The answer matches the required structure and gets the main qualitative distinctions right: timing-only versus routing versus decision-policy change, four-eyes and compliance order, earlier readiness versus earlier completion, and why suppressing rework is not a pure speedup. Those strengths are undercut by repeated quantitative and logical errors in the results that the task is specifically testing.

The Monday K-337 bound is arithmetically wrong. Monday 11:00 to the next Monday 09:00 is about 166 hours, not 190, so the saving versus the factual ~214 hours is about 48 hours, not 24. That incorrect 24-to-111-hour range is reused in the table and derivation. In K-219, the same D2 start is described as both about 4 hours earlier and about 11 hours earlier than D1’s Tuesday 09:30 start; the clock times support only the latter. The outcome column also uses “cycle time” for savings, and within K-219 the sign flips between scenarios.

The 100-case analysis covers every requested interference theme, but the capacity math is unreliable. It treats 400 hours of C1 review as one week’s capacity, says D1 would sit idle when the policy applies only to similar handwritten cases, and converts a 1.5–3.5-hour earlier arrival into 150–350 hours of extra assessment work. “2 resources × 2.5 h → 20 cases/day” does not follow. The pilot power claim is also wrong: a 4-hour effect with SD 3 and 15 per arm is far more than 60% power, and same-week randomization ignores the queue contamination the case is about. Invented rates (about 65–75%, 15%, 5%) are stated more precisely than the evidence supports.
