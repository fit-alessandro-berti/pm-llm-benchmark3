7.8

The memo is a strong, evidence-tied advisory piece: three genuinely different interventions (routing gates, evidence capture, fulfillment accuracy), explicit sacrifices, segment splits, and an experiment plan that watches final resolution rather than first close. It is not nearly flawless.

The clearest factual slip is in the diagnosis: Premium low-risk complaints are described as “lower on manual (6% vs 3%).” The data are 6% fast and 3% manual, so the parenthetical reverses the comparison even though the surrounding sentence points the right way. That is an accuracy and clarity failure on a segment the redesigns treat as speed-critical.

Process-mining precision is looser than the prose suggests. Chaining `Auto 3600 → Fast approval 2550 → Dispatch 2300 → Close 2320` presents directly-follows edge counts as one path frequency. Those edges do not identify the same cases: fast approval also closes directly (350) and complains (140), and dispatch-to-close (2320) is not the continuation of the 2300 fast-approval dispatches (escalation also feeds dispatch; 260 dispatches complain). The qualitative “high-volume corridor” claim holds; the path arithmetic does not.

Compliance controls are collapsed. R1 allows manual review or fraud review before fast approval above EUR 400. The alignment finding is specifically dispatch without risk review on high-value devices. Redesign A treats manual eligibility and fraud/risk as substitutes for both, so a high-value claim can still be dispatched with no risk review and still be described as guardrailed.

The Premium repair-quote problem is aimed at the wrong lever. The temporal deviation is `Manual eligibility review → Repair quote`, and the stated cause is waiting for part-price confirmation among three technicians. A Premium fast-lane in eligibility review does not shorten that wait. Meanwhile the table marks the current process “dominated? yes,” which contradicts both the instruction not to claim Pareto dominance from these data and the memo’s own point that Premium low-risk must keep an ~8h path to avoid 24-hour service credits. Dominance here is conditional and segment-specific; “yes” overclaims.

Smaller gaps: calling the manual-inflow increase “small” sits badly next to the stated +15–35% and the 82% utilization cliff; an evidence stage in front of every case can threaten that same Premium 24-hour window and is underweighted in Redesign B’s sacrifices; the pilot sequence does not isolate interference (Pilot 1 shrinks the fast-approved high-value population that Pilot 2 then studies). The 30–45% evidence reduction is appropriately used as a prompt-given expectation, not a measured result, and the stop/go ranges stay qualitative. Those strengths keep the score well above a merely competent generic redesign, but the inverted rate, the DFG chaining, the merged control rules, and the dominance claim are enough to keep it out of the top band.
