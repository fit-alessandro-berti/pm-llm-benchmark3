The required seven sections and the A–L table are present, and the broad judgment that skipping authorization and unscanned dispatch are unacceptable is sound. The write-up still misreads the event log, invents figures, and contradicts its own control policy.

The timeline treats Day 0 morning as a total stoppage with no processing, which the log does not show. It then says the pharmacy fill rate “recovers to 61%” in partial recovery. That 61% is the manual-phase rate, and no later fill rate is reported. Stabilization is described as if dispatch-without-scan and phone proof of delivery were still the live paths. The stabilization log has returned to authorization, reservation, pick, quality release, dispatch, and proof of delivery, with proof-of-delivery remediation and audit remediation beside it. Fitness and fitting-trace rates (0.96/91%, 0.71/44%, 0.82/59%, 0.90/76%) are unused, so the conformance collapse and incomplete rebound never become evidence.

The activity counts are systematically misassigned. The hospital fast lane is 1,000 cases (920 continuing), while 1,600 is the separate skip-authorization branch; the table attributes those 1,600 skips to the fast lane. Skip-path cases are called shipped, although only 1,500 reach the snapshot check. Snapshot deferrals of 580 are blamed on stale inventory even though the edge is the noncritical-deferral path. All 3,600 manual packs are treated as post-hoc validation work. The 2,600 figure is the phone-proof edge, while inflows to unscanned dispatch are 1,800 plus 1,450. The 2,200 spreadsheet invoice holds are called remediation, which is a later stabilization activity. The 2,300 audit figure is an inflow over the period, and 2,100 of those reach the end; it is not a measured queue of 2,300 open cases.

Causal claims run against the clock. Hospital median cycle time moved from 8 hours to 10 hours, so critical orders did not ship faster. Temporary release is credited with moving controlled-substance dual-control gaps from 2.9% to 0.5%. That 2.9% occurred while temporary release was in use, and 0.5% appears only after systems return. Most packs still bypassed temporary release, and 1,450 released orders still went to unscanned dispatch. Pharmacy median time of 28 hours is offered as recovery evidence against a 23-hour baseline. Baseline and stabilization hospital fill rates of 99% and 98% are not in the record. A 50% remediation reduction and a 1%-of-revenue trigger are likewise unsupported. “Dual-control missing” is repeatedly upgraded to “violations.”

Classification does not hold together. A and J are called resilient while the same answer says authorization was bypassed and priority rules were informal. H is a weak compensating record, especially for controlled drugs, yet it is grouped with the two hard skips. K and L are absent from the resilience-versus-noncompliance classification, and L is still described as having restored traceability for 2,300 cases. The fairness harm is placed on hospitals, whereas the measured damage is pharmacy fill at 61%, cancellations at 7.8%, and much worse pharmacy and wholesale cycle times. Recall-sensitive product risk is barely carried. Indicator targets sit near the still-elevated stabilization rates rather than the baseline.

The recovery design contains the required headings, then reopens the failure. Tier 3 may “defer or skip” authorization, inventory, pick, and traceability, including for wholesale orders that can still be cold-chain, controlled, or recall-sensitive. That conflicts with retiring those shortcuts and with the later ban. “Restore order intake first” repeats the partial-recovery failure, when intake returned while inventory and scanning were still unreliable. “Daily recounts every 6 hours” is internally inconsistent, and blockchain-style hashing does not close the missing quality release, temperature record, dual control, or scan.
