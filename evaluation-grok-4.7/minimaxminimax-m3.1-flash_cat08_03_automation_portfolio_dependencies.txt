The final recommendation is coherent and well grounded: Portfolio P (A2, A4, A6, A12) respects the four-module cap, puts a real control module in the first wave, keeps A11 and other downstream automation behind prerequisites, and explains cycle time, waits, rework, handovers, workload shift, and control risk with the given DFG edges and resource constraints. The two-deep-integration limit is applied explicitly, and compliance accountability is not treated as automated away.

The central flaw is a repeated misreading of the artifacts. “Already digital for 72% of cases” belongs to Receive application, not Register. Register still occurs on 11,880 cases at 8 minutes, and it is position 2 in 98% of cases. Using the 72% figure to downgrade A1’s direct labor scope in the matrix, Portfolio Q’s effects, and the final trade-off is incorrect. The separate claim that an 8-minute touch and a 1.5-hour wait are not the dominant delays is sound, but the headline argument is partly carried by the wrong premise.

A7 is also underweighted relative to the stated problem. A 58-hour external wait, 19% lateness, and a hard packaging dependency is the clearest synchronization constraint, yet it ranks ninth, below A3 (4-hour KYC wait and severe false-negative risk). Portfolio B includes it, but the ranking still leans too far toward feasibility and away from critical-path delay.

Section 4 covers all six effect dimensions and cites activities and DFG counts, but product and exposure variants are thin inside those effects (Equipment 71% versus Working-capital 18%, and High-exposure month-end behavior, are mostly elsewhere). The compliance-first set also leads with A3, the module with the worst false-negative exposure, even though that risk is later flagged.
