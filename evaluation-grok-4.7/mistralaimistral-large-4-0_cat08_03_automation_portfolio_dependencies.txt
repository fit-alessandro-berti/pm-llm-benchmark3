The write-up covers every required section, uses most directly-follows and control figures correctly, and rightly refuses to lead with registration. It is not a reliable portfolio recommendation. Several claims that drive the ranking, the phasing, and the control case are inaccurate or internally inconsistent.

The resource model is wrong in a way that repeats. KYC check sits in Compliance ops. The 3 FTE are compliance specialists, and the network only shows a handover from KYC ops onto two seniors. A3 is still described as relieving those 3 FTE, and the compensating control then says every hit must be routed to them. That cannot both reduce specialist load and send an 11,500-case activity to three people. A6 is likewise said to “relieve 9 FTE,” but those analysts also perform financial-document review and the much larger credit-analysis load. Normalization frees only a slice of that pool.

The ranking does not follow the paper’s own process logic. Aggregate delay is dominated by customer signing (44h, 7,300 cases), credit-analysis wait (28h), contract preparation (22h), financial-document review (18h), and external valuation (58h). A10 is scored as only a medium process benefit and ranked 10th. A7, the explicit synchronization point, is ranked 9th, below A1, which the text itself calls not process-optimal. A5 is rated a low downstream-bottleneck risk even though faster document completion raises arrivals into the analyst pool, the same mechanism used to mark A1 high risk. A12 is ranked 4th and treated as fixing provisional signing, four-eyes breaches, and contract loops. The observed remediation edge is the contract-preparation loop (650 / 510). Four-eyes is a segregation failure between credit analysis and committee packaging during the month-end rush. A remediation workbench whose own note is that it may expose policy ambiguity is not a preventive control on either signing or four-eyes.

Section 2 asserts that A6 and A4 are the two deep core-banking integrations and that A5 is light and A12 medium. Nothing in the evidence establishes that. Loan activation, contract generation, and registration are at least as plausible core-banking integrations. That unsupported assignment becomes the rollout skeleton. Section 2 also says those two deep integrations occupy the first six months; section 6 starts them only in months 3–6.

The effects analysis quantifies results the evidence cannot support: entity-mismatch wait to under 8 hours, normalization wait to under 4 hours, external valuation from 58 hours to under 24 hours by triage, normalization accuracy of 95%, provisional signing down 80%, four-eyes under 1%, and end-to-end cycle time down 25%. A7 automates a 20-minute internal request, not the external wait. The recommended set also leaves both longest waits, signing and valuation, untouched, so the 25% cycle-time gate is not credible, especially for equipment finance, where valuation is required in 71% of cases.

The recommended portfolio is never given the section 4 treatment. Its trade-off says it sacrifices A1 and A2 to avoid a faster conveyor into a jam, but it does not own the larger sacrifice of A7 and A10. A4 is over-read from the smaller edge into contract preparation (380) rather than the main return to KYC (1,160). Shipping A5 alone for a quarter conflicts with the overload thesis and is not gated on analyst utilization. A12 arrives only in phase 3, so the first live wave does not strengthen controls. Variant behavior is thin: weekly high-exposure committee cadence, judgment-heavy credit analysis, concurrency of KYC and document review, and product-specific critical paths do not change the portfolio. A6 extraction error feeding credit decisions and committee packs is also underweighted, while A8 is penalized for the same garbage-in problem.

Portfolio C puts contract generation ahead of reliable KYC and normalized figures. The specialist, integration, and control-mechanism errors are material, not cosmetic, so this stays well short of a defensible first-year decision.
