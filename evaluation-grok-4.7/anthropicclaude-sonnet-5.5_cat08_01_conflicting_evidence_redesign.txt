The diagnosis correctly locates the speed/cost versus quality/compliance conflict in segment mix, and the three redesigns are genuinely different control points rather than Lean slogans or a single “review everything” prescription. Fitness 0.91 versus 68% fitting traces, the three move-on-log deviations, R1–R3, the 14.1% versus 3.3% rework split, the 24-hour Premium credit threshold, the 82% review-utilization cliff, idle inspectors, and the Monday fraud bottleneck are all used in the right places. Sacrifices are explicit, dominance is refused, and the pilot sequence checks whether harm is merely pushed downstream. That architecture is strong. The score is held down by repeated process-mining unit errors and a few unsupported magnitudes.

The performance DFG is misread. Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval is mean 1.0 hours and median 0.4 hours. Calling that hop a median of about 1 hour swaps the two statistics on a cited artifact. Approval → dispatch at “~2 hours” matches the 1.8-hour median and is fine.

DFG frequencies are then treated as case counts in the evidence loop, despite the answer’s own 26% repeat rate. Request missing evidence has 1,430 entries and 320 abandonments; “22% abandonment” divides events by events. Repeats mean unique cases are fewer than 1,430, so the case abandonment rate is higher than 22%. The same slip produces “~370 cases” with two or more requests (26% of 1,430 entries) and the derived “110–170” avoided repeats. Those figures size Redesign B’s benefit and are not directional in a harmless way: the denominator is the wrong object.

“Roughly 46% of reviewer workload is rework-induced” over-interprets the inflows. Manual review’s 2,600 entries are 1,400 from intake, 890 from evidence, and 310 from complaints. The 310 are reopens. The 890 are not. Auto screen sends 650 cases to evidence, and evidence sends 890 on to manual review, so a large share of that 890 is the first manual review, not a repeated one. Manual review only sends 780 cases back to evidence, and many of those abandon or go to fraud instead of returning. Workload share of evidence handoffs is about one third; rework share is smaller and not identified by this graph. The later claim that this headroom makes Redesign A’s +210 to +490 inflow “survivable” inherits that inflation. Applying the prompt’s 15–35% inflow increase only to the 1,400 first-pass entries is one possible reading, not a measured one.

Two smaller attribution slips sit beside those. “Of those [3,600 auto-screened cases] … another 240 reach Fast approval via fraud” scopes the 240 as if they all came from the auto cohort; fraud review also receives 360 cases from manual review and 220 from evidence. All 260 dispatch → complaint events are likewise charged to Fast approval, although escalation also feeds dispatch. The second error is small; the first is a real scoping mistake. The data-caveat list is also selective: dispatch 2,365 versus 2,580 and manual review 2,600 versus 1,400 are flagged, while fraud review 980 versus 760 is not, even though that gap is large enough to caution against any exact path share.

Redesign B says downstream logic “stays as today” and then says R1/R2 become explicit preconditions. The following clause only implements an evidence-complete flag, which is R2. R1 is a different rule: value above EUR 400 needs manual or fraud review before Fast approval. Enforcing R1 would move high-value volume into the constrained review queue and would change the 18–22% fast-path complaint rates that B says it leaves untouched. The write-up holds both positions.

Redesign C’s automated compatibility check “in minutes” is outside the evidence. The prompt’s lightweight pre-dispatch gate adds 6–18 hours depending on resource design. A minutes-long automated gate may be a reasonable invention, but it is presented as if it sits inside that evidence, and it is what keeps Premium accessories inside 24 hours. The “26–38 hour” high-value cycle uses the 20-hour Premium fast-path base; Standard high-value starts at 28 hours, so the same gate is 34–46 hours. Both ranges are labeled as hypotheses, which limits the damage, but the illustration picks the favorable base.

The comparison table, as rendered, has empty sign slots (“slower … path ()”, “review labor (0/)”), so two required cells do not actually state a direction. Week-or-case alternation for the first pilot also fights the answer’s own calendar finding: fraud review is already distorted on Mondays and after campaigns, and week blocks alias that pattern.

What keeps this well above a mid-score is the segment logic. Premium accessories are not forced onto a 46-hour manual path that breaches the credit threshold for a 3-point complaint gap and a EUR 95 wrong-part exposure. High-value fast approval is treated as the actual compliance and EUR 410 problem. Standard accessories are left on a visible 72-hour tolerance. The experiment gates the load-adding rule change on measured review headroom, requires complaint follow-up past the 150–180 hour tail, and separates provisional from final close. Those are the right controls. The answer is operationally sophisticated and numerically loose exactly where a process-mining argument has to be tight.
