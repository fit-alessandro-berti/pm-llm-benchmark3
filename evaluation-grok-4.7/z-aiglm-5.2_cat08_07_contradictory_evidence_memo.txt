6.4

The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision and covers every required section, but it is not nearly flawless. Several errors sit on the exact points the task tests: process-mining interpretation, fact versus inference, and value of information.

The headline choice is sound. National scaling and a full rollback are rejected for the right reasons, simple low-risk houses are treated as the only clear beneficiary, and survey skipping plus billing-before-activation are not allowed to ride along unexamined. Segment figures, handover reduction, complaint and second-visit rates, and the main alignment findings are mostly used correctly.

The fitness/precision account is wrong in a way that repeats across the memo, the evidence table, and the conflict section. Precision of 0.51 means the discovered model permits behavior not seen in the log. The write-up instead treats it as proof that the log itself is chaotic and that the model “allows the process to do anything.” Generalization falling from 0.70 to 0.56 is never used. That is a central process-mining miss, not a wording nit.

Facts are also bent. The 34% simple-house share is written as if it were the handover reduction and then reused as national volume, despite pilot regions having more apartments and older buildings. Overall p95 (45 to 62 days) is attached to apartments and old buildings. Visible cost savings and customer satisfaction are assigned to simple houses without segment cost or NPS. Alternative C is misread as suspending merged validate-and-schedule. “Unambiguous” and “proven” overstate a six-week, non-randomized pilot whose risk attributes are missing before week 3.

The recommendation contradicts itself. Other segments are reverted to the baseline process, while coordinator ownership is scaled and a shared-team backup is added as if that backup fixes 93% utilization. Absence cover does not remove overload, and that backup is adopted before the comparison that would test it. Thresholds of 95% rule accuracy, apartment complaints under 7%, and apartment p90 under 35 days are unanchored to baseline segment levels or to the cost of errors.

The value-of-information ranking is not really value of information. M1, the randomized test of survey skipping, is placed near the bottom because it is slower, not because it would change the decision less. M7 is credited with explaining low precision, which monitoring two deviations cannot do. The largest threats to the decision—region mix, incomplete segment attributes, cost censored after week 4, a 30-day complaint metric inside a six-week window, and sparse start timestamps—are largely left out of the assumptions column. Survey-team utilization falling to 52% and second-visit utilization rising to 92% never enter the action.
