3.5

The write-up keeps the nine required headings and names the right method families, but the estimands, censoring logic, and code are wrong in ways that would change the analysis.

Section 1 never says how a resolution at 76 hours, an open case at 92 hours, a withdrawal at 27 hours, and a transfer at 68 hours enter each estimand. “Not resolved by 72 hours” is then defined as withdrawal, rejection, transfer, or still open, which mixes early competing exits with remaining unresolved at the horizon. The second estimand is titled as resolution after 72 hours and described as resolution within 72 hours. Withdrawal and rejection are called SLA failures even though a withdrawal at 27 hours has already left the process inside the window. Censoring is described as “still open at 72 hours,” which misses open cases whose age is below 72 and are censored at that earlier age.

The encoding table pastes “right censoring if still open at 72h” onto resolved, rejected, withdrawn, and transferred rows. Sensitivity alternatives are “same as above.” Transfer-as-censoring is described as if those cases were transferred at 72 hours. A multi-state branch is said to support analysis of outcomes after transfer, although post-transfer resolution is unobserved. Open cases above versus below 72 hours are not separated into already-observed non-terminal passage of the SLA versus still-in-risk-set censoring.

Section 3 lists S01–S15 as completed resolved cases. That list includes open, rejected, withdrawn, and transferred cohort-A cases and omits cohort B. Informative censoring is described as open cases still under observation, which is ordinary right censoring when the cutoff is independent of the latent time. The required points are missing: cohort imbalance and non-random, incomplete policy rollout, and the difference between active cases younger than 72 hours (S10, S15, S24, S29, S31) and those already past 72 without a terminal status (S09, S11, S25, S26). Long gaps (S11, S25, S26) are not used.

Kaplan–Meier is recommended as the primary method because it “handles competing risks.” It does not; it treats competing events as censoring and estimates a cause-specific quantity. The same section then says to avoid Kaplan–Meier analyses that fail to account for competing risks. Landmarking is described as analysis after 72 hours or after transfer, rather than as a way to condition on history available at a landmark without immortal time. Restricted mean survival time is called restricted mean time loss. The multi-state model required in the comparison is not actually compared, and nothing is justified from n = 32, sparse priority-by-cohort cells, or the share of open and competing cases.

Section 5 labels current stage, rework count, and escalation timing as baseline covariates that are stable over time. Those are post-origin, time-varying, and, for escalation, post-treatment relative to the cohort policy. Time from escalation to resolution is an outcome. The final inactivity gap is a summary up to the event or cutoff. The diagram sends resolve, withdraw, reject, and transfer only through Escalate, omits AutoCheck and Rework, and cannot represent the observed prefixes, including cohort B’s Submit→AutoCheck→Screen order and resolution or rejection directly from Screen or Review.

Section 6 repeats transfer scenarios only. “Treated as censoring” is then described as excluding transfers. Withdrawal versus rejection, gap-driven informative censoring, and cohort-policy compliance are not varied. The statements about what could reverse do not name a conclusion computed from these cases.

Operational advice gives an arbitrary 0.3 alert, treats priority like a fairness group, and moves from scores to process changes. It does not separate operational success (Resolve) from the 72-hour clock, and it does not define alerts as remaining-time risk for open cases given current age and prefix. Calibration at this sample size is asserted rather than designed.

The code keeps only a corrupted 15-row cohort-A extract: priorities are reassigned, S12’s duration becomes 0, gaps are overwritten, and prefix, rework, escalation, and S16–S32 are absent. It encodes one resolved-versus-censored flag, so withdrawal, rejection, and transfer are silent censorings; the status map looks up `transfer` while the data say `transferred`. There is no second estimand, no risk-set or cumulative-incidence table, no cohort or priority stratification, and no sensitivity implementation. Prefix parsing calls columns that were never created, so it fails, and PM4Py (`parse_event_log_string`, `get_variants` or prefixes) is absent. lifelines is named with no fallback. That both invents observations and misses the required control-flow step.

Section 9 does list timestamps, external transfer outcomes, workload, assignment, and rollout compliance. It still omits the observation-cutoff and case-start dates as the censoring mechanism, and it does not tie each gap to which estimand is currently unidentified.
