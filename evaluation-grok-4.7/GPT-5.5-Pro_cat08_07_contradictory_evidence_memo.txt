7.5

The memo reaches a defensible hybrid decision and covers every required section, but it is not nearly flawless. Several technical, evidentiary, and internal-consistency problems keep it well below a top score.

The fitness-versus-precision explanation is partly wrong. Low precision means the discovered model allows unobserved combinations. The mining note limits that claim to skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start. The answer adds permit absence and complaint escalation to that list. Those two are alignment deviations of a different kind: missing permit checks are move-on-model (the model expects a check the log lacks), and complaint escalation after billing is move-on-log. Both hurt fitness. Putting them in the “model permits too much” list blurs the exact conflict this section had to explain. The business sentence about a fast path with weak guardrails is still sound.

The decision scope drifts. The memo limits coordinator ownership to simple low-risk houses. The final recommendation then adopts “C-style” ownership more broadly, with backup coverage and workload caps, while restricting merged validate/schedule to simple houses. Those are different operating models. An executive cannot tell which one was approved.

Evidence strength is overstated in places. Alternative D calls the simple-house gains “proven.” They are observed in a non-randomized, six-week, four-region pilot whose segment and building-risk attributes are incomplete before week 3. The table’s phrase “missing early pilot attributes” never becomes a constraint on the rollout that depends on those labels. The memo also treats estimated full cost as worse without the prompt’s own limit: complaint-handling cost is missing after week 4, and EUR 198 versus EUR 207 is a small, incomplete gap.

Comparability is under-examined. Pilot p95 cycle time is 62 days inside a six-week pilot window, against a ten-week baseline. That raises truncation and follow-up bias for the tail, which is central to rejecting national scale. Pilot regions also contain more apartments and older buildings than the baseline. That mix point appears only as an SLA-row assumption. It belongs in the median-versus-tail explanation: a harder mix makes the median gain more credible and the aggregate tail harder to attribute solely to Redesign R. Within-segment deterioration still supports not scaling R unchanged.

The value-of-information ranking is usable but slightly circular. M6 is framed so a weak rule only suspends skipping outside simple houses, even though bad risk labels could undermine the immediate simple-house fast path. M7 is described as something to do “before any rollout” after the recommendation has already rolled out part of R. M5, which could reverse coordinator ownership, sits below start-timestamp instrumentation, which mostly refines delay diagnosis.

Smaller defects add up. “Doubled” for manual activation is 7% to 15%. The complaint-row “assumption” is about cost-data coverage, not about the complaint rate. “Accountability improved” is design intent, not a measured result. The handover-of-work network and the working-together network are collapsed into “the network” in the memo. The Installation visit → Activation test drop from 78% to 60% is never used. Rollout thresholds end at “agreed tolerances” and “overload,” with no numeric bar for p90, first-time-right, complaints, or 60-day cost.

What holds the score up is the decision itself. National scale and full rollback are both rejected for the right reasons. Segment winners and losers are distinguished. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are mostly separated. All five metric conflicts are addressed, and M1–M8 are tied to decisions. The remaining gap is precision under incomplete evidence, not the direction of the recommendation.
