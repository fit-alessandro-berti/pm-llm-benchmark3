5.5

The write-up has the right overall shape: availability is not use, rollout was targeted, a single “reduces cycle time by X%” claim is not identified, median and p90 are not the same estimand, clusters are too few, and expansion should carry an evaluation. Those strengths are undercut by arithmetic and window choices that change the substantive findings, by identification claims that outrun the design, and by code that does not reproduce the reported analysis.

The quantitative spine is not reliable. Alpha’s January–March p90 average is 98.5 hours, not 95.1, so the late-window p90 change is about +7%, not +10.9%. Delta’s pre escalation average is about 7.5, not 7.9; Epsilon’s is about 8.7, not 9.3, and July–August escalation is 10.8, not 10.6. Alpha’s April–June CSAT average is about 3.84, not 3.88. Beta’s early rework average is about 11.9, not 11.6. The dose-response coefficients (−0.18 hours per fast-path point, and the other rounded terms) are not derived in the text and are not what the later script estimates. The “~5%” chance statement and the “50% of the gain is reversion” bound are invented, not calculated. Scenario A is labeled a null and then concludes that an effect of at least 2% exists.

The pre/post window reverses Gamma and flatters Alpha. Gamma adopts in July, but the baseline stops in March, before the May complexity shock. From May–June to July–August, Gamma’s median falls from about 45.7 to 37.2 hours and p90 from about 100.5 to 84.9; rework falls from 14.6 to 12.6. The report instead says Gamma’s median is essentially flat (−0.8%), p90 worsens by about 10%, and rework barely rises. That is the opposite of the adoption-aligned contrast. Alpha’s full post period (April–August) has a median of about 48.5 versus 48.9 pre-adoption, not a 6.7% improvement. July–August is the best slice, and it is also when never-treated branches recover from the May–June peak. Pairing adopters with Delta, the control that deteriorated most, further inflates the percentage-change “DiD,” which is not a difference in levels.

The clearest process facts are missed. Escalation rises by exactly 2.0 points in March in every branch, including Delta and Epsilon, when the fraud desk starts. Rework jumps by roughly 2–3 points in May in every branch, treated or not. Those common shocks, not automation timing, drive much of the pre/post escalation and tail movement. The report still calls a p90 worsening the most robust automation finding at confidence 65, and an escalation increase “likely causal” and “statistically robust” at confidence 70, with no test and five clusters.

Identification is looser than the tables imply. Several estimands are marked identifiable when the data only support descriptive contrasts. Fast-path share is an endogenous intensity, and there is no instrument, despite the IV language. Stuffing event-time dummies and fast-path share into one regression does not separately identify availability, intensity, and dynamics. Adjusting for post-rollout arrivals, FTE, and complexity is treated as helpful control, not as a possible bad control. “Downward bias” is used both for overstated benefit and for masked benefit. Regression to the mean from selecting slow branches would make automation look better; the selection row states that direction and then labels it downward. Relative-time “leads” are described as month +1 and +2 pre-adoption. Three pre-months cannot support the quadratic regression-discontinuity-in-time alternative. Synthetic control with two donors is appropriately doubted, but the primary design still leans on never-treated branches whose complexity and cycle-time paths are not parallel, and it does not use Gamma’s not-yet-treated months as the staggered comparison for Alpha and Beta.

The mechanism table’s beneficial-versus-harmful signatures are often unreadable because the direction is missing. Gamma’s June FTE loss is placed after the July go-live. The claim ladder’s “Observed” bullet asserts a 2–7% median improvement and an offsetting effect, which is an interpretation and does not match Beta or Alpha’s full post window. The conditional causal claim assigns a 3–6% effect to simple cases, which were never measured. Section 1 runs slightly past the word cap; the rollout section is far beyond 250–400 words.

The recommendation, embedded evaluation rather than an immediate X% rollout, is the right family of decision. The design is not. A four-week 50/50 case split, then full crossover, contradicts the months-long ramp the report itself documents. Within-branch randomization shares specialists and the fraud desk, so controls are exposed; that interference is not designed around. “June 2024” is the wrong year. The green rule says “all three” conditions and then lists six, and it treats an escalation increase as failure after the mechanism section treats that increase as possibly better triage. Routing, staffing, and case mix are not frozen.

The script does not close the loop. It uses subplots after saying it will not, hard-codes a January–March label while Gamma’s filter keeps all pre-adoption months, builds relative time with a 30-day truncation that collides distinct months, claims branch and month fixed effects it does not include, drops the never-treated branches from the “ITT,” fits unclustered scikit-learn OLS, and can fail on the chained adoption mask. Warnings about five clusters and aggregated medians are present and appropriate; they do not repair the models. Causal caveats are explicit, which is required, but the printed associations are not the numbers used in the assessment.
