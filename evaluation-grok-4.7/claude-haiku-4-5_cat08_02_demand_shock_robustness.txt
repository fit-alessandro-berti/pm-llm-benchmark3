5.5

The write-up has the required seven-part shape, real scenario splits, on/off triggers, and several redesign-caused risks. It is not reliable operations advice. Counts, capacities, and bottleneck order repeatedly contradict the logs.

The mix-shift story starts at registration and then treats preparation as starved. In the observed peak, registration is the short queue (2.8 h at 85%), while specialist review (36 h), medical validation (22 h), assay (18 h), and technical validation (16 h, 101% utilization) are already saturated together. Preparation is queued at 11 h, not idle. That peak is the demand-mix case the redesign is supposed to absorb, and it is harsher than Scenario A on the routing that matters: releases through medical validation rise from about 26% to about 50%, assay-to-repeat outcomes from about 6.7% to about 15%, and specialist holds several-fold per week. The package never computes those rates, then treats a 23%→38% medical-path jump and a 7%→15% repeat jump as hypothetical stresses beyond history.

Headcount and arithmetic are invented and then used as mechanisms. “One of two” medical validators is not in the data; 4,190 cases over eight weeks is not 5.2 per hour; normal direct recollections are 760, not ~420, and a 9% rate is not 760 requests. Repeat assay → medical validation (80) is described as feeding technical validation. Scenario B’s utilization formula stays near 72% while also claiming congestion severe enough to cut urgent batch bypass to 40%. Scenario E says the assay queue grows because preparation waits for technical validation, which reverses the flow, and it calls release a bottleneck although medical validation → release is 2 h / 5 h.

The standing redesign repeats those mistakes. Junior medical validation for routine work adds a gate that most technically validated cases already skip (11,700 direct releases). Those junior validators are taken from technical validation, the pool already at 101% with a 16 h peak queue, so the “+30–40%” medical capacity is not net capacity. Repeat work is pushed toward evening and night processing even though repeats already cluster after night shifts and M2. A single-machine fallback cannot absorb combined M1/M2 load at 71% normal utilization, let alone 98% peak. Outsourcing 5–10% of complex panels cannot move a 22–36 h queue by the claimed hours. Triggers such as a 25 h medical queue or a 30 h specialist queue sit inside the observed peak, so the “temporary” policies would stay on for the whole peak. Urgent p90 is targeted below 18 h when the normal-period p90 is already 19 h. The stated clerk error rise after 9 h shifts, and the clerk time spent explaining recollections, are never wired to the extra shifts or the new triage contacts.
