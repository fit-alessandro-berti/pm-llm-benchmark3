The redesign is structurally complete and often directionally sound, but several evidence claims that drive the policies are numerically wrong. Those errors are large enough to mis-set triggers and to overstate how much the package helps.

The unequal observation windows are treated as comparable counts. Normal is 8 weeks and peak is 3 weeks, yet technical validation to medical validation (3,900 vs 3,420) is called similar volume, and hold-for-specialist (290 vs 610) is called a 2.1× increase. Weekly rates are about 2.3× and 5.6×. That understates the peak load the bottleneck story is built on. Utilization and queue data still support medical validation and specialist review as constraints, so the qualitative sequence survives, but the process-mining comparison does not.

The recollection control limit is miscalculated. 780/8,530 is about 9.1%, not 4.8%, and the right pre-analytical base is 9,730 exits (about 8.0% to recollection). The dashboard then anchors green/amber/red to a baseline the cited ratio does not have. Batch-wait time is also taken from the wrong edge: test preparation to batch wait is 14h at peak, while time in batch wait is the batch-wait to assay edge at 22h. Normal on that edge is already 9h, so the green band (median under 4h) would alarm outside disruption.

Batch math is inconsistent. Cutting size from 12 to 8 raises setups per batched sample by 50%, not a 33% penalty. The “about 340 extra setups” figure applies that change to 8,140 assay runs, which is the direct preparation-to-assay path, not the batch path (about 1,030–1,180 cases). The efficiency warning is therefore several times too large.

The false-rejection break-even is wrong. If true rejections prevent a recollection and false ones create one, net rework rises only when false rejections outnumber true ones, near a 50% share of rejections, not 5%. The 25% capacity loss from one absent medical validator assumes an unstated headcount of four. Effect sizes in the trade-off table (+5–15%, +20–35%, rework to 8–9%) are not derived from the logs, and I10’s sign (“−10–20% cycle time”) fights the “+ means faster” convention used in the other rows. Subtracting the 36h specialist queue and the 22h medical-validation queue, then adding only 12h transport, also treats sequential internal waits as fully avoided and ignores partner processing time.

Medical-validation relief is mis-assigned. Most normal releases already go technical validation to release (11,700 vs 3,900 to medical validation). Peak shifts that mix because urgent and complex-panel work needs medical validators; complex-panel cases are 3.1× more likely to need them. An evening medical shift and routine auto-release therefore help urgent and complex work more than routine, which is the opposite of the table’s largest routine gains.

The scenario set, conditional surge rules, deactivation triggers, downstream-overload notes, and self-inflicted risks (mode hysteresis, traceability, age priority versus the 18h urgent limit) meet the redesign brief. The grade stays at 6.0 because the operational numbers an ops team would actually monitor or cite are not reliable.
