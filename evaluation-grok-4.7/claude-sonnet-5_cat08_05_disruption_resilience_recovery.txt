The memo hits every required section, uses most headline rates correctly, and lands the right central thesis: hospital service was protected by visible triage, while authorization skips and unscanned dispatch created concealed liability, and Day 7–21 cycle times recovered ahead of the audit tail. Keep/modify/retire calls for B, C, G, I, and J are directionally sound, and the control matrix, exit rule, and ops-versus-compliance indicator are concrete.

It is not tight enough for a high score. The fast lane is not an occasional bypass: 920 of 1,000 fast-lane cases go straight to the snapshot with no authorization activity, so it is a structured skip, and it is a distinct log node from C, not “indistinguishable” from it. Grouping K/L with true resilience contradicts the memo’s own later claim that after-the-fact remediation is not real-time control. F is treated as having reduced controlled-substance errors even though dual-control gaps rose from 0.2% to 2.9% and 1,800 packs bypassed temporary release entirely; blaming that spike on ambient authorization skips (C) mixes control types. “Almost fully recovered” cycle time rests on the hospital median only; pharmacy and wholesale medians and p90s stay well above baseline. The T3 claim that reconciliation “is not yet present” is not supported, because no partial-recovery DFG is given. Snapshot validity is 4–6 hours in one place and same-day in another; a 72-hour standing-authorization list softens the shortcut the memo says to retire. G’s 1,800+1,450 unscanned handoffs are equated with the traceability gap, but a 9.5% exception rate is only about 530 cases, and those two edges are never reconciled. Stale snapshots are credited with the whole 12.5% rework rate. Temporal-profile findings (backlog wait beyond baseline p95, highly variable phone-POD delay) are unused, E drops out of the resilience/shortcut/ambiguous split, and the redesign never staffs the compliance bottleneck that made skips likely.
