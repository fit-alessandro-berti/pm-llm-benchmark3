The write-up has the required nine-part structure, a real policy comparison, and several correct demand aggregates, but the staffing conclusion is built on a misread skill matrix, contradictory arithmetic, an objective that points the wrong way, physically impossible delay figures, and a simulation that does not implement the stated model.

The decisive evidence error is the skill matrix. Farah is Software-qualified at 1.40 from 08:00–12:00, Emil is Electrical-qualified at 1.30, and Chen is qualified for both Electrical and Software across 08:00–16:00. The answer drops Farah’s Software skill and Emil’s Electrical skill in both the prose and the `SPEED` table, then states as fact that software coverage exists only from 12:00 and that no resource is both Electrical- and Software-qualified. That last claim also contradicts the answer’s own Chen row, which correctly gives him both skills. Morning software is therefore not impossible, Chen/Emil/Farah can run ES sequentially without a team-transfer penalty, and the “structural” P1 ceiling is not established.

The capacity denominators that carry this claim are not auditable. Software is given as 420 minutes because Chen and Emil are each assigned 210. Chen’s net shift is about 450 minutes, not a 12:00–16:00 slice of 210, and Emil’s net shift is about 450, not 210. Mechanical at 1,350 matches only Ada+Ben+Deepa and silently drops Farah; Electrical at 870 matches no stated subset and omits Emil. Offered-load ratios of 35.6%, 91.3%, and 117.8% are arithmetic on those denominators, not a demonstrated bottleneck. Per-skill maxima also cannot be added across multi-skilled people. A joint capacity problem may still exist — base work is on the order of 2,700 unit-minutes before slowdowns, against about 2,490 staffed minutes — but that is not what the answer proves.

Several published calculations are internally false:

- The pre-noon P1 software expression \(7(0.10)(0.25)+9(0.15)(0.25)\) equals 0.5125, not 1.0084. All-day P1 of 5.44 is right, so the share is about 9.4%, and \(1-0.185=0.815\), not the stated 67.3% ceiling. Even that bound treats every pre-noon software P1 as an automatic miss, which a noon start would not imply.
- U1’s \(27.2(0.15)=1.02\) is false, and 0.15 is not the software probability. The 523.6 versus 378 minute comparison is not recoverable from the stated morning stock plus 12:00–16:00 demand.
- U2’s renormalized branch counts, 4.917 / 12.324 / 5.058, are correct. U4 is not. Doubling the electrical tail cannot reduce mean service from 32.70 to 32.27. Correct doubled-tail means are about 29.6, 37.1, and 44.5, not 33.05, 32.27, and 51.
- Base branch counts \(n_M=18.09\), \(n_E=24.30\), \(n_S=12.85\), total P1 of 5.44, and U1’s 27.2 morning arrivals and 9.051 all-day P1 cases are correct. The midpoint mixture is not: Intake is 6.94 rather than 7.18, Mechanical 26.30 rather than 26.60, Electrical 32.82 rather than 32.70, and Validation 15.74 rather than 15.02. Software 38.50 is the only exact mean.
- ME “before coordination” is 32.7 versus 67.3, but \(26.6+32.7+8=67.3\), so coordination was included on the sequential side only. ES does the opposite: 46.5 is \(\max+8\) and 71.2 is the sum without it.

Assumption 3 says block volumes stay unchanged and therefore total arrivals need not stay 42. Those cannot both be true. The robustness score subtracts SLA, excess utilization, CV, and P90 and is then maximized. Every term has the same sign, so neither max nor min makes high SLA good while high utilization imbalance and tail delay are bad. \(F_1\) is a coherent minimization. \(J\) is not.

Section 4’s base mean delay of 20–35 minutes cannot be elapsed time to Validation completion. Ordinary service alone is already well above that, and the same section claims an uncleared software backlog. The table always favors Policy C, with base max utilization of 65–80% under full pooling despite an alleged overload. The ranges are labeled as illustrative, but they contradict the answer’s own definitions. Policy A/B’s parallel test, slack \(> 0.75(\text{slack}-8)\), is true for essentially any slack above about −24 minutes. Policy C’s later wording is the test that was probably intended.

The code is not an executable check on sections 2–4. `advance_calendar` reads an undefined `scenario`. U3 sets Chen’s capacity to 0 and then divides by it. Intake and Validation never receive a service duration. `project` divides by the speed multiplier while `start` multiplies by it, so scoring treats slow resources as fast. Preemption increases fraction complete instead of adding 10% of remaining work, and it does not free the resource. Cases are scanned in arrival order, so the priority band does not govern the queue. Queue wait is computed after queue-entry is set to the current clock, so it is identically zero. Break time can be charged as work. The parallel comparison mixes a clock time with a duration. Coordination is charged whenever two branches occur, including sequential work. U5 removes 30 minutes from every resource except Chen, although the outage only blocks Intake.

Sections 6, 7, and 9 are the strongest part: starvation, gaming, interpretability, sojourn-versus-service, parallel double-counting, handover logging, and a shadow-mode pilot are handled concretely. That does not repair the recommendation. Policy C is preferred because it supposedly manages a missing morning software resource and an unattainable mixed P1 target. Both premises are unsupported by the supplied matrix, so the robustness ranking is not earned.
