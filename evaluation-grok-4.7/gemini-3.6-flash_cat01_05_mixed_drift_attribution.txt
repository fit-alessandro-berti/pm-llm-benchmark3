6.7

The core chronology is right and the required structure is largely filled in, but several quantitative claims are wrong, a few causal statements outrun the aggregates, and parts of the monitoring design contradict the same data.

The timeline correctly separates the March label break from later rework, queue growth, the priority rule, renewal season, and the December mix of lower load plus intervention. Onset types are differentiated, and the mapped-fitness reading of March is the right one. Interval edges are still loose: the 15–30 June window cites July cycle time, and the May–June supplier row blends April’s 18% with June’s 46% and 95 hours. Evidence of 80 for priority redistribution treats a pattern the monthly totals are explicitly said not to reveal as if it were observed. The October–November senior-assessment jump is scored as seasonal behavior without using the stated fact that senior assessment was recorded inconsistently.

Section 2 is inside the word limit and makes the right qualitative points. It then overstates them. Mapped fitness of 0.90 does not prove that execution “remained compliant.” The raw–mapped gap is about 0.32–0.40, not 0.35–0.38. Request Info moving from 12 to 29 is not a doubling, and 4 to 22 is not a quadrupling (5.5×). Those two false fold-changes are repeated in the attribution. Duplicate `Open Case` appears as `Open Case → Register`, so the claim that duplicates create loops or parallel splits is unsupported. Missingness is about 4.8% in March and peaks at 6.5% in July, not “>6% post-migration.”

The baseline arithmetic is mostly correct, and the L1 figures 3, 40, 59, and 64 match the seven supplied edges. The distance still double-counts one rework loop because `Assess → Request Info` and `Request Info → Collect Documents` move almost in lockstep, with no dependence caveat. The change table mixes percent changes for volume with percentage-point changes for shares, both written as “%.” Mapped fitness is not −0.06 in both July and November; only July is 0.86 (−0.06), and November is 0.87 (−0.05). The cycle-time split assigns +0.9h to +1.75h to service when the only service fact is about +0.6h on `Assess`, and the four hour ranges do not partition +17.5h. That is invented precision. The second logging change point, 15 June, is not a logging break: missingness rises from March and peaks in July at 6.5%, not 6.2%. May is the clearer early performance break (+4.5h median, +13h P90) and is missing from the prose change-point list. Multiple mechanism-specific change points is the right conclusion.

The eight hypotheses and the discrimination grid cover the required mechanisms and segments. Ranking pure logging first is ambiguous, because that hypothesis explains March and fails afterward. In the staffing row, a uniform queue increase is placed in the population-drift column; that signature is capacity/performance, not case mix. Supplier–complexity confounding, priority mediation, and pre-May complexity of Supplier Z are handled well.

The segmentation rules are usable, but priority and resource never become segment variables, and the minimum-support rules are not defined on the cross-classification, so the plan does not actually control tiny cells. Standard, non–Supplier Z, post-mapping cases are a good control stratum and a poor primary population: that slice removes the supplier and renewal shocks that dominate the observed drift.

The monitor set is broad and correctly rejects one universal threshold. It still omits an outcomes monitor. The post-migration resource baseline “<2%” is incompatible with the observed 4.8–6.5%. Benjamini–Hochberg is not applicable to these fixed business thresholds, because no p-values are defined. The queue/service baseline of about 5.5 is not derivable once other activities also consume service time. Year-over-year seasonality is the right idea and depends on history this extract does not contain.

Part A embeds the tables accurately, and the L1 and simple rules are transparent and executable. Validation is only length and fitness bounds. Baseline changes and the fitness gap are computed and never printed. Part B copies the log before mapping, but it never calls `pm4py.format_dataframe`, which was required. Deduplication on case, activity, and timestamp does not remove duplicated `Open Case` events that carry distinct timestamps. DECLARE is absent; the log-skeleton attempt is only optional, so that part is acceptable.

The attribution is in range and correctly treats March as syntactic and supplier rework as the best May–August account, with staffing versus priority left entangled. It then says the March fitness drop “conclusively proves” there was “performance deterioration”; March cycle time did not deteriorate. The claimed r = 0.94 between Supplier Z share and rework is false: on the twelve monthly pairs the correlation is about 0.81, and November’s 29% rework at only 27% Supplier Z is the point that correlation is supposed to survive. September did not resolve rework to baseline. “Quadrupling” of senior assessment is wrong again, and the conditional rate also changed (about 4/21 versus 22/41), so mix alone does not generate that edge. December’s joint seasonal and intervention improvement is left out of the attribution. The revision triggers themselves are the right tests.

Rule 1 fires for every month from March onward, because missing-resource share stays above 4%, and it would quarantine operational metrics even though the answer elsewhere says missing resources do not move timestamp deltas. Rule 4’s joint pattern does not occur: after July both the median and the P90 are far outside +10% and the P90 increase versus June is not >25%. Rule 7’s “policy violations” reading ignores an incomplete normative model, especially inconsistent senior assessment. Rules 2, 3, 5, and 6 are directionally usable, with thresholds that remain heuristic.
