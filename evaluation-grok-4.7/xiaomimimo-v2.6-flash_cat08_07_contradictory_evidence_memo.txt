8.5

The memo is a strong, decision-ready synthesis: it rejects national scale and full rollback for the right reasons, recommends a segmented hybrid with explicit pauses, and ties rollout to measurable thresholds. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are separated in a full evidence table; all five metric conflicts, alternatives A–E, and M1–M8 are covered with real process-mining use (fitness/precision, alignments, DFG, temporal profile, resource and social networks, segments). Value-of-information ranking is sequenced and conditional rather than a flat list.

Deductions under a strict reading:

- The executive memo claims incomplete risk/segment attributes and truncated complaint cost “both bias the pilot’s apparent gains upward.” Cost truncation can inflate the visible-cost gain. Missing attributes do not directionally bias aggregate duration, cost, or quality; they weaken segment attribution. That overclaim sits in the decision section, while the table treats the same gap more carefully.
- Low generalization is glossed as the model being “less representative of plausible behaviour” in the same breath as low precision meaning over-permissiveness. Those are different process-mining properties; the sentence blurs them. “Combinations we never intended” also overstates what low precision shows (extra behavior relative to the log, not intent).
- “Rework up roughly 50%” is loose: failed visits rose about 46% relatively, second visits about 64%, manual activation more than doubled, and first-time-right fell 6 points. The direction is right; the summary figure is not.
- Completion-only timestamps plus a 6-week window make right-censoring a plausible assumption, which the table labels correctly. The memo then treats the observed tail as “probably a lower bound,” firmer than the cohort definition supports.
- Extending coordinator ownership to rural and business cases “once headroom exists” is thinner than the segment evidence (both still lost first-time-right and gained complaints). The 80% utilization cap is a reasonable control, not a result implied by the data.
- The broader-rollout gate requires simultaneous wins on tail, rework, complaints, cost, NPS, and utilization. That is a conservative gate, but it sits awkwardly next to the memo’s own point that no design dominates every metric.

Numbers checked against the brief are otherwise accurate, the word limit is respected, and the recommendation does not pretend Redesign R is uniformly good or uniformly bad. The remaining gaps are local overclaims, not a weak decision.
