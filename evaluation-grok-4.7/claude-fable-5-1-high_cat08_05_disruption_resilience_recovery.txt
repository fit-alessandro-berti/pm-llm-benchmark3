7.5

The review is unusually complete and mostly faithful to the logs: every required section is present, the phase metrics and most DFG arithmetic check out, and the resilience/noncompliance split, product-tiered controls, and post-recovery tail are handled seriously. It is not nearly flawless. Several quantitative and causal claims are wrong or internally inconsistent, and those claims sit in the argument management would act on.

The central abductive claim does not hold together. Section 2 treats the pack split as intentional sampling (1,650 temporary releases versus 1,800 packs sent straight to dispatch). Section 3 then says temporary release “saturated” at 1,650 and that dispatch-without-scan absorbed the overflow. Those are different mechanisms. The frequency DFG also shows 1,450 of the 1,650 released orders still moving to dispatch without scan, so the scan gap is not the residual of the release control. Skip-authorization is likewise described in the source as an ambient, history-based practice; reading it as pure spillover once manual authorization hit 2,500 is stronger than the log supports.

Three figures are off in a way that changes the risk story. “69% of triaged orders passed through an authorization step” counts the hospital fast lane (1,000) as authorized. In the manual DFG that lane goes to the inventory snapshot (920) and has no authorization arc, so the explicit authorization share is 2,500/5,100, about 49%. The claim that skipped authorizations mean roughly 25–30 orders “would normally have been stopped” treats a compliance hold as a stop; at baseline 520 of 700 holds return to validation, so the expected hard stops are on the order of 7, not 25–30. The “~30% dual-control gap if nothing had been done” has no basis in the product mix or the DFG.

The excess-gap arithmetic itself is right (about 1,500, 640, and 390 above baseline rates on 52,000 cases). Later wording slides into calling 640 and 390 the cold-chain and controlled shipments without documentation. Gross counts are about 850 and 500. T4’s 1,200 proof-of-delivery remediations are successors of T4 dispatches, not the 650 T2 dispatches that never received a phone confirmation. Calling partial recovery “more dangerous” than pure manual is also too strong: every rate and the fitness improved; only absolute exception counts rose with volume.

Smaller overclaims accumulate. The 200 authorization cases and 200 release cases with no outgoing arc are unfinished in the window, not shown to be held, lost, or stopped. About 150 packs, 120 deferrals, and 220 snapshot checks are simply dropped. Snapshot staleness is asked to explain the whole T4 reconciliation load. “2.2–2.75× baseline” slightly misstates the control ratios, and the fairness indicator mixes a +135% increase with a 3× ratio threshold. The process tree omits hard constraints the prose already states, including never deferring controlled orders.

What keeps the score from falling further is the discipline elsewhere. Baseline, T2, T3, and T4 service and conformance figures are generally exact; in-flight work, absolute-versus-rate exposure in T3, and the audit queue after apparent recovery are correctly seen; patterns A–L are classified with product and segment scope; and the redesigned model has real triggers, minimum controls, contemporaneous evidence, a split between reconstructable records and unrecoverable deviations, and falsifiers in the trade-off statement. The errors are local, but they are concrete and they sit on the causal spine of the answer.
