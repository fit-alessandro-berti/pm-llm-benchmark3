4.8

The write-up covers every required section and reaches a conditional, pilot-first recommendation that separates East from North/South and warns against reading success off early averages. Those are real strengths. Under a strict reading, the forecast is still not reliable: several headline numbers point the wrong way, queueing arithmetic from the given table is missed, and the PM4Py sketch would not measure what it claims.

**Mechanism map.** Management’s own handover counts do not show a nominal reduction. Summing the eight listed types gives about 343 handovers per 100 cases today and about 359 after the change. The local technical hop moves from 36 to 41 and the return hop from 31 to 36; the map still labels that pair a reduction. Legal/Risk volume rises from 9 to 12 per 100 while those specialists already sit at 91–93% utilization, and one of three supervisor posts is removed while approval handovers rise from 42 to 49. The map treats “+15% utilization” as higher throughput. At these occupancies that load is a queue-stability problem. Several claimed sensors are the wrong objects: a handover-of-work network stores resource-to-resource frequencies, not wait times; a working-together network does not measure utilization; a subcontracting network does not establish a single point of failure. Direction markers are missing throughout (“36  41”, “utilization 15%”, “Role clarity ”), so the size and sign of many effects are ambiguous. Resilience is stated only as a loss; pooling three technicians can also improve cover for absence, which is the open question the current 8.9 h / 18% rework cross-team path raises.

**Scenarios.** The three bands are not ordered. Cross-team handovers are already projected to fall from 6 to 3 (about 50%); the optimistic band’s “20% reduction” is weaker than the management estimate it is supposed to beat. The central band says both “10% fewer handovers” and that new shared-service handovers offset the gain. Cycle-time anchors are team-specific figures treated as organizational: 7.4 d is South’s median (the volume-weighted team medians happen to lie near 7.5 d), while the adverse 90th percentile of 16.4 d → 20.5 d is North’s tail. East’s current 90th percentile is already 22.8 d, and a volume-weighted reading of the three 90th percentiles is about 18.6 d, so 20.5 d is not an adverse organizational tail. “+12% median” and “+25% at the 90th percentile” are also out of scale with the tabulated waits, which are hours on cycle times of about 7–9 days, unless utilization crosses 1 and the queue becomes unstable. That instability is the plausible adverse path for Legal/Risk (roughly 0.93 × 12/9 > 1) and is never calculated; the adverse band instead stops at “12 h+”. “Rework 20%”, “SLA miss 50%”, and “SLA miss rate 2%” have no operator, so a relative change, a percentage-point change, and a new level cannot be told apart. Optimistic “early warning signs” are failure signals (`reassignment_count` spikes, rework where `local_expertise_flag` is false). If those appear, the optimistic scenario is already false. The optimistic band also ignores the removed supervisor.

**Indicators.** The leading/lagging split is the right idea and several business signals are well chosen (shared-service wait, reassignment, rework by complexity, tail cycle time by segment). The PM4Py column is often the wrong function. `format_dataframe` does not aggregate `reassignment_count`. Handover edge weights are not a daily backlog. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` adds case-level waits, so it does not yield the wait at Technical Shared Service. Handover discovery does not stratify `handover_reason`. `get_variants` does not compute SLA-miss correlation. `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` does not produce a `case_duration` standard deviation by team. A performance DFG is arc timing, not a case-level 90th percentile. Learning is not “% local vs shared” inside a working-together network.

**Hidden risks.** All requested themes are present, including tail delay, complexity, local knowledge, fairness, learning, accountability, and exceptions, and East vs North/South is explicit. Detection methods repeat the same mismatched objects. Threshold language jumps from hour-scale medians to multi-day queues without the utilization mechanism that would justify the jump. Stricter central QC can also inflate `rework_flag` as a measurement change; that alternative is absent.

**Pilot.** Scope, window, segmentation axes, guardrails, feedback, and rollback are all present, and keeping local supervisors out of phase 1 is a sensible de-risk. The comparison design is confounded. East is inside the treatment and also listed as a historical baseline, so East has no concurrent control. South is an untouched concurrent control but is described as a historical baseline. Routing 50% of North’s cases to the shared service while the other 50% keep `N_Tech` shares one specialist and one local practice, so informal advice leaks across arms. High-priority cases are excluded, which drops the fairness and tail risks the pilot most needs. Guardrails are weakly tied to the given base rates: high-complexity rework “>15%” sits next to current post-handover rework of 14% (legal) and 18% (cross-team), and East’s SLA miss rate is already 9.8% with no East guardrail. The expansion rule “rework rate ** baseline +2%**” is missing its operator. Section 6 then treats shared-approval role ambiguity as something this pilot can reveal, while section 5 keeps local supervisors in place.

**Recommendation.** “Pilot East and part of North; do not scale on early averages; keep embedded specialists if North worsens” matches the evidence better than a full rollout. It is still ahead of the design. It does not require a capacity fix before more Legal/Risk and difficult quality-check work enter a queue whose specialists are already above 90%, and it judges a four-month pilot’s lagging outcomes on a four-week stabilization story for the leading set only. Reject thresholds (rework above 12% where local expertise is false; high-complexity 90th percentile up more than 15%) are not tied back to current team rates of 8.2–11.5% or to East’s 22.8 d tail.

**PM4Py sketch.** The prompt allows pseudocode, but this is presented as runnable analysis and it will not run as written. `filter_event_attribute_values` does not take a timestamp range or `level='timestamp'` (`filter_time_range` does). `view_handover_of_work_network` and `get_waiting_time` are not PM4Py entry points. `get_all_case_durations` returns a list, which has neither `.median()` nor `.quantile()`. Event-level filters on `team` drop other teams’ events inside the same case and break handover and cycle-time statistics precisely where cross-team flow matters. `df_north` and `df_high_complexity` are built and then unused. The pre/post windows are hard-coded to 2023 and do not match the pilot calendar. Specialty, `customer_segment`, and SLA misses are named and not computed. Unused imports (`dataframe_utils`, `log_converter`) do not affect results. Pre and post networks are discovered and never differenced, and raw handover counts are not volume-normalized.

The piece is a complete brief with the right caution about averages, tails, East, fairness, and learning. The grade stays below 5 because the central capacity contradiction in the supplied tables is unexamined, scenario magnitudes are internally inconsistent, and the measurement plan attributes the wrong process-mining objects to the decisions it asks management to take.
