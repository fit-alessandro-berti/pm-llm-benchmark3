6.4

The answer is conceptually strong and operationally serious, but it is not reliable enough for a high score. Headline flag contrasts, the rejection of cross-world natural effects, and the separation of occurrence, waiting, and escalation are right. Several computed subgroup facts, one falsification implication, and the pilot’s actual contrast are not.

The flag-level means check out: triage wait 0.24 h versus 2.48 h, service 0.60 h versus 0.95 h, conditional manual wait 6.5 h versus 1.88 h, cycle time 16.5 h versus 8.2 h, review 10/10 versus 4/10, and escalation 6/10 versus 2/10. T1/T2 versus T3/T4 is also exact. Those results do not carry over to the other rows. High complexity is 8/8 reviewed and 7/8 escalated, not 6/8 and 5/6. Medium cycle time is about 8.9 h, not 8.2 h. Flagged high-load manual wait is 7.42 h and cycle time 18.0 h, not 6.92 h and 17.6 h. Flagged normal-load figures are 5.13 h and 14.25 h, not 5.88 h and 14.9 h. Escalated flagged cases average 21.4 h and non-escalated flagged cases 9.15 h; the table’s 20.3 h and 10.8 h do not match the cases, and those two rows do not sum from their own stage cells. The prose then calls 10.8 h a median. High-complexity cycle time is not “irrespective of flag”: flagged high cases average about 22.4 h and unflagged high cases about 15.4 h. M2 is the reviewer for 5/10 flagged cases; R20 is unflagged and should not be in that list.

The estimand section correctly treats the flag as a policy bundle and prefers interventional or separable contrasts because shared queues and exposure-induced confounding break natural direct and indirect effects. It still merges separable effects with interventional effects, and it calls triage resource a collider when, for triage timing, assignment is chiefly a mediator of priority. The manual-wait discussion also ties that wait to senior capacity. The graph is useful, including other-case interference, but `arrival_load → priority_lane` and `manual_queue_wait → manual_service` are poorly motivated, and there is no direct flag-to-escalation edge despite that pathway being claimed later.

The RDD row reverses its own implication. A jump in review with no jump in cycle time would weaken occurrence mediation. A cycle-time jump at a smooth risk cutoff is the signature that the flag bundle adds delay. Complexity is also not a negative-control exposure; it has a substantive processing effect. The other designs—queue state, shift-cluster assignment, g-computation, tipping points, and future-arrival placebos—are specific and mostly well aimed.

P2 is a sensible direction, but the pilot does not test it cleanly. Mandatory review is reinstated for high complexity or a score of at least 0.70, which retains review for nearly the same cases the flag already selects. The eligibility window is not a near-threshold window. Overflow pooling and a live escalation-queue rule mix P3 and P4 into the P2 arm, while the synthesis still says escalation criteria are kept and that P2 directly identifies occurrence mediation. There is also no within-flag reviewed-versus-not contrast anywhere in these data. Missing operators make several policy-effect cells unreadable, and the stated P2 cycle-time levels do not follow from the review rates given in the same row.

The event log is constructed correctly: absent stages stay absent, waits are not zero-filled, and both DFG calls and explicit stage times are present. The small-sample warning compares event count with 10, so it will not fire for these case subgroups, and `SeniorTeam` is an invented resource. The six reversal conditions are concrete, especially timestamp meaning and reviewer risk compensation. The synthesis repeats the false “irrespective of flag” claim and locates the main queue delay in the senior queue even though the manual-review queue is the larger and more consistent delay.
