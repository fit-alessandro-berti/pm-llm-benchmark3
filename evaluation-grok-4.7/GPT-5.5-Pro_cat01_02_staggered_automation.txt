7.5

The arithmetic that can be checked by hand is accurate, the required structure is complete, and the headline judgment is right: a single “reduces cycle time by X%” claim is not defensible, median and p90 must be separated, and expansion only makes sense inside a design that creates a real control. The score is held down by timing mistakes, an over-strong conditional claim, and a gap between the stated design and the evidence actually computed.

The adopter pre/post table matches the data under the stated windows, including Alpha median −0.4 h (−0.8%) and p90 +9.7 h (+9.8%), Beta +1.9 h and +9.8 h, and Gamma −6.5 h (−14.8%) and −9.3 h. The two diff-in-change rows also check: Alpha/Beta January–March to April–June versus Gamma/Delta/Epsilon is about −4.2 h median, −6.1 h p90, −2.0 pp rework, +0.9 pp escalation, and +0.08 CSAT; Gamma versus Delta/Epsilon is about −3.8 h, −7.1 h, −2.1 pp, +0.9 pp, and +0.10. Availability, intensity, targeted rollout, co-occurring shocks, aggregation, and CSAT nonresponse are kept apart in section 1. The estimands cover ITT-like availability, intensity, median, tail, rework, escalation, CSAT, and dynamics, and the threat list covers the required failure modes. The code validates the panel, avoids subplots, builds relative-time indicators, clusters by branch, and labels the regressions as non-causal.

The main quantitative problem is that full-window averages hide the relevant break. Alpha’s April go-live, still at 34 FTE and 28% high complexity, has median 48.3 and p90 97.3, essentially unchanged from the January–March means of 48.9 and 98.5, while rework falls from 13.2 to 11.2. The p90 blow-up is May–June (112.6, then 119.9), coinciding with the loss of two FTEs and the complexity jump, not with availability itself. “Worsened in April–June” is therefore too coarse, and the +9.7 h post average is not an adoption effect. Gamma’s raw −6.5 h / −14.8% uses April–June as the pre window, which already contains the May spike; a January–June pre mean is about 40.6 h, so the raw drop is closer to −3.4 h. That sensitivity is missing, yet −6.5 h still anchors the reported raw range.

Pre-trends are described but not sized. From January to March, control medians move on the order of −1 to +3 h while Alpha/Beta are roughly flat. A differential of that size, extrapolated through a short post window, can account for a substantial part of the −4 h diff-in-change. The scenario interval “0 to −5 h” includes zero, but it is not tied to that pre-trend magnitude, to the routing reallocation, or to Alpha’s branch-specific staffing loss. The intensity results (−0.62 h median per +10 pp, and the implied −1.5 to −2.1 h) are internally consistent with each other, yet the answer never explains why they are about half the unadjusted diff-in-change. The obvious reason is that Epsilon’s complexity and cycle-time shock is larger than Alpha/Beta’s, so the unadjusted contrast credits automation for a common case-mix deterioration. Those coefficients are also more precise than five clusters can support, even with the warning attached.

Identification is directionally sound, especially the rejection of a single two-way fixed-effects average, but it is not carried through. The preferred group-time event study is never estimated; the only fitted availability model is the static two-way specification the prose warns against, and the relative-time dummies are unused. “Possibly lagged outcomes” do not belong in the adjustment set in a short panel. Gamma’s readiness pilot is not treated as contamination of the April–June control group. Routing is discussed as a covariate, not as a displacement that also treats Delta and Epsilon by removing cases. Classic regression to the mean is emphasized even though Alpha/Beta do not revert downward in raw levels; the live threat is differential trends plus bundled shocks. Capacity is under-read: Alpha’s arrivals per FTE rise by about 9, roughly 29%, with a flat median, which is the clearest descriptive sign of load absorption rather than a pure cycle-time gain, and it is left in the table without that interpretation.

Section 7 and the claim ladder do not match. P90’s own scenario interval is about +5 h to −8 h, confidence 30, with a safe claim that tail reduction cannot be asserted. The “conditionally causal” bullet still says automation may have mitigated p90 deterioration by several hours. That picks the favorable side of an interval that includes harm. Escalation at confidence 60 is also high relative to a diff-in-change of only about +0.9 pp on top of a uniform March fraud-desk shift of +2 pp.

The recommendation category is the right one, but the design recreates the original problem. Leading with Epsilon because it is weaker repeats the selection rule that sent automation to historically bad branches; randomization is offered only if leadership permits it. The 3-hour median and 2-hour p90 gates are not derived from a minimum detectable effect, a capacity constraint, or the pre-trend bound. Shared fraud-desk interference is monitored, yet the scale-up rule is still written as a branch-level before/after threshold.
