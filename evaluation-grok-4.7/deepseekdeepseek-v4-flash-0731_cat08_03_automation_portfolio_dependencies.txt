The answer covers all seven required sections, uses the case data rather than frequency alone, and reaches a defensible conclusion: do not automate registration first, and spend the four-module budget on entity mismatch, statement normalization, valuation triage, and committee-pack assembly. That strategic direction is sound. The score is held down by repeated misreads of the process evidence and by a load-bearing control claim that the chosen modules do not actually support.

The candidate matrix is complete and mostly well judged. A11 is correctly marked unsuitable until controls are reliable, and A1’s downstream-bottleneck risk is correctly rated high. Two dependency claims are too strong. Missing-document nudging is treated as a prerequisite for statement normalization, but the directly-follows counts show most reviewed files move straight to normalization (about 5,050) rather than through the missing-document loop (3,300). KYC hit pre-classification is also treated as if it identifies entity mismatches. Those are different exceptions: hit resolution sits inside KYC, while entity mismatch is a separate activity with its own loop back to KYC (1,160) and a smaller path to contract preparation (380).

The effects section systematically treats median wait before an activity as time that automating that activity removes. The clearest error is saying the compliance portfolio reduces the 4.0-hour wait before KYC. That queue sits in front of the activity; a hit pre-classifier works inside it. The same pattern appears when current exception rates are described as reductions (“reduces 38% mismatch rework,” and the same wording for the 16%, 13%, and 15% rates). Those figures are baseline exception rates, not expected savings, and the 13% request-rework rate is conflated with the 19% late external valuation. The weekly committee cadence is also applied to all 3,900 committee cases, although the case limits that cadence to high exposure.

The recommendation depends on calling committee-pack auto-assembly the control-strengthening module compliance requires. The candidate is an assembly tool whose stated risk is garbage-in, garbage-out. It can be designed to flag four-eyes conflicts, but it does not, as specified, fix provisional signing (6.5% of cases), the rule that KYC must precede signing, or contract-to-decision mismatches (8.5% overall, 14% on credit-line renewal). The risk section then lists contract-rule checks as a four-eyes compensating control even though that module is not in the portfolio. Phase 0’s “control shell” does not repair this: the first six months operate A4 and A6, neither of which is principally a control module.

Process coverage also misses constraints the case told the analyst not to ignore. KYC and financial-document review run concurrently even though the system records them in sequence, and that concurrency never enters the dependency or cycle-time argument. The recommended portfolio’s residual path is understated: the missing-document loop (52% repeated, and relationship managers often cause it), the 44-hour signing wait, the 28-hour wait and 110-minute judgment time in credit analysis, and back-office slack after 15:00 are not part of the final trade-off. Gate criteria stay at “if wait is not reduced” and “above baseline,” which is too loose to govern a rollout.

The portfolio logic, resource-shift awareness, and refusal to activate loans or generate contracts on dirty upstream data are good. The grade is not higher because the control justification, the waiting-time arithmetic, and the omitted concurrency and rework loops are material, not cosmetic.
