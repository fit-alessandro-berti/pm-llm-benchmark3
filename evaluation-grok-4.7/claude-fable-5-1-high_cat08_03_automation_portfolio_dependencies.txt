The recommendation (A5, A6, A9, A4), the refusal to automate registration or loan activation first, and the treatment of four-eyes, KYC-before-signing, audit trails, and specialist concentration are coherent and mostly grounded. The score is held down by quantitative overclaims, one DFG misread, a dependency contradiction, and a process change whose risks are not analyzed.

The labor products check out: register ≈1,584 hours, financial-document review 6,900, normalization 6,500, credit analysis ≈16,317, entity mismatch ≈1,604; 2,800/3,300 ≈85%, 1,160/1,750 ≈66%, and 650/7,600 ≈8.5%. Loop, variant, and pool facts are generally used in the right places. Ranking A11 last, holding A3 until escalation capacity exists, and pairing A5 with A6 match the resource note and the control findings.

The annualization does not. Frequencies have no stated window, yet hours are repeatedly “per year,” and mismatch labor is “about a third” of three specialists. That capacity share is only true under an unstated ~1,600 hours per FTE. On that same basis, ~30,000 analyst hours against 9 FTE is roughly double a plausible year and is never reconciled. Relative effort across activities is still meaningful; the capacity fractions are not established.

Classify → KYC 7,250 versus Classify → financial-document review 3,940 is treated as the 8% misroute. Those arcs are the main recorded split (and the log already flattens concurrency). The 8% is a separate exception rate. That misreading is repeated as evidence of wrong control paths.

Section 2 says contract generation depends on A3/A4 data quality. The rollout builds and gates A9 before A4 and leaves A3 to year 2. Machine-readable KYC status could stand alone, but the text as written contradicts its own sequence. A4 is also said to unblock the KYC → credit-analysis arc of 4,200; that arc is the direct flow, not the 1,750 mismatch cases.

Raising collateral requests at classification is a sound synchronization idea and is correctly kept outside the module budget. It is recommended without the failure modes that follow from the same evidence: an 8% classification error rate, valuation required for only 71% of equipment finance and 18% of working capital, and external cost on cases later declined. “A7 instead of A9 is only slightly better for cycle time” needs a product mix the log does not give. Median waits are also added per avoided document loop (8h + 18h + customer delay) as if medians were path times.

“No pool sees extra inflow without matching capacity” is too strong. Mismatch resolution releases stalled cases downstream; faster contracts move work to signing and activation. The steady-state point is also missed for A1: with a 1.5h wait and afternoon slack, registration is not metering annual volume, so “it floods analysts” overstates a small release. The better objections — few hours, short wait, 4% rework, position 2 in 98% of cases — are already sufficient. Missing-document requests are a back-office activity; describing A5 as shifting chasing from analysts and relationship managers, and elsewhere as giving the back office more follow-up, blurs whether that pool’s load rises or falls.

The required sections, columns, three contrast portfolios, control constraints, two deep integrations, and segment-level metrics are all present. Gates such as “agreed accuracy threshold” and “critical-path share falls from 82%” are not yet operational definitions. The preamble sits outside the specified top-level sections. None of these overturn the portfolio choice; together they keep the answer well short of nearly flawless.
