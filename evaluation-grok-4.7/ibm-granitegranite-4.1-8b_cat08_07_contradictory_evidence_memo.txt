5.5

The memo lands on a defensible hybrid (alternative E) and hits every required section, but it is not decision-safe. The operating policy contradicts itself, the process-mining diagnosis is wrong, and the value-of-information ranking does not govern the action it recommends.

The executive memo and the final recommendation describe different rollouts. Section 1 scales Redesign R for simple low-risk houses with survey skipping disabled. Section 6 disables survey skipping and post-billing activation only “for all other cases,” which leaves those two changes in place for the scaled segment. Management cannot tell which process simple houses would actually run. Calling that package “Redesign R” is also inaccurate: R is four bundled changes, and the memo is silently dropping two of them.

The fitness/precision explanation inverts the diagnostics. Fitness 0.94 means the discovered model can replay the pilot log; the mining team attributes that to many cases following the simplified coordinator path. Precision 0.51 means the model allows too many unobserved combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start. Frequent move-on-log alignments are mismatches, not a reason fitness is high, and “varied handovers” is not one of those alignments. Handovers fell. Generalization falling from 0.70 to 0.56 is never mentioned. DFG edge shifts, the handover network versus the working-together network, and the utilization swing (field survey 78% to 52%, second-visit team 66% to 92%) are likewise unused, so the capacity consequence of pausing survey skips is invisible.

The median-versus-tail story treats the gap as a clean treatment effect. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, segment and building-risk attributes are missing before week 3, the windows differ (10 weeks / 24,000 cases versus 6 weeks / 9,200), and start timestamps are sparse. Those are facts about identification, not background color. The evidence table records headline KPI movements and then labels causal claims as interpretations, but it leaves the main confounders out and misfiles some stated findings as assumptions. “3 handovers” drops the “3+”. Old-building harm is pinned on the skip rule; the alignment evidence points to missing permit checks, a different mechanism. Visible cost EUR 184 to 161 versus an incomplete fully loaded estimate of EUR 198 to 207 is presented as if the hidden-cost increase were already measured.

The M1–M8 ranking disagrees with its own rationale. The text calls M1 and M6 decisive for the skip rule, then ranks M6 sixth and groups M3 with the ancillary items after ranking M3 third. M4 can overturn the claim that the redesign saves money, and M7 watches the exact billing-before-activation and missing-permit behaviors the memo says it will pause, yet both sit below timestamp instrumentation and reason codes. M5 is said to be capable of showing that coordinator ownership is unsafe, while sections 1 and 6 scale that ownership before the test. The 8–12 week window cannot deliver M4: p95 cycle time is already 62 days, and downstream cost is defined over 60 days after billing start. Thresholds are not identified from the evidence: first-time-right at 80% is below the 82% baseline, and coordinator utilization at 85% is an invented resilience cutoff. Alternative A is dismissed rather than given conditions under which it would be reasonable.

The posture—do not scale nationally, do not roll back the simple-house gain, unbundle the harmful rules, measure before a wider rollout—is the right neighborhood. The memo does not specify one policy, does not separate mix shift from mechanism, and does not tie the next measurements to a fork that would actually change that policy.
