7.6

The design gets the central scientific point right and then loses consistency when that point is turned into an estimand, a cohort rule, and code. Rejected, withdrawn, and open cases are correctly treated as undefined or censored rather than as non-defaults; the 12.5% versus 7.4% illustration is arithmetically right; the 48,000-case partition adds up; leakage, temporal validation, and performative deployment effects are handled at a high level. The score is held down by contradictions in the primary outcome, a stage-1 clock that does not match the estimand, and an identification gap in the validation scheme.

The primary target is a policy-relative cumulative incidence of “activated within 60 days and defaulted within 90 days,” with rejection, withdrawal, and transfer as competing events. That is a defensible factual estimand, and the integral correctly uses an activation sub-density rather than a single-event hazard. Section 1 then puts unresolved-at-day-60, including accepted-but-not-taken-up, into \(Y\) as NTU/UNRES, “a real outcome state, not censoring.” Section 2 assigns all 5,100 still-open cases to administrative censoring at the event cutoff, and Section 3 makes “censor F2” the primary analysis, with NTU only a sensitivity. Those cannot be the same estimand. A case observed with no terminal event through day 60 has completed the horizon; censoring it past day 60 is a different statistical object from calling survival-to-60 a state. The text also never states that an acceptance is labeled NTU only after the 60-day window has been observed, which is the only reading that keeps later activation possible.

The skeleton implements the weaker reading. `t_decision` is the minimum of acceptance, rejection, withdrawal, and transfer, and `s1_days` is time to that timestamp. Acceptance therefore ends stage 1, even though the declared stage-1 causes are activation, rejection, withdrawal, transfer, and NTU. Any accepted case without `Account Activated` becomes `F_accepted_not_activated` immediately, including a late-December acceptance that is still inside the activation window when the log stops. The hooks then tell the reader to expand `(s1_days, status)` into the person-period likelihood. For activated accounts that time is time-to-accept, not time-to-activate, so the incidence being summed in \(F_{BAD}\) is not the incidence defined in Section 1. `t_act` is stored, but the variable the pipeline actually builds for stage 1 is the wrong clock. The day-60 cap is also absent, so long-open cases are censored at 31 December 2025 rather than resolved at the horizon.

A second structural gap is the regime term. The estimand is P2-specific, and Sections 1 and 3 specify a regime indicator plus interactions, while Section 5 tells the model to include that indicator. The primary split trains and tunes only on starts before 1 July 2025. A regime coefficient and its interactions are not identified there; fitting them as specified yields a constant. Transport, recalibration, and a P2-only stage 1 appear later as extra checks, which is the right instinct, but they do not repair the primary specification. The deployment step then refits on all data and recalibrates on P2, using the same P2 cases in both steps. The test window is also not cleanly pre-migration: starts through 14 October can still activate or be reviewed after 15 October, so stage-1 outcome events in “test” cross the rename boundary that the monitor split was meant to isolate.

Smaller design defects sit on the same as-of theme. The straddler flag is listed as a cohort feature, but it is defined by a decision time that has not happened at a 48-hour landmark, so it leaks future timing; the code happens to omit it. Repaired resource experience still counts only post-1 January 2024 history, so it rises with calendar time across a temporal split, and no burn-in is specified. “About 7%” refers to all resolved applications, not to the undecided-at-landmark population actually scored. The 13 August 2025 maturity bound is about a day early relative to a 15 February 2026 cutoff and a 185-day lag. B1 is described as still inside the 90-day window, while the code uses `age <= 90 days`, so the completed-window boundary falls into the in-window bin.

The leakage table itself is strong: `final_limit`, `decision_reason`, `collection_status`, post-decision events, same-day `documents_total`, as-of `risk_score`, extraction-time experience, and `label_available_at` are all given the right disposition. Two implementation misses undercut the audit. `risk_score_asof` is `groupby.last()`, which is the last event’s value, not the last non-null value the comment and the availability rule require. Duplicate removal collapses any same canonical activity within 60 seconds and does not require a differing `source_system`, so legitimate rework can be deleted and then appear in loop counts. `split` is joined into the modeling frame and is not in `FORBIDDEN`. Feed-confirmed defaults with no `Default Recorded` timestamp are placed at day 90, which moves event times to the horizon.

Prefix rules, the refusal to pad short traces, landmarking, label-time constraints on target encoding, and the explicit rejection of `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` and `insert_case_service_waiting_time` are all sound. The PM4Py calls that remain (`format_dataframe`, `discover_dfg`, `get_prefixes_from_log`, `get_variants`) match the intended use, and survival fitting is correctly left outside PM4Py. Those are real strengths. They do not offset an outcome definition that disagrees with its own censoring rule, or a cohort builder that would estimate a different stage-1 time than the one the study claims to target.
