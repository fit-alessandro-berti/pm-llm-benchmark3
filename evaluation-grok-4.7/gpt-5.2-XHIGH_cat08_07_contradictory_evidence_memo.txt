7.7

The memo is a coherent, constraint-respecting decision document: it keeps national scale and full rollback off the table, separates a facts list from a causal reading, uses segment heterogeneity, and ties measurements to go/no-go choices. Numbers and process-mining claims that are stated are generally faithful to the evidence. It is not nearly flawless.

The operational plan contradicts itself. Immediate actions suspend survey skipping for apartments, old buildings, and medium/high-risk addresses, which should push field-survey load back up from 52%, and in the same step move capacity off the survey team onto second visits and activation. Survey utilization is an outcome of the skip rule being reversed, so that reallocation is the wrong direction.

About 19% of pilot volume is left without a path. Business customers (manual activation and billing disputes) and rural installs (weaker rescheduling, worse tail and first-time-right) are neither simple low-risk houses nor the apartment/old-building control set. The hybrid never says which process they stay on.

Case-mix is treated as an unsigned uncertainty. The pilot regions already had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline. That mix works against a faster median and can inflate the aggregate tail, complaints, and rework. The memo never uses that direction, so the median gain is under-credited and the aggregate deterioration is over-attributed to the redesign.

Duration evidence is firmer in the recommendation than the log supports. Start timestamps are sparse, and the pilot window is 6 weeks against a 10-week baseline, while p90 is 38.5 days and p95 is 62 days. Those tails are then turned into numeric rollout gates. The same section requires thresholds “sustained for ≥4 weeks,” which cannot fully observe a 30-day complaint rate or the 60-day cost study ranked first for decision value. The gates also mix an unstated 5% p90 tolerance, a 5-day p95 tolerance, a <1% billing-before-activation cap, a <2% permit-absence cap, and an 85% coordinator-utilization cap without labeling them as management choices or tying them to a baseline rate.

Fact and interpretation still bleed. The reported shift in complaints from waiting for an appointment to “installed but not working” and “billing before activation” is paraphrased as an inference. Missing permit checks are flagged as possible logging gaps, then made a stop-the-line rule without that caveat. In the fitness/precision explanation, sparse start timestamps are offered as a reason the model is permissive; timestamp sparsity undermines duration measurement, while low precision is the model allowing extra paths.

Process-mining coverage is incomplete relative to the evidence package. Generalization (0.70 to 0.56), the SLA split (worse overall, better for simple houses), the new combined validate/schedule edge, and the temporal worsening from installation to activation in apartments are thin or absent. The simpler handover network and the more centralized working-together network are collapsed into one fragility claim. Scaling the simple-house path in 4–6 weeks also does not make complete building-risk attributes a precondition, despite incomplete classification before pilot week 3 and the misrouting risk noted earlier.
