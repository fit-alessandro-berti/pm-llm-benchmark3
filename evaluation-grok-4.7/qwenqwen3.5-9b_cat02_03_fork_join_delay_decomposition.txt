3.5

The decomposition formulas are structurally sound, and the worked cases F02, F04, F07, and F11 match the timestamps. That is where the reliability ends.

The case table is wrong for 8 of 12 cases. `join_ready` is often not the latest branch end (F03, F05, F08, F10, F12), so the critical-branch labels contradict the table’s own end times. True control is B in 8 cases, A in 2 (F01, F11), and C in 2 (F04, F08). The claimed 4/4/4 split matches neither the data nor the table (that column is 6/4/2). Downstream queue is positive in every case (8–20 minutes); it is not zero in F01, F05, F10, and F12. Reported means for B/C queue, C service, and synchronization wait disagree with both the evidence and the table, and the sync-wait ranking is inverted.

Section 4 treats shortening A as able to increase `join_ready` and produce a negative benefit. A max join cannot move later when one argument decreases. The masking point is missed: under a fixed final-station start, those join reductions are absorbed by downstream wait. Counterfactual 2 uses a false F12 baseline (about 35 minutes saved versus 15). Counterfactual 3 assigns 20 minutes saved to cases whose own downstream figures are not 20.

Section 3 ranks A as the main driver; section 5 then prioritizes B queueing. The third recommendation is to standardize A service, which is already 50 minutes in every case. “Increases `final_end`” reverses the effect of extra final capacity.

The Python would fail before printing (`datetime` minus `datetime.time`, `int`/`Timedelta` on series). Even if repaired, counterfactuals 1–2 recompute the join but leave `final_start` and `final_end` unchanged, so savings are zero. It does not call `pm4py.format_dataframe`, does not use completion `time:timestamp`, and does not report per-case savings. The double-count argument invokes a zero-length interval instead of showing that the three branch waits triple-count one fork-to-join window.

Section 7 does cover causality, capacity, preemption, and release policy versus queueing.
