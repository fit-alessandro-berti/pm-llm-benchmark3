The answer covers every required heading, all fifteen cases, and a real split between control-flow faults, overlays, and resource rules. Several hard calls are sound: SUP-703, SUP-708, and SUP-712 are serious noncompliance; SUP-704 fits FD-7; SUP-705 and SUP-714 are outside expired authority; SUP-701 is compliant. The judgment also rejects parts of each brief. Those strengths sit beside material failures of evidence, semantics, and the revision the task demanded.

Section 1 stipulates CS-9, the overlay windows, and the CRM break, but it drops PD-3’s mandatory automated master-data validation and treats several contested points as settled. The burden of proof is usable, then not applied: ambiguous scope on SUP-709 and SUP-710 is later called a high-confidence model defect. “Concurrently” is used loosely for an unordered partial order.

The prosecution is not the strongest process-aware case. It never uses the aggregate record (312 auto-approvals, 14 ineligible, 9 incomplete eligibility records, missingness rising from 2% to 29%, six post-pilot cases, 47 information requests). It calls FD-7 “emergency emails,” although the source is a dated directive. It labels SUP-712 “fraud” when the evidence shows one person under two aliases, not intent. It spends the anti-retroactivity argument on SUP-707, which does not violate CS-9, and it does not concede the genuine model and logging defects the prosecution was required to concede.

The defense overreaches in the other direction. It says cross-system audit trails “confirm” SUP-706’s notification; the link is a mutable text field written two days later. It excuses SUP-705 as good-faith continuity. It never cabins SUP-703, SUP-708, SUP-712, SUP-714, SUP-715, or the ineligible auto-approvals, so it does not meet the requirement to acknowledge real risk and the limits of informal practice.

The matrix is complete and often precise, but some reversal conditions are off-target, and SUP-706 is described as both proved and indeterminate. Classifications then repeat that contradiction: the dominant reason asserts that the notification occurred. SUP-711 is determinate duplication of one compliant registration, not an indeterminate case. SUP-713 is marked compliant without dealing with mapping approval a week after the event. Severity and confidence are missing for SUP-701, SUP-702, and SUP-704. System-level “moderately impaired” and “obsolete” never reckon with the aggregate counts.

The layered architecture is the right idea and the wrong details. Two business days is equated with 48 hours in the prose and the overlay table. The COI control bans a shared reporting line unless a waiver exists; the stated duty is disclosure, and no rule prohibits the relationship. Withdrawals, which the task required, are absent. Reactivation is one sentence and is not in the policy table. POWL, DECLARE or log-skeleton constraints, pandas checks, and organizational controls are not actually assigned.

The Python model has the right surface inventory and would not run as a sound POWL. The same `sanctions` and `activate` objects are placed in more than one XOR branch, so those nodes have multiple parents. `skip_info` is unused. `LOOP(quality, request_info)` means quality first and then optional request-and-quality cycles; SUP-707 is two information requests before any quality review, and N5 places those cycles before the decision. The comment misstates that loop. Once the standard branch is chosen, activation is mandatory: there is no rejection after both checks, no withdrawal, and no reactivation without registration. Pilot and emergency traces remain language-fit outside their dates, risk, spend, and master-data rules. The limitations note mentions data, identity, and time, but not these control-flow holes. The logging schema covers notification IDs, aliases, overlay IDs, and dual timestamps, and omits rule/version IDs, delegation evidence, a full eligibility snapshot, and an authority artifact.
