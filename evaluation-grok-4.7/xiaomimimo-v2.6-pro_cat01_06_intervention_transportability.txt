The answer is a strong transportability analysis: it keeps Site A’s nine-hour drop unidentified, refuses to start document checks before originals, ties the effect to partial order and binding edges, and specifies a segmented pilot with a manipulation check, a control arm, and stop rules. Format, required nodes, and the statutory split are handled carefully. It is not nearly flawless.

The quantitative layer does not hold together. Arrival rate is about 26 cases per working day in the capacity arithmetic and about 0.78 cases per calendar hour in the Little’s-law WIP figures, so the “~34 cases in flight” and verification-WIP claims mix clocks. Little’s law is then called consistent with the median shift, but it identifies a mean, and with p90/median ≈ 2.5 the mean and median are not interchangeable. The density share used to turn a 2.4–3.4 hour stratum shift into a 1–2.5 hour pooled median is asserted, not derived. Scenario signs are inconsistent: “3 to 5 h” means improvement, while “+0 to +3 h (worse)” means deterioration. Several p90 intervals, especially +8 to +22 hours, are not mapped from the stated evidence.

The design contradicts itself. Track 2 in the intervention and the POWL model runs identity and document checks concurrently after originals receipt. The recommendation and the preferred scenario tell the second wave not to parallelize document check and only to absorb identity work into the mail delay. The pooled “3 to 5 h” benefit also assumes that identity is newly overlapped with the 18-hour originals delay, despite the directly-follows counts already showing substantial registration-to-originals and identity-to-originals sequencing.

The pilot has a real interference problem. Case-level randomization plus a rule that a reviewer never works both arms, applied to pools of 4, 3, and 2 people, splits scarce capacity and can create the assessment and legal congestion attributed to parallelization. Originals-receipt time is a weak negative control because the lock and logging workaround change that timestamp. The sample-size note is partly wrong: with the stated log-cycle-time SD of about 0.72, 80% power for a 15% median reduction is about 300 per arm, not 250. The 12% figure (~500) is right.

Smaller faults remain. “Register is the only genuine predecessor” is not true for Site B document check. The 2.4-hour lower bound and several WIP, throttle, and validity parameters are unanchored. Verification productivity per FTE at B includes assessors and legal staff, unlike Site A. The assumption register never tests the calendar-versus-working-time choice that drives the WIP math. The PM4Py construction is substantively right and appropriately limited, but it was not shown to run.
