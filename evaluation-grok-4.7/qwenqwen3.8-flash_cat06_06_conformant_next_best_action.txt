The answer is a strong, well-structured conformance analysis: it keeps second-officer review mandatory, ranks immediate execution and self-certification last, runs alternate screening in parallel rather than treating it as a substitute for review, and explicitly prices the spillover onto P-901. The six required sections, labels, and A–G coverage are all present. It is not nearly flawless.

The clearest error is the false-rejection probability in the Action F continuation. Ultimate conformant approval is given as 0.55–0.75, so rejecting now is a false rejection with probability about 0.55–0.75. The answer states 0.25–0.45, which is the probability that the rejection would have matched the conformant outcome. It then calls the full conditional harm band of €15k–€80k an “expected” harm. Section 3 later points the probability the right way, but it never corrects the number.

The fallback timing does not close. Action C needs about 40–90 minutes. A switch “by 60–65 minutes” finishes after the 90-minute deadline, so it cannot protect the deadline value that justifies interrupting P-901. The later trigger (rerank as soon as a firm S4 arrival estimate exists) is the coherent rule, and it conflicts with that clock-time fallback.

S4’s review is given as 35–75 minutes. That duration is stated only for expedited review by S2. Using it as S4’s time is what makes the primary plan “likely” to miss the deadline and is not identified as an assumption. Action C is also timed at 40–90 minutes while a sequential 0–15 minute post-evidence routing step is added; the upper end is then about 105 minutes, not 90.

Ranking is under-justified at the margin. Section 5 says that if P-901’s deadline value is comparable to €20k–€35k, the expected spillover is only several thousand euros, which is smaller than P-884’s deadline benefit, yet the deadline-risking S4 plan stays first without a dominance argument that survives that arithmetic. Evidence request (6–24 hours, almost certain deadline loss plus hold harm) is ranked above a fast conditional rejection without a direct comparison. Citing the four-eyes rule against immediate execution is slightly off: that action fails because there is no second officer, not because the two reviewers are the same person.

The primary family of recommendation is still the right one: no execution until an independent second review, with screening in parallel and P-901 left intact unless a late S4 forces an explicit spillover choice. The probability inversion and the inconsistent decision clock are enough to keep it well below a near-flawless score.
