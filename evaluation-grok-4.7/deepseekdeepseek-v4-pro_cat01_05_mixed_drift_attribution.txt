6.2

The write-up keeps the right qualitative story and hits the required sections, but the quantitative core is unreliable and several mandated checks are missed or overstated.

The mechanism timeline is directionally sound: March is logging and semantic change, May is supplier-linked rework, June–July mixes capacity loss with the priority rule, October is seasonal case mix, and December is confounded. Mapped fitness is correctly treated as evidence of later real drift. The hypothesis, segmentation, discrimination, and monitor tables cover the required mechanisms and artifacts, and sections 2 and 9 fall inside the word limits.

The baseline-relative table does not use the baseline it declares. It says Jan–Feb averages, but cycle time and fitness are differenced from January, while several percentages use the two-month mean. March median cycle time is about flat versus that average, not −0.3 h.

The DFG distances are not the Euclidean distances of the supplied rates. With the constant edge dropped, January versus later months is about 1.7, 19.1, 27.7, 12.9, 29.2, and 17.1. Only May matches the reported table. The text also calls seven relation rates eight. Ranking May, July, and October as large is still right, but the published measure is not reproducible. The code would compute a different baseline and different distances from the prose.

The service-time split is internally inconsistent: +0.6 h is about 3% of the +17.5 h cycle-time rise, yet the decomposition assigns service 0.9–1.5 h and 5–10%. The hour ranges look more precise than the aggregates support.

Section 3 required at least two candidate change points for logging, behavioral, and performance drift. Only logging and behavioral get one each.

Causal language is too strong in places. Supplier Z’s share roughly triples from January to May, or rises about 2.4× from April; it does not quadruple. March’s raw-fitness drop is not “completely” explained when mapped fitness still falls by about 0.02–0.03. “Little room for alternative explanations” and December’s claim that the gain exceeds a seasonal decline are not earned. Both median and p90 rising does not show that standard cases absorbed the priority rule.

Monitoring thresholds are poorly anchored. Rework above 15% and missing resources above 5% stay breached for most of the year, so they do not distinguish new drift from the new level. A service-time alert at 6 h, or rule 7’s “service changed by less than 10%,” would miss the observed move from about 4.8 h to 5.4 h. Choosing complete-resource cases as the primary population conflicts with the stated nonrandom missingness.

The Python sketch is structured correctly, but `resource_key` is not a standard `format_dataframe` argument, `len` on the monthly frame counts events rather than cases, and the change-point rule uses the standard deviation of two points and only rework.
