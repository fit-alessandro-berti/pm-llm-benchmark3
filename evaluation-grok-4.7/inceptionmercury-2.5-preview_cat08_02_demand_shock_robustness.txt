4.7

The write-up follows the required seven-part structure and correctly avoids a blanket capacity increase, but it is not a reliable redesign. Several routing rules contradict the log, multiple triggers fire and cancel under the same peak conditions, and the quantitative claims are often inconsistent with the DFG and the resource notes.

## Stress scenarios

The four scenarios cover the required disruption types, but they are loosely specified and one restates history. The winter wave is about +40% volume with Urgent at 30% and Complex-panel at 22%. Observed peak is already about +43% (9,900 cases in 3 weeks versus 18,400 in 8 weeks) with Urgent 29% and Complex-panel 20%. Scenario 1 is therefore the calibration period, not a further stress, and Routine and Repeat-after-failure shares are never set.

The M2 outage is treated as a 50% capacity loss plus “M1 fatigue” that raises repeats. The resource note says M2 has the higher failure rate and the faster runtime, and repeats cluster after night shifts and M2 use. Losing M2 removes the failure-prone machine and an unequal share of runtime. Combined peak utilization is 98%, so equal-capacity loss would leave M1 with demand near twice its capacity, not “>120%.” Those two figures cannot both be right.

The specialist scenario overstates the gate. Peak flow is Technical validation → Medical validation 3,420, → Release 4,050, and → Hold 610. Hold is the minority path. A five-day absence blocks that subset; it does not stop Complex-panel or medical validation as a class. The observed 36 h specialist queue already includes a specialist who is out only every second Friday, so it is the wrong magnitude for a five-day blackout. “High-risk” is not a case type in the data.

The courier scenario mixes a 08:00–10:00 window with “120% of normal daily intake in the first 4 hours,” and it applies peak clerk utilization (85%, 91%) without saying whether the compression sits on normal or peak load. Registration clerks, courier intake, and pre-analytical check are separate pools; the text collapses them into “clerks.”

## Bottleneck migration

The winter-wave paragraph labels pre-analytical check as the primary bottleneck because peak utilization is 96%. At that same peak, technical validation is already at 101% with a 16 h queue, assay at 98% with 18 h, medical validation at a 22 h queue, and specialist review at 36 h. The migration sequence is asserted rather than calculated. The sharp measured shifts are unused: batch-wait share of preparation exits rises from about 4.4% to 12.7%; assay-to-repeat share from about 6.7% to 14.8%; specialist-hold share of technical-validation exits from about 1.8% to 7.6%; and the medical-validation branch becomes roughly as large as direct release. Urgent bypass of batch wait falls from 74% to 58%.

“Relieving pre-analytical does not help if assay is starved” uses the wrong mechanism. In that scenario assay is the saturated station. Relieving pre-analytical adds WIP in front of assay; technical validation is what loses a steady feed.

The validation-gap paragraph says medical validators cannot proceed without specialist sign-off. Direct Technical validation → Medical validation remains open, so a specialist absence piles cases in Hold and can starve medical validation of Complex-panel work while Urgent cases that skip Hold still move.

The courier paragraph correctly points at intake, pre-analytical defects, and the two accession specialists. It stops before the rebound: after the morning bulge clears, preparation and assay absorb a burst, and recollection returns 42 h later in normal conditions and 60 h later at peak, tying up future intake because clerks spend time explaining errors.

## Robust package

I6 is a reasonable pre-analytical control, but the mechanism is misstated. Early rejection within 2 hours stops poor samples entering preparation and assay. It does not, on the evidence, shrink the recollection loop. Pre-analytical → Request recollection is 760 of 18,120 (about 4.2%) normally and 780 of 9,730 (about 8.0%) at peak; Request recollection → Receive sample is 540 and 520. The package claims fewer loops and, two sentences later, more recollections. The triage also consumes pre-analytical time at a station that is already at 96% in the peak, and faster clearance of good samples loads preparation and then assay. That downstream load is only a table cell.

I7 is robust to the 10:00 and 17:00 arrival spikes and little else. It does not relieve an M2 outage, a specialist absence, or a mix shift toward medical validation. Calling I6+I7 a core that stabilizes most of the four scenarios overclaims coverage. Smoothing also delivers a steadier stream into assay machines that are already at 98%.

Modified I4, cross-training one technical validator, contradicts its own safeguard. Peak technical-validation utilization is 101% and the median queue is 16 h. The stated pullback is “if the technical-validation queue grows beyond 16 h,” which is the ordinary peak state, so the extra accession capacity disappears exactly when the two specialists are at 91% and a 14 h queue. “Without depleting technical validation” is incompatible with moving a person out of a pool that is already over full. The 4-week cross-training lead time is omitted, so this cannot be a reactive control. Faster accession correction also pushes the newly unblocked cases onto preparation and assay.

Repeat-after-failure is 8–9% of volume and consumes assay capacity twice. No core intervention addresses it.

## Scenario policies

The Urgent bypass rule is largely the current process, and it is already failing at peak: bypass falls from 74% to 58% because it fragments machine schedules. The proposal does not mention fragmentation. The on-trigger (Urgent above 25% of WIP, or pre-analytical queue above 9.5 h) and the off-trigger (technical-validation queue above 12 h) are simultaneously true in the observed peak, where pre-analytical median queue is 9.5 h and technical-validation median queue is 16 h. WIP mix is also the wrong base: slow Complex-panel and Repeat cases accumulate in WIP, so Urgent share of WIP is not arrival share. The conflict note says strict Urgent-first starves Routine and Complex-panel. The policy protects only a Routine batch cap of 12, which is the current batch size implied by I2, and then marks Complex-panel as unaffected.

I3’s trigger includes “M2 failure mode detected,” and the action routes Routine volume to M2. That routing is unavailable when M2 is the machine that failed. There is no separate M2 utilization series; only pooled M1/M2 utilization is given, so a 95% M2 trigger has no measurement. Sending the largest case class onto the higher-failure machine increases the repeat loop that consumes assay capacity twice. The switch-off (M1 under 90% and M2 stable) does not repair that interaction.

I10’s 30 h on / 10 h off band is a usable hysteresis, and peak specialist median queue is already 36 h, so outsourcing would be on through a normal peak, not only during absence. The 12 h transport delay is never compared with Hold → Medical validation medians of 18 h / 48 h. Returned results can also arrive later as a bolus at medical validation. I5 never appears in the trade-off table. Its on-condition uses technical-validation utilization above 80% (normal is already 76%) to start a medical-validation shift, and it does not say whether the evening shift is overtime for a pool whose errors rise after 9 h.

## Trade-off table

The columns are complete and I5 is missing. I6+I7 are scored Neutral for Urgent, Routine, and Complex-panel, which erases the point of adopting them: the recollection return costs 42–60 h and Urgent is penalized after 18 h, so inflow defects hit Urgent first. I4 is Neutral on every outcome, including cases stuck in a correction queue that moves from 3 h to 14 h. Dynamic priority scores Complex-panel Neutral, against the stated starvation conflict, and scores rework Neutral while Urgent bypass fragments assay schedules. I3 scores Routine Neutral on the same row that says Routine repeats may increase. “Next bottleneck” often names the donor pool (technical validation) rather than the station that receives the extra flow.

## Residual vulnerabilities

The section does include redesign-caused harms: I4 draining technical validation, Urgent priority stretching Routine, partner traceability, and evening-shift fatigue. Two of the sharper self-inflicted risks are absent. I3 feeds M2, which is the machine already tied to repeats and night-shift clusters. I6 can raise the immediate cancellation and physician-contact load (recollection → cancel is 290 of 830 normally and 370 of 890 at peak) while the pre-analytical bench is saturated. The residual list also treats Routine harm as a problem only if the batch cap is ignored. With the cap held at today’s size, Urgent-first still displaces Routine and Complex-panel inside assay and preparation.

## Dashboard

Leading and lagging lists are separated, then the thresholds are inverted or unmeasured. Pre-analytical “queue depth” is given in hours, with a warning above 6 h and a threshold above 5 h. Recollection has a warning above 10% and a threshold above 8%, while the trade-off table watches 10%. Peak recollection is already about 8% and overall rework is 11.8%, so an 8% recollection threshold is red for the whole peak and does not discriminate. Medical backlog uses 10 and 15 cases; the log gives hours, not queue counts, and at roughly 470 peak cases per day those counts are not an interpretable band.

Urgent p90 is given a target under 24 h. The contract penalty starts at 18 h, and normal-period Urgent p90 is already 19 h. The 18 h SLA line and the 24 h p90 target disagree. Technical-validation queue, the 101% pool, is not a daily indicator. Specialist hold hours are listed as lagging; queue time is a leading signal, while SLA compliance and cycle-time tails are the outcomes. Complex-panel and Repeat-after-failure tails (p90 210 h and 240 h at peak) are unmonitored, as are urgent-bypass rate, M1-versus-M2 repeat rate, accession queue, and cancellation rate.

The operating philosophy—pre-commit a small inflow package and switch the risky rules with explicit triggers—is the right shape. The package as written would send Routine work to a failed analyzer, turn Urgent priority on and off in the same peak week, and watch warning bands that sit on the wrong side of their own thresholds.
