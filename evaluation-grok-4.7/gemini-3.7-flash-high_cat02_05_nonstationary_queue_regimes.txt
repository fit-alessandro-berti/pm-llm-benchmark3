The regime story is coherent and several central results check out, but the write-up is not numerically or conceptually reliable enough for a higher mark. Outage versus recovery is handled properly, the April 20 mix/service step and the April 29 staffing step are real, and most primary-regime means match the table: baseline weekdays average 100.14 arrivals and 99.57 completions (net +4), the outage day is 105 versus 58 (net +47), mix-shift weekdays are 114.0 versus 100.57 (net +94), and the intervention weekdays are 115 versus 121 (net −18). Open-hours Little’s Law scaling, \(W=(L/\lambda)\times 12\), is applied with the right units, and the code would reproduce the regime split, hourly Fano factors, and those flow times. The April 7 peak mismatch is a genuine audit finding: bins 08:00–10:00 are 17/101, while 09:00–11:00 is 32/101 against a reported 31%.

The required pooled comparison does not survive recomputation. All-weekend arrivals are \(359/8=44.88\), not 45.38. All-weekday staff is \(123/20=6.15\) FTE, not 6.20. Weekday mean median service is \(611/20=30.55\), not 30.95; weekday p90 is 66.6, not 67.85; weekend p90 is 68.25, not 69.75; weekday complex share is 33.35%, not 34.50%. Those figures are repeated in the prose and are not produced by the script. Mix-shift weekday WIP dispersion is about 4.54, not 4.39. The stated pre-shift complex share of 21.8% does not match the pre-April 20 rows. R4’s \(\rho\approx 0.77\) is inconsistent with the same lognormal mapping that gives about 0.80.

Several interpretations overreach the evidence. Baseline operation is called a stationary M/G/c in statistical control, while the same answer’s April 7 hour-bin Fano factor is 3.23 and is described as diurnal non-homogeneity. That hour-bin CV is then inserted into Kingman’s formula as \(c_a^2\), with the claim that waiting time triples. Bin-count CV across a scheduled day is not the interarrival \(c_a^2\), and Kingman does not apply to the supercritical mix-shift regime. Mean service is inferred from a lognormal fitted to the median and p90, then shown as traffic intensity at high or very high confidence, without a sensitivity bound. The directional overload conclusion is still supported by completions stuck near 100 against about 114 arrivals, but only if mean service exceeds roughly 38 minutes; 43.9 minutes is a model, not a measurement.

WIP is treated as a backlog trajectory. Mix-shift weekday means go 37, 41, 46, 44, 43, 49, 50, so the build is not monotonic. Weekend means fall sharply (Friday 43 to Saturday 23) while weekend net flow stays positive. On April 7, hourly net flow plus mean WIP about 20 implies a start near 18 and a close near 20, so a material set of cases sits through the closed 12 hours. Section 6 nevertheless marks baseline carry-over as low and says the queue clears daily. The 2.43-hour figure is an open-hours mean only; subtracting the 25-minute median from that mean to get 121 minutes of waiting mixes a median with a mean. R5 and R6 ratios are labeled both “classical Little’s Law fails” and “approximate only,” and are then read as the delay facing current arrivals during a +13 case/day drift.

The one-hour peak-offset explanation fits April 7, 15, and 25, but not April 21, where 08:00–09:59 is about 10% and 09:00–10:59 is about 37% against a reported 47%. The staffing date is treated as a clean break even though the prompt says the start time was not logged. “Permanently” increased weekend capacity is stronger than one observed weekend at 3.0 FTE. The roster sketch sums to 10 overlapping FTE in late morning, not the stated 8. The 7.5 FTE recommendation does not carry forward the coding-change ambiguity raised earlier, despite the same-day jump in peak share being the best evidence that April 20 is more than a relabeling. The segmentation “heuristic” hard-codes those dates rather than finding them.
