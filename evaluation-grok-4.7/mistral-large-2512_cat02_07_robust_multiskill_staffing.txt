4.2

The write-up follows the required nine-part outline and transcribes the demand, skill, shift, and service tables correctly. That is the main credit. The quantitative argument does not hold together, the recommendation contradicts the comparison the author just reported, and the simulation does not implement the policies or the penalties it is supposed to test.

**Capacity model.** A multiplier of 1.25 means the resource takes 25% longer than the base draw. Section 2 treats multipliers as capacity weights: Farah’s electrical 1.40 is counted as 1.40 capacity units, Emil’s 1.30 as more capacity than Chen’s 1.00, and validation “capacity” is the sum 1.15+1.00+1.10+1.00+1.00. That inverts speed. Qualified people are also dropped or included off shift. Morning mechanical capacity omits Farah, who works 08:00–12:00. Afternoon electrical capacity is only Deepa and Emil, omitting Ada and Chen, both still on shift. Afternoon software includes Farah, whose shift has already ended. Breaks are not removed. Intake is never loaded. The same full block of each person’s time is given to every skill they can do, so the resulting “utilization” is not a person’s utilization and cannot be compared with the 90% fragility threshold.

The arithmetic that is shown is not stable. For 08:00–10:00 electrical, the text writes \(7\times(0.25+0.10)=3.85\). The product is 2.45; 3.85 is \(7\times0.55\), i.e. E+ME+ES, which the formula does not say. Expected electrical time is \(28.5\times0.82+52.5\times0.18=32.82\), not 32.67. Later blocks then drop the ME branch, so electrical load is not defined the same way twice. U2 renormalization is wrong: doubling E and ES on 12:00–14:00 gives total weight 140 and E share 40%, not 43.8%; on 14:00–16:00 the total is 142, not 112, and E share is about 42%, not 53.6%. U3’s afternoon ratio divides by Emil alone (1.30×120). Chen is the absent person; Deepa is still there, and Chen was not even in the base afternoon denominator, so the jump from 49.7% to 88% does not measure Chen’s absence. U1 applies the 08:00–10:00 type mix to both morning blocks and then calls 29% utilization a bottleneck. U5 uses 7 base arrivals in a scenario that has already scaled that block by 1.7, treats the intake outage as zero arrivals rather than a processing blackout, and still labels a self-computed 33% utilization a bottleneck. Offered load also excludes handover, setup, the 8-minute parallel coordination delay, and the 10% restart penalty, without a bound on how large those terms are.

**Parallelism and SLA feasibility.** The parallel rule is “sum of the two branch durations, including coordination, is less than the sequential duration.” That sum is essentially \(T_1+T_2+8\), which is always larger than sequential \(T_1+T_2\), so the rule never chooses parallel. The relevant comparison is the makespan \(\max(T_1,T_2)+8\), plus extra handovers, against \(T_1+T_2\) on one cross-skilled resource. That omission matters because P1 is an elapsed-time target of 120 minutes. Sequential ME/ES, complex software, slow multipliers, and a 7-minute handover on a resource change can exceed 120 with an empty queue. A best-path ordinary electrical case on Chen sits under 120 only if handovers are avoided; the same case on Ada, or an ES case run sequentially, does not. The tables report base P1 attainment of 92–95% and mean “delay” of 35–55 minutes without stating whether delay is wait or sojourn, and without checking this service-time floor. Mean sojourn of about 45 minutes is already tight relative to ordinary processing time and implausible as sojourn once ME/ES and complex tails are in the mix.

**Policies.** The three policies are recognizable, but they are not implementable as written. Strict specialization says each person keeps only a primary skill, then assigns Chen both electrical and software and Farah every skill. Reservation in the hybrid policy names Ada, Chen, and Deepa but never says how much time, how many slots, or what a P1 case does when the reserved person is busy. The preemption floor is 5 minutes in the assumptions and 10 minutes in Policy 3. SERPT is used to pick a server, which is a fastest-eligible-resource rule, not shortest expected remaining processing time among cases. The case-level team-change penalty (mechanical versus electrical/software, unless the same person keeps the case) is conflated with the resource-level 5-minute setup for switching skill family between consecutive cases. Overtime of 30 minutes never enters a rule.

**Table and recommendation.** Section 2’s worst base electrical utilization is about 50% on the author’s own arithmetic. Section 4’s base maximum utilization is 85–90% for strict specialization and 75–80% even for pooling. Those cannot both be right, and neither is computed from a stated queueing or simulation model. U1–U5 rows drop P2 SLA, fairness, handover burden, and failure mode, so the required policy-by-scenario comparison is incomplete. Ranges have no method: not seeds, not bounds, not sensitivity.

Section 5 then says the hybrid keeps P1 SLA above 85% and maximum utilization below 95% in every scenario. The same table shows hybrid U5 at 70–80% P1 SLA and 95–100% utilization, and U1 at 80–85% and 90–95%. On every reported delay, SLA, utilization, and resilience cell, full pooling is better. Hybrid’s base failure mode is “P1 starvation,” which is the failure the reservation was supposed to prevent. The fairness objective adds utilization, utilization standard deviation, and raw handover minutes with coefficients 0.1 and 0.5, so the minutes term swamps the rest. Neither objective is evaluated on the table. Robustness across U1–U5 is never defined as worst-case SLA, regret, or a satisficing constraint. The fallback also requires handover burden below 15 minutes per case in order to switch to the policy the author says has the highest handover burden.

**Process mining and code.** The event list misses pause/resume and a real queue-entry event, and it does not record planned versus actual service. Case-level output of `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is treated as stage waiting. `filter_case_performance` with both bounds at 120 does not measure the share of cases completed within 120. `get_resource_utilization` and `discover_bottleneck_net` are not analyses that support the claims attached to them. Drift is named and then not given a method.

The Python block encodes the tables and calls the requested PM4Py functions, but it is not evidence for Sections 2–5. U4’s tail multiplier is never applied. The outage is attached to a block label, `09:30-10:00`, that is not in the demand table, so it never fires. Arrivals are exact truncated counts, not a stochastic process, and they are packed into roughly the first half of each block, so later blocks can overlap or be skipped. There is one seed and no uncertainty summary. If a case is not free-assigned at arrival, it is not reliably woken; unfinished cases are dropped from the metrics. Completion advances the activity index twice, which skips diagnostic work. `handover_count` and `setup_count` are never incremented, so handover burden is identically zero and the 7-minute and team-change delays do not occur. Parallel execution is never chosen. Priority is FIFO, not P1 before P2 before P3. Strict mode is first-fit in name order, so Ada absorbs electrical work. Hybrid sorting compares `Resource` objects and raises `TypeError` when two candidates share a priority. Preemption is disabled for the policy that allows it, is enabled for the others, and tests the P1 case’s own remaining time rather than the victim activity. Utilization resamples new service times instead of using simulated work. Breaks, shift end, and the 30-minute overtime cap do not constrain the completion clock. The event log mixes lifecycle labels into `concept:name` and leaves `start_timestamp` null, so the PM4Py service-time calls do not measure activity service.

**Validation section.** The limits list is generic and does not say what this input cannot identify: SLA percentages, the ranking of these three policies, or a person’s utilization under shared skills. The pilot removes P1 cases, which means it cannot test P1 reservation or the P1 target, and it runs treatment and control at the same time on the same staff, so the two policies contaminate each other.

The directional story — electrical work is the scarce skill, afternoon coverage is thinner than the morning, Chen’s absence and an electrical surge move the bottleneck, and pure specialization is brittle — is plausible. It is not established by the calculations, and the selected policy is not the best trade-off on the numbers the answer itself reports.
