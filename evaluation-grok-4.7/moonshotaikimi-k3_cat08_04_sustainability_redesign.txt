The final answer meets every required section and constraint. It defines a partial order-to-delivery boundary, separates measured kWh, kgCO2e, and mass from unmeasured upstream and end-of-life burdens, and refuses to treat avoided downtime as a carbon offset. The mix-weighted baseline is arithmetically correct: about 7.4 kWh, 10.3 kgCO2e, and 0.35 kg scrap per order, with made-to-order work carrying roughly 87% of energy and 85% of scrap and critical orders about 41% of transport. All twelve options name a path or segment, a mechanism, service/quality/resilience effects, and log evidence. The effects table uses the required columns and stays qualitative except where the prompt supplies anchors. Rebound coverage includes batching, obsolescence, mode shift, packaging, rework, partial shipments, and customer behavior. The package keeps true downtime on a fast justified route, retains air and dedicated vans when slack or SP2 requires them, and keeps reinforced packaging under Q1. Validation covers SP1/SP2/Q1, segment service, exception governance, absolute versus intensity metrics, and promise-date gaming.

The deductions are for repeated and smaller rigor problems, not for missing structure. Transport is written throughout as “kgCOe,” dropping the 2 from kgCO2e. The same path switch used to call a move from 52 to 4.8 kgCO2e a large transport effect is labeled only a small-to-moderate energy effect, even though that path pair is 7.0 versus 1.2 kWh. Those path contrasts are also confounded by production mode, packaging, and which orders can actually switch, and the table presents them too cleanly as intervention effects. “1,010 cases” conflates DFG transition counts with cases. “At least 85%” is slightly looser than the stated “above 85%,” and the 75–83% air reduction is a derived precision the prompt does not itself state. Shared-lane savings are called moderate-to-large without a magnitude in the evidence. None of these reverse the recommendation, but they keep the answer short of nearly flawless.
