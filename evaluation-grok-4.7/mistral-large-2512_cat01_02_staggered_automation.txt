The write-up follows the required section structure and correctly treats availability, use, selection, co-occurring shocks, tails, and customer-satisfaction nonresponse as distinct problems. Those merits do not offset incorrect arithmetic, contradictory signs, an identification strategy the code does not estimate, and reported tests that the code never runs.

Section 5 does not match the data under the windows it declares. Alpha’s early post means are roughly right, but Beta’s April–June rework mean is 11.9, not 11.3, so the reported −1.5 point change is wrong. Gamma’s January–March satisfaction mean is 4.06, not 4.02. The never-treated April–June levels are also wrong: median cycle time is about 37.4 to 43.7 hours, not 38.3 to 44.5, and the escalation change is about +1.7 points, not +1.0. Subtracting the adopters’ percent change from the controls’ percent change is not a difference-in-differences when the bases differ. The resulting “−13%” bound is arithmetic, not a sensitivity result. Calling the controls’ worsening an “improvement” reverses the exercise.

The Gamma window is the more serious design error. January–March is not the pre-period for a July adoption. From April–June to July–August, Gamma’s median falls by about 6.5 hours and its p90 falls by about 9 hours. The published table instead reports −0.8% and +9.7% by comparing July–August with January–March. That choice also misses Gamma’s untreated deterioration through June, which tracks the controls. Section 5 then says Gamma “improved” before adoption while citing 36.4 → 39.6. Cycle time rose. The later drop back toward the winter level makes regression to the mean more plausible, not less. Section 6 says selection and regression to the mean overstate benefits, so the two sections disagree.

Signs are not stable inside the answer. Section 4 says Alpha and Beta medians fell and Gamma’s p90 fell. Section 5 shows the opposite for those windows. Section 7 says Gamma’s tail improved and that Alpha’s rework rose by 1.3 points; section 5 says Gamma’s p90 rose and Alpha’s rework change was zero. The mechanism table’s expected signatures are blank, so it does not actually state the beneficial or harmful pattern.

The reported correlation p-values, the intensity coefficient −0.25 with interval (−0.48, −0.02), and the placebo p-value 0.87 are not outputs of the supplied program. The program contains neither the correlation nor the placebo. Raw within-branch associations are near zero for Alpha and positive for Beta, so a precisely estimated −2.5 hours per 10-point intensity increase is not credible as a clustered five-branch result. The code also drops Delta and Epsilon, omits calendar-time effects, and lets the relative-time reference category fall wherever the formula interface drops it. That is not the never-treated event study described in section 3. “Branch × month fixed effects” in the second alternative is saturated in a branch-month panel and cannot be estimated.

Several estimands are marked identifiable despite confounding. The availability contrast for the tail is defined only on adopters, so it has no comparison. The complexity interaction is an ecological branch-month product, not a case-level heterogeneity effect. Conditioning on arrivals and staffing changes the intervention, because the routing campaign and several FTE changes coincide with adoption; the answer never separates the rollout package from an effect holding load fixed. Arrivals per FTE are never calculated, even though Alpha’s load rises from about 30 to more than 40 cases per productive FTE.

The claim ladder and section 7 overstate what leadership can say. A uniform percent reduction is the claim the memo was supposed to test, and it is not put in the unsupported category. “Up to 10%” and confidence 60 in a median reduction do not follow from a range of −13% to +5% or from early post-period medians that worsened. The escalation claim is labeled observed while asserting a cause, and confidence 80 ignores the March fraud-desk jump of about 2 points in every branch. The ladder is ordered by causal ambition rather than by support.

The rollout choice is the right family of decision, but the design repeats the failed branch-level comparison: one treated branch, a pre-post 10% rule, no freeze on routing or staffing, and no case-, day-, or team-level assignment that would make the next rollout informative.
