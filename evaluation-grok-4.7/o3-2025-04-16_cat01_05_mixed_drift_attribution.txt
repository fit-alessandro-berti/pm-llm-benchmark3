## 1. Drift timeline
The March raw-fitness break, the May rework jump, and the October volume/complexity rise are real. Several headline measurements are not. Supplier Z moves from 18% in April to 43% in May (+25 pp), so “+18 pp” is a misread. Missing-resource prevalence goes from about 1.0–1.1% to 4.8% (about 4.4×), which the text rounds to 5×. March–April is not a gradual logging recovery: raw fitness falls from 0.55 to 0.51, duplicate `Open Case` rises from 4% to 5%, and missing resources rise from 4.8% to 5.2%. No clean-up is visible.

The July row assigns “22/100” `Assess → Senior Assessment` to the 2026-07-01 priority rule. The normalized fragment shows 9 in July and 22 in October, when high-complexity share is 41%. The same row states that high-complexity wait fell and standard wait rose. The monthly aggregate does not contain that split. October p90 is 116 h; the peak is November at 121 h. June–August is treated as gradual staffing drift even though median and p90 cycle time had already moved in May, before the 15 June exits.

## 2. Raw-versus-normalized interpretation
This is the strongest section. The raw-versus-mapped gap is correctly tied to `Eligibility Screen` and duplicated `Open Case`, and the text correctly says that a successful remap does not prove unchanged timing, repetition, or frequency. Calling the reference model a Petri net is an extra assumption. “Mapped fitness remains flat” after May overstates stability: mapped fitness eases from 0.93 in February to a 0.86 trough in July, a small residual that is consistent with extra rework. The March-versus-later contrast itself is sound.

## 3. Quantitative drift decomposition
Most February-relative table entries check out. Errors remain: March p90 is +1 h against February’s 67, not +2; December rework is 21 − 11 = +10 pp, not +9; December missing-resource change is +4.4 pp, not +4.3.

The stated Euclidean distances do not match Euclidean distance on the seven rates, scaled or unscaled. Unscaled distances versus January are about 1.73 (March), 19.1 (May), 27.7 (July), and 29.2 (October). Divided by 100 they are about 0.017, 0.191, 0.277, and 0.292. The reported 0.012, 0.058, 0.082, and 0.109 fit neither calculation. The lower-bound claim is also inconsistent with the assumption beside it: zero differences on omitted edges leave Euclidean distance unchanged, so that assumption makes the fragment distance exact. Unknown omitted edges are what would make it a lower bound.

The p90 decomposition (67 h to 104 h, +37 h) assigns +12, +7, +3, and +15 h and labels those shares 35%, 20%, 10%, and 35%. The hour shares are actually about 32%, 19%, 8%, and 41%. The +3 h service component does not follow from a +0.6 h median `Assess` service change, and no segment-level times exist from which to pin +12 h of case mix or +7 h of rework. About +17 h of that p90 move is already present in May, before the June staffing cut, so the residual cannot be attributed primarily to lost assessor capacity. A 15 March “duplicate clean-up” change point is not in the timeline or the series. The multiple-change-point conclusion is right; the dates and magnitudes are not reliable enough to use.

All eight required mechanisms appear, and several discriminating tests are the right ones (supplier-split DFG, service-versus-wait, class-specific waiting). The evidence cells overclaim. Nothing in the tables shows that rework rose inside non-Z cases. “July Senior Assessment 22/100” repeats the October figure. The +15 h wait is carried forward from the unsupported decomposition. The May–August p90 path (71 → 84 → 95 → 104 → 110) is roughly steady, so “nonlinear surge” is not established. Rank 6’s skipped-validation signature conflicts with `Register → Eligibility` staying at 100 and eligibility-to-documents staying in the mid-90s.

A separate conceptual error runs through ranks 4 and 6: the July priority rule reorders the queue. It does not by itself create `Senior Assessment` events. That arc’s frequency should be read against high-complexity share and the historically inconsistent recording of senior review. October’s 22/100 lines up with the complexity jump; July’s 9/100 does not show a control-flow break.

## 5. Behavioral, population, and logging discrimination
The supplier, complexity, migration, staffing, and label comparisons are the right cuts. Two designs are weak. Alignment cost is a conformance measure; a priority rule can redistribute waiting time with no change in trace alignment. Comparing May with July as “staff loss” mixes the May supplier shock, the 15 June exits, and the 1 July priority rule. On the migration row, “skeleton identical ⇒ logging” is written into the behavioral-drift column, so the three pattern columns are not used consistently.

## 6. Segmentation plan
The minimum-support rules conflict with the early-year base rates they are meant to compare. January supplier Z is about 14% of 620 cases, under the 150-cases/month rule; January high-complexity volume is about 130, under the 200-case rule. Those rules drop the pre-period cells a difference-in-differences needs. “/8 assessors” invents a headcount. Section 9 later calls four leavers “20% of that role,” which implies a different headcount of about 20. The primary monitoring population is non-Z, standard complexity, current labels. That is a useful control stratum. As the primary population it removes the supplier and complexity slices where the documented shocks concentrate, so the main mechanisms would be invisible there.

## 7. Monitoring architecture
Ten monitors cover labels, duplicates, fitness, DFG distance, rework, wait, service, mix, missing resources, and an outcome SLA, with distinct thresholds and named owners. Calibration and seasonality handling are thin. Mapped fitness never falls below 0.86, so an alert at 0.85 would stay silent through the observed trough. Duplicate rates stay at 4–6% and missing resources at about 5–6% after March, so the 3% and 5% alerts would remain on unless the post-migration baseline is explicitly reset. The DFG threshold 0.06 has no defined scale. “Reference window” is filled with measurement-window lengths. Year-over-year baselines need a prior cycle that this log does not contain. Holm–Bonferroni is named without a family, alpha, cadence, or allowance for dependent overlapping windows. Aggregate median wait cannot see the priority redistribution the case itself flags, and there is no temporal-profile monitor distinct from the two single-activity time rules.

## 8. Reproducible Python and PM4Py plan
Part A does not embed either table; it reads `monthly_summary.csv` and `dfg_rates.csv`, which are not supplied. The validation assert requires seven rows per month, while `dfg_dist` indexes edge names as columns. Those shapes cannot both be true. `float(baseline[c])` is applied to a one-row `DataFrame` slice and will not coerce cleanly. Distance is unscaled `np.linalg.norm`, which disagrees with the scaled figures in section 3, and the DFG fragment has no rows for February, April, June, August, or November, so the monthly apply step fails. `fitness_gap` is mapped minus raw (about +0.35 to +0.42 after March), while the flag tests `fitness_gap < -0.3`, so the logging break never flags.

Part B never calls the required `pm4py.filter_time_range`, `pm4py.discover_dfg`, `pm4py.get_variants`, or `pm4py.discover_temporal_profile`. It uses `time_filter`, discovery factories, and `variants.log.get` paths that are not the current manual API, and it calls `pm4py.format_dataframe` without importing `pm4py`. `monthly_dfg` does not group by month. The mapping helper copies the frame before replacement, which preserves the original, but it neither drops duplicated `Open Case` nor computes a comparison.

## 9. Provisional attribution
The confidence ordering is directionally sensible, and the March logging conclusion is the one claim that is actually tight: performance is stable from February to March while raw fitness collapses and mapped fitness returns to about 0.90. After that, the attribution states results the aggregates do not contain. Within-supplier rework is not observed, so “behavioural reactance” at 80% confidence is not identified. Supplier share reaches 46% in June, not “exactly” on 5 May, and rework stays elevated after Z’s share falls in September. Four leavers are not shown to be 20% of the role. No Little’s-law calculation is presented. July senior-assessment arcs do not triple (4 → 9 versus January, or 7 → 9 versus May). “Preliminary analyses” of faster high-complexity exit are not in the evidence. A 60/40 seasonal-versus-backlog split for October–November is manufactured; the incremental autumn move coincides with volume and high-complexity share while Z’s share is already down. December cites overtime and then says staffing stayed constant. The revision tests also slip chronologically: capacity loss cannot be the producer of a May rework spike.

## 10. Monitoring decision rules
Seven rules use the required IF / THEN / BECAUSE shape. Rules 3 and 5 are the best: rework is escalated only when it concentrates in supplier Z, and the priority rule is reviewed only with a class-specific wait comparison. Rule 4 treats a wait increase at stable service time as a staffing shortfall. Extra `Request Info` loops can lengthen the queue with unchanged assessor service time and unchanged headcount. Rules 2 and 7 inherit the calibration problem above: duplicates remain above 3% for the rest of the year, and mapped fitness stays above 0.85 throughout the observed stress.

The write-up would be usable only after the July/October senior-assessment date, the DFG distances, the cycle-time shares, the stratified claims, and the code are rebuilt from the event log. As it stands, the narrative is ahead of the measurements.
