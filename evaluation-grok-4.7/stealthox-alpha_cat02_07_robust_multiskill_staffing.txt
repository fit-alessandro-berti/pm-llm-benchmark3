6.2

The write-up has the required structure, a real staffing insight, and a recommendation that is not just “whatever minimizes base-case mean delay.” It is not reliable enough to audit or implement as written. Several load figures, the uncertainty scenario that most changes the mix, the comparison table, and the recommended policy’s own code disagree with each other.

The structural reading is mostly right. Software after 16:00 collapses onto Emil, mechanical coverage ends with Deepa at 18:00, sequential ME/ES sits close to the 120-minute P1 target once handovers are included, and pooling buys slack by spending handover and setup time. Mean service times, daily skill demand, P1 volume (about 5.4), the U4 mean shifts, and the 16:00–18:00 software load (about 92 minutes versus 90) check out. Assumptions A1–A8 are labeled, and the two objectives do cover SLA/tail risk and fairness. Sections 6, 7, and 9 are the strongest: preemption and P3 starvation, the PM4Py warning that case waiting time is not stage queue time, and a shadow-then-switchback pilot that keeps P1 on the safer rule.

The capacity table does not. It treats every qualified resource as fully available to every skill at once, then mis-counts blocks on top of that. Electrical 10:00–12:00 is about 415 effective minutes, not 391. Software 12:00–14:00 is about 192, not 210. Mechanical 14:00–16:00 is 336 only if Deepa’s 14:00–14:30 break is ignored; with the break it is about 312. Electrical 16:00–18:00 is about 189 if Deepa is at 1.00, not 165 (that figure uses her 1.25 mechanical multiplier). Electrical 18:00–20:00 is about 92, not 69: Emil’s break is 16:00–16:30, and the same block correctly gives him 120 software minutes. Those errors move straight into the shock story. U5 morning electrical is called 68% even though U5 also removes Chen, the fastest morning electrical resource; without him that load is over 100%, not 68%. F3 compares about 164 base-minutes of mixed work with 120 clock minutes and still omits Emil’s 1.30 and 1.10 multipliers, so the evening overload is understated. F4’s “108 minutes, so P1 ME/ES must be parallel” assumes a split across resources. One cross-skilled owner avoids the extra handover and the team-change penalty; sequential ME on Deepa is closer to 90 minutes. ES on one person remains tight, so the parallel rule is justified for ES and for tails, not as a blanket mean-path necessity.

U2 is interpreted two ways. The prose doubles E and ES counts and gets about 571 electrical minutes in 12:00–16:00, which matches that reading. The simulator only doubles those shares and renormalizes inside a fixed arrival total, which raises electrical demand by roughly 20%, not by a factor of two. Section 4 then presents estimate bands as if the simulator had confirmed them. It cannot have, under that code. Policy A makes the gap worse: Mechanical is only Ada and Ben, so there is no mechanical resource after 16:00, and Software excludes Farah, so a Chen absence leaves no software resource until Emil at 12:00. The table’s base P1 SLA of 88–94% and U3 band of 70–82% do not describe that policy. Policy B’s base maximum utilization of 85–92% also sits badly next to the claim that no routing rule can clear Emil’s evening software spike.

Policy C’s text and code diverge on the mechanisms the recommendation credits. The 20-minute preemption guard is absent. Victim choice uses `(priority, −remaining)`, which preempts a P2 before a P3. P3 is parallelized whenever both home resources are free, despite the sequential rule. P1 parallelism is abandoned immediately if both resources are not free, rather than held as an attempt. Emil’s afternoon reservation is skipped for every P1 and every overflow case, which is exactly when he is most likely to be pulled off software. Delay statistics drop unfinished cases instead of censoring them at the horizon, so the worst scenarios look better than the stated SLA rule. `pm4py.get_service_time(..., 'MEAN')` does not return per-activity mean service time and can abort the handover-network call that follows it. O1 is also not “equivalent” to minimizing CVaR of delay.

The recommended hybrid is a defensible trade-off: protect the single-skilled evening software resource, parallelize tight P1 branches, and open the pool only after a wait threshold, with a staffing change rather than a cleverer queue once Emil is saturated. The numbers used to show that it wins under O1 across U1–U5 are not internally consistent, and the code does not implement that policy. Under a strict audit standard that is a clear miss, not a near-miss.
