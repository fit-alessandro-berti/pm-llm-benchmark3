The answer meets the required structure and is strongest as a recovery redesign: minimum controls are split by customer and product risk, bare authorization skips and scanless dispatch are fenced, and audit remediation is treated as residual debt rather than proof of real-time compliance. Most phase metrics, fitness figures, and cited DFG counts (3,920; 3,600; 2,600; 1,050; 450; 900; 300; 2,300) match the record.

It is not nearly flawless. The baseline window is mislabeled “Day 30 to Day 1,” which is a material temporal error for a before/during/after analysis. T3 is given dominant variants—reconcile-manual-reservation and elevated compliance holds—that appear in the stabilization DFG, while no partial-recovery DFG is supplied. “Dual-control missing only 2.9% (not worse)” is misleading: that rate is about 14.5× the 0.2% baseline, and it coexists with an adaptation that was supposed to cover every controlled-substance order. Linking pattern C to the 9.5% traceability-exception rate conflates an eligibility failure with custody, scan, proof-of-delivery, and temperature gaps; the move-on-model findings point to those latter gaps. Pattern E is asserted to feed the entire 12.5% rework rate without a quantitative bridge. The T2 risk posture places concealed noncompliance “elsewhere,” although the hospital fast lane itself sometimes bypassed authorization and dispatch-without-scan was a general path (1,800 + 1,450), of which only 2,600 reached phone proof of delivery. That 650-case custody leak, plus other DFG drop-offs, is unused. “Bounded” control loss understates roughly 12× cold-chain and traceability deterioration. Severity levels S1–S3 are named and then barely drive the model, and the “any hold for 30 minutes” trigger is unclear for a confirmed cyber event. Several comparison operators and arrows are missing, so the fitness path collapses to “0.960.710.820.90” and multiple indicator targets lose their direction.
