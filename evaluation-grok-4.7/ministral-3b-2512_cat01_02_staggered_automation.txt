**3.5**

The write-up has the required skeleton and restates several institutional facts correctly, but its quantitative core, identification logic, and code are unreliable. A decision-maker who trusted the headline numbers would be misled.

**Section 1** is the strongest part. Availability versus use, targeted rollout, routing, staffing, the May case-mix shift, the fraud desk, and CSAT nonresponse are all named, and the word count is in range. Gamma is still lumped into “selected for poor performance,” although the problem states a readiness pilot. The flat 4–6% `automated_fast_path_pct` in every branch before availability is never flagged, so a measurement problem is treated as a true zero-use baseline.

**Section 2** covers the requested estimand families, interference, and treatment version. The definitions are weak. Several “not identifiable” reasons are false: a learning curve does not require data beyond the within-branch monthly series already supplied, and an FTE interaction is computable from the given FTE column. Fast-path substitution is given `p90_cycle_h` as its outcome. The policy population (Delta and Epsilon, which were not the high-volume weak branches) is never separated from the effect on already-treated Alpha and Beta. Identifiability is collapsed into “selection happened,” which is a different question from whether a stated assumption would identify an estimand.

**Section 3** states the parallel-trends assumption backwards (“No parallel trends post-adoption”). The short justification, well under 150 words, claims the event study “avoids selection bias.” Comparison with never-treated units does not remove selection on poor historical cycle times; that selection is why regression to the mean threatens the contrast. Heterogeneous timing, already-treated controls, and negative weights are not discussed, despite staggered adoption being the design problem. Synthetic control is proposed with two dissimilar donors and without an interference assumption, even though the fraud desk serves every branch.

**Section 4** lists the requested mechanisms, then mishandles the signatures. Automated triage is described in the problem as sending more borderline cases to the fraud desk, yet the beneficial signature is a lower escalation rate. Alpha’s escalation move from 7.8% to 11.6% includes the March jump that occurs in every branch before any go-live. Gamma’s April-to-July use jump is adoption, not learning; Alpha and Beta are the gradual-use cases. “Response rates stable” is invented: branch-month response counts are unavailable. “No within-branch time series” is false for this panel.

**Section 5** fails quantitative discipline. For Alpha and Beta, April–June is after the 1 April go-live, but those months are labeled pre-adoption. Rework rows are filled with escalation figures (Alpha 10.8–11.6, Beta 10.3–10.8, Gamma 9.2–10.7). Reported changes are not reproducible from the stated windows: Alpha’s p90 “+8.3h” matches July 109.6 minus January 101.3, not April–June versus July–August; Gamma’s median “−1.9h” does not match those months either. The never-treated comparison says adopters’ medians are about 10–20% lower and illustrates that with Delta at 34.4h versus Alpha at 43.3h, which is about 26% higher, and 34.4h is Delta’s January level rather than a contemporaneous control. Beta’s p90 is said to fall about 1.5h (12%); April 95.6h to August 99.2h is a rise. Pre-trends are signed incorrectly: Alpha 46.9 → 49.4 is not a decline, and Gamma 36.5 → 39.6, then 45.9 before July, is a worsening, not an improvement. The subsection never discusses regression to the mean. There is no real sensitivity analysis for two outcomes, and median and tail behavior are later collapsed into one adoption story. The common March +2 percentage-point escalation jump and the May complexity/rework shock in Delta and Epsilon are the natural benchmarks and are not computed.

**Section 6** hits most required threats but omits case mix as its own row. Selection is given the wrong bias direction: targeting historically slow branches makes regression to the mean overstate cycle-time improvement. Other bias directions are generic.

**Section 7** assigns 60% confidence and a “conditionally causal” 10–20% median reduction after Section 2 marked the intention-to-treat effect unidentified. The numeric range (−5h to +8h) does not match that percentage. Tail, rework, and escalation ranges inherit the bad windows. The safe claims still offer leadership a percentage effect.

**Section 8** correctly chooses embedded evaluation rather than an immediate unqualified expansion, and it asks for path-level times, fraud-desk waits, and a staged rule. The design then repeats the original selection error by picking Epsilon for the worst recent tail and calling August’s 91.4h its pre-adoption p90. A January–March 2027 “pre-period” discards eight months of existing pre-data and delays the rollout the label says to expand. Treatment assignment is specified as regression dummies. The post-period length is missing. The escalation “cap” (15% if already above 12%) is not operational. Holding out a control after Delta also adopts is unresolved.

**Section 9** does not run. `OLSDF` is not a statsmodels estimator, and `pd.compat.StringIO` is not a valid loader. Trends are drawn with `plt.subplots`, which the specification forbids, and the unstacked series would plot months rather than branches. The Gamma pre/post split uses calendar month &lt; 4, so April–June are treated as post. The relative-time line uses a Python conditional on a Series and then `diff().cumsum()`, which is not event time. Cluster-robust standard errors are available in statsmodels and are not used; the printed warning says they are unavailable. There is no warning about regression on aggregated medians and essentially no comment stating identifying assumptions. The intensity fit is labeled an “AT effect.”

**Section 10** puts a 10–20% median reduction on the conditionally causal rung. The observed bullet treats a confounded escalation rise and an unclear median change as facts. CSAT response rates are “unstable” here and “stable” in Section 4. The “Not supported” bullet is worded so that the unsupported claim is itself a negative conclusion.

The piece shows awareness that the rollout is endogenous, use is gradual, clusters are few, and a single “reduces cycle time by X%” sentence is risky. Those virtues do not survive inverted assumptions, swapped metrics, a reversed inequality used as the main effect, and a script that cannot execute.
