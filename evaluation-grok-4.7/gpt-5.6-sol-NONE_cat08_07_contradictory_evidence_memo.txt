7.5

The memo reaches a defensible hybrid (selective use of the redesign plus controlled learning) and meets the required structure: decision first, fact/interpretation/assumption split, all five metric conflicts, A–E, a full M1–M8 ranking, and explicit pause/scale/measure/threshold guidance. It does not treat Redesign R as uniformly good or bad, and it uses fitness, precision, alignments, the temporal profile, the directly-follows graph, and resource networks as decision evidence.

The evidence table still misstates facts. Apartment and old-building first-time-right changes are written as “8pp” and “11pp” with no sign, while the source changes are −8pp and −11pp; the quality row’s “fell 82% to 76%” is also easy to misread. Those cells are supposed to be the factual layer.

Several decision-relevant facts never enter the argument. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than baseline, so aggregate tail, rework, complaint, and SLA deterioration is confounded; segment shares (only 34% are clear beneficiaries) are unused; business and rural results are barely carried into the choice. Medium-risk apartments improved on the median (−2 days) while the tail worsened, so the median-versus-tail story is flatter than section 3 claims. Low generalization is treated as doubt that six-week behavior will hold elsewhere; that metric is about model overfitting, not geographic transportability.

“Coordinator ownership more broadly” never specifies geography or segment, despite 93% utilization. Scaling a “low-risk” path is recommended before the risk rule is validated, which the memo itself flags as uncertain. Ranking full downstream cost above rule validation and a stratified survey-skip experiment overstates what cost can change: a better cost result would not justify routine billing before activation or skipping surveys where quality already deteriorated. Tail gates stay softer (“no material deterioration”) than the non-inferior quality gates.
