7.3

The answer covers all seven required sections, uses most figures accurately, and draws the right broad line between compensating controls and throughput-preserving control loss. It is not nearly flawless. Several claims are unsupported or misleading, the timeline underuses the cycle-time evidence, and the recovery design conflicts with its own control logic.

**Timeline.** T0, T2, and T4 exception rates, fill rates, cancellations, rework, and fitness figures match the record. T2 move-on-log, move-on-model, and temporal deviations are identified correctly. T1 is invented beyond the evidence: nothing establishes that processing had not started or that informal triage had begun that morning. T3 is also over-specified. Backlog prioritization and manual-reservation reconciliation appear in the stabilization DFG, not in a partial-recovery DFG, yet they are stated as T3 variants. Cycle time is largely absent from the timeline despite being the clearest before/during/after series. Hospital median moves 8h to 10h to 9h to 8h, while pharmacy moves 23h to 54h to 38h to 28h and wholesale 36h to 96h to 84h to 58h. At T4, pharmacy p90 is still 74h against 49h and wholesale p90 is 120h against 72h. That asymmetric recovery is central temporal evidence and is not synthesized. Arrow loss in the temporal-deviation sentences also makes two findings hard to read. “Control-substance” is a wording error.

**Adaptation table.** Counts for B (2,500), D (3,920), E (3,600), H (2,600), J (1,500 and 1,050), K (1,400), and L (2,300) match the DFGs. Intent and keep/modify/retire judgments are generally sound, especially retiring unconstrained authorization skips and unmitigated scanless dispatch. Several evidence cells overreach. J’s resilience cell says the 1,050 “continued through the process” and omits that 450 of 1,500, or 30%, went directly to cancellation. K’s 1,400 were not all processed to a next step: 900 go to pick and 300 to cancel. H says phone proof let invoice closing proceed; the DFG sends those cases to spreadsheet invoice hold, and 2,100 end from that hold. E treats the phase-level 12.5% rework rate as evidence of pick-list reconstruction specifically. F’s 2.9% missing dual control is 14 times the 0.2% baseline; calling that “not higher given the crisis” is an unsupported mitigation. L’s claim that audit remediation improved conformance is the clearest error. Fitness rose because more traces fit the normative path. Invoice close to audit remediation is itself a temporal-profile deviation, and post-close repair is not real-time conformance.

**Resilience distinction.** The definition is sharp and applied well to C and G: preserved movement without a compensating control is concealed noncompliance. The classification then becomes inconsistent. E, I, and J are placed among true resilience or risky shortcuts and again among ambiguous adaptations. Observed K was after-the-fact reconciliation with a long tail; the text treats the redesigned continuous version as if it were the observed adaptation. The DFG linkage that most tests the distinction is missed. Of 1,650 temporary-release cases, 1,450 continue to dispatch without scan, so F was largely undone by G. Of about 3,250 scanless dispatches, 2,600 reach phone proof, leaving a large remainder without even that weak evidence.

**Recovery model.** All six required components are present, and the three-way evidence classification is the strongest design choice: alternative evidence, contemporaneous compensating evidence, and unresolved exception, with a ban on turning the third into a completed record. The design still has logical gaps. Risk rows are written as alternatives, while a real order can be hospital-critical, cold-chain, and controlled at once; the strictest applicable controls need to accumulate. The low-risk row requires a temporary release check on every ambient order, which recreates the scarce-pharmacist bottleneck that the incident exposed and conflicts with expanding F only to high-risk classes. “Documented conditional release” is undefined and reopens an authorization bypass. Escalating on any missing temperature or dual-control record is uncalibrated: baseline already has 0.4% and 0.2% missing rates. Category 2, “performed but not recorded,” can become retrospective reconstruction unless it is restricted to independent contemporaneous evidence.

**Indicators and recommendations.** The indicator set hits throughput, service protection, traceability, control completeness, backlog age, rework, and fairness, with a useful fitness comparison. Thresholds are not tied to baseline or to the observed breach levels, so the indicators are labels more than operational tests. The eight recommendations have effort, benefit, and residual risk, and the priority order is sensible. Benefits stay generic. The claim that portable capture “eliminates” scanless dispatch exceeds what the residual-risk cell allows. Stale-snapshot expiry is left out of the priority list even though D’s time decay is one of the incident’s clearest mechanisms.

**Trade-off statement.** The decision is directionally right: accept delay and notified deferral when high-risk controls remain, and reject unlogged authorization skips, scanless dispatch, and equivalence between later remediation and in-line compliance. It does not state the fairness decision the data forces. Pharmacy fill fell to 61% while hospital fill held at 91%, and non-hospital cycle times remained far above baseline after hospital time had recovered.

The answer would be usable after correction. The conformance claim for L, the softened reading of J, the unsupported T1/T3 variant story, the missed release-then-scanless path, and the non-cumulative control design are material under a strict reading.
