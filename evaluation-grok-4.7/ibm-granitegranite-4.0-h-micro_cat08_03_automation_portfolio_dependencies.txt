3.5

The write-up covers all seven required sections and names the right portfolio types, but it is not a reliable process recommendation. Several ratings contradict the case, the rollout breaks stated constraints, and the effects analysis never uses the process-mining evidence it was required to use.

The matrix misreads the evidence. A1 is rated low downstream-bottleneck risk even though auto-registration is explicitly described as exposing classification, KYC, and document-review queues; its exception risk is also mislabeled as classification error, which belongs to A2. A6’s direct labor saving is given as medium, while the brief says analyst-time saving is high, and its prerequisite is wrongly tied to KYC rather than document quality. A7’s process-level benefit is described as low and as “improving the loan risk profile”; triage is a synchronization play against a 58-hour external wait, not a risk-rating tool. A9’s legal and term-mismatch risk is rated low despite 8.5% remediation overall and 14% on credit-line renewal. A11 is rated high first-year suitability even though activation is unsafe until upstream controls are complete. A4’s critical-path effect (31-hour wait, on the critical path in 82% of cases where it appears) is placed in the downstream-risk column, and its prerequisite is wrongly stated as “KYC must already be automated.” Ratings are also inconsistent: some cells are High/Medium/Low and others are unscaled prose.

The dependency map hits the five required headings but gets important links wrong. It omits A3 from human escalation, despite a 3-FTE specialist pool and dependence on two seniors. It never chains missing-document loops to normalization, normalization to credit analysis, or KYC, normalized statements, and valuation to committee packaging. It treats the two-deep-integration limit as if A8 and A11 were the only modules that can be integrated. That is not what the constraint says.

The portfolios are thin and partly mis-aimed. Each uses only three of four allowed slots. The quick-win set (A1, A5, A10) has no control-strengthening module, so it fails the first-wave compliance requirement, and it claims little rework reduction while A5 targets the 3,300/2,800 missing-document loop and a 52% repeat rate. The bottleneck set omits A4, the clearest low-frequency blocker, and its stated sacrifice (“increases dependency on external valuers”) describes a dependency that already exists. The compliance set assigns A4 a control role it only partly has, misses four-eyes design, and claims customer friction from modules that are mostly internal.

Process effects are generic and sometimes reversed. No variant or directly-follows relation is cited: not the 98% registration position, KYC/document-review concurrency, the entity-mismatch loop back to KYC (1,160) or the leak to contract preparation (380), collateral on 71% of equipment finance, provisional signing, or month-end four-eyes violations. The quick-win resource claim puts KYC and document review in the back office. The bottleneck portfolio says analyst demand rises because input quality improves, which inverts the case mechanism, and it claims fewer contract-control violations without any contract-control module. The compliance portfolio says cycle time rises and waiting time stays flat, which erases A4’s 31-hour wait, and it credits four-eyes enforcement to modules that do not separate credit analysis from committee packaging.

Risk treatment names the required topics but maps them to the wrong activities. A3 false negatives are described as missed entity mismatches. Four-eyes is applied to contract generation rather than to different people completing credit analysis and committee packaging on high-exposure cases. Compensating controls stay at generic logging and human review, with no specialist capacity, override audit, PDF-quality threshold, or provisional-signing gate.

The rollout is the clearest failure against the constraints. Months 1–12 implement five modules (A1, A5, A10, A3, A6) against a four-module first-year budget. The first wave still does not strengthen controls. The two core-banking integration slots are never assigned. Gates invent precise cuts (20%, 30%, 40%, 50%) that the case does not support, and the “first-year” plan continues through month 24. A4 never enters the sequence. Monitoring tracks satisfaction and duplicate applications rather than waits, rework, directly-follows loops, false negatives, provisional signing, four-eyes breaches, or scarce-pool utilization.

The close does not recommend one portfolio. It says the highest-frequency activity should not come first, then starts with A1, which is that activity, and postpones the compliance portfolio until after the first wave. It never argues from the actual binding constraints: afternoon slack in a 12-FTE back office, three compliance specialists, analyst load unless normalization improves, the external valuation wait, and the entity-mismatch critical path.
