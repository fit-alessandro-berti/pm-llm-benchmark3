The memo reaches a defensible choice—segment-limited deployment, restored activation and permit controls, and measurement before any wider scale—and it keeps worsened tails, rework, and early billing in view. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are separated with unusual care, and the conflict explanations use fitness versus precision, alignments, temporal profiles, DFG shifts, segment splits, and both resource networks rather than treating the metrics as a single score.

The rollout rule is not internally consistent. “Median improves by at least two days” is paired with a one-sided upper bound on the difference that only has to fall below zero. That bound can confirm a negligible gain while the two-day claim rests only on the point estimate. Tail, quality, and complaint gates are closer to coherent non-inferiority rules, so the speed gate is the outlier. Requiring the cost bound to sit at or below zero, a 0.5-point complaint margin, and a usable interval on p95 is also left without a sample-size or clustering check. Those standards can be infeasible in a four-region design and, taken together, quietly demand near-dominance even though the prose says small trade-offs remain open.

Cost incompleteness is named, and the EUR 14 versus EUR 46 decomposition is arithmetically right, but the likely direction is missed. Complaint-handling cost drops out after pilot week 4 while complaints rise, so the EUR 9 full-cost gap can understate harm. The memo also says complex orders encounter incorrect survey skips, then retreats to “explanations to test.” The segment note supports that only as an interpretation; no false-skip rate is shown. Simple-house SLA improvement is unused, and it stays slightly unclear whether verified low-risk houses may still skip surveys.

Value-of-information order is otherwise sound: control reality and classifier validity can change immediate containment before a randomized skip test is ethical or informative. The recommendation correctly refuses both national scaling and a full rollback.
