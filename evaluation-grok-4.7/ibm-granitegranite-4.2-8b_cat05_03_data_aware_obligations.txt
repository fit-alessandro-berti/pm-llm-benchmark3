4.5

The final write-up follows the required headings and gets most release-time outcomes right, but it is not a reliable formalization. One planted edge case is misclassified, several sections contradict each other, and the required checker is not executable and would not reproduce the narrative even if the crashes were removed.

**Case verdicts.** TX-301, TX-303, TX-304, TX-305, TX-307, and TX-309 are classified correctly, with the right triggers: invalidated standard review, missing `Compliance_Approve` after enhanced review, sanctions rescreen, unknown amount, PEP without enhanced review, and a post-review increase above 20%. TX-302 and TX-311 are also correctly `COMPLIANT`, and `INDETERMINATE` for TX-310 is a defensible reading of contradictory exemption evidence. TX-306 is labeled `COMPLIANT`, which is the right result for a sub-20% increase, but the same row says the R1 “after latest material change” test “cannot be satisfied strictly,” so the label and the reasoning conflict.

TX-308 is wrong. R4 states that a decrease does not by itself invalidate a review. The enhanced review of 140,000 remains valid after the drop to 100,000, the foreign high-value condition still holds, and release is compliant. Treating that decrease as a material change that forces a new enhanced review erases the distinction the rule draws and is inconsistent with accepting TX-306’s smaller increase.

**Temporal semantics and rules.** Blanks, `event_index`, and the separation of release state from later settlement or database insert are mostly understood. The formalization then undoes that. Section 1 defines a material change as a change after the latest review, which is circular, and says the obligation snapshot is the first moment a trigger becomes true. R1 and R2 are evaluated at `Release_Funds`. That first-trigger rule would mishandle TX-311, where the foreign high-value condition was true at review and false at release because jurisdiction became DE. Section 3 uses the release snapshot anyway. The rule table also merges the required exception and missing-data columns, and the pseudo-logic never actually decides a verdict.

**Required counterfactuals.** TX-305 and TX-310 are handled adequately. The TX-302 counterfactual is not: if EX-77 were invalid, the case would still be compliant because an enhanced review already occurred after the only amount was known and before release, with no later invalidating change. The TX-308 alternative gets the time order backwards. TX-311’s tie-break discussion is conceptually right, but the write-up dates the enhanced review at 16:15. Both that review and the jurisdiction update are at 16:20; only `event_index` orders them.

**Code.** The checker misses a required `pm4py.format_dataframe` call, does not show a DECLARE or trace-order check, and does not emit evidence timestamps or unknown-value flags. The embedded CSV moves TX-311 off the shared 16:20 timestamp, so the tie-break cannot be tested. Exemption matching reads `case_state['time']`, which is never stored. Comparing a missing enhanced-review index with the latest change raises `TypeError`. R1-not-applicable is treated as R1 failure, and the R3 flag is both inverted and then consumed backwards, so cases with no sanctions activity are marked noncompliant. The routine would crash or disagree with the narrative table on the cases it got right.

**Remaining sections.** The control-flow critique does cover the six requested failure modes and is the strongest part. The logging recommendations cover immutable release payloads, effective versus signature time, exemption scope, and stable ordering, but they omit rule version, which was explicitly required.
