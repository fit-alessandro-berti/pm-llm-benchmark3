7.5

The write-up meets the required seven-part structure, uses the logs instead of a blended average, and usually traces the next overloaded station. It is not nearly flawless. Several numerical and causal claims are wrong or overstated, and one pillar of the redesign rests on a misreading of the evidence.

The cross-training plan reverses the given constraint. “Cross-trainable in 4 weeks” sits on the technical-validator row and matches I4 (validators learning accession correction). The redesign instead qualifies registration clerks as technical validators inside that same four-week window and treats that as the answer to the only pool already at 101%. That is an unsupported transfer of a regulated sign-off skill, and the table then calls four weeks a sufficient lead time. The conditional single-validator swing back to accession is more consistent with the note than the standing rule is.

The ~400 “extra” medical referrals are arithmetically real (peak medical routing 3,420/8,080 = 42% versus roughly 27% from applying the 3.1× complex-panel factor to the peak mix). Calling that gap defensive escalation that a release-rule freeze can remove is not. The same gap is what a one-factor mix model cannot see: urgent propensity, genuinely harder winter results, and M2 failures. The table assigns all ~400 a week to routine cases that can safely stay on direct release. Freezing them has an unexamined clinical-miss risk, and it does not follow from the DFG.

I3 is misstated in the trade-off table. Dedicating M1 to urgent and complex-panel work gives complex-panel cases machine access; it does not park them until urgent work is finished. Section 2 had this right. Bundling I1 with I3 in one cell hides that difference. The same row’s “spoil 24 tubes” conflicts with the standing choice to keep batches at 12.

Batch-wait is compared on raw DFG totals across unequal windows: 760 over eight weeks versus 1,180 over three. Weekly entries rise from about 95 to about 393, not by half. The parenthetical match to urgent non-bypass counts is real within each window (about 860 versus 760; about 1,210 versus 1,180), but reusing 3,300 — earlier defined as peak weekly volume — for the eight-week urgent total makes the check look like a calculation error.

Medical-validation load and utilization are left unreconciled. Inflow to medical validation rises from about 530 to about 1,370 cases a week (about 2.6×), while utilization only moves from 58% to 93% (about 1.6×). A 22-hour queue at 93% is a variability and shift-coverage problem as much as a headcount problem. One triggered evening shift is not sized against that gap. The same issue is handled well for specialist review (87% utilization, 36-hour queue, Friday at zero) and then ignored for the medical pool.

Smaller inconsistencies add up. Recollection pressure is 4.2% to 8.0% of checks in the migration section (760/18,120 and 780/9,730) and “about 4.6%” to “about 9%” on the dashboard, because the later figures fold in accession-to-recollection leakage without saying so. The specialist trigger compares a WIP cap of 20 cases with a weekly inflow of about 36 starts. “Each” peak queue is not large enough on its own to exhaust 18 hours; 9.5 hours at pre-analytical check and 11 hours at preparation are not. The 30% routine floor is never checked against the multi-assay load the introduction itself says the order-level log understates, nor against repeat-after-failure cases consuming two assay slots.

The scenario set, the refusal to sum edge medians, the identification of technical validation as the only sustained overload, the urgent-bypass erosion from 74% to 58%, and the redesign-caused failure modes (fragmented M1 runs, the complex-panel start cap, an oversensitive 2-hour screen) are the parts that hold. Those strengths do not cancel the mis-assigned training constraint or the overclaimed referral freeze.
