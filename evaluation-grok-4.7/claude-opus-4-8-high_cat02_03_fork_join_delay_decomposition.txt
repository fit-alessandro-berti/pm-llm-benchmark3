6.5

The fork-join framework is right and most of the arithmetic checks out, but the write-up contradicts itself on several required results and one of those contradictions sits in the double-counting argument the task explicitly asks for. Under a strict reading that is not a near-flawless analysis.

The definitions in §1 are the correct ones: upstream is fork minus arrival, branch queue is start minus fork, service is end minus start, `join_ready` is the max of the three ends, sync is join minus that end, stage is join minus fork, downstream is final start minus join, and flow is final end minus arrival. The critical-path identity `flow = upstream + stage + downstream + final service`, with stage equal to the critical branch’s queue plus service, is the right way to avoid double counting. The algebraic identity for the sum of sync waits is also right.

The concrete demonstration is not. For F07, C ends at 10:03 and join is 10:55, so sync_C is 52, not 37, and the sync sum is 77, not 62. Section 2 later states 52, so the document disagrees with itself. With the printed figures, 62 is less than the stated stage of 75, so the example fails the sentence it is meant to support. “Several times over” is also false for three branches: one sync is zero by construction, and the other two are each at most the stage, so the sum is strictly under twice the stage. In this log it is usually below the stage and only barely above it on the longest cases (F07 and F12: 77 versus 75).

The case table is otherwise accurate (upstream, queues, services, join clock times, downstream, final service of 20, and flow all match the timestamps), and the four worked cases are numerically correct. F10 is not. Join is given as 11:20, which is B’s end; A ends at 11:15. The critical column says A. The note immediately below, the 2/8/2 counts, §3, and §4 all say B. The required case table therefore disagrees with the rest of the answer. The F04 write-up calls C’s win “the only case”; F08 is also C-critical, and §3 says so.

Aggregate service, queue, completion, and sync figures are correct: A 50 / 0 / sync sum 132; B 37.5 / 19.17 / sync sum 52; C 20.17 / 10.42 / sync sum 365; critical counts 2, 8, and 2. Ranking B over A over C, on frequency, queue-plus-service, and tail rather than mean service, is the right conclusion. The near-tie discussion is directionally fair, but the “about 10–20 minutes” list includes F10 (5 minutes) and omits F06 (15 minutes).

Counterfactual totals are right under the stated fixed-downstream assumption. Cutting A service from 50 to 40 saves 5 on F01 and 10 on F11, and 0 elsewhere (15 total). Halving B queue saves 10, 10, 10, 15, 15, 12.5, 5, and 15 on F02, F03, F05, F06, F07, F09, F10, and F12 (92.5), with F01, F04, F08, and F11 unchanged. Halving downstream saves half of 176, which is 88. The zero-benefit logic for a noncritical branch is correctly applied. The F02 gloss is not: a 50% cut of a 20-minute queue is a 10-minute move, and B lands exactly on A, so the whole cut converts. The cases that are actually capped are F05 (12.5 available, 10 realized) and F10 (10 available, 5 realized).

The final-station masking claim is only partly earned. Resource IDs and intervals are in the log, and they do not show both servers busy during the long waits. Assignment alternates strictly. F03, F06, F07, and F12 wait while their assigned server is already idle; F10’s 20 minutes is about 5 minutes of F2 occupancy and 15 minutes of idle time after F2 frees; F05, F09, and F11 do match residual busy time. “18–20 minutes precisely in later cases when both resources are likely busy” does not survive that calendar: F04 is early and only partly busy, F12 is last and has a 10-minute idle wait, and downstream is not monotone from F01 to F11 (8, 15, 15, 18, 10, …, 20, 20, 10). Assumption A1 is labeled, which is good, but the cross-case effect is ignored: F05’s wait is exactly F03 occupying F1, so moving an earlier join can change a later downstream wait rather than leaving it fixed.

Section 5’s two redesigns follow the evidence, and the policy-versus-queue caveat on the final station is the right risk. “F07 from 130 toward ~100” assumes essentially all of F07’s 15-minute downstream disappears plus the 15-minute B-queue saving; the counterfactual that was actually computed only halves downstream and would land near 107. The pilot measures are appropriate.

The Python meets the requested structure: in-memory table, minute decomposition, critical-path identity assert, three counterfactuals with per-case and aggregate savings, an interval dataframe, `format_dataframe`, `get_service_time` with a fallback, and `discover_performance_dfg`, plus a correct comment that a directly-follows performance DFG cannot recover a max-join or synchronization slack. The tie rule is weaker than its comment. If two ends are equal, queue plus service equals end minus fork for both, so the load comparison never breaks a tie; alphabetical order is the only rule. No case in this log ties, so the printed critical branches from the code would still be right, including F10 as B.

Section 7 correctly flags non-causality, possible preemption inside one-minute complete events, a single morning ramp, fragile near-ties, and no rework. It overstates what is unidentified. Concurrent overlap is observable: four A intervals overlap, which is a hard lower bound on A capacity, and the same exercise is available for B and C. Final-station idle-versus-busy is likewise observable, as above, so “queue versus release policy” is only partly ambiguous, not an open binary.
