The design is conceptually serious: it keeps default conditional on activation, refuses to code rejects and withdrawals as non-defaults, accounts for all 48,000 cases, and separates several missingness mechanisms. It is not statistically tight enough to carry the study as written. Several of its own rules contradict each other, and the validation calendar is incompatible with the maturity rule.

The prediction time is specified two different ways. Section 1 sets it to the earlier of the k-th event and the decision event, which places t* on the decision timestamp. Section 4 and the code cut the trace strictly before Accepted, Rejected, Withdrawn, or Transferred. Those are different prefixes. The pre-decision rule is the coherent one; the section 1 wording would admit the decision into the time cut.

The 3,600 immature activated accounts are given three roles at once: excluded from training, usable in the survival likelihood, and pooled with permanently missing partner feeds. The primary estimand is then called both a cause-specific hazard and a cumulative incidence among label-mature accounts. On fully matured binary labels those are just a proportion; cause-specific hazards and Aalen–Johansen incidences are not interchangeable. The prose never settles whether the 3,600 enter the primary likelihood. Inverse-probability weighting for missing feeds is only valid inside the time-mature activated subpopulation. Fit on all activated accounts, “label missing” mixes not-yet-due outcomes with permanent non-response, so the weights absorb calendar time.

The reject-inference “bound” is not a bound. Setting the rejected default rate between the activated rate and 1 assumes rejects are at least as risky as activated accounts. An assumption-free range for an unobserved binary outcome is 0 to 1. Linkage is also thinner than the write-up: the linked 60% is not shown to be random, and the flow never says whether a recovered partner identifier is already inside the 48,000.

The validation dates conflict with the maturity arithmetic the answer itself states. With a 90+35 day rule and extraction on 2026-02-15, an activation after about 2025-10-13 is immature. The 2025-10-15 migration is therefore outside every fully buffered supervised label. The proposed test from 2025-10-01, meant to isolate migration, has essentially no mature defaults. The broader test (starts on or after 2025-07-01) is identifiable only for fast activations before mid-October, which selects shorter cases. The rolling-origin sentence makes the problem worse: requiring test labels to be mature at the origin empties a prospective test. Training labels must be mature at the origin; test labels are read later, with the resulting fast-activation selection reported. That correction is not in the design. `policy_period` also has no variance inside this split, so it cannot be estimated.

The skeleton repeats the maturity leak. `label_mature` is computed once against 2026-02-15, then cases are cut by start date. A case that starts in March 2025 and activates in August 2025 is placed in train even though its label does not exist at the July 2025 origin. Section 7 forbids that. `state` and `label_mature` are joined into the modeling frame and are not on the banned-column list; both are known only after the prefix. `collection_status` is retained for label building without a 90-day window, so later collections can redefine `default_90d`. Cohort counts, the decision truncation assert, train-only DFG and variants, and the refusal to invent a PM4Py survival API are sound. The code does not implement per-origin maturity, elapsed-time prefixes, or the missing-feed weight, and “last” risk score depends on row order after the filter.

Section 8 then claims calibration on a post-policy temporal test. Under these dates that claim is ahead of the labels the design can honestly use.
