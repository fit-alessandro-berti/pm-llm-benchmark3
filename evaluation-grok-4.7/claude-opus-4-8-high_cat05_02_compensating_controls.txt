The memo is a serious control analysis: it splits segregation into distinct threats, refuses to treat a logged activity or a legitimate outcome as proof of sufficiency, and reaches defensible labels on the clearest cases (AP-202 and AP-208 failed; AP-203 met under ED-1; AP-205 stopped before completion; AP-204 failed in substance despite two user IDs). The expected-loss discussion, settlement-based timing semantics, and alert-versus-proof monitoring are strong. It is still short of a high score because several claims conflict with the facts, with the required labels, or with the memo’s own test.

The recall assessment for AP-201 is wrong. Release is 08:06, settlement is 10:00 the same day, and audit starts at 09:00 the next day, about 23 hours after settlement. The memo calls that “after the 24-hour recall window” and codes the audit as outside one business day. Those are different clocks, and on the settlement clock the audit begins inside the usual recall period, at the edge. Corrective capacity is thin, but the stated reason is inaccurate. That error also feeds the evidence flag.

Section 2 contradicts itself and then contradicts AP-207. “Full” timing is defined as review before settlement, while the partial-equivalence bullet puts the same post-release, pre-settlement pattern in the partial bucket. AP-207 is exactly that pattern, and ED-1 condition 6 allows approval before settlement. `OBJECTIVE_MET` is the better case call; the rubric cannot also say that this timing is only partial equivalence.

Two rows break the required single classification. AP-206 is `OBJECTIVE_PARTIALLY_MET (lean MET)`, and section 5 then says the case leans met. AP-209 is `OBJECTIVE_PARTIALLY_MET / INDETERMINATE`. The pair, shared workstation, and AP-204’s fabricated receipt make independence unproven; with no defect on AP-209 itself, the single supportable label is `INDETERMINATE`. Offering both labels records the dilemma instead of resolving it.

AP-208 is correctly failed, but the prose rationale misses two explicit ED-1 breaches: EUR 900,000 is far above the EUR 100,000 cap, and the bank account changed three days earlier. The write-up centers the validation outage, the unsigned checklist, and post-settlement ratification. Those are real failures; they are not the whole permission breach. AP-203’s “no material missing evidence” also overreaches: L3 is never established for m02, and the source itself says the ED-1 conditions appear mostly satisfied.

The code would reconstruct the log, parse UTC timestamps, separate AP-205, and its manual same-user list is right (AP-201, AP-202, AP-203, AP-206, AP-207, AP-208). It then undermines that care. `filter_four_eyes_principle` is called, but the result is stored as violations without taking the complement, even though the stable documented behavior keeps cases that comply. Only the filter sits in `try/except`; a `format_dataframe` failure aborts the manual evidence. The facts table invents data: vendor age of 30 days for AP-202, five years for AP-205, token-before-prepare true for AP-208 when only “token active” is known, and manager independence true for m21 and for AP-204/AP-209, where no manager acted and m21’s independence is not given. `NO_MANAGER_BEFORE_RELEASE` and the one-business-day audit flag are also applied to ordinary two-person payments, although the one-day rule is stated for emergency same-user payments. The banner says the output is evidence, which limits the damage, but several flags would still point a reviewer at the wrong defect.

Smaller weaknesses accumulate. “Substantive-adjacent” for m04 is undefined. Full equivalence demands collusion resistance that ordinary four-eyes itself does not provide, so the test mixes “equal to FEP-1” with “sound against every threat.” Section 4’s choice to treat pre-settlement manager review as preventive against the loss is coherent; calling that same act corrective before money has left is the loose part.
