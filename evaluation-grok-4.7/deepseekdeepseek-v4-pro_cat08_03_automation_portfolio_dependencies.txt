The final answer meets the required seven-section structure, covers all twelve candidates, and correctly refuses a frequency-led registration-first plan. It also respects the four-module cap, the control-module requirement, analyst overload, auditability, and the two-deep-integration limit. Those strengths do not offset several material reasoning failures.

The matrix is not a ranking; it follows candidate order. Concurrency of KYC and financial-document review is effectively ignored, so cycle time is treated as if those waits stack. The afternoon slack in the 12-person back office is never used. Customer signing’s 44-hour wait, which sits on nearly every completed case, is left out of the bottleneck portfolio and out of the final cycle-time claim.

The recommendation, A2, A5, A6, and A9, is a plausible set, but the trade-off is poorly defended. A4 is deferred on the false claim that classification must first clean entity data; mismatch resolution depends on KYC and specialist capacity, not product classification. The same answer that discounts A1 because a short service time does not remove a queue then treats contract generation as a major cycle-time lever despite a 22-hour wait and unclean upstream KYC. A9 is also scheduled in the first six months, before document quality, normalization, or KYC clearance improve, which conflicts with the answer’s own garbage-in logic and with the ban on downstream automation while upstream controls are unreliable.

The closing claim that four-eyes violations will be measurably lower is unsupported: none of the four chosen modules enforces separation of credit analysis and committee packaging. Provisional signing is waved through as a side fix rather than a designed control. Effect sizes such as “single-digit hours,” 95% accuracy, and a 40% remediation cut are invented precision. Risks do cover false negatives, drift, audit trails, and four-eyes, but false positives and the recommended portfolio’s own interim workload shift are thin.
