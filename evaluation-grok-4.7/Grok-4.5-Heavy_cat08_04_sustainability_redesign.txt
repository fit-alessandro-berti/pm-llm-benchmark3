The answer covers every required section, stays largely inside the event log, and avoids a blanket ban on air or dedicated vans. The deductions below are for concrete inaccuracies and unresolved trade-offs, not for missing structure.

The system boundary is usable, but it narrows `energy_kwh` to CNC, heat treatment, and inspection while stock paths still carry about 0.8–1.4 kWh/order. Reverse logistics from damage returns is used later as an emissions effect without being placed inside or outside the boundary. The claim that every indicator is directly derivable overreaches: defect-escape rate, damage returns, and R1 alternative-route availability are not event attributes. Low discovered-model precision, which the log attributes to emergency and partial-shipment combinations, is never carried into the indicator or validation design.

Several effect statements mis-cite the evidence. The ground reference “≈31 h” is the Standard-stock Regular segment median; the stock–standard–ground path median is 34 h, and Standard-stock Service-contract currently sits at 18 h. Air’s 24 h transport-time advantage is not reconciled with how much priority picking would actually recover, so acceptability for that segment is asserted rather than shown. The S2 table treats the drop from 2.8 to 1.3 kWh/item as the effect of the capped policy, yet that energy rate is observed at batch sizes 12–20 whose median batch wait is 38 h. A 12–24 h cap would likely forgo part of that gain. “52 → ground levels” for shared emergency lanes equates a dedicated-van path average with parcel-ground intensity; shared courier load-factor gains are not quantified in the log. The 1.8 kg scrap figure is the full CNC_B rework-path average, not the marginal effect of moving eligible work to CNC_A. S11’s table shows lower cycle time and lower lateness by citing the 79 h / 24% partial-plus-backorder path, while hold-for-full or a production substitute can retain most of that wait; cancellation risk is acknowledged in prose and then omitted from the resilience column.

Mechanism and package logic slip in a few places. S7 proposes slower inspection to fix a failure the log attributes to inspection queues and rushed checks, which can worsen the queue. S1, S4, and S5 are scored as separately large transport reductions and then described as jointly cutting dedicated-van volume, although S4 and S5 do not act on that path. The recommendation also preserves dedicated options for tight-slack Service-contract orders, which collides with SP2’s Critical-downtime justification rule. Made-to-order Service-contract, at 12% of orders and 12 kgCO2e average transport, is left outside the explicit shipping rules. Route changes are marked quality-neutral despite packaging rework being triggered by route/geometry mismatch. Combined delay from batch caps, CNC_A queues, and added inspection on the already late made-to-order segments is not assessed before the package is declared within acceptable bounds. S1 and S4 are the same shipping decision counted twice.

Rebound coverage is broad and includes batching, obsolescence, mode shift, packaging, rework displacement, consolidation backfire, misclassification, partials, and customer response. Validation includes SP1/SP2/Q1, segment service, exception review, and an R1 disruption check. Pre/post path comparisons still lack a case-mix control, and rollout “by warehouse or region” is not supported by the log.
