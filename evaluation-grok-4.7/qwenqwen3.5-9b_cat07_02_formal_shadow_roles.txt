5.4

The write-up has the required six-part shape and the right broad story: front-office intake, technical resolution, quality, a commercial shadow around Giorgio, Mateo as an escalation broker, and Erik as a scarce security control. It also treats substitution as slower and provisional, and it states benefits and risks of shadow work. That is not enough to carry the analysis. Several load-bearing claims are wrong, and the role table forces people into clean clusters the evidence does not support.

The clearest factual errors are these. Erik’s 55 critical cases are not the most of any resource; Mateo has 76 and Hana 62. Erik’s distinction is intensity (median 0.86), not the raw count. Mateo’s 44 A9 events are his ten-week total; the Hana absence was 21 preliminary approvals, 13 later rechecked. Labeling all 44 as substitution inflates his authority. Kai and Lina did about 350 A1 events, not ~450, and they have zero A13 events. The 17 reopens were downstream rework traced to incomplete entitlement notes, not their own reopen rate. Ivan and Julia did 608 quality checks, not ~628. Hana–Mateo similarity of 0.54 is the second-lowest selected pair, not high similarity. The temp “overqualified if they touch critical work” test is backwards, and telling the quality analysts to train on knowledge-base updates reverses the data: they already own A14 (96 and 104), while the front office barely does it.

The structure also flattens the distinctions the scenario is built to expose. Nora is placed in human front office even though she only registers and validates, never gives a standard response or closes, and would not share that activity profile. Giorgio is listed as a commercial member although he is closer to Amira (0.84) than to Fatima (0.71); that contrast is never used. His commercial counts are real (72 A7, 88 A8), but only 31 and 18 of those fall in the absence window, so routine hybrid work and a six-day substitution are collapsed. Amira, Ben, the temps, and the bot share one complexity band of 0.11–0.34 despite medians of 0.34, 0.29, 0.18–0.19, and 0.11. Chen’s 24 security reviews and 42 critical cases versus Dina’s 6 and 9 are noted, then still parked in one role whose peripheral list omits the front office’s real secondary work (Amira’s 75 diagnoses and 42 negotiations) and Mateo’s actual high-count activities (close, validate, standard response). Section 2 never treats quality or security as roles of their own. The six sequence fragments, shifts, and the automation boundary are barely used.

The code names the requested discovery and similarity functions and warns against reading clusters as authority, but it is not coherent. `format_dataframe` is called without a timestamp and with parameters that are not PM4Py-style. Handover and working-together calls use invented arguments. `temporary_substitution` is read from a resource-level complexity file even though it is an event attribute, so substitution weeks are never removed before role discovery. The weighted score references an `activity_counts` column the frame does not have.

Operationally, protecting Chen, keeping Fatima’s sign-off, and not hiring temps on volume are sound. The knowledge-base recommendation is not, and the two cautions do not repair the earlier count and authority mistakes.
