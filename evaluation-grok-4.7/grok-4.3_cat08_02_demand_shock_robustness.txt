4.5

The write-up follows the required seven-part structure and avoids a blanket “add capacity” or always-on priority rule, but its process reasoning is unreliable. Several load-bearing claims contradict the logs, and the redesign protects the wrong stations.

**Scenarios.** S1 is not the observed peak. Weekly volume rises from 2,300 to 3,300, about 43%, while S1 uses a 20–25% increase and cuts Repeat-after-failure from the observed 9% to 6%. S2, S3, and S4 then stack invented magnitudes (repeat edges “>1600,” validator utilization “>110%,” registration queues of 6–8 h, a 40% intake spike) on unequal baselines. S3 also assumes an unstated medical-validator headcount.

**Evidence and migration.** Absolute DFG counts are compared across 8 weeks and 3 weeks. Recollection is treated as flat at 760 versus 780; the rate roughly doubles, from about 4.2% to 8.0% of receipts, and weekly volume of those loops nearly triples. Repeat assays rise from about 6.7% to 14.8% of assay outcomes, which “1260 vs 1140” hides. Most repeats return to Assay run (990/1140 and 1080/1260); only 80 and 120 go to Medical validation, so S2’s claim that repeats lengthen the medical queue is the wrong path. S2 also has the queue direction backward: fewer valid runs reaching Technical validation reduces that station’s inflow. The claim that technical validators cannot release without medical sign-off is false: direct Technical validation → Release result is 11,700 of 15,890 normal exits and 4,050 of 8,080 peak exits. In S1 the text names both Technical validation and Assay machines as the next bottleneck. Peak queues are longest at specialist review (36 h), medical validation (22 h), assay (18 h), and technical validation (16 h, 101% utilization). Pre-analytical at 9.5 h is real, but it is not the first constraint in every scenario. S4 imports the peak batch-wait time of 14–22 h into a normal-volume day.

**Redesign.** The core package does not hit the first or second bottleneck of S2 or S3. I8 is described as not moving work onto Pre-analytical check; faster registration advances arrivals into a station already at 96% utilization and a 9.5 h queue. Stricter I6 reduces assay arrivals; the table’s “assay overload if triage is too strict” inverts that mechanism. The real I6 cost is false recollection, and those loops already take 42 h / 60 h and consume clerk time. Partial I4 is called robust and neutral while Technical validation is the only pool over 100% and accession correction is two people at 91%. Diverting validators there loads the tighter pool. I7 is both “always on” and the S4 switch, and I1’s off-trigger can never clear if M2 utilization stays above 95% for reasons I1 does not fix. I5’s “no next bottleneck” skips the required downstream effect. I3, the intervention tied to M2’s higher failure rate, is never tested against S2.

**Table, residuals, monitoring.** Several rework cells are unlabeled fragments, so direction of effect is unclear. The self-inflicted risk is weak: early triage exposes metadata defects rather than masking them, and a reject rate is compared with the 7.5–11.8% rework baseline. Larger leftovers are omitted: assay capacity near 98%, technical validation made worse by I4, and specialist review still a single-person queue of 36 h. The dashboard warns on registration (peak median 2.8 h) and omits technical-validation queue, assay queue, and specialist queue. Its bands also disagree with the policy triggers (medical queue 10 h versus 12 h; specialist queue absent; M2 repeat rate instead of the M2 utilization trigger). Leading and lagging metrics are distinguished, and the urgent batch-bypass rate is a genuine variant signal, but the daily set would not see the stations that actually break.
