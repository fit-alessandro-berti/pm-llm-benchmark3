The write-up has the right skeleton and a real competing-risk vocabulary, but it misstates the one fact that decides whether a 72-hour probability is identified, then builds the headline numbers and the code on that mistake.

The extract does not follow every case to 72 hours. Five open cases are censored earlier: S31 at 23, S15 at 35, S10 at 47, S29 at 51, and S24 at 67. Section 1 says every recorded time is greater than 72 and that the extract therefore shows each case’s status at the deadline. Section 4 repeats that a binary 72-hour calculation needs no survival estimator. Both claims are false. Those five cases are not known to be event-free at 72, so censoring them at 72 invents follow-up. The code then asserts `duration_or_age_h.min() >= 72`, which fails immediately (the minimum is 9). Nothing after that line runs.

That error corrupts the estimands. The crude 9/32 resolved-by-72 figure keeps those five cases in the denominator as if non-resolution were observed. It is not an Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence. With withdrawal/rejection and transfer treated as competing events and the five opens censored at their ages, the resolution CIF by 72 is about 0.30, not 0.281. A resolve-only Kaplan–Meier that censors every non-resolution has survival at 72 of about 0.639, not the stated 0.564. Section 6’s bounds, 9/32 to 12/32, only toggle the three transfers. They omit the five pre-horizon opens, and they let S32 move the by-72 result even though S32 is observed unresolved until transfer at 96. The sentence that assigns the lower resolution bound to “eventual post-72 resolution” of all three transfers points the bound the wrong way.

The same collapse appears in the implementation. `product_limit_cif` decrements survival only at the cause of interest, so competing events are censored rather than carried as Aalen–Johansen jumps. Under that function, transfer-as-censor and transfer-as-exit produce the same resolution curve, and the “linked” scenario is an explicit no-op. The sensitivity comparison the task requires is numerically empty. A second crash follows even if the first assert is removed: `derive_variants` returns `(variants, source)` and the caller unpacks it in reverse, so the frequency assert fails. The PM4Py string is `case_id:…,activity:…`, not a table `parse_event_log_string` can read as those columns.

Other concrete mistakes sit on top of that. In the four-case table, S03 is unresolved at 72 in one column and “had resolved by 72, although late” in the next. E3’s formula is \(P(R>72,D>72)\) while the sentence also treats transfer as leaving the state. Section 5 says 9/32 contain Rework and then names the only three such cases, S07, S13, and S27. It lists S27 as repeated Review; S27’s prefix has one Review. The cohort “no disposition by 72” total of 11 counts every open case plus the two transfers at or before 72. The code instead counts opens aged ≤72 plus transfers by 72, which is 7, and drops S09, S11, S25, and S26, the cases actually known to be undisposed at 72.

What keeps this from a failing grade is the material that is actually sound. The estimand list separates net non-resolution, cumulative incidence, remaining internal workload, and conditional residual risk, and it says these answer different decisions. Withdrawal and rejection are treated as competing exits in the prose, with S05 and S06 used properly. Escalation and full prefixes are recognized as time-varying and leakage-prone. Cohort B is not treated as a randomized policy effect. The gap cases, the small priority counts, calibration limits, and the missing timestamps, external outcomes, cutoff, workload, and compliance data are the right caveats. Those parts are careful. They do not repair an unidentified 72-hour target reported as if it were fully observed, or a single code path that cannot print the summaries it promises.
