7.4

The forecast is complete, balanced, and faithful to the given handover and team figures, but it is not nearly flawless. Sections 1–6 meet the required structure and correctly refuse a full rollout, distinguish East from North/South, and treat tails, fairness, learning, and accountability as first-class outcomes. The score is held down by quantitative gaps and by code that would mis-measure the comparison it describes.

The mechanism map covers the required links and correctly reads the management tables as more specialist and approval handovers, not a net handover reduction (listed edges rise from 343 to 359 per 100 cases). It also keeps the hour-level wait increases (technical 3.2h to 4.0h, return 1.6h to 2.2h, approval 2.4h to 3.0h, Legal/Risk 9.6h to 10.5h). Specialist utilization is only a measured workload, not a mechanism. A 15% utilization increase is infeasible for Legal at 93% and Risk at 91% under either a relative or a percentage-point reading, and the map never says so. Pooling N_Tech 71%, S_Tech 68%, and E_Tech 82% is likewise left unquantified, so the East benefit and the North/South loss stay qualitative.

The three scenarios include every requested heading and the early-warning signs are operational. The optimistic case still assumes Legal/Risk capacity is expanded or shielded from Quality Check, which is outside the proposal, and it claims fewer unnecessary specialist referrals while the same proposal raises technical referrals from 36 to 41 and Legal/Risk from 9 to 12. The central claim that the median “may improve slightly” is not tied back to the tables. Frequency-weighted post-handover wait rises by about 1.3 hours per case (5.9h to 7.2h), which is small against 6.8–8.6 day medians. Hour-scale median shifts cannot by themselves move day-scale cycle time; the tail risk is backlog, unowned time, and rework loops. That scale distinction is not made in the scenario section.

The indicator table correctly separates leading from lagging measures, requires case-mix stratification, and refuses to treat utilization or a single timestamp as waiting time. Putting activity-mix change in the days-to-weeks bucket and skill loss in the lagging bucket is coherent. Guardrails in the pilot are directionally right but have no numeric thresholds, and “two consecutive review periods” has no defined period.

Section 4 does cover the required hidden risks, including utilization near saturation. The pilot is staged, reversible, stratified, and explicit that changing every queue at once confounds the result. The recommendation is conditional and states what early averages, cross-team counts, and utilization must not be taken to prove. That part is sound.

The PM4Py sketch names every requested analysis and its caveats about queue timestamps, SLA fields, open cases, and changing `team` are right. The implementation is not. `n_cases` returns `len(pm4py.get_variants(log))`, which is a variant count, not a case count, and the promised per-100-case normalization is never computed. `discover_performance_dfg(..., aggregation_measure="median")` does not match the public signature; median is the `aggregationMeasure` parameter of the performance DFG variant, so this call fails on standard PM4Py. `observed_case_values` and `case_sla_breach_from_source_data` are undefined. `compare_slices` still filters the mutable `team` attribute after the text says to use a stable originating team. `case_metrics` takes `.first()` for specialty, complexity, and segment, which is wrong when those fields are absent on the first event. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is then passed straight into `get_service_time` with no check that the output type or columns match. Top-20 variant filtering is computed even though the rest of the answer says the rare paths are where the damage appears.
