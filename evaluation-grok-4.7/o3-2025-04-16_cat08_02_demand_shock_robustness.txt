4.5

The write-up has the required seven sections, four scenario types, policy triggers, a complete trade-off table, and a few sound design instincts (partial M1 reservation, intake throttling, partner-lab use aimed at specialist review). Those merits do not survive a check against the logs. The quantitative backbone is invented and, in the main demand scenario, contradicts the evidence given.

**Scenarios and migration.** S-1 is roughly the observed peak, not a harsher case. Weekly volume rises from 2,300 to 3,300 (+43.5%), with Urgent 29% and Complex-panel 20%. S-1’s +45% and 35/22 mix is only a small step past that, yet it claims pre-analytical 118%, preparation 125%, assay 133%, and medical validation 140%. Observed peak utilizations are 96%, 89%, 98%, and 93%. The migration story is therefore detached from the only empirical stress test in the packet. S-2 states M2 becomes “10% slower”; the notes say M2 is faster and fails more often. The “>4 h past preparation” penalty rule is not the 18-hour release clause. S-3’s 170% medical-validation figure needs an unknown headcount, and the claim that technical validators rework borderline results to dodge specialist review is behavior the log does not show. S-4’s elasticity, “1% more wave amplitude adds 0.7% to next-day Urgent cycle time,” has no basis. Specialist review, the worst peak queue at 36 h and already one-deep every second Friday, is not the primary constraint in the staff-gap scenario.

**Process-mining evidence left unused.** The DFG contains the actual migration. Direct technical release falls from about 74% to 50% of technical-validation outcomes. Medical validation rises from about 25% to 42%. Holds for specialist review rise from about 1.8% to 7.6%, with the downstream edge moving from 18 h to 48 h. The batch-wait share triples (about 4.4% to 12.7%), and Batch wait → Assay run moves from 9 h to 22 h, while Urgent batch bypass falls from 74% to 58%. Assay repeats rise from about 6.7% to 14.8%. Pre-analytical recollection roughly doubles (4.2% to 8.0%), and accession-correction paths that end in recollection rise from 14% to 26%. Normal Urgent p90 is already 19 h against an 18 h penalty line. Almost none of this is used. In its place are recollection cuts of 760→300 and 780→350, “7% assay time,” and “0.8 FTE,” with no derivation.

**Redesign logic.** R-A screens 40% of intake but claims about a 60% cut in recollection loops. The table then says the freed work may move the bottleneck to preparation; removing defects reduces preparation load. R-C sends two technical validators to accession correction 40% of the week, which is 0.8 FTE, then calls the loss 0.4 FTE. P-3 puts those same people onto medical validation. Accession cross-training does not confer medical-validation authority, and technical validation is already at 101% in the peak. R-D reports 0% effect on Routine and Complex-panel while reserving scarce M1 time and “SLA met” for Urgent, which treats priority as free on a pool at 98%. P-2 orders preparation overtime so WIP is ready for catch-up, after S-2 correctly warned that unconstrained WIP causes a technical- then medical-validation whiplash when M1 returns. Courier smoothing is credited to the outage and staff-gap scenarios, where arrival shape is not the constraint.

**Residuals and dashboard.** “Both machines are the same model” and “paediatric over-rejection” are not in the evidence. The dashboard never watches the signals the log says move first: case mix, Urgent bypass versus batch-wait share, M2/night-shift repeat rate, accession queue (two specialists, 14 h peak), specialist-review age, or the share of cases with a queue over 12 h (22% → 54%). Thresholds such as 120 cases, 4–9% rejects, and 1.2 h arrival spread are unanchored. Leading and lagging metrics are at least separated, and chain-of-custody plus LIS latency are genuine redesign-caused risks.

The package looks like a control design, but its loads, effect sizes, and one activation rule fight the DFG and the queue table. That is below a passable use of the process-mining evidence.
