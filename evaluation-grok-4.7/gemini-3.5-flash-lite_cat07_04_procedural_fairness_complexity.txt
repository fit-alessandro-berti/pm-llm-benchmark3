The write-up follows the required outline and is often numerically faithful, but it is not a reliable fairness assessment. It blurs case mix with procedure, over-reads a few queues, and states a firmer verdict than the adjusted evidence can carry. The PM4Py section would not execute the analysis it claims to specify.

The largest analytical problem is causal over-attribution. Language-support and accommodation waits (3.8 and 4.7 days) apply only to V4 and V5, about 24% of Group B and 31% of Group C. Those segments cannot, by themselves, account for the group cycle-time gaps, and they do not line up with appeals. Group C has the accommodation queue and only a +1.1 percentage-point adjusted appeal difference. Group D, which rarely uses those support paths, has the largest adjusted appeal gap (+5.7) and the worst raw approval rate. Section 3 still treats support bottlenecks as producing higher downstream appeals. That inference is inconsistent with the adjusted table.

Procedural burden is also mis-specified. Incomplete-documentation shares are case mix. The burden evidence is the residual routing after controls, especially Group B’s +9.5 percentage points on Request Missing Documents and +8.0 on Supervisor Review, against only +2.0 supervisor review for the much higher-risk Group D. Those are the clearest “still concerning after complexity” results, and the path discussion barely uses them. Shared-step waits that are not the specialist queues are likewise underused: Completeness Check to Request Missing Documents is 0.4 versus 1.6 days, and Supervisor Review to Decision Draft is 1.0 versus 1.9. Calling the 90th percentile cycle times “wait times” is simply the wrong metric.

Section 2 names the right tools but never gives an identification rule that separates disparity, discrimination, legitimate routing, and uncertainty. There are no intervals, no within-variant and within-resource contrasts, and no warning that matching on risk and completeness can block a biased mediator. Section 4 then states four mechanisms as if they generate the pattern. Handoffs are only shown to be more common, not shown to cause rework or appeals, and “automated” fraud routing is not in the evidence. “Legally necessary” is stronger than the stated policy.

The bottom line declares the process procedurally unfair and the burden “unjustifiable,” while dropping Group D and treating Group C as applicants who require accommodation rather than a group that requests it more often (62%). Residual approval gaps are a few points, with no uncertainty, and the support delays are raw conditional waits. A calibrated conclusion would split timeliness and staffing concerns from still-indeterminate outcome discrimination.

The code has the same surface-level quality. `filter_paths_performance` is called with two activity strings and 3.0/30.0. The facade expects one `(source, target)` path and performance in seconds, so this neither selects that arc nor the multi-day bottleneck. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is given a `resource_key` it does not use for this calculation. One pooled DFG is dominated by Group A, the Group B filter is never compared with anything, and the log skeleton is discovered once with no cross-group conformance check. As an operational plan, it does not support the fairness question.

Mitigations cover the required list and real trade-offs, but the risk-score row collapses audit into “remove proxies” from an automated model the scenario does not establish, and it omits the integrity cost of weakening a fraud control. Capacity, SLA, and handover proposals are reasonable and correctly do not delete rights-preserving steps.
