7.2

The write-up is complete, constraint-aware, and right on the main qualitative calls: K-337 changes decision policy rather than merely deleting time; final compliance and four-eyes stay intact; earlier readiness is not the same thing as earlier finish; and C1 reservation is a scarce-server displacement. Those strengths are undercut by temporal arithmetic that does not follow from the mechanisms given, plus a scale analysis that stays too mild.

The partial order is mostly sound, but Rule 6 is truncated. Request More Info is allowed from Compliance Review as well as Assessment, and the precedence set only emits it from Assessment. The intervention classification is otherwise right: K-219 is routing, K-337 is decision policy, K-104 is scheduling plus extra prep, and K-411 is unlocked concurrency plus logging.

K-219 contains a direct cycle-time contradiction. The base case keeps Assessment on Tuesday evening and the manager signature on Wednesday morning, which is the factual finish window, then still claims a 4–10 hour reduction. If Notify does not move, end-to-end cycle time does not move. The table’s “6–20 hours faster” band never includes that zero-gain case, even though the reversal paragraph later admits fixed manager windows can wipe the gain out. The best case, 14–24 hours with a same-day signature, also ignores the Tuesday 08:00 A2 backlog of about 6 hours. With other work held fixed, a Tuesday-morning decision is not available, so the top of that range is not a feasible scenario under the stated snapshot.

K-104 has the same loose coupling between story and hours. Moving the final review from Wednesday midday to Wednesday morning, plus 1–3 hours less re-reading, supports roughly a half-day, not the stated 8–18 hour best case. An 18 hour gain needs a Tuesday-night C1 start and a manager who signs outside the observed morning decision pattern; that path is not the one narrated. The table’s “~4–18 hours shorter” also fails to line up with the derivation’s base of 3–6 hours and worst case of 0 to +4 hours. The critical-path point itself is good: D1 still dominates until assessment, and only queue position plus less re-reading can move compliance.

K-411 is better calibrated. Parallel identity and document work can pull readiness forward by a few hours, and the Wednesday assessment queue can absorb most of that. The 0–8 hour band is defensible, though a case ready by early Tuesday afternoon could, if A2’s 6 hour morning queue actually clears, reach a same-day assessment and a larger gain. That upside is omitted rather than bounded.

The 100-case discussion names the right mechanisms, and the interference table is usable, but the magnitudes are soft. C1 and D2 are single servers. One hundred similar K-219 cases, even at a few hours each, are many weeks of D2 work, not a bottleneck that merely “vanishes” the head-start. One hundred K-104 preliminary screens are net new C1 hours unless every screen is fully offset by a shorter final review; “worse tails” understates a capacity break. Weekend clustering of forced K-337 decisions onto Friday C1/M1 is only sketched.

The ranking is still the right one. K-411 is the most robust because it uses already-legal concurrency and fails visibly. K-337 is correctly last and correctly called the dangerous speed-up. K-104 is the least transportable local win. The pilot table has every required row, but Tuesday/Thursday treatment days are confounded with the week’s strong load cycle, and case-id parity is not randomization. The assumption audit never states operating hours, despite overnight document work and morning-only decisions driving several of the hour claims.

Format, mandatory control-flow, uncertainty language, and reversal conditions are all in place. The score is held down by inconsistent cycle-time arithmetic on K-219 and K-104 and by scale effects that are directionally right but quantitatively too gentle.
