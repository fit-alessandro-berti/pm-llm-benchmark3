7.5

The memo reaches a sound, constraint-respecting decision: keep Redesign R only for conservatively defined simple low-risk houses, stop survey skipping on riskier buildings and billing before successful activation, and require segment-level evidence before any wider rollout. Figures match the brief, alternatives A–E are assessed with real conditions, the five metric conflicts are explained in the right direction, and facts, interpretations, and assumptions are mostly separated.

The score is held down by analytical errors and omissions. The evidence table treats “complex segments did not benefit” as factual, even though medium-risk apartments improved median duration by 2 days while worsening on the tail, first-time-right, and complaints. Handover reduction is attributed to coordinator ownership, although the merged validate/schedule path also removes handovers. Generalization falling from 0.70 to 0.56 is folded into “weakly constrained,” which confuses it with low precision. Low precision is a property of an overly permissive model; the memo sometimes states it as if it were itself proof that the process is uncontrolled.

Several required process-mining results are thin or unused: temporal-profile relations, DFG edge shifts, the field-survey utilization drop from 78% to 52%, the complaint shift from waiting to “installed but not working” and “billing before activation,” and the distinction between move-on-log and move-on-model. High discovered-model fitness is not reconciled with frequent alignments, which only makes sense against a different, normative reference model.

The action plan is internally inconsistent. An 8–12 week study cannot deliver 60-day post-billing cost for cases enrolled through the window. Ranking a randomized skip test first is premature while building-risk attributes are incomplete and the predictive rule is unvalidated, yet the recommendation still talks about expanding eligibility. Section 6 then launches every measurement at once, so the value-of-information ranking does not govern the decision. Rollout thresholds such as “agreed tolerance” and “toward baseline” remain too loose to adjudicate a later go/no-go.
