6.4

The answer covers every required section and is directionally sound on illusory handover cuts, East versus North/South, tails, fairness, learning, and a conditional pilot. Under a strict reading it is not nearly flawless: several quantitative claims conflict with the given data, and the PM4Py sketch would mis-measure the comparisons it is meant to support.

The central scenario is internally inconsistent on time scale. It has high-complexity shared-service waits of about 8 hours and rework up about 0.5 points, then a 10–20% worsening of the 90th-percentile cycle time. Baseline 90th percentiles are 16.4–22.8 days. Eight hours is a fraction of a day, and a 0.5-point rework change cannot add multiple days. The same gap appears in the optimistic 5–10% median-cycle-time gain. Summing the published handover frequencies times median waits moves expected handover delay by roughly 1–2 hours against medians of 6.8–8.6 days. Those cycle-time percentages are not entailed by the waiting-time story in the same scenario.

Legal/Risk queueing is too mild relative to the stated load. Legal and Risk are already at 93% and 91% utilization, and routed volume rises from 9 to 12 per 100 cases, about a one-third increase. That push is large enough to drive utilization through 1, so the queue does not settle at 11–13 hours or “stay near 9–10 hours because triage improves.” Broader detection increases arrivals. The answer elsewhere states that waits grow non-linearly near full utilization, then does not apply that mechanism to the scenario numbers. The central case also assigns triage mainly to the Legal/Risk queue, while the stated staff risk is that the technical pool absorbs legal/risk triage and difficult quality check on top of the jump from 36 to 41 technical referrals.

Section 4 misstates Little’s Law. \(L=\lambda W\) is a steady-state identity. It does not say that queue length grows over time unless arrivals fall or capacity rises. That is the stability condition \(\lambda<\mu\). The same paragraph says average cycle time can stay stable while waiting time climbs because work accumulates in queues. For a fixed case, accumulated queue time is part of cycle time, so that pair of claims needs an explicit mix or service-time offset, which is not supplied.

The code sketch has operational errors. `filter_event_attribute_values` takes a collection of values; passing `"North"` iterates characters and filters to nothing. `compare_stratum` drops events and then recomputes cycle time from the remaining timestamps, so a cross-team case is timed only on one team’s fragment. The later case-level filter does the opposite and can assign the whole cycle to any team that touched the case. Neither defines an owning team. `wait_since_prev_h` is inter-completion time, including the previous activity’s service, but it is printed as queue waiting. The handover-rework table keeps every handover in a case that has any rework flag, so it is not rework after that handover. The first event of each case is counted as a handover because `resource != NaN` is true. Working-together, subcontracting, and resource-similarity results are computed and then discarded. `outcome == "SLA_miss"` and `customer_segment == "language_need"` are invented encodings. Performance-DFG values are left in raw seconds.

Smaller problems add up. The optimistic “total handovers fall 5–10%” is not reconciled with the management table, whose listed edges sum slightly higher (about 343 to about 359 per 100 cases). The pilot uses 8- and 12-week windows for 90th- and 99th-percentile cycle times without a completion rule for cases that are still open, which biases exactly the tail metrics the design treats as guardrails. Informal local advice is correctly described as easy to miss in the log, while a numeric guardrail still depends on those handovers being recorded.

The mechanism map, indicator split, rollout sequencing that starts where E_Tech is already saturated, and the refusal to treat an early average cycle time as success are the parts that hold up.
