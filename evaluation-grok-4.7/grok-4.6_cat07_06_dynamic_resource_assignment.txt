6.7

The roster is mostly constraint-aware and the write-up covers every required section, but the policy is not internally executable: the scorer would not produce the published assignments, the tightest SLA case is justified with a handover cost the author’s own evidence contradicts, and the urgency term is numerically inert for most of the queue.

The recommended board does satisfy the hard gates that can be checked directly. Security performers are skill 3 (Hugo on C001 and C015, Gita on C008). Approvals are finalized by Farid at skill 3. No urgent or high case goes to an unsupervised skill below 2. Bruno, David, and Iris receive no new performing work. Quality batching of C006 with C012 meets the normal/low and >24h rule. Jonas stops at exactly four new cases. C007 and C016 correctly stay with competent owners under high complexity and high handover cost. The stated decision order matches priority, then SLA, then complexity. Training is counted only where the matrix supports it, and skill 0 on Security for Eva and Jonas is correctly treated as a hard block rather than a supervised performance.

Those checks do not make the algorithm and the table the same policy. On C003, Farid is an eligible diagnosis trainee (skill 1, training priority diagnosis, low overtime, 6.5h, only two prior batch items). Under the published normal weights, his workload term beats Amal’s by enough to win, because remaining hours are never reduced by work already given in the batch and each new assignment costs only 0.08. The resilience term does not protect him either: it penalizes scarce specialists only for the skill the case requires, so the sole skill-3 approver is freely scored as a diagnosis trainee. The table nevertheless gives both diagnosis files to Amal and a third case, C010, to Farid. Section 2.5 promises that every row stores the top-two scores; section 3 never computes any. The board is a manual plan presented as the output of EMSA.

C001 is the sharpest substantive error. The same answer treats a high-complexity owner change as a median +2.7h wait, then clears that cost with a 15-minute briefing so Hugo can fit a 0.88 security review inside a 3h SLA. Applying the cited 2.7h figure leaves about 0.3h of work time, so the recommended move can miss the SLA as well. Keeping Celine as performer is also bad (skill 1, median +3.3h). Both horns fail, and the text does not say so. It also rewrites constraint 5: supervision by Gita or Hugo is feasible, which is exactly when the problem still prefers continuity, and the extra “service time would miss the SLA” escape is the author’s addition. A pair working the file, or an explicit dual-miss escalation, is the analysis the evidence supports.

Calibration undermines the weight story. `U = priority_w * sigmoid(-slack/4h)` is already near zero once slack exceeds roughly half a day, which is most normal and low cases (about 0.02 at 18h). Multiplying by a normal weight of 0.14 makes urgency a rounding error, so training and skill decide those cases. The claim that all signals are min-maxed into [0, 1] is not implemented; U, S, and C can exceed 1, and non-owner continuity can go negative. Prose and code disagree on the service-time penalty (`4 - skill` versus `max(0, 3 - skill)`), the Security skill-3 bonus, the skill-2 training score of 0.40, the extra resilience penalties, the training-quota boost, batch merging, and the tie-break (owner and overtime risk are missing from the sort key). `assign_batch` therefore cannot be audited against section 2.

C003 is also the wrong place to buy the 20% training quota. It is a repeated diagnosis loop at reassignment count 2; moving it to a skill-1 trainee creates the >2-owner condition the answer itself links to 1.8× rework. Celine is skill 3, low overtime, and not yet assignment-capped. The “SLA ≥ 16h allows a trainee” exception fights the loop-break rule in the same section. One clean training case, C009, would miss 20% (1/9). The quota is met by the risky case. Farid’s diagnosis training priority is never discussed, so the feasible training set is understated.

Capacity is asserted rather than checked with the service function the policy defines. Eva is given C016 and C005 (about 4h and 2.7h at the sketch’s default 2h base) against 5.0h remaining. Celine is given a 0.81 rework with a 6h SLA plus two review gates inside a 6h day, while supervision is declared a half-load and then omitted from `new_load`. Jonas is used exactly like the first-available sink the comparison criticizes, across quality, eligibility, and rework, up to the cap.

The PM4Py sketch names the right family of techniques, but several audit mechanics are wrong or empty. `get_service_time` returns a scalar, not a per-activity map with `.get`. The SLA filter is an explicit no-op (`0` to `1e12`). Policy deviation joins the case’s last event resource to the recommended assignee, so a later activity by someone else is counted as non-compliance. Training equality assumes `state["train"]` matches `required_skill` despite labels such as `diagnosis` versus `Diagnosis`, which would zero the training KPI the quota depends on. Handover rate based on `nunique(resource) > 1` mixes history with this decision.

Section 4’s directional comparison is fair and refuses a false optimum, and the baseline sketches of C001→Celine and C002→Jonas under first-available, and of Security collapsing onto Hugo under specialist-only, follow the stated tie rules. Section 5 is specific and non-punitive. Those strengths do not repair the contradiction between the scorer, the evidence, and the board a team would actually run.
