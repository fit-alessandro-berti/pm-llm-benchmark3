The answer is a careful, mostly accurate causal assessment: availability is separated from fast-path completion, staggered adoption and interference are taken seriously, median and p90 are not collapsed, the “reduces cycle time by X%” claim is rejected, and the rollout is tied to a learning design. Checked pre/post changes, control differences, and control-specific ranges match the panel (for example Alpha median −0.40 h and p90 +9.65 h; Delta/Epsilon early-window changes about +5.22 h and +16.05 h). Format, required estimands, mechanisms, threats, and code obligations are met. The score is held down by internal inconsistencies and by analysis that is weaker than the identification argument.

The largest logical problem is Section 7. The ranges (−6.39 to −2.58 h for the median, similarly one-sided ranges for p90, rework, escalation, and CSAT) are envelopes of descriptive differences in changes. The same rows say a causal worsening remains possible, and Section 5 correctly says these exercises do not produce a finite causal bound. A one-sided interval cannot be the plausible causal range once common shocks, routing, staffing, and spillovers are admitted. Confidence 80 in “suggestive relative improvement” for both median and p90 is too high for three pre-months, five branches, and co-timed demand, mix, and FTE changes. The safe claims in the last column are better calibrated than the confidence column.

Section 5 averages away the dynamic path the primary strategy says matters. Alpha’s median is 48.3, 49.5, 53.5, 47.9, 43.3 after adoption: it worsens through June and improves only as arrivals fall and fast-path share peaks. That non-monotonicity is mostly invisible in the −0.40 h post-minus-pre figure. The least confounded contrast—April versus January–March, before the May complexity jump and Alpha’s FTE loss—is not reported. July–August improvement is also shared with never-treated branches; the Gamma window adjustment handles this only in aggregate. Workload is never quantified, although arrivals per productive FTE rise sharply at Alpha and Beta and are a direct competing explanation for flat medians and worse tails.

The intensity “analysis” is an uncontrolled April-to-August co-movement. Fast-path completion is selected on case difficulty and on not escalating, so it is a poor dose. The code then fits the naïve two-way fixed-effects specification the prose warns can mix staggered effects with bad weights, builds relative-time indicators, and never estimates them. Clustered standard errors are correctly labeled unreliable, but the only printed coefficients are the ones the identification section declines to treat as the target. The Delta-versus-Epsilon ranges are not produced by the script.

Smaller gaps: rework and escalation sit on long plateaus, so “monthly” changes contain less information than the tables imply; Beta’s April FTE gain is concurrent with adoption and is not singled out; the conditionally causal ladder bullet states that an estimator could work under assumptions, without a conditional magnitude; branch-level randomization of Delta versus Epsilon cannot meet the decision rule that uncertainty exclude a consequential loss. That limit is acknowledged and partly offset by the call for team- or case-level randomization, prespecified guardrails, and no silent routing or staffing changes.

No material arithmetic error turned up, and the qualitative conclusion—do not issue a single percent effect; do not read raw tail worsening and relative differences as the same fact; expand only inside an evaluation that can still be stopped—is the right one. The deductions are for over-narrow ranges, overstated confidence, and a quantitative section that does not execute the event-study design it recommends.
