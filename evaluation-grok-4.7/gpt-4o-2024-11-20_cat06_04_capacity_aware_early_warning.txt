The memo gets three structural calls right: I-03 is ineligible once diagnosis is complete, I-05 is suppressed as an unchanged repeat while I-07 can re-enter on the new log, and one slot is held back. Those points are not enough. The decision rule is not deployable, contradicts itself, and the impact figures cannot be reconciled with the case data.

The calibration adjustments reverse the stated bias. SME underprediction means true breach risk is higher, so the probability should move up by about 0.04–0.09. Strategic Enterprise overprediction means true risk is lower, so it should move down by about 0.05–0.12. The memo does the opposite, and by a single point inside each range. That error drives eligibility: I-04 at 0.48 and I-08 at 0.43 clear the 0.50 gate only after the upward Strategic Enterprise shift, while I-10 is pushed further below it. The same section then imposes a hard 0.50 screen, which the task rules out as a sufficient rule, and does not show a numeric score that subtracts €450, false-alarm burden, and a congestion-dependent spillover.

The live portfolio does not follow the rule that is written. I-02 is a second alert with no new event and is still selected. I-04 is selected with about 10 hours of slack in the overpredicting segment. I-07 is selected on a robust benefit that is thin: even the top of 0.20–0.32 times €5,000, minus €450 and €50, is only about €1,100, and the floor is about €500. I-09, still in diagnosis with 2.5 hours left and a lower-bound benefit around €2,350 before uncertainty discount, is told to wait with no review time. I-06, whose jump may be an imputed queue field, is also left to wait with no quarantine or repair step, even though diagnosis can finish and the swarm then does nothing. I-01’s public-sector range is treated as unusually uncertain, while I-02’s narrower 0.08–0.22 range is treated as reasonable. “Wait” has no trigger, owner, or deadline.

The impact table is not auditable. Upper-bound risk reductions on the three chosen cases sum to 0.22 + 0.28 + 0.32 = 0.82, and the lower bounds sum to 0.38. The table’s 1.3–2.1, 0.9–1.5, and 0.4–0.8 expected breaches do not match those ranges. Gross benefit at the top of the supplied effects is about €5,500 + €16,800 + €1,600 = €23,900, not €67,000–€112,000. False-alarm burdens (€600 + €2,000 + €50) never appear. Spillover euros are small enough to look immaterial, with no value, congestion, or 8–20 minute delay shown. The adverse row still nets well above €15,000 on its own figures, yet the text calls that outcome marginal or negative.

Repeated-alert policy is only partly operational. The I-05 / I-07 contrast is right, and a 10-point change or a real new event is a usable materiality test. “Two full cycles” is undefined as clock time, and “two SLA thresholds” does not name a re-entry condition. Alert suppression is also conflated with the separate ban on a second swarm.

Fairness covers the required mechanisms, but the safeguards fight the rest of the policy. A 20% weekly quota is not tied to daily four-slot capacity and can force low-effect work. The fairness reserve for sparse segments is announced, then the only Non-Native-Language Support case is deferred and the spare slot is kept for a regulatory arrival instead. Charging spillover only through a later audit leaves the ranking unchanged. Label separation is named, without a treatment flag, pre-swarm features, or a design that can estimate effects when treatment is chosen from the score.

Monitoring lists the right families and then sets an 80–90% utilization target that presses the reserve and the option value of idle slots. Comparing outcomes with ordinary non-intervened cases does not identify the swarm effect, because those cases were not selected at random. There is no numeric rule for when calibration error, realized risk reduction, or queue delay should change the allocation rule, and no monitor for failed live features like I-06’s missing queue field.
