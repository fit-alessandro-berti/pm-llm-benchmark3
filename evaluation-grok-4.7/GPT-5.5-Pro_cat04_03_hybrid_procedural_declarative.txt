7.2

The hybrid design is conceptually strong: R5 and R6 are kept apart, unconditional legal-opinion precedence is correctly rejected, `altresponse` is distinguished from `response` and `chainresponse`, at-most-once fits the log skeleton, and the eight overall verdicts match the chosen strict policy. It is not nearly flawless. Several encodings do not mean what the verdict matrix claims, and a few justifications do not hold.

The log skeleton is not executable as written. In PM4Py, `directly_follows` is a closed-world allow-list: every consecutive pair must be in the set. An empty set therefore rejects every trace longer than one event, including T1–T3. The text treats emptiness as “no directly-follows constraint” because the three checks interleave, and then marks the log skeleton `pass` for those traces. That defense is not the checker’s semantics. The matrix is right only under an unstated open-world reading. The same section’s reason for an empty `equivalence` is also weak. R11 adds `Suspend Vendor` and another `Verify Bank Account`; it does not by itself split the counts of `Activate Vendor` and `Release First Payment`. Those two are still paired by `always_after`. Either suspension may omit payment, in which case that `always_after` rule is too strong, or it may not, in which case equivalence is safe. Both cannot be justified at once.

The procedural projection collides with R11. `Verify Bank Account` is both a once-only parallel check and the later re-verification, under the same label, and it stays inside `BACKBONE_ACTIVITIES`. A second verify is a move-on-log in this tree, so a trace that obeys R11 fails the canonical procedural check. Log-skeleton frequency is deliberately left open for that activity; the process tree is not. No test exercises this, so the matrix hides it.

R3’s state machine only fails if `Reject Vendor` or `Activate Vendor` occurs while a request is pending. A request opened after activation, then answered, produces no finding, even though the response cannot precede the final outcome. `always_after` from request to response is also weaker than `altresponse`: one late response can cover several earlier requests. That gap is not stated.

The mapping table often names two “primary” formalisms and only sometimes says which layer is authoritative (R1, R3, R7, R8). Section 10 repairs some of this, but not the table the task asked for. Confidence values of 0.95 and 0.80 do not soften `conformance_declare`; they only look softer. Section 9 admits that, while the dictionary still presents them as graded strictness.

Temporal arithmetic is right: seconds, `zeta = 2`, T7 at 34 h gives z ≈ 3.67, and the 2 h payment has z ≈ 1.94. Because every supplied mean is exactly `2 × std`, every lower bound is 0, so no positive duration can be “too fast.” Calling the 2 h payment “just inside” the 0–144 h band obscures that the lower tail cannot fire. T5’s temporal cell is `pass` even though the clarification pair was never observed.

T6 is handled correctly under both resolutions: strict legal fails at `Activate Vendor@13`; without an emergency attribute the operations reading is `policy-dependent`, and 23 − 13 = 10 h would meet a 24 h post-activation clock. T4 fails only on Cora performing both verification and manager approval. T8 fails as soon as rejection co-occurs with activation. Those verdicts are the best part of the answer. The remaining defects are semantic, and they are large enough that the dictionaries and the matrix cannot be taken as one coherent executable model.
