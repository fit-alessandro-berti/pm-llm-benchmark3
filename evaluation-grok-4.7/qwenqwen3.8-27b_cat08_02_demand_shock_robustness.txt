**8.0**

The redesign meets the required structure and is strong on scenario coverage, case-type trade-offs, trigger-based policies, downstream overload, self-inflicted risk, and leading-versus-lagging monitoring. It correctly refuses strict urgent-first dispatch and routine-release holding, and it uses the main DFG shifts (recollection, batch wait, assay repeats, medical validation, specialist hold) in the right direction.

It is not nearly flawless. The mix-shift bottleneck sequence starts at pre-analytical check and accession correction, while the peak evidence already shows technical validation at 101% utilization and the longest queues at specialist review (36h), medical validation (22h), and assay (18h). That is a flow story, not the binding-constraint order. Several DFG comparisons use raw counts across an 8-week and a 3-week window (holds 290 versus 610; repeat-to-medical 80 versus 120) without weekly rates, so the magnitude is understated. Recollection is described as re-entering registration, but the DFG returns those cases at Receive sample; clerk load is from explaining errors, not a second registration. M2-specific repeat thresholds (10%, 15%, 18%) are stated as if measured, although only a higher M2 failure rate is given. The routine-batch activation rule is written as “M2 repeat rate 10%,” which is not an operable on/off condition. Proposed surge sizing is not checked against the 93–101% peak utilizations, and automating registration is not tied clearly enough to the assay bottleneck it can expose.
