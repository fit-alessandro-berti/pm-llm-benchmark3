6.6

The control analysis is substantial and often well judged, but it is not close to flawless. Several case facts are mishandled, the equivalence rule is internally unstable, and the required program does not run.

The strongest work is the refusal to treat a log event as a sufficient control. Same-user cases are not all failed, and distinct-user cases are not all passed. AP-202 is correctly a failed, conflicted override with no timely recovery. AP-204 is correctly a formal four-eyes pass whose objective still failed on a fabricated receipt, without treating the shared workstation as proof of collusion. AP-205 is correctly stopped before completion. AP-206 is correctly left indeterminate because the token timestamp may be either a real ED-1 breach or a logging defect. AP-209 is appropriately separated from AP-204. The hybrid reading of a manager action after release but before settlement is coherent, and the monitoring section properly separates alerts from proof.

Those judgments are undercut by concrete errors.

AP-203’s audit did not occur before settlement. Release was 10:08, settlement was 12:00, and `Audit_Review` was 15:00. The manager review was pre-release; the audit was about three hours after settlement. The corrective-capacity claim that “review and audit occurred before the 12:00 settlement” is false and makes the emergency package look stronger than the timeline supports.

AP-208’s vendor does not fail the 180-day rule. Eight months is longer than 180 days, so vendor tenure is a satisfied condition. Amount, the three-day bank change, the validation outage, post-settlement ratification, and the four-day audit are the failures. The failed classification survives, but the stated rationale and the code’s `vendor_active_180d = "NO"` do not.

AP-201’s recall conclusion is too coarse. Audit was about 24 hours 54 minutes after the 08:06 release, but settlement was 10:00 and the audit was 09:00 the next day, about 23 hours later. On a settlement-based 24-hour recall clock, the window was still open. “Largely lost” and “after the usual recall window” are not established.

The sufficiency test says a fully evidenced ED-1 package could be case-equivalent, then withholds `OBJECTIVE_MET` whenever release-stage independence is absent. Every valid ED-1 case lacks a second releaser by definition, so the test makes full equivalence unreachable for the very package it says can achieve it. AP-207, where the known ED-1 conditions are actually supported, is the case most affected.

The code also fails as an executable check. `pm4py.format_dataframe` is called with `activity`, `timestamp`, and `resource`; the usual parameters are `activity_key` and `timestamp_key`, and `resource` is not a formatting argument, so mainstream PM4Py raises `TypeError`. If that call were corrected, `bool(case_mask & log[ACT].eq(...).any())` still raises `ValueError` because `.any()` applies only to the comparison and `bool` is then called on a Series. The later pair merge uses `validate="one_to_one"` even though AP-204 and AP-209 share the U12/U13 pair, which is a many-to-one merge and also raises. The embedded facts add further errors: AP-201’s stated absence of a bank change is encoded as unknown; AP-202’s audit is described as about two days although it was on May 12; narrative settlement times for AP-201 and AP-203 never enter the manager-timing flags; and the u12/u13 history of 37 payments is reduced to 2 on AP-209. The version caveat and the “not a verdict” label are appropriate, but they do not make this block executable or factually aligned with the case table.
