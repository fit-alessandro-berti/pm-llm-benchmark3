The memo meets the required structure and reaches a defensible recommendation: do not scale Redesign R nationally, do not fully roll back, and instead limit the proven simple-house package while restoring activation-before-billing and permit checks. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are separated in one table with enough items, and the five metric conflicts are explained with real mechanisms (over-generalization, mixture plus queueing, lost redundancy, accounting-boundary shift, and segment heterogeneity). Process-mining evidence is used, not ignored: fitness/precision, alignments, DFG edges, resource loads, social networks, and segment splits all inform the decision. The value-of-information ranking is the strongest part. It orders M6, M3, M2, M4, and M1 by chance of changing the decision per cost and time, sequences M1 after the cheaper diagnostics, and correctly treats M7 as a control rather than new information.

The score is held down by material slips. The memo’s case against rollback says a queue-driven tail would survive a return to baseline, but the 93% coordinator load and the second-visit bottleneck are features of R, so rollback would remove that queue. “Non-simple 47%” is wrong: apartments plus old buildings are 47%, while non-simple volume is 66%, and business and rural cases also worsen on tail, first-time-right, and complaints. The interpretation paragraph collapses four distinct levers into “removing handovers,” which later sections describe more carefully. “Validated” simple-house results overstate an observational, region-skewed pilot. The precision gate of 0.75 sits below the baseline 0.79 without explanation, and a zero billing-before-activation gate conflicts with the allowed exception path. The six-week window versus a 62-day p95, and the incomplete use of the address-validation and apartment activation temporal findings, are missed limits on the tail evidence.
