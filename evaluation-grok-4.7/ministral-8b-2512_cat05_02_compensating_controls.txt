4.5

The write-up has the required headings and several defensible headline labels, but it is not a reliable conformance assessment. Classifications often land near the right bucket for the wrong control reason, and the mandatory checking code would not execute or implement the specified tests.

Section 1 names five threats, yet the prevent/detect/correct assignments are frequently wrong. MG-4 is treated as detective/corrective for unauthorized payment even though its value is preventing release or irrevocable settlement. RA-7 is called corrective for bank compromise, although audit is detective and only partly corrective while recall or recovery is still possible. Collusion coverage is too absolute: ED-1’s independent manager can address operator collusion and still fails against a conflicted or rubber-stamp approver. The decomposition misses the threats the facts actually turn on: goods or services not received, shared or borrowed credentials, conflicted approval, and warning overrides. AV-2 is also under-specified as “duplicate/sanctions” despite the stated ownership, limit, PO-match, and seven-day bank-change checks, and over-specified as if those checks covered erroneous payment generally.

Section 2 never defines “merely mitigating,” so one of the four required sufficiency grades is missing. The fully-equivalent criteria are a flat checklist, not a decision rule separating full equivalence, partial equivalence, mitigation, and ineffectiveness. The same-expected-loss discussion does not explain alternative standards such as tail loss, irreversibility, accountability, evidence quality, or threat coverage. It then uses AP-207 as “partially equivalent” because MG-4 was “late,” which contradicts both ED-1 and this answer’s own later OBJECTIVE_MET label: ratification was before settlement, by an independent manager, after a real review.

Section 3’s labels are the strongest part: AP-202 and AP-208 failed, AP-205 was stopped, AP-204’s distinct users did not meet the objective, and AP-203/AP-207 are plausible ED-1 cases. The supporting cells are not. AP-202 says AV-2 “blocked” the payment; the log and facts show a warning that u23 overrode. AP-201 lists preventive controls as “None” despite a full AV-2 pass and an independent pre-settlement review. Corrective capacity is systematically “None” because no defect was found or settlement occurred; that confuses realized correction with capacity. AP-201’s audit starts inside 24 hours of settlement, and AP-203, AP-206, AP-207, and AP-209 were still inside a normal recall window. AP-204’s fabricated receipt supports OBJECTIVE_FAILED, but “collusion likely,” “loss irrecoverable,” and high confidence overclaim the facts; design documents are missing, the actors deny collusion, and recovery is unstated. AV-2 did not cover that receipt failure, and the complete absence of MG-4 is omitted. AP-209 is explicitly “the same as AP-204” even though no defect was found. The missing-evidence column repeatedly stores conclusions (“No collusion risk,” “No residual risk”) rather than evidence gaps.

Section 4 does not choose one semantics for manager action after release but before settlement. One row calls every pre-settlement MG-4 preventive with no hybrid, and another calls post-release MG-4 detective/corrective, so the exact overlap is classified both ways. Pre-settlement approval can still stop irrevocable loss; it is not corrective merely because release already happened. AV-2 is described as blocking failed checks, omitting the warning-and-override path that defeated AP-202. ED-1 is labeled a preventive control before settlement even though it is a conditional permission whose audit limb occurs afterward.

Section 5 meets the five-case minimum but misstates mechanisms. A late token in AP-206 is a permission defect, not a loss mechanism, and no loss occurred. AP-209’s “repeated collusion” did not produce a demonstrated loss. AP-204 already had a second user; the counterfactual is independence, not the mere existence of another releaser. AP-202’s chain broke at same-user override of a warning with a supervisor’s credential, not at failure to notice a bank change AV-2 had already flagged. Ranges stay at bare high/medium/low labels.

Section 6 lists the requested monitoring topics, then invents thresholds and places ordinary control failures under “Proof of Misconduct,” including late approval, a missed recall window, and a recent bank change. The alert-versus-proof sentence is grammatically incomplete, and evidence collection is thin: credential identity, token-versus-prepare timestamps, review artifacts, conflict register, workstation identifiers, and outage logs are not specified. A raw same-user rate also ignores that ED-1 permits some same-user payments.

Section 7 misses the specification. The CSV stops at AP-205. The code never calls `pm4py.format_dataframe`. It does not call `pm4py.filter_four_eyes_principle`; it imports a nonexistent filter API and assumes, contrary to the usual default `positive=True` behavior, that the result is the violating same-resource cases. Blocked releases are sought with a nonexistent trace-attribute filter. The case-fact table fabricates `before_release` manager timing for AP-204 and AP-209, which have no manager event. Emergency eligibility tests only a token flag, amount, and the literal `same_day`, omitting vendor age, bank-change, no-override, manager independence, and the one-business-day rule. The converter parameters and subsequent dataframe indexing of an event log would fail. The closing note correctly says the output is not a verdict, but the output could not be produced as written.
