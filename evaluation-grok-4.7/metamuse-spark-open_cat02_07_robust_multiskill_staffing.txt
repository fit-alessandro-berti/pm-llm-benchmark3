The written analysis is a serious capacity argument with the right control-flow structure, but the required quantitative comparison and the required Python do not support it. That is enough to keep this well below a strong score.

The offered-load arithmetic is mostly auditable and correct. Block capacities sum to 2490 minutes, raw offered work is about 2721 minutes (109%), and the 14:00–20:00 shift collapse is real: mechanical supply is zero after 18:00, and software in 16:00–18:00 sits on Emil alone. Midpoint expectations (Intake 6.94, Mechanical 26.3, Electrical 32.82, Software 38.5, Validation 15.74) and the doubled-tail expectations check out. U1–U5 are distinguished rather than treated as one generic overload, U2’s “double then renormalize” ambiguity is bounded, and the recommendation does not simply minimize base-case mean delay. Assumptions, two objectives (SLA/tail and fairness/robustness), three operational policies, handover/setup/parallel/preemption rules, PM4Py event attributes, and a guarded pilot are all present under the required headings.

The core results contradict that load model. Section 2 concludes the base system is about 136% loaded after multipliers and penalties and accumulates on the order of 11 cases of backlog per day. Section 4 then gives Policy C a base maximum utilization of 92% and a mean delay only about 25 minutes worse than full pooling (235 vs 210), while still claiming about 88% of P1 cases finish within 120 minutes. If aggregate offered work exceeds aggregate staff-hours, resources cannot all sit at or under 92% and still clear nearly the same work as a policy reported at 105%+. Reservation idle time would widen delay, not shave it by 12%. The “90% bootstrap / 5 Poisson replications / calibrated Monte Carlo” wording presents those figures as simulation output; they are hand-set and are not what Section 8 computes.

Policy A is operationally inconsistent with the shift table. Strict mechanical eligibility is Ada and Ben only, so Deepa is excluded and mechanical capacity is already zero from 16:00, not merely from 18:00. The failure-mode column still says “18–20 mechanical starvation.” Objective weights are also typographically empty (`J1 = ·E[D] + ·P95[D] + …`), so O1 is not a reproducible formula.

Section 8 does not meet the reproducibility bar. The fairness line is a syntax error (`utils=[r["busy"]/((…)*100 for r in resources]`), so the script cannot run. Even if parsed, it would not implement the analysis: every resource’s `next_free` starts at 08:00, so Deepa, Emil, and Farah are assignable outside their shifts; each case is pushed through Intake, diagnostics, and Validation before the next case is considered, which locks future resource time and invents idle gaps; preemption is a 7% coin-flip, not a once-per-case restart of remaining service; Policy C’s reservation is a random chance of skipping Emil; `mins_in_block` is unused. PM4Py calls sit in `try/except` and the success path never actually separates stage-level queue time from service time. A labeled scaffold was allowed; a script that does not parse, and that contradicts the shift constraints the prose treats as decisive, is not that scaffold.

Sections 6, 7, and 9 are the most reliable part of the answer: gaming, P3 starvation, skill erosion, validation synchronization, interpretable dispatch logs, and a P1-protected pilot are concrete. They do not repair the inconsistent policy table or the broken executable.
