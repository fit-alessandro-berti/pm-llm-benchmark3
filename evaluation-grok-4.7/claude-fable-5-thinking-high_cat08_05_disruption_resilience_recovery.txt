7.3

The analysis is structurally complete and often sharp, but it is not nearly flawless. Several quantitative and logical errors sit on the exact points the task asks to be judged: DFG grounding, the resilience/noncompliance split, and recovery design.

The clearest factual error is the dispatch count. Manual pack sends 1,800 cases straight to Dispatch without scan, and Temporary release control sends another 1,450, so the activity is entered about 3,250 times. Phone proof of delivery is a later arc of 2,600. Section 1 treats 2,600 as the handoff volume “of which 1,800” bypassed release. That “of which” relation is false: 1,800 is not a subset of 2,600. The same 2,600 figure is then reused in the table, indicators, and recommendations as the size of the largest traceability gap, understating it and hiding the roughly 650 dispatch-without-scan cases that do not even continue to phone proof.

The cold-chain rate is used in two incompatible ways. Section 3 says 4.8% of shipments have unknowable temperature history. Section 6 says 4.8% of cold-chain shipments. The source never defines the denominator, and the answer does not notice that it switched claims.

Pattern K is misclassified. The resilience test in section 3 is control equivalence at the time of the transaction. K is post-hoc book repair, grouped by the source with L as hidden after-the-fact workload. Listing K with true resilience, under a sentence about keeping controls alive during the incident, breaks the answer’s own test. L is handled more carefully than K, which makes the split look ad hoc.

The recovery model contradicts itself. DOM exit requires inventory-reconciliation variance already below threshold, while the reconciliation flow is defined as starting on exit and finishing within five business days. Those cannot both be the exit rule.

Timeline handling is uneven. T0–T2 is well grounded, including the hospital-versus-pharmacy asymmetry and the move-on-model gaps. T4 then treats the Day 7–21 aggregate as an ending state (“ending at 2.2%”) and says median cycle time looks recovered while citing only the hospital median of 8h. Pharmacy-standard is still 28h versus 23h and wholesale-bulk 58h versus 36h, with p90s of 74h and 120h still far from 49h and 72h. Two of the three stated temporal-profile deviations are also dropped: backlog-to-pick exceeding baseline p95, and the highly variable delay from unscanned dispatch to phone proof. Only the audit tail is used.

Smaller overclaims accumulate. Manual authorization is about 49% of the 5,100 triaged orders, not “~45% of triaged orders.” Snapshot staleness is said to feed the whole 12.5% rework rate. Deferral is said to have “directly enabled” the 91% hospital fill rate. The fast lane is still described as sometimes bypassing authorization, although 920 of 1,000 fast-lane cases go straight to the snapshot with no authorization activity on that path. The 200 “still open” audit cases are a hedged reading of 2,300 versus 2,100, but the same flow-imbalance logic is not applied to the dispatch arcs, so the method is selective. Calling T3 the most dangerous phase because a Day-0 snapshot would then be 3–6 days old is speculative: no partial-recovery DFG shows that snapshot use continued, and every reported control metric improved from T2 to T3.

What keeps this well above a mid-range score is the judgment that actually matters. C and G are correctly refused as resilience. B, I, J, and the priority function of A are kept only with compensating controls. After-the-fact remediation is explicitly not treated as real-time compliance. The redesign has triggers, risk-tiered floors, contemporaneous evidence, a no-bypass release gate, and a rule that recovery is not declared when cycle time normalizes. The trade-off statement tells management what degradation to accept and what shipment to refuse. Those are the right conclusions, reached despite the counting error, the K contradiction, and the exit-rule conflict.
