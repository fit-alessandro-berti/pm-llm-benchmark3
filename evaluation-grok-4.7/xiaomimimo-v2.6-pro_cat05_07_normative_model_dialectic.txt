7.7

The final judgment is strong and mostly well-supported, but it is not nearly flawless. Three concrete errors in the concluding analysis and the required revision keep it well below a top score.

The dialectical structure holds up. Both briefs are actually argued, concessions are real, and the judgment rejects specific overclaims rather than averaging them. M0 is read correctly: the checks are concurrent and both mandatory before the decision, approval precedes activation on the standard branch, and notification is an M0 duty rather than a CS-9 duty. SUP-708, SUP-712, SUP-703, SUP-705, and SUP-714 are classified for the right dominant reasons, and the matrix is traceable to the case facts and the aggregates.

The final conclusions still contain hard defects. Section 5.2 repeats the claim that 10 of 15 traces require no misconduct finding. That set includes SUP-712 and SUP-715, which the same judgment classifies as NONCOMPLIANT. “Two-thirds without misconduct” is therefore false on the answer’s own table. Section 5.3 also invokes an authority “Test F” that was never defined.

The revised model does not implement the architecture’s own scope rule. S2 says reactivation does not require `Register_Supplier`, but M1 places `register → stage` above every route, including reactivation, so SUP-710 remains nonconforming for a reason the revision claimed to remove. The data layer contradicts the emergency route: `R-C4` requires approval before every activation, while FD-7 and `r_emergency` allow retrospective approval, so SUP-705 fails a rule the model just authorized. `evaluate_case` also never checks that an emergency route is inside FD-7’s window, and the demo invents the master-data timestamp that the judgment treats as missing. Those are failures of the required model and data proposal, not presentation nits.
