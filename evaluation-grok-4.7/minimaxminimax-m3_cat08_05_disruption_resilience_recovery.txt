The answer covers all seven required sections and correctly treats throughput preservation, after-the-fact remediation, and control loss as different things. That conceptual core is not enough to offset several concrete errors.

The timeline misplaces the stabilization directly-follows graph. Reconcile manual reservation (1,400) and proof-of-delivery remediation (1,200) are Day 7–21 counts, but they are described as appearing in partial recovery (Day 3–6). Later text then treats them as having persisted from that earlier phase.

The fast-lane reading contradicts the graph. Emergency triage splits into manual authorization (2,500), skip authorization (1,600), and the hospital fast lane (1,000). Those are sibling branches. The table uses the 1,600 skip cases as evidence that the fast lane itself bypassed authorization, and section 3 then says the fast lane preserved the 91% hospital fill rate because it was supported by the manual authorization check. Neither claim follows from the flows.

The recovery design is internally inconsistent. Section 4 allows sample-based authorization for ambient pharmacy and wholesale orders and sample release checks for some priority-1 orders, while the hospital rule, recommendation 2, and the management statement require an authorization check on every order and full release control for hospital orders. The model also calls a priority-1 limit a deferral SLA even though priority 1 is not the deferral path.

Smaller fidelity problems add up. Stabilization rework (1.7×) and cancellations (1.4×) are grouped into a “2–4× baseline” claim that fits only some exception rates, and not at 4×. Manual picking is said to support the “F-pattern” of dispatch without scan; that pattern is G. Fitness of 0.90 is treated as not a sign of recovery, although fitness and fitting traces rose sharply and the residual gap also includes live deviations, not only the audit tail. The 1,400 reconciliations and 1,200 delivery remediations are attributed wholly to the snapshot and unscanned dispatch. The pharmacy fill rate of 61% is used as evidence that those shortcuts bought little throughput, although deferral was an intentional capacity shift. “Cancelled correctly” and “explicit” prioritization go beyond the evidence.

The resilience-versus-noncompliance distinction, product-specific gates, and hidden post-recovery workload are otherwise well developed, but the temporal and graph errors sit on the criteria that matter most.
