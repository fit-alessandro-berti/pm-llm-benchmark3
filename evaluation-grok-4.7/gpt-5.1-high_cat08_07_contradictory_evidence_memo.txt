7.5

The memo reaches the right decision family and meets every required section: it refuses both national rollout of Redesign R and a full rollback, separates fact from interpretation in the table, explains all five metric conflicts, and gives each alternative and each measurement a decision link. It is not nearly flawless.

The precision explanation is technically wrong. Precision is about behavior the model allows but the log does not show. Calling that gap “more than is actually normative” mixes precision with conformance to a desired process. Missing permit checks are also a move-on-model problem, not simply extra paths that lower precision. Generalization (0.70 to 0.56) is never used.

The largest evidence omission is case mix. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline, segment and risk attributes are incomplete before week 3, and the windows differ (10 weeks / 24,000 cases versus 6 weeks / 9,200). None of this appears as a fact. The assumption that these gaps only understate Redesign R’s cost and risk is one-directional and partly unjustified: a harder mix can overstate aggregate harm, and incomplete attributes also weaken the simple-house result used to justify scaling. Sparse start times do not clearly bias risk upward.

The action over-claims the mechanism. Simple houses improved, and the temporal profile points to faster validation-to-scheduling. The answer still keeps the full package there, including survey skipping and billing before activation, even though the skip rule was for medium-risk addresses and billing-before-activation is not shown to be safe in that segment. Coordinator ownership is then scaled nationally, ahead of any test of absence and resilience, while that test (M5) is ranked sixth. That stretches Alternative E into an untested national organizational change. Rural harm is mainly rescheduling under fewer handovers; the same survey, permit, and activation rollback prescribed for apartments and old buildings does not address it.

Value of information is complete but mis-ordered relative to the decision. A randomized skip experiment (M1) outranks an observational check of the predictive rule (M6) for deciding where skipping may expand. Full downstream cost matters, but it would not by itself authorize broader rollout while tails, rework, and activation order remain worse. Survey-team utilization falling from 78% to 52% and second-visit utilization rising from 66% to 92% are ignored, so the capacity consequence of restoring surveys is missed. The rollout gates are also uneven: some tolerances are numeric and unexplained, “p95 not materially worse” is undefined, and there is no requirement for complete risk attributes, mix adjustment, or adequate sample before a segment is treated as proven.
