**5.5**

The required seven-part structure is present, the four disruption types are covered, and the answer does use DFG counts, utilizations, edge times, and variant behavior while refusing a blanket “add capacity” plan. That is not enough to carry the grade. Several final claims are factually wrong or internally inconsistent, and a required deliverable—the trade-off table—does not actually state effects.

The trade-off table’s effect cells are largely unreadable (“may if overrejection,” “14h ~810h”). Direction of impact is missing on most case-type and rework cells, so the section fails its job. Separately, I4 is scored as high coverage for the M2-outage scenario even though peak technical validation is already at 101% and the intervention pulls people out of that pool. Urgent-first dispatch is then said to push the next bottleneck to registration and pre-analytical work, which sit upstream of the priority rule.

Data handling slips in the same way. The 62 h peak figure is the Routine median, not an overall median, yet the dashboard treats it as the system baseline and sets a single ~70 h target. Urgent p90 is monitored against the failed peak value of 33 h rather than the 18 h penalty threshold, so “acceptable” is defined as not getting worse than an already unacceptable peak. The 22 h medical-validator queue is also attached to the Hold → Medical edge, whose peak median is 48 h. I3 is treated as current routing in one place and as a candidate rule in another.

The redesign-caused vulnerability is the right kind of point, but the “5–10% as observed in pilot programs” claim is invented. WIP thresholds such as “>20 cases” and “>50 holds” are likewise stated without a Little’s-law or flow-count derivation. Repeat-after-failure’s double assay consumption and the every-second-Friday specialist gap never become operational controls. The scenario set and bottleneck narratives are directionally useful, but these errors are material under a strict reading.
