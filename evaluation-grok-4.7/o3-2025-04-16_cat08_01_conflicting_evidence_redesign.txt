The write-up has the required shape and three genuinely different levers, but several decision-critical figures are invented or contradict the evidence, so an executive could not rely on it.

The diagnosis names the right tension: the frequent auto-screen to fast-approval path (2,550) is quick and 31% cheaper before rework, yet fast-approval cases reopen at 14.1% versus 3.3% on the repair path, and premium high-value complaints are 22% versus 5%. That synthesis is the strongest part. It then breaks. “32% of high-value devices” violate R1 appears nowhere in the log; 32% is the complement of the 68% fitting traces, reassigned to a segment and a rule. That figure is repeated as the current compliance baseline. “Shrinks or reverses” overruns the given result, which is still 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and credits. No fine amounts are in the data. The 88-hour p90 is the evidence-request to manual-review edge, not an intake-to-manual round trip. The €410 figure is the expected cost of a wrong high-value replacement, not an average across high-value cases. The 14.1% rate is for cases that use fast approval, not for the dispatch edge. “8–13 hour median” is only the low-risk fast path; high-value fast medians are 20 and 28 hours. Fitness 0.91 versus 68% fitting traces, low precision, R3, and complaint-after-close are listed or ignored rather than interpreted.

Redesign A is a real segment split plus the suggested pre-dispatch gate, and it correctly keeps low-risk accessories (43%) on the fast path. The effect sizes are not defensible. Forcing high-value claims off the 20–28 hour fast path onto the 96–120 hour reviewed path cannot be a “+8–20 hour” change; that range only resembles the 6–18 hour gate, which is not the path A actually imposes. “Eliminates >90% of R1/R2 violations” is unsupported. R2’s exception is premium low-risk accessories only, while A still fast-dispatches standard low-risk accessories. A also never books the premium service-credit exposure once cycle time passes 24 hours, then calls high-value experience simply “better.” The stated bottleneck move from fraud review to manual review is backwards-leaning: compliance routing is likely to add fraud work on a desk that is already tight on Mondays.

Redesign B is the most coherent alternative. It aims at the 22% unclear-evidence segment, the 26% repeat-request rate, and the stated 30–45% portal effect, and it honestly leaves R1 and wrong-part selection alone. “Removes the 34-hour delay” still overstates a 30–45% cut in repeats of one return edge. Front-loaded abandonment is acknowledged, as the prompt requires.

Redesign C is structurally different, but the flow and the mechanism disagree. The flow sends unknown defects to repair as well as high-value devices, which is about 57% of volume, not 35%. Those cases already have the worst abandonment; a quote-and-approval path is a poor default for missing evidence. “Removes the 22% complaint rate” and “very low” compliance risk are too strong. A 10% fraud sample does not satisfy R1, the fast path still does not establish evidence completeness, and doubling the escalation board can increase R3 breaches because that board is the bypass around manual review. The 12-hour hold is treated as safe for the premium-accessory 24-hour credit threshold even though that segment’s fast median is 8 hours. Current 96-hour medians are reused as if a large volume shift onto three technicians and part-price confirmation would not move them. The 12.5% high-value repair-rework rate is never carried into this repair-default design.

The comparison table does not meet its own job. Cost, quality, and resource cells have no readable direction, despite the instruction to show qualitative signs. Dominance language is appropriately cautious, but the current-row “32% violations” contaminates the comparison, and A’s cycle-time cell is the same understatement as above.

Failure modes exist for each design, yet A’s experiment later allows reviewer utilization up to 90% after citing a sharp queue break above 82%. Stage-3 NPS point thresholds and a −70% wrong-replacement target have no baseline. Opt-in repair biases the cohort. Downstream credit, re-dispatch, matched history, and token replay are the right kinds of checks; they do not repair thresholds built on the bad 32% baseline and the wrong cycle-time delta.
