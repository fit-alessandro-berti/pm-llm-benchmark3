7.5

The diagnosis is directionally right and unusually complete, but it is not nearly flawless. March is correctly treated as a semantic/provenance step, later mapped-fitness and rework movement is not waved away as logging, and the mechanism-specific change points, segment plan, and monitor set are usable. The arithmetic in the baseline-delta table and the L1 fragment distances checks out. The score is held down by mislabeled magnitudes, an under-identified time split, and a few claims that the aggregates do not support.

The timeline misstates the staffing window. The 2026-06→2026-08 row cites median 36.5h and p90 84h. Those are May values. June is already 40.8h and 95h, so the cell folds the pre-departure May rise into the capacity interval and overstates how much of the slowdown starts at the June 15 exits. July’s senior-assessment move is only 7→9 per 100 cases, and most of the decision-edge decline is already present in May (83→76, then 76→72). Tying that decline to the July 1 priority rule is stronger than the step size. August is the cycle-time peak (45h / 110h) while Supplier Z share and rework are already easing; that lag is missing, and it is the observation that most cleanly separates backlog and capacity from the supplier onset.

The DFG section computes the right L1 numbers, then summarizes them poorly. Cumulative distance to January is October 64, July 59, May 40, December 36, March 3. Calling May and October the largest structural shifts ignores July’s near-peak level. Adjacent jumps do make May (+37) and October (+36) the largest upward steps, but that is not what the sentence says. The distance also double-counts one rework loop: `Assess→Request Info` and `Request Info→Collect Docs` move almost in lockstep, so May–July distances are inflated relative to the single October escalation edge. `Assess/SeniorAssessment→Decision` is included without subtracting a censoring interpretation the answer itself raises elsewhere. Omitted edges are correctly called a lower bound.

The cycle-time shares are not a decomposition. No loop duration, stratum-specific time, or queue series is in the monthly table, yet May rework is given 45–70% and July queueing 40–60%. Those bands can be made to sum to 100, which makes them look like a partition. Rework changes cycle time only by adding service and waiting, so the “control-flow” share is not separable from the queueing share. The prompt allowed ranges; it also forbade manufactured precision. Service below 10% is the only component the given service-time contrast actually supports (about 4.8h to 5.4h against median increases of 5–18h). May is also missing as its own performance change point despite +5.4h median and +16h p90 before anyone left.

Section 2 is within the word limit and makes the right logical distinction: mapped fitness near 0.90 does not prove the business process was unchanged. It then treats the fast-variant collapse as evidence that survives label normalization. The prompt says variant percentages were taken from operational tables and that only the mapped-fitness series was label-normalized. The March–April stability of those variant rates is what actually argues against a label artifact; the answer skips that step and simply asserts post-normalization persistence. “Longer Collect Documents waiting” in the May attribution is not in the data. The reported waiting increase is before `Assess`.

The hypothesis table covers the required mechanisms and is better on evidence-against than most writeups. Rank is undefined, and putting supplier×staffing interaction last understates the account the rest of the paper argues. Pure priority redistribution predicts reallocation, not the observed aggregate median rise; that contradiction is not used as evidence against hypothesis 4. The discrimination grid has the right artifacts and a typo (“escation”). The quasi-control choice—standard, non–Supplier-Z—is coherent, with the conditioning risks named.

Monitoring is the strongest design section: separate thresholds, seasonal references, owners, and false-positive sources. Benjamini–Hochberg across these monitors is not credible without a dependence caveat; rework, fitness, variants, and cycle time are one signal. Rule 4 hardcodes `after 2026-07-01`, so it is a retrospective note rather than a standing rule. Rule 3’s “materially” is less operational than the numeric rules around it.

The Python is organized as specified, embeds the right tables, and the PM4Py names (`format_dataframe`, `filter_time_range`, `discover_dfg`, `get_variants`, `discover_temporal_profile`, `discover_log_skeleton`, `discover_declare`) match the 2.7 wrappers. Caveats are printed. `discover_monthly_dfg` does not aggregate by month; monthly use is only implied by composing the time slice. The Open Case dedup indexer is fragile. Part B is otherwise a reasonable unexecuted scaffold.

The provisional attribution’s confidence labels are the right ones: March high, May medium–high and confounded by non-random supplier mix, June medium because rework mediates load, July low–medium, October high for mix and only medium for the senior-assessment label, December medium because overtime and season end together. That section, plus the revision conditions, is where the causal restraint holds. The grade would move above 8 only if the staffing-window numbers, the July-versus-May distance gloss, the variant-normalization caveat, and the non-identified time shares were corrected.
