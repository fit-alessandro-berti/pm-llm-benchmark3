5.5

The write-up is structurally complete and often sharp about the real operating constraints, but the decision-facing numbers contradict each other, several scenario loads are arithmetically wrong, and the reported “simulation results” are not credible outputs of the supplied model. Under a strict reading, that breaks the recommendation.

The capacity skeleton in §2.1–2.2 is mostly auditable and correct. Expected base service times (6.94 / 26.30 / 32.82 / 38.50 / 15.74) match the two-uniform mixture. Shift and break minutes are right: 2,490 paid minutes, Farah only through 12:00, Emil the only 1.0× software resource and only from 12:00, no 1.0× mechanical coverage after 16:00, and no mechanical resource after 18:00. Task counts from M/E/S/ME/ES shares are right, and pure service of about 2,721 minutes (109% of regular time, 102% of regular plus 30 minutes overtime) is the right aggregate. The morning software hole, the 16:00 staffing cliff, and the evening mechanical blackout are the correct bottlenecks. U1’s +11.2 arrivals and ~5.6 morning P1s are right. U4’s decision to leave Intake and Validation tails unchanged matches the prompt.

Those strengths are undermined by load math that cannot support §4 or §5.

- The “+68 / +85 / +60 / +92 / +62 / +35” overhead line (+402 minutes) is not derived. Adding it to produce “3,123 minutes, 125%” is not an auditable offered-load result.
- Arrival-block service is compared with in-block staff as if a case’s Intake, both branches, and Validation all fell inside the arrival block. That misstates time-phased utilization even when the daily total is right.
- “Primary M / E / S minutes” count the same person-minute in one skill column while that person must also cover Intake, Validation, and the other skills. The daily 900 / 900 / 450 figures are availability ceilings, not additive capacity.
- U2 is wrong under the stated rule (double E and ES percentages, renormalize, arrivals unchanged). Afternoon electrical base load rises from about 341 minutes to about 405, not 438, and net pure service rises by roughly 8 minutes, not 144. Renormalization cuts mechanical work almost as fast as it adds electrical work. “2,865 pure minutes” and “438 vs 420” do not follow from the scenario.
- U4 is wrong. Activity-specific tail doubling adds about 60 + 105 + 77 ≈ 242 diagnostic minutes, not 275. The 275 figure is about a 15.5% uplift on all diagnostic minutes, i.e. software’s uplift applied to every skill.
- The 09:30–10:00 U5 intake freeze holds about 11.9 × 30/120 ≈ 3.0 cases, not ~3.5.
- Sequential-on-Deepa ME time is 26.30×1.25 + 32.82 + 5 ≈ 70.7 minutes, not 69.2. E[max(M, E)] ≈ 36.5 is asserted with no distributional calculation. “8.2 to 15.4 extra minutes” covers only some secondary skills (0.25×32.82 and 0.40×38.5); Deepa’s mechanical penalty is about 6.6 minutes.

The central inconsistency is worse than those local errors. Pure service already exceeds regular-plus-overtime capacity before multipliers, handover, setup, or skill mismatch. Section 4 then reports, for the preferred policies, max utilization 84–87%, about 1.2 deferred cases, P1 SLA 96–98%, and P1 p90 of 88–92 minutes. If every resource is at most 87% of 2,490 paid minutes, throughput is at most about 2,170 resource-minutes, or about 2,350 with full overtime. That cannot absorb 2,721 minutes of pure service, still less 2,721 plus a 9-minute-per-case ownership overhead. Closing a multi-hundred-minute gap with 1.2 deferred cases (about 80 resource-minutes) is impossible. Section 2 and §4 cannot both be true, so the robustness ranking is not evidence.

§4 is also not the output of §8. The code prints 8-seed means to one decimal and a backlog column. The table instead gives unexplained bands (96%–98%, 9.2m) and calls them multi-seed DES results. The prompt allows labeled estimates. These are presented as experimental measurements, and they are uniformly flattering to the policy the text recommends.

Policy design itself is concrete and operational: eligibility, slack score, tiered overflow, P1-only parallelism, preemption guardrail, and a late mechanical boost. That part meets the brief. The simulator does not implement the policies as specified, and the gaps are not identified as omitted mechanisms.

- P4’s fairness ratio uses full-shift paid minutes as the denominator, so utilization stays far below 0.88 until late in the shift. The 88% cap barely acts. Priority ranks of 1,000–3,000 also swamp the fairness penalty. P4 cannot be doing the work §5 credits it with.
- U1/U5 dispatch turns on `scenario_name`, an oracle shock label, rather than the queue, absence, and outage triggers in §5.
- Farah is not dedicated to morning Intake/Validation in code. Any qualified resource may take Intake. Ownership is a −250 score bonus, not the hard continuity rule in the prose.
- The run stops at 20:30. P3’s 24-hour SLA, the 18-hour aging promotion, and “overnight carryover is safe” are outside the experiment. Uncompleted P2s are censored at 600 minutes and uncompleted P1s at 240, so p90 is pulled down. Mean delay is computed only on completed cases, which rewards policies that strand hard work.
- Handover +5 is implemented on diagnostic skill-family changes, while the prompt’s extra 5 minutes is a team boundary and can apply on Intake→diagnostic as well. A3 states a skill-family rule; the code applies it only when both activities are diagnostic.
- Free resources are scanned in name order, so Ada systematically wins ties. That biases a fairness metric.
- Preemption is not written to the event log, despite §7 requiring pause/resume attributes. Partial work disappears.

Given that model, recommending P4 as the normal mode is not supported even by the answer’s own table. On that table P3 has better mean delay, p90, P1 SLA, and P2 SLA in every reported row. P4’s U5 P1 SLA is 86–90%, below the 90% target, while the prose says the architecture holds P1 p90 at 88–92 minutes in base and 104–118 across shocks. Those figures match P3’s column, including U5 at 118, not P4’s U5 value of 124. The “~640 minutes, 1.3 FTE” recovery, “>85% of cases” lose the validation handover, “~65% fewer preemptions,” and “2 to 4 secondary tasks per day” are uncalculated effect sizes. Routing also cannot create the missing morning 1.0× software hour or the post-18:00 mechanical hour. A robust recommendation had to put shift coverage, explicit same-day shedding, or a relaxed fairness cap on the efficient frontier. §5 treats dispatch rules as if they remove a structural deficit.

§1’s two objectives have the right shape (SLA shortfall plus p90, and a minimax of tail, util above 90%, spread, and handovers). They are not evaluated. No scenario weights, α, or γ are applied, and the impossible §4 cells are what actually drive the choice. Assumptions A1–A6 are labeled and mostly reasonable. A5’s choice to make breaks and shift ends non-preemptive is a fair modeling choice.

§7 is the strongest section. The attribute list covers queue entry, eligible set, assignment reason, skill, multiplier, planned versus actual service, handover, setup, coordination, preemption, policy version, shock flag, and SLA deadline. The parallel-branch warning on `insert_case_service_waiting_time` is the right PM4Py point, and stage queue time is defined separately as start minus queue entry. The code calls `format_dataframe`, `insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_service_time`, and `discover_handover_of_work_network`. The printed label “Inter-Stage Gap Time” for `@@waiting_time` is still the wrong name whenever branches overlap, which the prompt explicitly flags. `discover_dfg_typed` in the prose is not a reliable PM4Py entry point. `hw_values.connections` may not match the current return type of the simplified SNA helper.

§9 correctly says block means cannot identify burstiness, within-case tail correlation, rework, or preemption fatigue, and the switchback-plus-shadow-mode pilot is the right design because case-level randomization would contaminate a six-person pool. The go/no-go bar (P1 SLA ≥ 92% and nobody above 88% on ordinary days) collides with the 109% pure-load result and would be infeasible without extra capacity or deliberate shedding. The pilot does not say how that collision would be interpreted.

Format compliance is good: nine required headings, four policies, base plus U1–U5, one Python block, tables encoded in memory, and explicit handover, setup, branch, and preemption parameters. Quality of conclusion is not. The useful product is the shift-and-skill bottleneck map and the specialization-versus-pooling mechanisms. The SLA, utilization, fairness, and robustness claims are not internally consistent enough to justify the recommended policy.
