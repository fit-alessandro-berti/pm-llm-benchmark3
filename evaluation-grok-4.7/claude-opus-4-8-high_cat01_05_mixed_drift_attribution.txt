7.3

The diagnosis is coherent and operationally usable: March is correctly treated as a logging/semantic break, later frequency and delay shifts are not collapsed into that break, and the monitoring design can separate a known renewal season from a new failure. The write-up still has material quantitative and attribution errors, so it is not close to exact.

The baseline-relative table is accurate (Jan–Feb means, percentage deltas, and mapped-fitness gaps all check). The L1 table does not. Against January, the seven-edge Manhattan distances are 3, 40, 59, 28, 64, and 36 for Mar, May, Jul, Sep, Oct, and Dec. The reported 3, 32, 49, 26, 62, and 33 are wrong on every non-trivial month, and they reverse May versus December (40 > 36, not 32 < 33). The code would have produced the correct sums, so the prose and the program contradict each other. Edge contributions also reject the claim that October is dominated by senior assessment and is not rework: senior assessment moves by 18, while the Request Info pair moves by 14+14. October adds a senior-routing component; it does not replace the rework shift.

The autumn case-mix claim is too strong. Senior-assessment counts per high-complexity case rise from about 4/21 (19%) in January to 9/28 (32%) in July and 22/41 (54%) in October. Holding the January conditional rate fixed, October’s mix would produce roughly 8 senior assessments per 100 cases, not 22. Holding July’s rate fixed still predicts about 13, not 22. That residual can be within-class routing, harsher cases inside the “high” bin, or the stated historical inconsistency in recording `Senior Assessment`. Section 9’s “primarily seasonal population drift” at ~70% confidence, and the timeline’s 82, do not reflect that split. Calling the summer complexity path “flat” is also wrong: high-complexity share climbs from 20.5% to 29% by August, about +1 point per month, before the October step.

The cycle-time shares are labeled as ranges, but they are not a decomposition. Service 10–20%, rework 25–40%, and queueing 40–55%, plus a case-mix term, are not additive and are not tied to hours. The only anchored piece is Assess service, +0.6h of a +13.5h median gap. The secular volume rise (about 612 to 715 cases before October) is barely in the queue account. “M/M/c,” “superlinear,” and “faster than either factor alone” are asserted without a counterfactual.

Smaller logic faults accumulate. The staffing row places “routing/rework unchanged but throughput falls” in the behavioral-drift column; that pattern is performance drift. An existence count on `Senior Assessment` cannot by itself separate more routing from more complete logging. July is a weak behavioral change point: rework’s step is May, and the July 1 rule is a queue policy. Rule 5 fires only if aggregate cycle time worsens, while its rationale is that redistribution can leave the aggregate ambiguous. “Logging drift confined mainly to March” describes the onset; duplicate `Open Case` and missing resources stay elevated through December. Hypothesis rank has no criterion, and rank 1 for pure logging is easy to misread as the main operational explanation. Benjamini–Hochberg across dependent monitors is extra machinery, not a fix for dependence.

What holds up is the part the decision rules depend on. Mapped fitness near 0.90 with raw fitness near 0.55, two new labels, and duplicate `Open Case` at the migration is a provenance finding. The May supplier jump aligned with the Request Info rates, the flat Assess service time against a much larger queue, the July rule being invisible in the monthly aggregate, and December’s intervention sitting on top of a seasonal decline are all supported and appropriately hedged. The segment plan and the twelve monitors are the strongest practical product: mapped versus raw fitness, supplier-stratified rework, class-specific waiting, and year-over-year case mix.
