**4.1**

The write-up looks structured and consultant-fluent, but it is empirically unreliable. Several load-bearing numbers are invented, at least one conformance fact is inverted, and the piece violates the prompt’s own constraints on false precision and Pareto claims.

**Diagnosis is directionally right and locally wrong.** The core contradiction is stated clearly: the frequent auto-screen → fast approval → dispatch path is attractive on speed and pre-rework cost, then loses much of that advantage after complaints, reshipments, and credits. That is the right problem. Artifact coverage is also broad (frequency DFG, performance DFG, R1–R3, token replay, alignment diagnostics, rework rates, segment outcomes, resource notes). Breadth does not rescue misuse.

The most serious process-mining error is the R1 claim: “240 cases (10% of fast approvals) skip manual/fraud review.” `Fraud/risk review → Fast approval` (240) is the compliant sequence. R1 requires Manual or Fraud *before* Fast approval for claims > EUR 400. Those 240 cases had a risk review. The actual R1-risk mass is in `Auto eligibility screen → Fast approval` (2550), which alignment already flags via “Dispatch replacement without risk review for high-value devices.” This is not a rounding issue; it is the wrong edge with the wrong meaning.

Other diagnosis errors compound that:

- Path “median 5.3h” is fabricated. Edge medians cannot be added to get a path median, and the given segment medians are 8 / 13 / 20 / 28 hours on the fast path.
- “72h median resolution time” misreads `Fast approval → Customer complaint/reopen` (mean 72, median 50). That is time-to-complaint, not complaint resolution. Resolution edges are median 31h (to Manual) and 42h (to Escalation).
- “median 10h wait for Request missing evidence → Manual review” cites the *opposite* edge. The stated edge is mean 34 / median 26 / p90 88.
- “60% of Premium high-value are auto-approved” is not in the data.
- Repair techs “idle 40% of time” is not in the data.
- “82%+ utilization” is a stated *threshold* at which the manual queue explodes, not a measured current utilization.
- Fitness 0.91 / 32% non-fitting is a global replay result, not a direct R2 count. The 120 Escalation cases are a *possible* R3 bypass, not a proven R3 violation, and “precision” is the wrong label for that.
- 320 abandonments are the `Request missing evidence → Claim abandoned` total, not an Unclear-defect count.

So the diagnosis names the right tensions and then repeatedly attaches the wrong quantity or the wrong semantics. Under a strict evidence-use bar, that is a large deduction.

**The three redesigns are conceptually distinct enough, but leak into each other and overclaim mechanisms.** A (segmented auto-rules + pre-dispatch gate), B (evidence portal + review capacity), and C (repair-first for high-value) are real different levers, not mere parameter tweaks. That is the strongest part of the answer. The prompt even supplies two of those interventions. The execution is sloppy.

A treats a 6–18h gate as a certain +6h, asserts a 50% cut in dispatch complaints, “90% of violations,” and “EUR 410 × 130 cases/year = EUR 53k.” The 130 and the annualization are invented; the log has no time window. A also expands Escalation and restates rule updates as a two-week change, ignoring the prompt’s 15–35% manual-inflow risk. The gate idea itself is well-grounded (wrong-part long tail; inspectors otherwise underused). The quantified benefits are not.

B is the right attack on the 26% repeat-evidence loop and the 140h Unclear-defect sink, and it correctly flags digital-literacy abandonment. Then it misfires: shifting one of two fraud specialists off Monday would likely worsen the stated Monday bottleneck; “Standard SLA degrades from 10h to 36h” recycles the same wrong 10h edge; portal lead time “3 months” is invented; and the mechanism section credits B with wrong-replacement savings (EUR 95–410) that B does not primarily cause.

C correctly uses the 14.1% vs 3.3% complaint split and the EUR 410 high-value mis-replace cost. It then violates the instruction not to treat the slow path as generally better. Premium and Standard high-value are collapsed, despite 20h vs 96h and 28h vs 120h, and despite Premium service-credit risk. “28h → 72h” is invented. “Parallel quality check” ignores that `Repair device → Quality check` already exists. Forcing more volume onto `Repair quote → Customer approval` also ignores the existing 70 quote abandonments and 12.5% high-value repair rework. Implementation is not “low.”

All three designs also nibble the same Escalation-board knob. That makes them less cleanly separable than the titles suggest.

**The table and dominance claims break the output contract.** The prompt forbids pretending exact effects or exact Pareto dominance are knowable. The table still prints −5%, −8%, +3%, −50%, −40%, −70%, +6h, +26h, +44h, and marks the current process “Dominated? Yes.” Current is *not* dominated: it is the best fit for Premium low-risk accessories (8h fast path, 6% complaints, credit exposure above 24h) and is competitive for Standard low-risk if status visibility is good. Writing “best-fit segment: None” for the as-is process is a real analytical miss. B’s “Conditional (if portal adoption high)” inverts the dominance logic: high adoption makes B *more* attractive, not dominated.

**Failure modes are acceptable, experiments are only half-thought.** Each redesign has two failures and a migrated bottleneck, and several are plausible. Missing, despite being in the prompt: A’s manual-inflow surge; C’s quote abandonment and repair-rework loop. The pilot sequence puts A first for good compliance reasons, then runs C on the *same* Premium high-value slice, confounding learning. B is given a multi-month build and then scheduled after a 4-week A pilot, instead of being developed in parallel. Stop/go thresholds are in the right spirit (complaint tails, utilization, abandonment) but several are unanchored to baselines. Downstream monitoring is the one part that actually answers “is this real or just shifted?”

**Net.** This is a coherent redesign memo built on a cracked instrument panel. Trade-off language and format compliance keep it from collapsing. The inverted R1 reading, repeated fabricated metrics, forbidden dominance claim, and failure to defend the current fast path for low-risk Premium cases keep it well below a passing-strong score. It is usable as a draft structure, not as a defensible recommendation.
