**6.0**

The answer is structurally complete and better than a generic Lean dump, but it is not close to flawless. It repeatedly treats inferred or misread quantities as if they were in the log, contradicts itself between design and table, and sells A and C as more distinct than they are.

**Diagnosis.** The contradiction is stated clearly and is the strongest part of the write-up: the 2,550-edge auto→fast chain is cheap and short *before* rework, then loses most of that edge once complaints, reships, and credits are included. Citing the 14.1% vs 3.3% complaint split, the 31%→9% cost erosion, fitness 0.91 / 68% fitting traces, and the Premium high-value 22% vs 5% outcome split is the right evidence set.

Then the diagnosis fabricates a key number. “32% of high-value devices skip Manual review or Fraud/risk before Fast approval” is not in the alignment diagnostics, the DFG, or the segment table. It is almost certainly \(1-0.68\), i.e. the share of *all* traces that fail the normative net, reassigned to one rule and one segment. That is a process-mining error, not a rounding issue. Other slips compound it: the €410 figure is the *expected cost of a wrong replacement*, not the average cost on high-value cases; the p90 of 88 h is the single edge Request missing evidence → Manual eligibility review, not an “Intake → Manual review round-trip”; and Fast approval → complaint (140) plus Dispatch → complaint (260) is not the same as “14.1% of Fast approval → Dispatch cases.” Five artifacts are named, but several are used imprecisely.

**Redesign A.** Segment-aware auto-routing plus a pre-dispatch gate is a defensible, non-generic move and correctly reuses under-used inspectors and the prompt’s own 6–18 h gate. The mechanism is still oversold. Forcing “else → Manual” sends ~57% of volume (everything that is not the 43% low-risk accessories) into a station that currently receives only 1,400/5,000 cases at intake. That is not the prompt’s “15–35% more manual inflow from a rule update”; it is a structural doubling-plus of the front door. The claim that this “eliminates >90% of R1/R2 violations” is unsupported. R2 is evidence-completeness before dispatch (with a Premium *low-risk accessory* exception); sending all Standard accessories down the untouched fast path can still violate R2. “Low-risk accessory OR Premium accessory” is also ambiguous—if “Premium accessory” were read as any Premium case, the design would put the worst compliance/complaint segment back on the path the diagnosis just condemned.

**Redesign B.** This is the most orthogonal of the three and the best use of the 22% unclear-defect / 26% repeat-evidence / 34 h re-entry cluster. The assisted channel is a reasonable answer to the digital-literacy abandonment risk the prompt flags. The write-up then over-claims the lever: a 30–45% cut in *repeat* requests is rewritten as “the Request missing evidence loop is removed” and as deleting the full 34 h mean. R1 is correctly left untouched; R3 is ignored here as everywhere.

**Redesign C.** Repair-default for high-value/unknown defect is a real alternative to A’s “review then maybe replace,” but the family resemblance is obvious: both exist to stop auto-replacing the 35% high-value slice. The 12 h hold plus 10% fraud sample is the distinctive mechanism, and it is poorly costed against the same data. Premium low-risk accessories sit on the fast path and incur service credits after 24 h; their fast-path median is already 8 h. A 12 h hold is not “short enough for the Premium accessory SLA.” Conversely, the 24 h credit threshold is then applied to Premium *high-value* customers, where the prompt never states that SLA. Doubling the escalation board is a capacity tweak, not a third logic. “Removes the 22% complaint rate” applies the Premium high-value rate to the whole high-value class (Standard is 18%).

**Table.** The comparison is the right artifact and correctly refuses exact ROI. It is also internally inconsistent with section 2. If A really routes all high-value work to Manual, Premium/Standard high-value medians move from 20/28 h toward 96/120 h, not “+8–20 h.” That +8–20 h only makes sense if the author silently switched to “fast path plus a short gate,” which is not the flow they wrote. Current-state “32% violations” repeats the fitness conflation. Arrow glyphs are missing, so cost, quality, and resource columns are partly unreadable. A and C share the same best-fit segment, which again signals weak differentiation. “Dominated? Conditional” is acceptable only if the condition is stated; it is not.

**Failure modes and experiments.** These sections are the second-best part of the answer. Bottleneck migration to reviewers (A), an assisted-evidence team (B), and part-price confirmation / technicians (C) is grounded in the resource notes, including the 82% reviewer threshold. The staged sequence (rules first because they ship in two weeks → portal A/B on the unclear-defect slice → opt-in repair-first) and the insistence on 30-day post-close complaint/credit/re-dispatch plus token replay are real process-mining experimental design.

They still mishandle their own numbers: Stage 1 targets reviewer utilisation “<90%” even though the prompt says queues blow up above 82%; Stage 1 stop/go vs a –40% complaint target is arbitrary relative to the 22%→5% segment gap; Stage 3’s –70% wrong-replacement-cost target is invented. R3 (reopened claims must pass Manual before close or denial) never becomes a design constraint, despite 310 complaint→Manual and 120 complaint→Escalation edges. Mid-risk devices, the low-precision discovered model, and the temporal-profile finding that Manual → Repair quote is too slow for Premium are left unused.

**Net.** Enough grounded DFG/conformance/rework/segment reasoning to clear a mediocre bar; too many invented percentages, one serious fitness-to-rule misread, an A-vs-table contradiction on cycle time, and two high-value-diversion designs dressed up as three materially different options. Under a strict reading that is a mid score, not a high one.
