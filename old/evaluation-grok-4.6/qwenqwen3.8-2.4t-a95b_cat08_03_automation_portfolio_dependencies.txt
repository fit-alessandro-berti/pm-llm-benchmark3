**7.0**

The final answer (sections 1–7) is a competent process-level advisory memo. It follows the required structure, refuses frequency-only ranking, keeps A11 off the first wave, treats exception handling and shifted demand as first-class constraints, and grounds most claims in the DFG, variant, resource-network, and control artifacts. It is not nearly flawless.

**What holds up.** The candidate matrix is the strongest part. A2, A4, A5, and A9 as the top four is a defensible process reading: classification is high-volume *and* path-setting; entity mismatch is low-frequency but 31h wait / 38% rework / 82% critical path; missing-document loops are the main internal rework engine; A9 is both labor and control. A1 is correctly treated as high direct benefit and high downstream-flood risk. A11 is correctly last. The dependency map hits every required theme (data quality, legal gates, specialist capacity, external valuers, two deep integrations). The three portfolios are actually different, not relabeled copies. The A1-should-not-go-first argument in section 7 is the right trade-off: A2 is the honest “high-frequency win,” because it is almost the same volume as registration and determines the control path.

**Material reasoning gaps.** The recommended “bottleneck/synchronization” portfolio does not cover the two largest synchronization facts in the evidence. Wait for external valuation is 58h median, 19% late, and a hard committee-pack prerequisite; A7 is deferred to manual triage. Normalize financial statements is 75 minutes, 5,200 cases, 20h wait, and the brief *explicitly* warns that cleaner inflow will overload the 9 credit analysts unless normalization improves. The recommended set (A2+A4+A5+A9) does exactly that—speeds routing and document completeness—then papers the analyst constraint with “WIP limits.” That is acknowledgment, not a process-level fix. A portfolio that names synchronization while leaving 58h external wait and the stated analyst-overload mechanism unaddressed is oversold.

**Causal slips.** A9 is repeatedly credited with making upstream data cleaner. A9 *consumes* KYC/decision data and blocks bad contracts; A2/A4/A5 are what clean the path. Waiting-before-KYC is also claimed to fall because of A2 “routing and parallelization.” Parallel KYC/document review already exists in practice; A2 mainly reduces misroutes, not the 4h KYC queue for the 11,500 checks that are already on the right path.

**Execution error.** Phase 2 says “A1 already in progress/completed for A2.” That is simply wrong and makes the two-slot integration plan briefly unreadable. A reader has to reconstruct that A2 was meant.

**Smaller defects that add up under a strict bar.** Rating language is inconsistent (Medium-High vs High-Medium; A12’s first-year cell hedges instead of rating). A3 is ranked 10th for good FN-risk reasons, then dropped into the compliance-first portfolio with thinner treatment of the 3-FTE / two-senior-specialist bottleneck that the same answer treats as binding elsewhere. Quick-win includes A10 without A9/KYC gates—the answer’s own later sections say that is unsafe—so the alternative is more of a foil than a responsible option. Sections 3, 4, and 7 repeat the same claims at length; the extra prose does not add new mechanism.

**Bottom line.** Strong portfolio reasoning and artifact use, with a clear, correct rejection of “automate registration first.” Not a 9–10 piece: the recommended four under-weight the two constraints the case itself flags as dominant (analyst overload without A6; external-valuation sync), and the rollout contains a real integration-plan error.
