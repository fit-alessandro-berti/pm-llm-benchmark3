**4.5**

The write-up has the right skeleton and a plausible multi-criteria story, but it does not survive a close check against the skill matrix, queue, or stated constraints. Several “recommended” assignments would be non-compliant if implemented, the scoring math does not do what the text claims, and section 4 presents the proposal as better on every axis.

**Section 1.** Hard vs soft constraints are mostly named, but the specific continuity rule (high complexity + high handover cost) and the 20% training quota are not operationalized as constraints. Overtime protection is written as a hard rule, then broken later. That already makes the rest of the answer internally inconsistent.

**Section 2.** The algorithm is incomplete and, in places, inverted.

- `SLA_Urgency_Score` depends only on the case. Adding it to `Score(C, R)` does not change which resource wins a given case. Urgency therefore does not rank candidates.
- Workload fairness is “inverse of current open cases **and remaining hours**.” Inverting remaining hours penalizes people with capacity left. That is the opposite of a capacity-aware rule.
- Weights are unspecified, case order is unspecified, and there is no real tie-break (owner, then skill, then open cases, then hours, then overtime band).
- Supervision is a filter slogan, not a pairing procedure. Quality batching and the 20% training quota never appear in the procedure.
- Bottleneck/resilience scoring is named, not defined. Specialist concentration is not capped.

A policy that cannot rank resources by SLA, mis-scores capacity, and does not enforce its own gates is not an implementable assignment policy.

**Section 3.** This is the core failure. The table is fluent and often wrong.

Constraint 4 (no extra work to `overtime_risk=high` unless the case is urgent and no safer option exists) is violated three times:

- **C003** is *normal*, 18h left. David is skill 3 but has 14 open cases, 3.0h left, and high overtime. Celine is diagnosis skill 3, 6 cases, 6.0h, low overtime. The text invents an “urgent need to break the diagnosis loop.” That is not the data.
- **C014** is *normal*, 25h left, medium handover. David is loaded again. Hugo is rework skill 3 with low overtime.
- **C010** is *normal*, 20h left. Bruno is high overtime (12 cases, 4.0h). Farid and Eva can approve. The same row also calls Bruno “low overtime risk,” then “high overtime risk.”

Training labels are systematically false, so constraint 3 fails:

- **C004** is eligibility; Amal’s training priority is diagnosis. Marking “Yes” is incorrect.
- **C006** and **C012** are quality; Jonas’s priority is security. Marking “Yes” is incorrect.
- **C010** is approval; Bruno’s priority is quality. Skill 2 is competent, not a trainee. Constraint 7 already lets him finalize.
- **C015** claims supervised security training for Eva. Eva’s security score is **0 (not allowed)**. So is Jonas. The legal supervised pool is David (2), Celine (1), Iris (1).

Real training credit is essentially **C009 only** (1 of 9 normal/low cases ≈ 11%), below the 20% rule. Missed, valid training seats include Farid on a non-urgent diagnosis and Bruno on a low-risk quality check if overtime is treated as a true exception rather than a casual override.

Ownership text is repeatedly self-contradictory: C002 “bypasses current owner (R6 is current, but keeps ownership)”; C005 “handover cost from R5 being current owner”; C015 “handover from R8 (already owner).” Those lines are not explainable to workers or auditors.

Other execution problems: C001 (complexity 0.88, high handover, owner skill 1) is not reasoned against the continuity-unless-unsupervised rule; C012’s “suitable for trainee” note is ignored; David cannot plausibly absorb C003+C014 in 3.0 remaining hours; Iris is unused (defensible on overtime) while David is stacked (not defensible).

Several rows are actually fine (C002/C011 with Farid, C005/C016 with Eva, C007 with Celine, C008 with Gita). That is not enough to salvage a table that violates the overtime rule, mislabels training, and misstates ownership.

**Section 4.** The question forbids treating one policy as an obvious optimum. The comparison table still crowns the proposal on every dimension (“Minimized,” “Optimized,” “Active,” “Preserved,” “High”). There are no baseline assignments for this queue, so claims about throughput, SLA, and fairness are unearned. First-available’s documented waiting-time benefit is flattened into “high SLA risk.” There is no discussion of where the dynamic policy is *worse* (weight sensitivity, slower decisions, under-use of idle specialists, supervision overhead).

**Section 5.** Tone is appropriate and not punitive. It is also generic and over-promises: overtime shielding and “training cases checked against SLA buffers” are exactly what the table does not do. It never explains supervision, security/approval gates, or why some owners keep weak-skill work.

**Section 6.** The indicator list matches the prompt and is the strongest section. Even here, `reassignment_count` filtered as strings `["2","3"]` misses ≥4 and type-mismatches a numeric field. `discover_batches`, `get_service_time`, and `filter_case_performance` are requested concepts and unused.

**Section 7.** This is an audit sketch, not “learn or audit.” Roles, similarity, and working-together graphs are computed and then discarded. API use is sloppy: handover/working-together results are NetworkX graphs, not `handover_net["edges"]`; `discover_working_together_network` is treated as both a single object and `(sa, sn)`. Nothing estimates weights, calibrates handover penalties against the stated 1.8× rework / 2.7h wait evidence, or maps discovered roles onto the skill matrix.

**Net.** Format-compliant and rhetorically complete, but operationally incorrect on the assignments that matter, mathematically broken on ranking, and non-compliant with its own hard rules. Under strict grading that is a mid-fail, not a near-miss.
