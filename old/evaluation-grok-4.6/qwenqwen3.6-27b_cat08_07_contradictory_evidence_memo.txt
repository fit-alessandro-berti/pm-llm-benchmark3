**6.4**

The answer is complete, correctly oriented toward a segmented/hybrid decision, and hits every required heading. It is not close to flawless.

**Fatal or high-severity issues**

The executive memo’s lead quantitative claim is wrong. Median cycle time fell from 14.2 to 10.8 days, a **~24%** reduction, not **42%**. That error sits in the decision document leadership would actually read. “Handovers cut by 65%” is also sloppy: the evidence is a drop in *cases with 3+ handovers* (34% → 12%), not a 65% cut in handovers.

The memo and the final recommendation contradict each other on post-billing activation. The memo keeps Redesign R (including post-billing activation) for simple houses and reverts it only for other segments. Section 6 pauses post-billing activation **for all segments**. That is a material policy inconsistency on the main compliance/CX failure mode.

The overall baseline-vs-pilot comparison is treated as if it were a clean causal contrast. It is not. Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings; segment/risk attributes are missing before week 3; cost data drop complaint handling after week 4; start timestamps are sparse; the pilot is 6 weeks while p90/p95 are 38–62 days and complaints are 30-day. A memo that does not put **case-mix confounding and incomplete observation** in the first rank of uncertainty overclaims what the metrics show.

**Section-level problems**

*Evidence table.* The fact / interpretation / assumption split is real and uses enough items, but it is uneven. “Billing Sequence Risk” is a bundle of a fact (alignment/DFG) and a risk judgment. “True Downstream Cost” is classified as an assumption when the *estimate* is a reported fact and only its completeness is uncertain. Apartment **median** still improved (−2 days); collapsing apartments with old buildings into one “worse” row blurs the exact conflict the next section must explain. Business and rural segments are almost unused.

*Metric conflicts.* All five required tensions are addressed and the median/tail and cost-shift stories are sound. The fitness/precision account is generic (“chaos,” “overly permissive”) and underuses the given artifacts: the team’s specific reading (skip survey × manual activation × second visit × billing start), **generalization 0.70 → 0.56**, temporal profile (install → activation worse in apartments; failed visit → second visit delay under 93% coordinator load), and the handover-of-work vs working-together network split. Those are required process-mining materials, not optional color.

*Alternatives.* A–E are evaluated with advantages, risks, and conditions, and A/B are correctly rejected. Conditions for A (“only if >80% simple houses”) are invented. C is undersold: suspending skip and post-billing activation is the highest-leverage control in the data, and E is not as cleanly superior as the write-up implies.

*Value of information.* Ranking M6 and M4 high is defensible. M7 is too low given frequent billing-before-activation, missing permit checks, and complaint shift to “billing before activation.” M8 is last despite NPS decline and a documented change in complaint *type*; that measurement would change whether speed-to-appointment is allowed to dominate “working service.” M2 is dismissed too quickly: sparse start times directly weaken every duration claim. The 95% rule-accuracy gate and 85% utilization cap are arbitrary numbers with no link to observed error rates or capacity.

*Final recommendation.* Option E is a reasonable choice and the pause/scale/measure structure is the right shape. It is weakened by the activation-scope contradiction, thresholds that look precise but are unevidenced, and no explicit rule for what to do if mix-adjusted results show the simple-house gain shrinks or disappears.

**What still works**

The decision is not “scale everything” or “roll back because some KPIs got worse.” Segment heterogeneity is the organizing idea. Visible cost vs rework, fewer handovers vs fragility, and simple-house vs apartment/old-building splits are explained in causal language rather than as a metric dump. VoI items are tied to decision change, not listed as a research wishlist.

Those strengths make this a competent strategy memo. The wrong headline percentage, the self-contradiction on activation sequencing, the weak treatment of confounding, and the fake-precise rollout gates keep it well short of a high score under a strict rubric.
