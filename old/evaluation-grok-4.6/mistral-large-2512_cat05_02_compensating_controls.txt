**5.6**

The answer is structurally complete and often reasons in the right register (form vs. objective, independence, timing vs. settlement, ED-1 as a conditional waiver). It is not close to flawless. Several factual mistakes, conceptual slips, and code defects are material, not cosmetic.

**What is actually strong.** Headings and the case table schema match the spec. The analysis does not collapse to “same user ⇒ fail” or “two users ⇒ pass”: AP-203/AP-207 can be met under ED-1, AP-201/AP-206 are treated as incomplete compensation, AP-204 fails despite nominal four-eyes, AP-209 is not given a free pass, AP-205 is prevented before completion. AP-202 and AP-208 are correctly treated as objective failures. Independence (m09 vs. m04/m02/m11/m15), override/credential sharing, recall-window timing, and AV-2 coverage limits are used, not just activity counts.

**Section 1 is thinner than it looks.** Six threat rows satisfy the “at least five” rule, but the mapping is crude. ED-1 is a *permission with preconditions*, not a preventive control that “partially prevents” error, fraud, or account compromise. Treating “emergency circumvention” as something ED-1 “prevents (conditions must be met)” is circular. RA-7’s corrective role (stop/recall/recover while the window is open) is mostly omitted; almost every cell is prevent/detect/fail. MG-4 is repeatedly reduced to “detects if reviewed,” which understates pre-release approval and ignores rubber-stamping. AV-2 is over-credited on “fraudulent vendor/bank account”: it can check listed attributes, not commercial reasonableness or who controlled a compromised account.

**Section 2 sets the wrong equivalence bar.** The criteria list is the right list (timing, independence, coverage, overrideability, recoverability, evidence, collusion). The *levels* are not. Full equivalence to FEP-1 cannot require covering “all material risks” including collusion and account compromise; FEP-1 itself does not do that. The test therefore measures an idealized super-control, not substitutability for four-eyes. Timing also inflates detective action before settlement into “partial equivalence” without requiring an independent human authorization act. The “same expected loss is not enough” paragraph is fine, but the framework is never applied case by case, so it stays ornamental.

**Section 3 has a clear factual error in a scored rationale.** For AP-201 the write-up says manager review *and audit* occurred before settlement. Settlement is 10:00 on 4 May; audit starts 09:00 on 5 May, after settlement and ~25 hours after release. Only the manager action is pre-settlement. “Recall possible (settlement at 10:00)” is also confused: settlement is when the payment becomes irrevocable for control purposes, not when recall becomes available. The **classification** (partially met, low residual, convenience not necessity) is still defensible; the **justification** is not.

Other case problems:
- AP-203 “missing evidence: none” is overconfident (L3+ not shown; review quality is asserted in the facts, but ED-1 completeness is not a blank check).
- AP-206 as PARTIALLY_MET is plausible, but the prompt itself flags the late token as delay vs. failed precondition. That is the cleanest INDETERMINATE case in the set; locking it as partial with a “false emergency” story in §5 over-interprets.
- AP-207 = OBJECTIVE_MET is acceptable under ED-1 (ratify before settlement, evidence reviewed), but it is not the same package as AP-203 (pre-release MG-4). The table does not show that gap in residual strength.
- AP-208’s failure is correct, but the rationale underplays two hard ED-1 breaks: EUR 900,000 > 100,000 and a bank change inside seven days. “AV-2 down + late ratify” is incomplete.
- AP-209 as PARTIALLY_MET is one legal reading; INDETERMINATE is better given missing org-design evidence and no found defect. Residual “medium” for a clean EUR 42k payment is loosely argued.
- AP-205 “nominal four-eyes: violated (blocked)” is sloppy. FEP-1 is defined on *completed* payments; the right nominal result is not applicable / no completed release.
- Corrective capacity is repeatedly written as “recall possible” whenever a settlement timestamp exists, including after the payment has already settled (201, 207, 209). That equates “a settlement time is known” with “correction still worked.”

**Section 4’s hybrid semantics are inconsistent.** Calling post-release / pre-settlement MG-4 “preventive for *future* losses and detective for the current transaction” is the wrong split. If the manager can still stop irrevocable settlement, the live payment is still in a preventive/corrective window versus *loss*; detective is the residual if they only review and cannot halt. The table then says MG-4 is not corrective “unless approval triggers a recall,” which contradicts the hybrid claim. ED-1 again appears as a preventive control rather than a gated exception.

**Section 5 is uneven.** AP-202, AP-204, and AP-208 counterfactuals are useful. AP-201’s “AV-2 amount-limit check breaks the chain” is weak for EUR 1,800 (that check will not catch a plausible mis-key inside the operator limit). AP-206’s “most plausible loss mechanism” as a fabricated emergency is not the most plausible reading of a documented 12:40 outage. The section also never states that a second releaser would *not* have stopped AP-204-style collusion if the pair were simply replaced by another stable pair—only that it would raise the coordination cost.

**Section 6 contains a direct misread of AP-204.** It tells the reader to flag same-user prepare/release “even if nominally compliant (e.g., AP-204).” AP-204 is distinct users. That is exactly the case that should teach *pair concentration* and shared workstations, not same-user rate. AP-206 is cited both as a medium late-approval example (manager approved *before* release) and as a low token-timing example. Thresholds (5%, 1%, 10%, …) are invented without a rationale and read like policy, not monitoring hypotheses. Alert-vs-proof is stated correctly.

**Section 7 is not a reliable checker.** The skeleton hits the seven required steps and labels output as evidence, not a verdict. Execution and measurement have several faults:
- Flags are computed on every event, then the print set is `Release_Payment` only. `flag_emergency_eligibility` compares the token to `row['time:timestamp']`, i.e. release time on the printed rows. For AP-206 the token is after prepare and before release, so the printed flag can say eligible and hide the actual ED-1 break.
- AP-205 never appears in the printed table, so the blocked-release path is computed and then dropped.
- Repeated pairs are hardcoded (`AP-204`, `AP-209`) instead of derived from the log.
- Case IDs are taken from `event['case:concept:name']` after `convert_to_event_log`. The stable source is `trace.attributes['concept:name']`; event-level case id is version-dependent.
- `filter_four_eyes_principle` polarity and return type (log vs. dataframe) are not handled. The assignment *asks* for violating cases; many PM4Py builds return traces that *satisfy* four-eyes, or a dataframe that this loop cannot iterate. The note about “edge cases / SYSTEM resources” does not mention polarity, which is the behavior that would invert every nominal result.
- UTC handling is incomplete (`pd.to_datetime` without `utc=True`; later aware/naive comparisons can throw).
- AP-208’s token timestamp is invented (`15:00Z`); facts only say the token was active.
- AP-202 has no settlement in the facts table used by the flags, so manager/audit timing relative to irrevocability is weaker than the prose claims.

**Net.** This is a competent control-objective essay with the right non-mechanical case pattern, damaged by at least two hard factual mistakes (AP-201 audit-before-settlement; AP-204 as same-user), a mis-specified equivalence standard, confused preventive/detective semantics, and checking code that would miss or invert important evidence. Under a near-flawless bar that is a mid-pack score, not a high one.
