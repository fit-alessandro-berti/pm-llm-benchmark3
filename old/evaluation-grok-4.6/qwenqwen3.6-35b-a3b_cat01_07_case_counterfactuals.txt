**4.6**

The writeup has the right skeleton—headings, tables, policy-vs-timing language, four-eyes awareness, and some interference vocabulary—but it is not a reliable counterfactual analysis. Under a strict reading it fails several of the stated evaluation criteria.

**Material temporal and mechanism errors**

K-337 is the clearest break. The table path correctly drops only the third loop, but the derivation says the intervention also cuts Document Check 2 and Assessment 2, then claims compliance “starts Tue afternoon instead of Wed” and that the decision “moves to Wed morning.” In the factual log, compliance already starts Tuesday 15:30 and the decision is already Wednesday 08:30. That is not a 3–4 day save; it is roughly the factual completion time. The cycle-time claim is therefore detached from the case.

K-104 attributes ~2 h to running Identity and Document Check concurrently. Those activities were already allowed to overlap (no originals). The factual delay is D1’s Monday queue, not a sequencing lock. The specified intervention is an early S1 packet and a provisional C1 slot. Treating Monday-afternoon C1 work as feasible despite a 20 h compliance backlog (and two extra urgent compliance cases by 16:00) treats reservation as new capacity rather than a reordering of a single scarce calendar.

Queue snapshots are cited and then ignored. K-219 says D2 has 4 h of work at Tuesday 08:00 and still starts at 08:00. K-411 starts D2 at Tuesday 11:30 even though D2’s Tuesday 08:00 backlog is 4 h. Earlier *readiness* is sometimes distinguished from earlier *completion* (K-219 base case, K-411 worst case), then contradicted by table ranges and by the synthesis claim of “predictable” K-411 gains.

**Inconsistent and underspecified outcomes**

“Cycle time 1.5–2.5 days” / “3–4 days” / “0.5–1.5 days” never states whether the figure is remaining duration or a reduction. K-104’s table (1.5–2.5 days) does not match its own best case (decision Wednesday 15:30 vs factual 18:30). Paths are smashed into unreadable concatenations (`RegisterTriage[Identity CheckDocument Check]…`) rather than explicit partial orders.

**Feasibility model is too coarse**

One global relation set forces `(Triage, Document Check)` and `(Assessment, Compliance Review)` on all cases, against the instruction not to flatten the four processes and against the originals / high-risk rules. Originals Receipt is missing. Conditional constraints are listed, but the partial order does not implement them.

**Capacity section is directionally aware, numerically sloppy**

C1 reservation, D1/D2 substitution, avoided rework, assessment saturation, and logging-vs-concurrency are all named. That is the strongest part of the answer. It is then undermined by incoherent scale claims: 100 C1 reservations in a week, and “each reserved slot reduces effective C1 capacity by ~5%” (which cannot be applied to 100 cases). The 100-case discussion treats reservation as added compliance demand instead of mostly re-timed demand plus extra preliminary hours, and it collapses four different policies into one “likely negative” slogan.

**Format and internal consistency**

Section 7 is about 190 words against a 220–320 requirement, and it overstates K-411’s predictability relative to §3. Assumption 8 (“K-337 does not violate risk thresholds”) conflicts with ranking K-337 as the policy-breaking intervention. Working hours, overnight document work, and weekend C1 availability are barely constrained.

**What is actually good**

K-337 is correctly classified as a decision-policy change, not a free time cut. Four-eyes and a post-assessment compliance review are preserved. K-219’s base case that the assessment queue can absorb an earlier document finish is the right kind of reasoning. The robustness ranking (K-337 last) and the pilot table structure are sensible.

Those strengths do not offset wrong mechanisms, a broken K-337 timeline, snapshot-inconsistent starts, and contradictory cycle-time numbers. This is a polished template around unstable case arithmetic, not a near-miss expert counterfactual.
