**7.4**

The answer is structurally complete, process-aware, and clearly better than a frequency-first plan. It still has enough attribution slips, internal inconsistencies, and unfinished dependency logic that it is not close to flawless under a strict reading.

**What it gets right**

It does not rank by volume. A1 is correctly treated as politically attractive and process-weak: 8-minute work, 1.5h wait, back office already idle after 15:00. A11 and A8 are deferred for the right reasons. A4 is used as the “frequency is misleading” case (31h wait, 38% exceptions, critical path in 82% of those cases). The DFG loops are used properly: document ping-pong (3300/2800), entity-mismatch return to KYC (1160), contract remediation (650/510), and the 380 `Resolve entity mismatch -> Contract preparation` arc as a control smell rather than a legitimate happy path. Concurrency of KYC and financial review is respected. Analyst overload after cleaner inflow is treated as a first-class constraint, which is why A5 is paired with A6. The two deep-integration slots are assigned to A4 and A9 instead of being wasted on registration or activation. Compliance accountability is not handed to a model.

**Material logical flaws**

The most serious issue is a broken prerequisite. Section 2 says A2 drives the A5 document matrix, KYC path, collateral route, and High-exposure controls, and that classification errors already misroute cases. The recommended portfolio then leads with A5 and omits A2 entirely. That is not a small omission: an 8% misroute rate plus the 7250/3940 split after classification means a product-specific checklist can request the wrong file set and encode the wrong control path. The answer identifies the dependency and then violates it.

A second inconsistency is A7. It is ranked 5th, given high process-level benefit on the longest wait in the log (58h), and then appears in none of the three portfolios. A “bottleneck/synchronization” portfolio that leaves out the main synchronization point needs a harder ranking consequence, or A7 belongs in one of the four-module sets. Calling the leftover a “readiness track” does not resolve the contradiction.

Section 7 also overclaims that A4 “addresses” concentration on two senior specialists. Sections 4–5 are more honest: an assistant can make those two people faster and more central unless backup coverage exists. The final argument quietly upgrades a residual risk into a benefit.

**Attribution and design nits**

“72% already digital” belongs to Receive application, not Register / A1. Four-eyes prevention is treated as a release gate but never funded inside the four-module / two-integration envelope; it is assumed into existence. The first-year suitability column uses a different scale than the rest of the matrix (Core / Conditional / Defer vs High–Low). A10’s 44h wait on 7300 cases is labeled a high process-level benefit, then dropped with less wait-inventory comparison than A1 received. Variant effects (Micro / SME / High-exposure × product) are sprinkled in, not analyzed. The 7250 vs 3940 post-classify split is underused, which is why A2 ends up ranked 11th despite being a control-path dependency.

**Net**

The recommended set A4 / A5 / A6 / A9 is still a defensible first-year portfolio, and the anti-A1 argument is the right one. The writeup is not a 9-level answer because it breaks a dependency it stated, ranks A7 as core then excludes it everywhere, and leaves several control/resource claims looser than the evidence it already assembled.
