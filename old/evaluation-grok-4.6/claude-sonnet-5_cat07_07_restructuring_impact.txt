**7.4**

The answer is complete, well-structured, and substantively stronger than a typical response: it stays agnostic, treats East as a different baseline, refuses to treat average cycle time as decisive, and conditions rollout on a pilot. Under a strict standard it is not near-flawless. Several conceptual slips, a structurally weak primary pilot, and a PM4Py sketch that would not run as written are enough to keep it well below the top band.

**What it does well**

Sections 1–6 follow the required headings and cover the mandated mechanisms. The East vs North/South distinction is carried through the mechanism map, all three scenarios, indicators, risks, pilot, and recommendation rather than mentioned once. The central scenario is the most credible of the three: nominal handovers fall, medians look acceptable, tails and team-level gaps worsen. Section 4 is the strongest part of the essay; the eight hidden risks are specific and tied to how averages form. The recommendation is correctly “pilot first,” and the list of invalid inferences from early averages is precise.

**Mechanism map (solid, not clean)**

Rework is only implied (referral leakage, fairness) rather than given its own causal row, despite being a required mechanism. Two process-mining claims are wrong or sloppy. `get_service_time` measures activity processing time, not queue delay; volume should raise waiting time, not service time, unless specialization or multitasking changes processing itself. DFG self-loops are repeat work, not waits. The map also frames centralization almost only as bottleneck formation. The main queueing argument *for* a shared technical pool is M/M/c pooling versus three separate M/M/1 queues; that competing mechanism appears only implicitly in the optimistic scenario. Calling the new system “M/M/1-like” is the wrong model for a merged specialist pool. “Queueing collapse” above 90% utilization overstates Erlang-C: waits grow nonlinearly; they do not discontinuously fail.

**Scenarios**

Optimistic, central, and adverse are differentiated on the required dimensions. Learning/skill atrophy is thin here and mostly deferred to section 4. The optimistic case leans on unlogged informal side-channels remaining intact, which undercuts the same formalization the redesign imposes. The adverse “hidden handover” story is plausible but not observable in the given log without additional instrumentation; it should have been labeled as such.

**Indicators**

The leading/lagging split is useful and mostly event-log-based. `discover_organizational_roles` is the wrong instrument for daily utilization; it clusters resources by activity profiles. Several leading indicators (informal escalation, helpdesk tickets) are not in the stated columns. `local_expertise_flag`, `handover_reason`, `priority`, and `outcome` are underused given they were provided specifically for this analysis.

**Pilot (main design flaw)**

Migrating “one team only” does not test a shared service. Multi-team arrival pooling, cross-team fairness, and central-queue saturation are the phenomena being forecasted; a single-team cutover cannot produce them and will understate congestion. The complexity-stratified alternative is better and should have been primary. Three months post-change is also too short for the expertise and learning effects the answer itself treats as multi-quarter. Guardrail thresholds (15%, 20%, +5pp) are arbitrary relative to existing team variation (East already has a much fatter tail). Legal/Risk at 91–93% utilization is acknowledged elsewhere but is not a hard capacity constraint in the pilot, even though the design *increases* their intake.

**Recommendation**

Correct and appropriately conditional. It should have named a preferred hybrid (retain local first-pass triage; centralize only overflow/high-complexity; do not fold difficult QC and legal/risk triage into the same technical queue) as the default modification rather than only as a rollback option.

**PM4Py sketch (largest technical deduction)**

This section is a readable analysis plan with the right function names, but it is not a correct PM4Py sketch.

- `discover_handover_of_work_network` (and the working-together / subcontracting calls) return NetworkX graphs, not dicts. `net_pre.items()` is invalid. After the reorg, node IDs change (`N_Tech` vs a shared-service resource), so an unmapped edge-weight join is not a before/after comparison.
- `discover_performance_dfg` is not unpacked, unlike `discover_dfg`; recent PM4Py returns a `(dfg, start, end)` triple for both.
- `get_service_time(..., aggregation_measure="perc90")` is not a standard aggregation (`mean`, `median`, `min`, `max`, `sum`, `stdev`).
- `insert_case_service_waiting_time` does not create a column named `case_service_waiting_time`.
- `filter_variants(log_post, variants_post, retain=False)` does not match the API and, if it did, would not isolate rare variants.
- SLA miss at 120 hours is inconsistent with the given data. Medians are already 6.8–8.6 days and SLA-miss rates are 6.5–9.8%, so a 5-day threshold would flag most cases, not a small tail.

Those are not cosmetic. Section 7 was a required deliverable; code that cannot execute and a threshold that contradicts the scenario data are material defects.

**Net**

Reasoning quality is high; execution is not exact. The essay would be in the 8.5–9.0 range if the mechanism map distinguished wait vs service time and pooling vs congestion, the pilot tested a real multi-team queue, and the PM4Py block used the actual APIs and a data-consistent SLA. Under the requested strict standard those gaps land it at **7.4**.
