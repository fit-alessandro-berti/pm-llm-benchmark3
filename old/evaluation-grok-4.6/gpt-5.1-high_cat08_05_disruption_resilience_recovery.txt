**7.5**

The answer is complete, correctly structured, and generally faithful to the case. It meets every required section, follows the four constraints in spirit, and makes the central distinction between mission-protecting adaptation and concealed noncompliance. It is not nearly flawless. Several process-mining and abductive misses are material, and a few claims are imprecise or slightly misaligned with the DFGs.

**What it does well.** Timeline T0–T4 is coherent and metric-grounded. Risk postures (“mission-protective but control-fragile”; “operationally recovered but compliance-burdened”) are the right framing. The A–L table is the strongest section: C and G are retired as control-removing shortcuts; B, I, J, and K are kept as control-preserving substitutions; A, D, F, H, and L are modified rather than romanticized. Section 3 does not treat every baseline deviation as failure, does not equate T2 hospital fill with resilience, and explicitly rejects audit remediation as a substitute for real-time evidence. The recovery model has the required parts (triggers, triage, minimum controls, evidence capture, backlog, reconciliation). Indicators and the management paragraph are decision-usable.

**Material analytical flaws.**

1. **T2 “constrained throughput” is wrong.** Baseline is ~2,000 cases/day (42,000 / 21). T2 is 5,600 cases in roughly 2.5 days (~2,200+/day). Volume was not the failure. Mix, cycle time, pharmacy fill (61%), cancellations (7.8%), and control collapse were. That is a temporal-reasoning error, not wording.

2. **DFG structure is underused, and one relationship is missed.** Emergency triage splits 2,500 manual auth / 1,600 skip / 1,000 fast lane. Manual pack 3,600 then splits 1,650 temporary release vs 1,800 dispatch-without-scan; **1,450 of the 1,650 “temporary release” cases still dispatch without scan.** F is not an alternative to G. It sits upstream of G. Treating F as primarily resilience while G is the “largest traceability gap” without noting that the QA workaround still dumped into the worst custody break is a serious abductive miss.

3. **Completion collapse is underplayed.** Baseline closes ~36,800 / 42,000. T2 ends ~2,100 via spreadsheet invoice hold plus ~430 cancels from 5,600 starts. That is a different process, not a slower version of the same one. Cycle-time and exception rates are discussed; the broken close-out path is not given enough weight.

4. **T3 is a first-class operating state and is under-designed.** T3 has more cases (11,400) than T2 and a distinct failure mode (intake up, inventory/scan unreliable). The answer describes T3 metrics, then the recovery model jumps from Emergency Operating Mode to “upon partial or full recovery.” The hybrid that actually ran for four days is the mode that most needs design, and it is the thinnest part of Section 4.

5. **Cyber is treated as availability, not integrity.** Printed morning snapshots and “customer history / standing authorization” are unsafe if the incident may have tampered with source data. The answer never asks whether the snapshot is stale *or untrustworthy*. For this event class, that is a real gap.

6. **Temporal-profile slip.** The log says `Backlog queue -> Manual pick list` *exceeded baseline p95*. The table restates this as “high variability.” Variability is the finding for `Dispatch without scan -> Phone proof of delivery`. Those are different diagnostics.

**Smaller issues that still cost points.**

- Process-mining fitness is proposed as a live trigger. Fitness is a retrospective model metric, not an operational sensor. Using `<0.9` fitness to declare EOM is a category error.
- E is classed as “primarily resilience” if scans are later reconstructed. That sits uncomfortably next to the instruction not to treat after-the-fact repair as real-time control; the table is better than Section 3 on this point.
- Hospital fast lane is only 1,000 / 5,600 cases. Outcome protection (91% hospital fill) came as much from deferral (I) as from the fast lane. The narrative slightly over-credits A.
- Recall-sensitive product is in the process context and in Band 1 of the redesign, but almost absent from the evidential reading of T2/T4.
- Fairness concerns are hung on A; the data more clearly implicate I and J (pharmacy fill 61%, `Backlog prioritization -> Cancel order` 450, T4 pharmacy/wholesale p90 still far above baseline).
- Recommendations are sensible but generic (playbook, kits, drills). They are only loosely quantified from *this* DFG (e.g., no “ban G even after F,” no T3-specific scan/inventory sync rule).
- Placeholders (X minutes, Band wait-time examples) are acceptable in a design, but several thresholds are not derived from the observed T2/T4 rates (traceability 9.5% vs 0.8%, rework 12.5% vs 3.5%, audit rem 2,300 of 35,000).

**Section-level.** (1) Strong, with the throughput error. (2) Strongest piece; keep/modify/retire calls are defensible. (3) Conceptually right; over-generous on E/F given the F→G path. (4) Complete vs the rubric, weak on T3 and on integrity. (5) Covers the required indicator families; leading/lagging split is clean. (6) Prioritized and residual-risk-aware, not tightly case-derived. (7) Matches the evidence: accept noncritical degradation; do not accept C/G or a standing audit tail.

**Net.** This is a high-quality advisory writeup that would be usable in a real incident review. Under a hypercritical bar it loses substantial credit for misreading T2 volume, missing the F→G linkage, under-designing T3, and treating a cyber event as a simple WMS outage. Those are not cosmetic.
