**7.6**

The answer is a complete, format-compliant, and conceptually strong piece of counterfactual process analysis. It hits every required heading and table schema, keeps the four cases in distinct partial orders, treats K-337 as a decision-policy change rather than a retiming trick, separates earlier readiness from earlier completion, and carries interference through to the 100-case rollout. It is not nearly flawless. Several local paths over-specify times the snapshots make hard to defend, a few probabilities are invented with too much precision, and a couple of capacity arguments smear incremental demand together with retimed demand.

**What is done well**

The normative skeleton is right. Register/Triage, originals-before-document-check, both checks before assessment, final compliance after the last assessment and before decision, four-eyes as A1 vs C1, and rule 7 rework are applied case by case rather than flattened into one sequence. K-411’s concurrency is allowed because originals are not required; K-219/K-337 keep Originals Receipt ahead of Document Check. The K-337 intervention is correctly read as binding C1 so rule 6 cannot reopen a second Request More Info. Preliminary compliance is not allowed to replace the final review.

Uncertainty is mostly handled as ranges, not point estimates. Queue snapshots are treated as forecasts. M1’s signing pattern is inferred from observed 08:30 / 14:00 / 18:00 behavior and used to cap how much upstream acceleration reaches Notify — the single best “readiness ≠ completion” move in the write-up. K-219’s overnight-D2 branch vs no-overnight branch, and K-411’s absorption of the gain into the assessment queue, are the right forks.

The system-level section is the strongest part of the answer. Reservation no-shows on a single certified reviewer, D1/D2 substitution plus clarification×(8–36 h) rework, bottleneck migration from suppressed loops onto C1/M1, Little’s-law compression at assessment, weekend-spanning external waits, and batch timestamps producing false concurrency are all actually discussed, not named and skipped. The ranking is justified on quality, spillovers, reversibility, and learning value rather than cycle time alone; putting K-411 first and K-337 below K-104 is defensible under those criteria. The two pilots have real stopping rules, spillover metrics, and logging checks.

**Process-feasibility and temporal issues**

The K-104 path is the main feasibility defect. C1’s Monday snapshot is 20 h of backlog and Tuesday’s is 16 h. The counterfactual still puts C1 on the clock Mon 14:00–15:00 and Tue 08:00–09:30, then again Wed 08:00–10:30. A reservation can preempt; it cannot create idle gaps inside a 16–20 h backlog. Those Monday/Tuesday intervals are written as if reserved capacity were spare capacity. The adverse-effects cell and §4 admit displacement, but the explicit path and the 5–8 h base saving treat the reservation as free to this case. Even the Wednesday 08:00 start jumps an 8 h forecast. For a *single* case that is a small perturbation; stating it as a scheduled clock time without saying “preempt ~2 h of whoever held that slot” is sloppy. S1 Mon 10:00–12:00 is also asserted with no S1 queue information at all.

K-219’s path cell is internally unclear: “Assessment A2 Tue ~09:00–14:00 start, done by ~16:30” collapses a 5 h start window and a 2.5 h service into one done-time that only matches the late start. Overnight D2 coverage is inferred from *D1’s* one overnight job on K-104 (a complex 8 h task that started at 21:40). That assumption is labeled weakest, which is honest; the base 8–16 h saving still leans on it.

K-411 uses “A2 evening slot (observed 20:00–22:30)” as independent capacity. That interval is K-219’s factual assessment. Holding other cases approximately fixed, A2 is busy then. A 17:00–19:30 K-411 assessment can sit *before* K-219 without collision; a 17:30–20:00 window cannot. The text never makes that cut cleanly.

Cycle-time arithmetic on the four factual spans (58.5 h, 48 h, 214 h, 29.5 h) and on K-337’s 104–166 h band is correct. K-104’s “~49–54 h” band does not include the stated ~10 h best case (~48.5 h). Trivial, but it is a numeric inconsistency in the required table.

**Uncertainty calibration and quality reasoning**

15–30% D2 clarification, 10–25% K-337 reject, and informal no-show rates are presented as if they were calibrated. They are guesses. That is acceptable only if clearly marked as uninformed priors; here they propagate into expected-outcome cells and into the 100-case rework math (15–30 loops) as if they carried data.

On K-337, the later factual path is under-used for *quality* evaluation. Document Check 3 received confirmation, Assessment 3 accepted the evidence, and the decision was approve. Ex ante, Friday’s decision maker does not know that. Ex post, a reject would likely have been a false reject, and a conditional approval would have been directionally cheap insurance. The write-up leaves reject at 10–25% without down-weighting it against that observed resolution. The time saving is robust; the quality distribution should have been more tightly anchored.

K-104’s claim that preliminary work saves 1–3 h is taken from the prompt’s guidance and applied reasonably, including the incomplete-packet worst case. The idea of deepening the read after documents exist (Tue morning) is actually better than a pure Monday scan. It just is not feasible at the times written.

**Interference and scale**

The 100-case analysis is directionally right and covers every required topic. Two distortions remain. First, 100 K-104-like cases already need ~200–400 h of *final* C1 review in both worlds; the incremental load is preliminary hours, front-loading into the worst backlog days, idle reserved slots, and S1 retiming. The sentence that 100 reservations commit “200 preliminary hours plus priority final slots — effectively C1’s entire week” mixes incremental demand with work that existed anyway. If prelim and final hours net out, the scale failure is sequencing and no-shows, not total hours — and 200 h of *extra* prelim would violate the same “save 1–3 h later” premise used at case level. Second, 100 high-risk cases in a week is already a C1 throughput impossibility before any reservation policy; that baseline infeasibility is underplayed.

K-219’s substitution arithmetic (D1 −400 h, D2 +500–700 h, net +100–300 h) is consistent. The clarification×external-wait nonlinearity is the right one. K-337’s migration onto C1/M1 and the behavioral hazard (assessors stop documenting discrepancies, or front-load RMI 1) are genuine and well stated. K-411’s Little’s-law point is correct at high A-pool utilization: removing ~1–2 h of *artificial* task-creation delay is a real sojourn-time cut only if it does not reappear as assessment wait; at Monday loads it mostly will, except where idle evening slots exist.

**Ranking, pilots, synthesis, assumptions**

The rank order is coherent: structural, capacity-neutral, reversible, measurement-improving fix first; policy-preserving but C1-hostile reservation second; large but irreversible policy change third; fragile routing change fourth. Rank 1’s “never time-negative absent logging failures” overstates the author’s own audit-remediation worst case. Pilots are operationally specified (eligibility, randomization, spillover metrics, timestamp audit, hard stops). Section 7 stays in band and answers the four required questions. The eight assumptions are labeled with the allowed tags and actually used; assumption 3 (D2 overnight) and 4 (M1 batches) are the ones that most affect the numbers.

**Net**

This is a strong specialist answer with real counterfactual discipline, not a generic process-mining essay. The remaining deductions are for over-precise infeasible C1 clock times against a documented 16–20 h backlog, smeared incremental vs retimed C1 demand at scale, unanchored probabilities, incomplete use of K-337’s observed confirmation for quality, and a misread of K-219’s A2 evening interval as spare K-411 capacity. Those are enough, under a strict bar, to keep it well below the near-flawless band.
