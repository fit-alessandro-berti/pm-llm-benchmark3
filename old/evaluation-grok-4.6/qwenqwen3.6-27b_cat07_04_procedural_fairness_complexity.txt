**6.3**

The answer is structurally compliant and substantively stronger than a typical write-up: it follows the eight required headings, respects the main constraints (raw rates ≠ discrimination; case mix ≠ automatic justification; risk scores are not treated as neutral; support steps are treated as a possible burden; it does not propose removing them), and the core distinction between legitimate routing and concerning waits/handovers is the right one. It is not close to flawless.

**Missing required dimensions.** Section 1 covers six topics but omits two that were named in the required list: **quality/accessibility of support** and **appeal/rework burden**. Those are not optional color. The data give appeal rates of 6–16%, rework of 11–24%, an adjusted appeal gap of +4.4pp (B) and +5.7pp (D), and qualitative notes on explanation quality and dignity. Leaving them out of the fairness frame is a real gap, not a formatting nit.

**Section 3 underuses the tables and overinterprets one result.** The strongest unused facts are V1’s 42% vs 18–22% “clean path” gap, V6’s appeal-path share (4% vs 13% for D), Completeness→Request Docs waits (0.4d vs 1.6d for B), and Supervisor Review waits (1.0 vs 1.9/1.6d). Calling Group D’s +0.9-day adjusted cycle time evidence that “fraud review is relatively efficient” does not follow: the controls may simply absorb the extra path, and D still has a large raw/adjusted appeal residual. That is a logic error, not just omitted color.

**Section 2 is complete on paper, thin as identification.** Matching, variants, resources, wait decomposition, sensitivity, and region/channel checks are all named. What is missing is how those pieces actually separate disparity, discrimination, legitimate routing, and uncertainty. Matching *on* `risk_score_band` and `documentation_completeness` can be a bad-control / post-treatment problem if those variables are themselves biased; sensitivity analysis is mentioned but not specified (what you drop, what you expect to move, what would falsify a bias story). That is the central methodological demand of the prompt.

**Section 7 presents invented PM4Py APIs as executable code.** “Pseudocode” does not excuse this. `format_dataframe(..., variant_grouping=...)` is not a parameter. `filter_paths_performance(..., min_frequency=5)` confuses path-time filtering with DFG frequency. `filter_case_performance(..., attribute=..., threshold=..., operator=...)` is not the real signature (min/max duration). `discover_declare(df, skeleton)` is not how Declare discovery works. `filter_variants` is required and never appears. Resource-key handling is ignored. The plan lists the right *names* and the wrong *contracts*; as an analysis plan it would not run and would mislead someone implementing it.

**Bottom line is slightly stronger than the answer’s own caveats.** “The process exhibits **procedural unfairness**” and “strongly indicates systemic design and resource allocation failures” sits poorly next to Section 5 (biased covariates, missing needs, unobserved workload, historically contaminated risk). The evidence supports *concerning residual procedural burden that is not fully explained by documented complexity*, concentrated in support queues and handovers. A calibrated verdict is closer to **indeterminate on outcome discrimination, concerning on procedure**. The rest of Section 8 is otherwise well disciplined.

**What still works.** Mechanisms 1–3 are the right mix of legitimate demand and concerning amplification. Limitations hit every required failure mode. Mitigations all include trade-offs and do not cheat by deleting rights-preserving steps. Adjusted numbers are generally used correctly. Prose is clear.

Net: a competent policy memo with a real methodological skeleton, marked down hard for omitted required dimensions, one unjustified causal reading, and a PM4Py section that fakes the API. Not a 7+ under a strict rubric.
