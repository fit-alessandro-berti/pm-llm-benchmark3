**7.3**

The memo is strategically strong and format-compliant, but it is not close to flawless. The recommendation (E), the refusal to scale nationally or roll back wholesale, the segment-level reading, and the use of process-mining artifacts are all defensible. The defects below are enough to keep the score well below the top band.

**What works.** The six required sections are present and used for a decision, not a recap. Alternative E is the right hedge: keep the proven simple-house gains, restore activation-before-billing and permit checks, and treat survey-skipping as an experiment rather than a rollout. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are separated in the evidence table with more than eight prompt items. The five metric conflicts are explained with the right mechanisms (happy-path vs. exception tail; fitness vs. precision as lost control; handovers vs. slack; cost-shifting; heterogeneous effects). A–E each get advantages, risks, and conditions. M1–M8 are ranked with an explicit VoI rule and fallbacks. Thresholds and a gate review are specific enough to be operational.

**Presentation errors that matter.** Comparison operators and minus signs are missing throughout. An executive memo that writes “p95: 4562 days,” “14.210.8 days,” “€184€161,” “fitness 0.870.94,” and “first-time-right  baseline  2pp” is not usable as written. F7 is the worst case: “apartments (8pp FTR …), old buildings (11pp FTR …)” omits the minus signs on the quality metrics. Surrounding prose (“worsened”) mostly saves the reader, but a decision table that can be read as the opposite sign on first-time-right is a serious defect in this genre.

**Overclaim and inconsistency.** The memo asserts “These are design consequences, not noise” while the table correctly treats causality as interpretation. That blurs the fact/interpretation line the prompt forbids. The €14 vs. €46 downstream add-on is treated as a known tripling even though the prompt flags the cost estimate as incomplete. Coordinator ownership is scaled immediately with an invented 85% utilization cap, while M5 (the test of ownership under absence) is ranked 7th — at odds with the caution applied to the skip rule. Ranking M7 first also stretches “value of information”: it is mostly an operational control already implied by the decision, not the highest-leverage uncertainty-resolver (that is closer to M4 or M1).

**Smaller nits.** “Second visits nearly doubled” (11% → 18%) is loose. Seasonality, four-region confounding, and bundle inseparability are acknowledged but then underweighted when ownership and merged intake are scaled on the same six-week, case-mix-skewed pilot. None of this overturns E, but a stricter memo would scale those pieces more narrowly until M4/M5 return.

The reasoning quality is high; the deliverable is not. Missing signs on the core numbers and a few overconfident leaps are enough, under a strict rubric, to land this in the mid-7s rather than the 9s.
