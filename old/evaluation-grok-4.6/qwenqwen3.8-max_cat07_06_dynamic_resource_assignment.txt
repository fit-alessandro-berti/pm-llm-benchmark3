The write-up matches the required seven-heading format, treats the problem as multi-objective, separates hard rules from preferences, and covers every requested scoring component. That structural completeness is the main reason it is not lower. Under a strict reading it is still far from a high score.

**Assignment decisions contradict the evidence the answer itself cites.** C001 is the clearest failure. It is an urgent Security Review with a 3h SLA, complexity 0.88, and an owner at Security skill 1. The historical note says weak-skill continuity adds a median 3.3 hours of service time and more rework, while an owner change adds 2.7 hours of waiting. Putting Celine on the case, even with Hugo supervising, is likely an SLA miss, not a reasoned trade-off. Constraint 5 allows continuity when supervision is feasible; it does not require keeping a trainee-level owner on a 3-hour enterprise security SLA. The same person is also given C007 (high, 6h SLA, complexity 0.81) with only 6.0 hours left. Those two jobs cannot both be done today. Acknowledging the risk does not make the recommendation sound.

**Specialist capacity is inverted.** Celine (Diagnosis 3) is consumed on a Security skill-1 task, while the repeated diagnosis loop (C003) is sent to Hugo (Diagnosis 2). That move also pushes reassignment_count to 3, which the answer’s own evidence links to 1.8× rework and 1.4× appeals. David is correctly protected on overtime, but Celine was the natural diagnosis specialist and was spent elsewhere.

**The 20% training claim is not robust.** Only C009 is a real training assignment (Amal, Diagnosis 1, supervised). C015 counts Jonas (Security 0) as a trainee even though skill 0 cannot perform Security Review at all. Relabeling him as a non-performing observer is a definitional dodge. If that credit is rejected, training share is 1/9, below the stated floor. There were cleaner options (Farid on a slack diagnosis case; a documented exception if Bruno were used on C012) that were not used.

**Several other loads are implausible.** Farid gets two urgent cases (C002, 2h; C011, 4h) plus C010 with 6.5 hours remaining — a single-point failure the resilience section never really solves. Eva keeps high-complexity C016 (7h SLA, 5.0 hours left) and is also asked to supervise C009. Jonas is parked at the 4-assignment cap by counting an observer slot.

**Baseline comparison is incomplete.** First-available and specialist-only are described in generalities. The answer never actually assigns C001–C016 under those two policies, so the comparison table is not an evaluation of this queue. That is a direct miss against the question.

**The PM4Py sketch names the right APIs but is not reliable.** `(sla_deadline - timestamp).dt.total_hours()` is not valid pandas. `Series.diff()` on resource names is not a correct handover counter. `filter_event_attribute_values` with a hardcoded list of complexity floats is an exact-match filter, not a threshold. Those are not cosmetic issues in an “implementation sketch” that is supposed to support audit.

**What is actually good:** hard vs soft constraints are separated well; high-overtime staff are not loaded without a story; Quality batching and Security/Approval rules are mostly respected; the worker note is non-punitive; audit indicators cover the required set; the policy does not pretend there is one optimum.

Those strengths make it a competent design memo. The operational contradictions, the stretched training quota, the uncomputed baselines, and the broken audit code keep it well below a high mark.
