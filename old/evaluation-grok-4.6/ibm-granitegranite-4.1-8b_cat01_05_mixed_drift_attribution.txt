The answer has the right *skeleton*—March is mostly semantic, later movement is real, and mechanisms are staggered—but it is not a reliable diagnosis. Quantitative claims are often wrong, drift dimensions are repeatedly mislabeled, and several required deliverables are incomplete or internally inconsistent. Under a strict reading that is a large penalty, not a cosmetic one.

## What is actually sound

The core temporal story is directionally correct: the March fitness collapse is a label/provenance shock; mapped fitness shows the business path did not collapse with it; May–July is a different phenomenon; October–November is seasonal load on top of leftover strain; one global change point is the wrong model. That is the main thing the task is testing, and it is present.

Section 2 also gets the conceptual point that mapping can restore conformance without proving the process is unchanged. The hypothesis list covers the eight required mechanism families. The “multiple mechanism-specific change points” conclusion is the right one.

That is not enough to carry the rest.

## Factual and quantitative failures

These are not rounding issues. They change the diagnosis.

1. **`fast_variant_pct` is inverted.** It *falls* from 58–60% to 45% in May, then to the mid-30s in autumn. The timeline and §9 treat it as a rise and then use that rise as evidence for the supplier shock. That is a direct misread of the table.

2. **Supplier Z share is confused with complexity.** Section 5 says supplier cases “become disproportionately high-complexity (pre-May 21% → May 43%).” 43% is `supplier_z_pct`, not the complexity rate of Z cases. High-complexity only moves 23% → 25% in May. This collapses the distinction the whole exercise is built on: a non-random supplier subset versus true case-mix drift.

3. **Service time is swapped with queueing.** The brief says Assess service time went about 4.8h → 5.4h and that *queue wait rose much more*. Section 4 cites “queue wait from 4 to 5.4h.” Section 3 then assigns service 20% of the cycle-time gap via a **1.2h** service increase that does not exist, while the same row also says waiting rose **4.6h** but only gets a 25% share. The decomposition is arithmetically inconsistent and contradicts the only service-time evidence given.

4. **The “baseline-relative” table is misaligned and partly invented.** Columns start at February but the first cycle-time / complexity / rework entries are January’s. August and the November peak (49.0h, 45% complex) disappear. DFG rates are filled in for February, April, June, and November, which were never supplied. A table that claims to be a change decomposition is neither baseline-relative nor faithful to the months that exist.

5. **DFG distances are not the stated measure.** Manhattan distance on the given rates vs January is roughly Mar 3, May 40, Jul 59, Sep 28, Oct 64, Dec 36. Reported values (2.0, 6.2, 8.0, 6.3…) are an order of magnitude off, and March is explained with raw `Open Case` duplication even though the fragment is already mapped and de-duplicated. If the author meant a scaled or per-edge average, that is not stated. As written, the distance analysis is not reproducible.

6. **December is a bad reference month for deterioration.** Using Jan 31.5h vs Dec 42.3h mixes recovery (Z down, overtime, quality campaign, lower volume) into the “deterioration” residual. The actual stress peak is October–November.

7. **Invented or garbled quantities.** “Staff per assessor falls” has no supporting series. “4.85.2%”, “2727%”, and similar concatenations are sloppy in a section that is supposed to look like measurement.

## Process-mining and causal errors

Drift dimensions are systematically wrong.

- May is labeled population/resource drift. The sharp signature is **rework / control-flow** (`Assess→RequestInfo` 12 → 24, rework 13% → 24%) after Z starts sending incomplete bundles. Population is only a mediator: Z is a non-random slice and was already somewhat more complex. Treating May as case-mix first buries the mechanism.
- June staffing is labeled control-flow/behavioral. The brief’s own clue is **resource/organizational + performance (queueing)** with little service-time movement. Rework was already up in May.
- July’s priority rule is real behavioral/control-flow change, but **Senior Assessment 22% is October**, not the July policy (9%). Attributing the October senior-assessment spike to July without the complexity surge is a mediation error.
- May is listed as a **logging** change point because raw fitness goes 0.51 → 0.49. That is noise on a series still dominated by the unmapped label. Logging change points are March (labels, `Open Case`, missing resources) and, at most, the persistent post-migration missingness regime—not the supplier date.
- Section 5 predicts *longer* waits for high-complexity cases after the priority rule. The brief says the opposite: priority should **shorten** high-complexity waits and **lengthen** standard ones. The discrimination table therefore cannot separate the hypothesis it is meant to test.

Causal language is also looser than the brief allows. “Evidence 85/80/78/90” looks precise and is not. Alternatives such as “coincidental volume shift” for a 18% → 43% supplier jump are not serious foils. Rank-8 “structural equation modeling” is ornamental.

Section 2 over-claims that missing resources “fragment trace completion.” Attribute missingness breaks resource/social/performance analyses; it does not, by itself, split traces. Duplicated `Open Case` and missing resources are also not separated by *analysis type* as requested.

## Requirement misses and weak monitoring

The format is followed at heading level and then thins out.

- Timeline: six rows, the minimum, and they omit August’s performance peak, November’s mix/volume peak, and December’s **mixed** recovery (load drop + overtime + quality campaign). Onset types never use “mixed” or “recurring” except one “seasonal,” despite explicit instruction.
- Section 2 is slightly over the 280-word cap and still misses the analyses-by-artifact discussion.
- Monitoring table has **nine** monitors, not ten, and **resource completeness is duplicated**. Required **event-ordering** and **outcome** (Approve/Reject) monitors are absent.
- Decision rule 1 is inoperable: after March, raw fitness lives near 0.5, so “raw fitness < 0.88 for two months” fires forever. Rules 2–3 are also close to the post-migration baseline (duplicates already 4–6%, missingness already ~5–6%). Rule 6 (`Senior Assessment > 20%`) fires every renewal season and fights rule 7. Nothing watches **priority redistribution** (complexity-stratified waits) or **mapped** fitness as the behavioral series.

Primary monitoring population = “all cases / process level” undoes the segmentation logic. After a semantic migration and a non-random supplier, the primary operational population should be a **mapped, de-duplicated** log, with Z × complexity as the first split—not the raw soup.

## Section 8 does not do what was asked

Part A must **embed** the two tables. It `read_csv`s files that do not exist. Baseline subtraction on a one-row frame, `dfg_rates[monthly_summary['month']==…]`, and `fitness_gap['2026-01']` are index bugs. Fitness “recovery” is never computed as a share of the March drop.

Part B was required to use `pm4py.format_dataframe`, `pm4py.filter_time_range`, `discover_dfg`, `get_variants`, `discover_temporal_profile`, and optionally skeletons/DECLARE, comparing raw vs mapped **without** overwriting. Instead:

- custom pandas slicing replaces `filter_time_range`;
- `format_dataframe` is never called;
- `discover_dfg(..., parameters={"variant": "alpha-miner"})` is API-incoherent (α-miner is not a DFG variant);
- DECLARE / log skeletons are omitted;
- mapping overwrites the working log;
- a stale 1.x `xes.factory` import is unused.

That section is not a usable PM4Py plan.

## Bottom line

A grader looking for temporal synthesis will see the right outline. A grader looking for process-mining rigor, causal restraint, and numbers that survive a check against the CSV will not. The answer repeatedly treats the wrong series as the signal, invents or shifts values, and then builds monitors and rules on those mistakes. Completeness of headings cannot offset that.

**4.2** — structurally complete, conceptually half-right, quantitatively untrustworthy.
