6.2

The memo is complete, on-format, and chooses a constrained option rather than naive scale/rollback. That is the floor for a passing answer, not the ceiling. Under a strict reading it is a competent brief with repeated overclaim, a few invented specifics, and several analytical shortcuts that a decision-maker should not have to correct.

**Format and decision hygiene.** Sections 1–6 are present and usable. The executive memo is under 700 words and is actually a recommendation, not a recap. It correctly refuses national scale (A) and full rollback (B). Those are necessary conditions, not strengths. The memo still violates the “do not merge facts and interpretations” rule in the one place executives will read: “evidence proves,” “backfired severely,” “dangerously permissive,” “stall indefinitely,” “game metrics.” The table later separates layers; the memo does not.

**Factual slips and invented operational detail.** The skip rule was for *medium-risk* addresses; the memo repeatedly calls it high-risk. “Halt national scaling” treats a four-region pilot as if a rollout were underway. Section 6 invents a “shift 15% of field-survey capacity” to second-visit teams—an unsupported number and a confused resource move (survey skill ≠ revisit skill). Complaint “escalations +3.7pp” conflates 30-day complaints (6.1% → 9.8%) with the DFG edge Billing start → Complaint escalation (3% → 8%). EUR 207 is an incomplete estimate; the memo and conflict section treat it as the true total. Fitness was not “achieved by sacrificing” precision; high fitness plus low precision means a too-general model, which the mining team already stated more carefully.

**Recommendation is internally inconsistent and coarser than the evidence.** Alternative E in the prompt is “deploy R for simple houses, *add controls* for apartments/old buildings.” The answer operationalizes this as a near-full revert. Section 1 reverts apartments, old buildings, and business; section 6 also reverts rural. That is a 34% keep / ~66% rollback, and it throws away coordinator ownership where the damaging mechanisms were survey-skip and post-billing activation, not ownership itself. C is therefore undersold: keep the coordinator, kill skip and post-billing activation, fix backup coverage. Segment medians also improved for apartments (−2d), business (−3d), and rural (−1d); only old buildings got worse on the median. Restricting the *entire* redesign to simple houses overfits one strong cell and ignores that the tail, not the median, is the failure mode.

**Metric-conflict writeup is the best section and still imprecise.** Fitness/precision, handovers/resilience, visible vs downstream cost, and house vs apartment are directionally right. The median-vs-tail paragraph is not: the median is not “the simple-house 34%.” Most segments got faster typical cases; complex segments blew up the tail. That is a different, more decision-relevant story. “Virtually any path” overstates precision 0.51.

**Process-mining artifacts are only partially used.** Alignments and a few DFG edges appear. Generalization (0.70 → 0.56), the handover-of-work vs working-together contrast, activation-specialist ownership after billing, SLA (worse overall, better for simple houses), and NPS (+21 → +17) are barely or not used. The serious methodological caveats are almost absent: 6-week pilot vs 10-week baseline, p95 = 62 days against a ~42-day intake window, sparse start timestamps (so waiting-time claims are weak), segment attributes missing before week 3, cost after week 4 incomplete, and pilot mix richer in apartments/old buildings. Item 7 names composition; the memo still treats headline deterioration as a clean treatment effect.

**Value-of-information ranking is list-complete, not decision-theoretic.** M6 first is defensible for *future* expansion of skip, not for the choice they have already made (stop skip outside simple houses). M4 and M5 are more decision-moving right now: whether even the simple-house case is net cheaper, and whether coordinator ownership can be kept outside that cell without a single point of failure. Ranking M7 last is hard to justify given billing-before-activation and missing permit checks—the live compliance failures. Immediate measurement also disagrees with itself: §1 says M2/M3/M4; §6 says M3/M4/M6.

**Thresholds are specific but arbitrary and stacked.** A 2% false-negative rate (undefined), 10% net savings, P90 below baseline *and* complaints ≤3% (tighter than the 6.1% baseline) is a political bar, not a value-of-information stop rule. Fine to propose numbers; not fine to present them as if they follow from the log.

**Net.** Structured, on-constraint, and better than scale-or-kill. Not close to flawless: invented staffing %, inconsistent revert scope, causal overclaim in the memo, a wrong account of the median, weak confounding/censoring discipline, and a VoI order that does not match the decision they just took. That is a mid-6 under a strict rubric.
