**7.3**

The final memo chooses the right strategic posture (hybrid E, not A or B), keeps facts/interpretations/assumptions separated in the table, and produces a usable go/no-go threshold. That is competent synthesis, not a top-tier one.

The most serious content error is the misuse of process-mining **generalization** (0.56) as evidence of organizational resilience. Generalization is about whether the discovered model will fit unseen cases; it is not a measure of backup coverage or disruption absorption. Pairing it with the working-together network does not fix the concept. For a task that explicitly tests PM literacy, that is a real flaw.

Required artifacts are unevenly used. Fitness, precision, alignments, social/resource findings, and segment outcomes are handled well. **Temporal profiles** (install→activation worsening in apartments; billing→complaint as a new long-tail relation; failed-visit→second-visit delay under high utilization) and **DFG edge shifts** are mostly background color, not decision evidence. Incomplete segment/risk attributes before pilot week 3 are also underweighted; the segment table is treated as more trustworthy than the data-coverage caveats allow.

Precision is slightly conflated with “invalid/undesirable” paths. Low precision means extra model behavior relative to the log; business undesirability comes from alignments and outcomes. The write-up often collapses those.

Presentation in the memo and final recommendation is sloppy for an executive artifact: missing comparators on the rollout threshold (`firsttimeright  82%`, `total cost  EUR 198`), smashed terms, `[Today]`. The threshold *intent* is recoverable from earlier text, but the decision rule itself is the one place that cannot be ambiguous.

VOI ranking is defensible (M1/M4/M6 high, M7/M5 lower) and alternatives A–E are evaluated with conditions. None of that offsets the PM error, the missing artifacts, or the unclear gate. This is a strong B, not nearly flawless.
