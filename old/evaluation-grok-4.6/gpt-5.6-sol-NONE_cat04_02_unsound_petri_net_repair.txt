## Defects and unclarities

**Repair C is mis-specified and oversold.** The header is “make analysis optional under waiver,” but the two edits also destroy the AND-split: analysis no longer starts in parallel even on the standard path, and it becomes strictly sequential after `t_standard_review`. That is two policy/sequencing changes, not one. The “Preserved intent” cell therefore overstates what C keeps. The exact-edits cell is sloppy (“add arc `t_waive_review → p_waived` is already present, so no edit there”) and would confuse anyone implementing from the table. A policy-minimal optional-analysis repair would leave `t_split → p_analysis` in place and skip/consume that token only on the waiver route. C is admissible as a deliberately different policy, but it is not a clean member of that family.

**The edit-cost argument for B is weaker than it sounds.** Counting each incident arc and each deleted node as separate unit costs is internally consistent, but the prose then claims those six edits are “minimal for fully deleting” and explains them as “two incident arcs, one incoming redirection, two nodes, and the join’s output arc.” That wording double-counts or at least muddies the redirection. Under a normal graph-edit convention, deleting a node already removes its incident arcs, so the same cleanup is closer to cost 4. The conclusion that A is cheaper than B still holds; the precision of the cost model does not.

**“Globally minimal” is slightly too strong.** A is the unique *valid, policy-preserving* cost-1 repair, and the answer’s reason (the waiver route must both test and consume the analysis token) is good. The net is still sound because the review outcomes are exclusive, but this is the main structural price of A versus B and is more precise than “two silent joins may confuse maintainers.”

The reachable non-final markings on the waiver route are both `{p_analysis:1, sink:1}` and `{p_analysis_done:1, sink:1}`. Section 3 later shows this; the verdict should not collapse them.
