**7.4**

The final six-section write-up is a strong, constraint-aware organizational reading of the summarized log. It does not cluster by raw activity volume, it uses complexity and substitution as first-class evidence, it treats shadow work as useful rather than illegitimate, and it keeps formal authority separate from behavioral similarity. Those are the hard parts of the benchmark, and they are mostly handled well. It is not nearly flawless.

**What holds up.** The ten-role table is process-owner plausible and does the work the prompt asked for: permanent front office is split from seasonal temps; Chen/Dina are not treated as interchangeable despite 0.94 cosine similarity; Erik is kept as a rare critical specialist rather than noise; Giorgio is not forced into either “front office” or “commercial”; Mateo is not collapsed into Hana. Core/peripheral distinctions are generally evidence-based (frequency plus complexity plus substitution plus authority). The required shadow/bridging discussion of Giorgio, Mateo, and Erik is specific, cites the right episodes (38→74 minute pricing delay; 13/21 approvals rechecked; two critical security waits), and states benefits and risks without moralizing. Ambiguous-assignment rows give two interpretations and a decidability test. The operational section’s two cautions are the right ones: do not staff off frequency, and do not promote a substitute into the role they temporarily covered.

**Analytical defects.** Several claims outrun the evidence. Giorgio’s 20 A9 events are labeled “preliminary” by analogy with Mateo’s documented week-5 screenings; the log summary does not say that. Calling Chen a latent security deputy is a staffing suggestion, not a finding: 24 A6 events can just as easily be technical configuration that touches security controls. Nora is said to have “no complex-case exposure,” which is false: 85 medium-complexity cases. Hana’s A13 is glossed as “reopen sign-off”; A13 is Reopen Request, and those 24 events could be initiations, not approvals. Putting Chen and Dina (and Amira and Ben) in single proposed roles while arguing they are internally tiered is a mild inconsistency with the instruction not to force clean clusters; the ambiguity section patches this but does not resolve it. The Fatima/Giorgio pair in section 4 largely restates Giorgio’s own row.

**Code defects (required section 5).** Illustrative code is allowed, but it must stay coherent with PM4Py-style APIs. Three problems are more than cosmetic. First, `discover_activity_based_resource_similarity` returns a resource–resource similarity matrix, not role clusters; feeding it through `normalize_cluster_output` is a conceptual misuse of a function the prompt explicitly required. Second, high-level `discover_organizational_roles` returns `Role` objects (`activities`, `originator_importance`), not sets of resource names; the normalizer would not extract members, so the later join to complexity/substitution would not work as written. Third, `critical_event_share` uses `x >= 4`, which is only correct if `case_complexity` was mapped onto {1,2,3,4}. If the column is the 0–1 score shown in the tables, every resource gets a critical share of 0. `threshold=` on the high-level discovery wrappers is also doubtful. The custom cosine profiles, substitution delta, and authority join are the best part of the code and partly redeem it.

**Net.** Excellent organizational argument with disciplined use of complexity, substitution, and authority; a few over-readings; and a required code section that would not execute or interpret PM4Py outputs as claimed. That is clearly above competent and clearly short of near-flawless.
