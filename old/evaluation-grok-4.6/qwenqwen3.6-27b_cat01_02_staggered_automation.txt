**6.0**

The write-up is complete, on-format, and conceptually in the right family: endogenous targeting, availability versus intensity, median/tail split, small-N inference, fraud-desk interference, and a refusal of the “automation reduces cycle time by X%” line. That is not enough. Under a strict reading, several **core quantitative and identification claims are wrong or unsupported**, the promised design is not the one estimated, and the code as written does not run.

### What is actually good
Section 1 stays descriptive and separates availability, `automated_fast_path_pct`, targeted selection, and measurement limits. The estimand list covers ITT-like availability, intensity, median, tail, rework, escalation, CSAT, and one heterogeneity contrast, and it flags SUTVA/versioning. The mechanism and threat tables are the strongest parts: fast-path substitution versus specialist congestion, learning, routing, staffing, survey selection, and aggregation are the right objects. The claim ladder is mostly well ordered. Recommending `expand with an embedded evaluation` is the correct policy class.

### Material quantitative failures
Adopter pre/post arithmetic is not clean. Gamma pre-period p90 is **85.8, not 87.4**, so the reported −2.5 h change is overstated (true window change is about −0.9 h). Alpha post escalation is **11.38, not 11.26**. Never-treated medians are printed as the broken fragment `34.439.0` / `41.440.8`. The intensity slopes and p-values (`−0.08 h/pp (p0.12)`, etc.) and the sensitivity OLS numbers (`−0.6 h (SE 1.1)`, `+3.2 h (SE 2.8)`) are not produced by the code and read as unsourced.

Worse than typos is the **control comparison**. The required never-treated contrast is waved off as “do not diverge sharply.” That is false on the same windows the author chose. Alpha+Beta median rises about +0.8 h while Delta+Epsilon rise about +5 h; p90 also rises **more** among never-treated. During the May–June complexity shock, already-treated branches deteriorate less than untreated ones. Section 7 then calls p90 “likely” worse because of automation. Absolute tails rose; **relative tails did not clearly worsen**. That inversion is a first-order error on this task.

Windows are also biased in a way the text does not own. Gamma’s “success” compares Jul–Aug (when **every** branch recovers as arrivals fall) with a pre period that includes the May complexity spike. Alpha/Beta post periods mix the May disaster, FTE loss, extra routing, and the August rebound. A serious analysis would have shown at least one alternative window and an explicit DiD table. Neither appears.

Load is under-quantified. Alpha goes from roughly 30 to 43 arrivals per FTE. Treating routing and FTE as separate footnotes, rather than a workload intensity series, makes the median/tail discussion thinner than the process story requires. The RTM paragraph is also backwards: selecting the historically worst branches is exactly the setting where reversion is a threat; “no sharp pre-break” does not retire it.

### Identification versus implementation
Callaway–Sant’Anna is a reasonable *name* to put on staggered adoption, and the text correctly rejects naïve TWFE. With five branches it is not an honest primary estimator, and the write-up never estimates it. Relative time is built in Section 9 and then unused. The “panel model” is pooled OLS of the median on `automation` plus covariates, **no branch FE, no month FE, no event study**. That is weaker than the identification section promised and is not a credible ATT.

Escalation is over-attributed to automation. The common March fraud-desk jump of about +2 pp is visible in every branch. Later treated-control gaps are smaller and coincide with routing and mix changes. Confidence 70 on an automation escalation effect is too high.

### Policy and code
The rollout class is right; the design is sloppy. Ramping to 100% automated while “keeping 10% manual” is arithmetically inconsistent. Within-branch randomization still shares the specialist and fraud-desk queues, so SUTVA is not solved by an internal 10% holdout. Choosing Delta because it is the easy branch maximizes cleanliness and **minimizes transport** to Epsilon (38% high-complexity). The decision rule ignores that.

Section 8 is short of the 250–400 word band. Section 9 has an execution-breaking bug: `pd.StringIO` does not exist (`io.StringIO` does). Trends are printed by flattening month/value pairs into unreadable sequences; `matplotlib` is imported and unused; only the median is modeled.

### Bottom line
This is a competent exam answer with the right vocabulary and the right high-level refusal. It is not a reliable causal-policy memo. The missed common Jul–Aug recovery, the misread control trajectories, the invented coefficients, the C&S/OLS mismatch, and the non-runnable script are enough to keep the score well below “nearly flawless.” A 6.0 reflects a structurally complete argument that fails the quantitative-discipline bar the rubric itself emphasizes.
