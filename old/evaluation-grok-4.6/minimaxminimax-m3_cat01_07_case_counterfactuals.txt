The write-up is well-structured and mostly constraint-aware, but it fails a core temporal test on K-337 and then builds ranking and synthesis on that error. That is not a cosmetic slip.

**What is actually strong.** Format compliance is high: required headings, two fenced feasibility blocks, one row per case, labeled derivation paragraphs, both required tables, eight labeled assumptions, and a 100-case interference discussion that hits C1 reservation, D1/D2 substitution, rework, assessment congestion, weekends, and timestamp fiction. The intervention typology is correct: K-104 is timing/capacity, K-219 is routing, K-337 is decision policy, K-411 is concurrency. Four-eyes, mandatory final compliance after the latest assessment, originals-before-document-check, and “preliminary screen ≠ final review” are respected. K-219’s queue-versus-skill tradeoff and rework branch are the right mechanism. K-411 correctly separates earlier readiness from guaranteed earlier completion and flags false concurrency. Those pieces would support a much higher score if the calendar work were reliable.

**The decisive failure is K-337.** Assessment 2 ends Friday; RMI 2 returns Monday; Document Check 3, Assessment 3, compliance, and decision follow that Monday–Wednesday. That Wednesday is the *following* week (2026-04-15), not the Wednesday after registration. Factual cycle time is on the order of **~8.9 days (~214 h)**, not ~46 h or ~70 h. Suppressing RMI 2 and deciding from Assessment 2 should finish Friday evening of week 1 (~103 h): a large shortening, with the quality/decision-policy cost the prompt already flags. The answer inverts this: it treats factual completion as the first Wednesday, then claims the intervention *adds* 30–60 h because compliance “moves” from Tuesday to Friday. Tuesday after Assessment 3 is week-2 Tuesday; Friday after Assessment 2 is week-1 Friday. The “rework luckily hit a low-C1-queue Tuesday” story is therefore calendar-impossible.

That error is not contained. Section 4 says K-337 is faster per case and saves thousands of external hours at n=100; Sections 2, 3, 5, and 7 say the same case gets slower. Ranking K-337 last *only* for quality risk would be defensible; ranking it last because “cycle-time impact is negative in the current case” is false. The synthesis then doubles down: “most dangerous despite potential speed” immediately denied by “it does not actually shorten.” That is exactly the confusion the rubric is designed to catch.

**Other temporal defects, smaller but real.** K-104 and K-219 absolute cycle times are off by a day (82.5 h vs ~58.5 h; 72 h vs 48 h)—an off-by-24 h count of Mon→Wed. Deltas for K-104 (~5–8 h) can still be in range; K-219’s “+15 h worst” without rework is not: even their slow clean path (Decision ~Wed 00:00) is still shorter than factual Wed 09:00. D2 starting K-411 at 11:30 ignores the Tue 08:00 D2 forecast of 4 h. Several decisions are parked in the late evening or at midnight with no M1 availability caveat.

**Feasibility nits.** C1 “background reading” Tue 06:00–Wed 08:00 while holding a 16–20 h backlog is not reconciled with “no two service intervals at once.” A reserved Wed 08:30 C1 start against an 8 h Wednesday queue is the intervention, but the overnight/pre-shift preliminary work is hand-waved. Overnight D2 work is more defensible because factual D1 already runs overnight.

**Interference and uncertainty.** Section 4 is the best part: convex C1 reservation collapse, D2 saturation plus nonlinear handwriting rework, A2 as the migrated bottleneck, timestamp integrity. Ranges and reverse-evidence paragraphs are used in good faith. They do not rescue a backwards week-boundary on the one case that exists to test rework deletion.

**Net.** Strong process-rule literacy and system-level thinking, broken by a first-order calendar error that inverts K-337, contradicts the capacity section, and contaminates the ranking and final claim. Under a strict bar that is a mid score, not a high one.
