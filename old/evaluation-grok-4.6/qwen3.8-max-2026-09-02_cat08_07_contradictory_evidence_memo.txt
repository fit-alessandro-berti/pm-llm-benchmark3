**7.1**

The final memo is complete, constraint-compliant, and chooses the right class of decision (hybrid E, not national scale or blanket rollback). It is not nearly flawless. Several structural and reasoning defects keep it well below a high score.

**What is done well.** The six required sections are present. The executive memo stays under 700 words and is actually a recommendation, not a recap. It refuses Alternative A, does not treat B as the default, and states the core trade-off: keep simple-house speed/handover gains, contain skip-rule and post-billing activation risk. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are labeled separately. All five metric conflicts are explained in process-mining terms (fitness vs precision, median vs tail, handovers vs resilience, visible vs downstream cost, simple houses vs apartments/old buildings). A–E are all evaluated. M1–M8 are all ranked. The close includes pause/scale/measure/gate language.

**Strategic synthesis is weak.** Section 2 is a near-complete transcription of the prompt (48 atomic “facts,” then 8 interpretations and 8 assumptions). That satisfies “at least eight items” and the no-merge rule, but it is not an evidence table a decision-maker can use. Related observations (cycle-time distribution, quality bundle, DFG exception edges, data-coverage limits) are splintered into one-row metrics. The task is judged on synthesis; dumping every number is a material miss.

**Value-of-information ranking is conceptually off.** M7 is ranked first, but alignments already show *frequent* billing-before-activation and missing permit checks. That is a control/kill-switch problem, not the highest remaining uncertainty. Decision-changing information is still mainly: causal effect of survey skipping (M1/M6), true 60-day cost (M4), and whether coordinator ownership is viable under absence (M5). Ranking a known conformance failure as top VOI confuses monitoring with information value. M4 at rank 4 is too low given that cost after week 4 is explicitly missing and could reverse the economic case.

**Unclarities and softness.**
- Survey skipping is paused for every named non-simple segment, but not explicitly for simple low-risk houses. Immediate actions for that segment list coordinator ownership and merged scheduling only. Whether the unvalidated skip rule continues there is left implied.
- Broader-rollout gates use “not materially worse” for p90 and full cost. That is not an operational threshold.
- The 85% utilization cap is invented without tying it to observed failure (93% load, longer failed-visit → second-visit delay). Reasonable as a management proposal; presented as if it were evidence-derived.
- Assumption A4 is misclassified: “measurement will clarify the EUR 9 gap” is a statement about a study, not an uncertain world-state. A8 is the actual assumption.

**Smaller analytical gaps.** The executive memo underweights selection/measurement limits (pilot mix, attributes missing before week 3, sparse start timestamps) even though the segment table already shows *within-segment* harm for apartments and old buildings. Generalization (0.56 vs 0.70) is tabled and then dropped. Low precision is slightly over-read as “the model cannot be used for conformance checking”; the real issue is that the discovered model allows too many paths, so it is a bad *normative* model.

**No major factual errors** in the metrics, DFG shares, alignments, or segment deltas, and the recommendation does not violate the hard constraints. That is why this is not a mid-range score. The remaining defects—mechanical evidence, confused VOI, vague gates, residual skip-rule ambiguity—are enough, under a strict standard, to keep it far from 9–10.
