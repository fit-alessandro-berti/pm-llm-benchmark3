**7.2**

The final write-up is a strong, estimand-first design that mostly matches the required format and the quality bar. It is not nearly flawless. A core sensitivity encoding is statistically inconsistent with the claim attached to it, and a few definitions stay looser than the rest of the analysis.

**What holds up.** Time origin, horizon, and the four estimands are operationally mapped. The S03 / S09 / S06 / S08 contributions are handled correctly under competing risks versus administrative censoring versus an already-observed 72h breach. The completed-case critique is concrete and arithmetically right: 4/13 late resolutions among resolved cases versus 18 known non-resolutions by 72h, 9 known on-time resolutions, and 5 still-unknown open-below-72 cases. Immortal time on `Escalate`, leakage from full prefixes / rework, open-below versus open-above 72h, and cohort confounding (priority mix plus incomplete AutoCheck compliance) are all named with case IDs. The method comparison recommends Aalen–Johansen for actual 72h incidence and landmarking for alerts, which fits these data rather than listing estimators generically. The single Python block builds the table, keeps open cases, encodes two estimands and three transfer rules, stratifies by cohort and priority, and constructs a PM4Py log without invented intermediate timestamps.

**The main technical error.** `sla_horizon_failure` is sold as the conservative transfer rule, but the AJ coding is not conservative. Moving a transfer at 26h or 68h to a competing event at 72h keeps that case in the risk set after it has left the system, raises later \(S(u-)\), and can **increase** \(\mathrm{CIF}_R(72)\) relative to competing-at-transfer. That is the opposite of “count as unresolved at the horizon.” The binary/current-status treatment of those transfers as failures is coherent; the time-to-event version is not. `n_at_risk_72` under this scenario is therefore misleading. Assumption C also blends two different ideas (horizon coding versus informative bounds) that should have been separated.

**Smaller issues that still matter.** E1 is written as both \(P(T_R>72)\) and \(1-\mathrm{CIF}_R(72)\) without always keeping latent net risk distinct from crude incidence. Discrete-time convention at exactly 72h is not pinned down. E4 is defined but the code never prints cause-specific CIFs for withdrawal, rejection, or transfer. The 12h inactivity threshold appears only in code. None of these sink the design, but under a strict bar they keep the score well below the top of the scale.
