**6.4**

The memo chooses the right family of action (E with measurement discipline, not A or B) and is mostly decision-oriented. It is not nearly flawless. Several factual, statistical, and design errors would mislead an executive, so the score cannot sit in the high band.

**Strategic synthesis.** Recommending hybrid deployment plus gated learning is defensible and respects the constraints: no national scale of the full redesign, no wholesale rollback that discards the simple-house gain. The trade-off is stated clearly. The memo still overreaches. “This proves the core mechanism can work when risk is low” treats an observational, composition-biased, six-week pilot with incomplete segment attributes before week 3 as causal proof. That is not warranted. The memo also presents three design choices as if they “drove” the harm; those are interpretations, and they leak into the executive section as established fact.

**Facts vs interpretations.** The evidence table is the strongest part: nine items, three columns, good use of fitness/precision, alignments, DFG shifts, temporal profile, resource networks, and segment outcomes. The table is then contradicted in the recommendation. Item 8 correctly flags that pilot segment shares may not be the national mix; Section 6 still writes “simple houses, low-risk (34% nationally).” The prompt says pilot regions had *more* apartments and older buildings than baseline, so the national simple-house share is likely *higher* than 34%, not equal to it. Using the pilot mix as a national routing volume is a material error. Item 4’s assumption that “3+ handovers” is compromised by sparse start timestamps is technically confused: handover counts come from resource/activity sequences, not start-complete pairs.

**Metric conflicts.** Four of the five explanations are solid and use the process-mining artifacts properly (permissive model vs alignments; bimodal duration; cost shifted from prevention to correction; skip rule failing on high-variance stock). Two failures matter. First, the resilience paragraph collapses into the broken sentence “Fewer handovers  more robust,” so the required contrast is unintelligible at the punchline. Second, “the average customer feels improvement” is statistically wrong: the median moved; the mean is unreported and would be pulled by a much worse p90/p95. High fitness / low precision is otherwise well handled.

**Decision alternatives.** A–E are evaluated with advantages, risks, and conditions. A and B are correctly rejected. C vs E is reasoned, though E is described as needing the unvalidated predictive rule for triage. That conflates a coarse building-type split (house vs apartment / old building), which does not require the skip model, with the medium-risk predictive rule the prompt actually used. Geographic scope is internally inconsistent: immediate “risk-based routing at Address validation” reads national, while coordinator ownership is capped at “>50% of regions” with no basis for 50%.

**Value of information.** All of M1–M8 appear, each with a decision-impact claim. Ranking M4 first is arguable, not forced: the incomplete 207 vs 198 already points the same direction, whereas M6/M1 and M7 more directly change the *scope* of E and whether activation/permit deviations are a hard stop. Ranking M5 last is inconsistent with the recommendation to scale coordinator ownership; if ownership is the piece you keep, absence/resilience is high-VOI, not residual. Invented accuracy cutoffs (70%, 85%) are unlabeled as judgment.

**Logical flaws in the rollout design.** The measurement plan cannot support the gates attached to it. An 8-week window cannot credibly estimate 60-day post-billing cost, 30-day complaints, or a p95 of 45 days; requiring those thresholds for “4 consecutive weeks” makes the inconsistency explicit. The observation window itself is ignored: a 6-week pilot reports p95 = 62 days, which exceeds the named pilot length, so right-censoring / follow-up rules are a first-order threat to every tail, cost, and complaint comparison. A process-mining memo that never mentions this is incomplete. The precision/fitness gate is also poorly posed: fitness ≥ 0.85 is *below* both baseline (0.87) and pilot (0.94), so it does not encode “more controllable process.” Kitchen-sink “all must be met” thresholds hide trade-offs the rest of the memo insists exist.

**Other deductions.** “Proves,” “34% nationally,” average vs median, the garbled resilience line, handover/timestamp confusion, and an experiment too short for its own KPIs are enough to keep this well below excellence. The skeleton of a good memo is here; the execution is not tight enough to trust without revision.
