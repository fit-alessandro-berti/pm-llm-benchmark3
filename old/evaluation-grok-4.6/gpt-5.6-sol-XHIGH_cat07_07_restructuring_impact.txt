**8.2**

The answer is unusually complete and, on the organizational-design substance, often excellent. It follows the required heading structure, stays agnostic about the change, treats East as a distinct case, and refuses to treat average cycle time as a sufficient statistic. The opening arithmetic on listed handovers (343 → 359 per 100) and the wait-pressure index (≈588 → 722) is correct and is the right way to challenge management’s “fewer handovers” claim. The three scenarios, the leading/lagging split, the censoring/Simpson points, and the phased pilot with concurrent controls are all at a high professional standard.

It is not near-flawless. Under a strict reading the following problems are material.

**1. Section 7 treats SNA objects as dicts.**  
`discover_handover_of_work_network`, `discover_working_together_network`, and `discover_subcontracting_network` return NetworkX graphs, not `{edge: weight}` maps. `network.items()` therefore does not yield handover edges. The `per_100_cases` helper would not run as written. Calling this “pseudocode” and waving at version drift does not excuse a wrong object model. `filter_variants(..., variants=..., retain=True)` also mixes in the `filter_event_attribute_values` signature.

**2. Before/after unit mapping contaminates the comparison.**  
The same `resource_to_unit` map sends `N_Tech`/`S_Tech`/`E_Tech` to `"Technical Shared Service"` on the *before* log. That invents a hub that did not exist, flattens the local specialist structure you are trying to measure against, and biases the grouped handover comparison toward “little structural change.” After go-live those resource IDs may not even exist. Before and after need different role maps.

**3. `local_expertise_flag` is used as if it were an independent outcome.**  
If the flag is assigned because an embedded local specialist touched the case, it will fall mechanically after centralization. Treating that drop as a leading indicator of expertise loss is partly tautological. The answer never inspects how the field is generated.

**4. Optimistic scenario quietly rewrites the proposal.**  
Optimism is conditioned on protected local consultation, surge headroom, and separated Legal/Risk/QC lanes. Those are modifications, not the stated design (one shared-service queue, specialists also absorbing legal/risk triage and difficult QC, one supervisor removed). That is honest as a *modified* case, but it is not labeled as such in §2, so the optimistic forecast is not a forecast of the plan management actually tabled.

**5. Quantitative baselines are under-used.**  
Team volumes, utilizations, P90s, and rework rates are given and are used well for the 73% → 84%/88% utilization note. They are barely converted into scenario numbers (East 8.6d/22.8d/11.5% rework; Legal/Risk already at 91–93% facing 9 → 12 referrals per 100). The Legal/Risk capacity shock is noted qualitatively and should have been sized. Guardrails (20%, 1.25, +2pp) are “illustrative” and not derived from the baseline variation the text itself says should set them.

**6. Handover totals are cleaner than the tables allow.**  
The 343 vs 359 comparison is caveated, but still presented as the headline. Cross-team cover may already sit inside other rows; Customer Contact, Quality Check, Rework, and Close are absent from both tables; Bot_Eligibility is ignored. The direction (formal interfaces up, ad hoc cover down) is right; the implied precision is not.

**7. Pilot design over-claims identification.**  
Hash-of-case-ID allocation and a stepped-wedge fallback are the right ideas. Difference-in-differences is named and then not specified (unit, time grain, case-mix adjustment, clustered errors, multiple testing across the large segment grid). Four weeks of shadow routing is short relative to the 12–16 week live window justified by monthly cycles. Concurrent process drift is not treated as a confounder.

**8. Smaller unclarities.**  
HHI and betweenness appear in the indicator table with no definition or code. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` without a start timestamp is more likely to fail than to “approximate.” SLA limits are correctly not invented, but then the SLA code path is inert. Role-clarity “entropy” is undefined. Several mechanism-map cells are overloaded enough that the causal chain (action → mechanism → log signature) is harder to audit than it should be.

**What remains strong enough to keep the score well above a mid-range mark:** East vs North/South is carried through every section; fairness and learning are first-class, not add-ons; origin-team preservation is the correct antidote to post-centralization `team` overwrite; open-WIP and completion bias are handled; the recommendation is “modify and pilot,” not a disguised full rollout; management is explicitly told not to read early averages as success.

The piece reads like a strong design-review memo with a sketchy, partly incorrect analysis appendix. That is clearly above competent and clearly short of nearly flawless.
