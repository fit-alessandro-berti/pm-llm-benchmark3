**6.4**

The answer is conceptually strong and format-complete, but it is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it loses a lot of credit for arithmetic inconsistency, poorly documented contrasts, a primary design it never estimates, and a “conditionally causal” point estimate that its own uncertainty analysis does not support.

## What is actually good

The piece understands the scientific problem. Availability is not use; adopters were selected on weakness; the March desk, April routing, May complexity shock, and FTE moves are contemporaneous; CSAT is a selected monthly mean. Estimands separate ITT-like availability, intensity, median vs tail, rework/escalation, CSAT, dynamics, and selection-on-weakness. SUTVA and treatment-version issues are named. The threat table is complete. The recommendation (Delta next, Epsilon held out, queue-state data, hard p90/CSAT guardrails) is the right *kind* of decision: it tries to buy both operations and identification. The claim ladder correctly rejects “automation reduces cycle time by X%.”

That is a solid graduate memo. It is not a clean one.

## Material quantitative failures

**Beta’s late median is wrong.** The text defines late post as Jul–Aug, then reports Beta as “+1.9 h overall / –0.9 h late.” Jul–Aug Beta is \((48.5+45.0)/2=46.75\) against a Jan–Mar mean of \(45.9\), i.e. **+0.85 h**, not –0.9 h. –0.9 h is August alone. That is not a rounding issue; it is a window switch that makes the early-adopter late-post story look better than the defined windows allow.

**The headline DiD is not recovered from the numbers as written.** Never-treated Jan–Mar → Jul–Aug median +3.6 h is correct. Alpha overall post is –0.4 h and Beta overall post is +1.9 h. Those minus +3.6 are **–4.0 and –1.7**, not “–4.5 to –5.5 h.” The advertised range only appears if one silently changes windows (Jul–Aug for treated, or Apr–Aug for controls) and/or uses the erroneous Beta late figure. Gamma’s “similar” –3.5 to –4 h likewise uses a *different* implicit control window (Jan–Jun → Jul–Aug) that is never stated. Approximate arithmetic was allowed; **undocumented, inconsistent contrasts were not.**

**“Late-post recovery stronger for adopters” is not generally true.** Versus the May–Jun peak, Epsilon recovers about as much as Alpha and *more* than Beta. The relative-median result is Alpha-driven and calendar-window-dependent. Averaging Alpha and Beta as a single early-adopter ATT hides that.

**Intensity results are asserted, not shown.** Raw correlations and “near zero or modestly negative after adjustment” are not produced by the code and cannot be audited. For a section whose job is quantitative discipline, that is a miss.

## Identification logic vs what was actually done

The primary strategy is a staggered event-study / never-treated group-time ATT. That is a defensible choice. Then:

- The post-table justification is ~100 words against a required 150–250.
- Relative time is built and printed, then **never used in a model**.
- The fitted models are exactly the naïve TWFE specs the prompt told the author not to treat as primary (`automation + C(branch) + C(month)`), including the Goodman-Bacon contamination path in which April adopters become controls for Gamma.
- With five clusters, clustered OLS is theater; the warning is present, but then Section 10 still offers “approximately 4–5 hours” as conditionally causal.

So the identification section is better than the analysis. A strict grader treats that as a logical flaw, not a presentation quibble: the estimand and the estimator are not the same object.

Other specification nits: E5’s contrast is “availability or intensity,” which is not an estimand. E8 is honestly labeled descriptive. Synthetic control with two donors is correctly flagged. Interference via the shared fraud desk is named but then ignored when converting DiD into a causal hour count.

## Process interpretation

The mechanism table is the strongest part of the memo: fast-path substitution vs re-labeling, specialist/fraud-desk congestion, intensity as fidelity, load vs FTE, rework displacement, median/p90 divergence, survey selection. Expected signatures are mostly right.

What a process miner should have *also* said, and did not: rework and escalation are stepwise and often literally constant for months. These are coarse constructed rates, not monthly realizations. Automated fast path is already 4–6% when `automation=0`. Equal-weighted branch DiD ignores that Alpha is much larger. None of that is fatal; omitting it is another small cut in a section that asked for process-aware discipline.

## Uncertainty and overclaim

Sections 3 and 7 are appropriately pessimistic (power “extremely limited,” median confidence 35, p90 20, CSAT 15, ranges that include near-zero). Section 10 then promotes a 4–5 hour conditionally causal effect. That is internally inconsistent. With N=5, endogenous targeting, a common May shock, unmeasured desk waits, concurrent routing and staffing, and a DiD that is not even stably computed, **that point estimate is not identified under the assumptions they themselves list.** The safe claim in the median row of Section 7 is the right one; the ladder oversells it.

CSAT and p90 treatments are better: they refuse a clean effect. Escalation as a possible design feature rather than a failure is correctly handled.

## Code and reproducibility

The script is self-contained, validates the panel, prints trends without subplots, defines windows, clusters by branch, and prints the required warnings. That meets the letter of item 9.

It fails the spirit of “reproducible” for this memo: it does not compute never-treated contrasts, DiD, correlations, event-study coefficients, or the sensitivity bounds that Sections 5 and 7 rest on. Date filters compare timestamps to `"2026-01"` strings. There is no visualization. Causal caveats in comments are good; they do not repair the design/implementation gap.

## Rollout

`expand with an embedded evaluation` is the only serious option and is argued in-range (250–400). Holding Epsilon out is the right control choice (high complexity, more like the treated sites). Expanding Delta first is operationally conservative and **weak for external validity** if the policy-relevant effect is “what happens at historically weak branches.” Randomizing go-live month across two remaining sites is not a design; it is a coin flip with no power. The –2 h / +3 h decision rule is more precise than the current evidence can support on a 1-treated + 1-control sequel.

## Strict scoring

A 9–10 required internally consistent windows, arithmetic that matches the stated definitions, an estimator that implements the stated design, code that regenerates the numbers used in the argument, and a claim ladder no sharper than Section 7. This answer is well above a mediocre policy memo and well below that bar. The errors that matter are exactly the ones the rubric named: quantitative discipline, identification logic, and treatment of uncertainty.
