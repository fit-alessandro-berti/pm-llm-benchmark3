6.1

The write-up is complete, follows the required six-section format, uses the mandated conformance labels, and gets the central normative point right: A and B are unlawful shortcuts and cannot be rescued by a favorable forecast; a second-officer review is mandatory; re-screening is not a substitute for that review; SAR filing is not an execution path; the override is not currently enabled. Parallel information-gathering and P-901 spillover are treated as first-class issues. That is the skeleton of a strong answer. It is not close to flawless.

The ranking is driven by an unjustified time model. Action C states a second-officer review of 35–75 minutes plus 5–15 minutes to record/execute. S4 is described only as becoming *available* in 35–65 minutes. The answer treats S4’s subsequent review as ~5–15 minutes and therefore concludes that waiting for S4 is time-competitive with interrupting S2 (45–95 vs 40–90 minutes) and usually better because it spares P-901. Nothing in the case supports a dramatically shorter S4 review on a €480k, risk-72, unresolved-match file. The conservative and process-consistent assumption is a review of similar length after S4 arrives, i.e. on the order of 70–140 minutes end-to-end—well past the 90-minute deadline. Under that reading, Composite 1 is not the time-feasible conformant default; it is a “protect P-901, likely miss P-884” alternative. The rank-1/rank-2 order, the “5–45 minute median buffer,” and the claim that S4 is only marginally slower than S2 are therefore poorly grounded. The sensitivity note for a 65-minute S4 arrival does not repair this, because the error is in review duration, not only in wait time.

The primary plan’s triggers are also internally inconsistent and, in one case, directionally wrong. Mixing “S4 ETA slips” with “screening confirms the fuzzy match → interrupt P-901” does not follow. A confirming hit raises the value of a careful independent review or of a documented rejection (F); it does not create a reason to raid S2 and injure P-901. Section 2’s tree (S4 still decides, or lean toward F) contradicts Section 1 and Section 6 on this point.

Worse, Section 6 treats “a credible sanctions-list intersection beyond the fuzzy name” as a reason to *revisit the emergency override*. That inverts the control. A confirmed sanctions intersection is a reason to stop, reject, and consider SAR—not to seek a Rule 10 execution exception. Override is for a justified operational emergency when the payment is still believed legitimate and both director authorization and a real incident ID exist. Using a stronger hit as an override trigger is a domain error in the final recommendation.

Smaller defects compound this. Rule 10, if both conditions were actually met, *is* a justified exception to the normal approval path; saying a valid override “would not displace Rule 6” muddles the exception logic (the path is correctly left disabled on the facts). Benefit/harm cells are unevenly quantified. The 12-minute-stale risk score, non-transportability of observational predictions under intervention, and the 0.55–0.75 approval range are barely used. Composite labels and the confidence column mix plan-quality with outcome-uncertainty.

Net: the answer would survive as a competent compliance-aware ranking if the S4 review-time assumption and the two bad triggers were fixed. Under a strict bar those are not nits; they change which conformant plan is first and what observation should cause a rerank. High 6 is appropriate; 8+ is not.
