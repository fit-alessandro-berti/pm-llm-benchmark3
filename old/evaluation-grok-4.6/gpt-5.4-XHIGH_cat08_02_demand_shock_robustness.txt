**6.8**

The answer is a competent operations redesign: it follows the required structure, converts DFG counts into weekly rates, refuses several of the obviously local optimizations (I1, I3-as-default, I4-as-peak-redeploy, I9), and treats bottleneck migration as the central problem. It is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading there are numerical errors, at least one TOC/logic mistake, a capacity hole, and a dashboard that fails to manage the trade-offs the redesign itself creates.

**What it gets right.** Weekly normalization of the two DFGs is the strongest move in the piece. Technical-validation→medical-validation (488/week → 1,140/week), hold-for-specialist (36 → 203), and repeat-assay (143 → 420) correctly show that peak failure is a mix and loop problem, not a uniform volume problem. Rejecting a single Urgent-first rule, hard M1/M2 dedication, and batch-and-hold of Routine releases is consistent with the conflict hints and the variant data (Urgent bypass already falls 74% → 58%; M2 is the high-failure machine; release is not the constraint). Scenario triggers with on/off conditions, and residual risks that include harms created by the redesign, are the right shape of answer.

**Numerical and evidence slips.** “Pre-analytical exceptions nearly double” is wrong. Received-sample exceptions go from (760+500)/8 = 158/week to (780+420)/3 = 400/week, about 2.5×. The medically validated share of technical-validation output is stated as “about 50%” in peak; (3420+610)/7240 = 55.7%. That understatement weakens the author’s own mix-shift argument. S4 treats the 60h recollection edge as something late detection “creates.” That edge is mostly external return time; early triage starts the clock sooner and saves bench/assay capacity, it does not remove 60h.

**Logical flaw in S3.** The claim that I4 is “most dangerous” in a medical-validator shortage because it would “reduce the feed to medical validation at exactly the wrong moment” is backwards. Peak medical-validation already has a 22h median queue. The station is overfed. The real damage of pulling technical validators is aging *all* work at a 101% station and blocking the Technical-validation→Release path that still carries a large Routine share. In constraint terms, S3 is exactly when you should *not* maximize the feed into clinical sign-off. The author has the right ban on I4 and the wrong reason.

**S1 sequence fights the author’s own numbers.** After showing that specialist-review load grows ~5.6× while starts grow ~1.43×, the write-up still narrates a generic upstream-to-downstream volume cascade (courier → pre-an → prep → assay → validation). The distinctive bottleneck of a mix shift, as opposed to “more volume,” is the clinical path. Courier and pre-an matter, but they are not what makes S1 different from a scaled normal week.

**Redesign holes.** The “fast exception lane” for accession correction is asserted after I4 is banned. Peak accession is two people at 91% with a 14h median queue. Priority does not create capacity. The 4-week cross-training note in the source is the obvious way to build *option* value in the normal period (accession 62%, technical validation 76%) and use it only when technical validation has slack; that distinction is mentioned in one clause and never made operational.

Worse, technical validation at **101%** utilization and 16h peak queue has no intervention except “do not steal from it.” Every assayed case hits this station. A robust package that leaves the only over-100% pool untouched is incomplete. There is no inbound cross-training, no simplification of the Technical-validation→Release rule set, no WIP cap in front of technical validation.

Design parameters stay verbal: “micro-slots,” “maximum wait cap,” “supervised repeat lane.” Without slot counts, wait caps, or a stated Urgent-share breaking point, the three-lane policy is a slogan. S2’s instruction to “stop concentrating Routine work on M2” switches off a dedication rule the robust package never adopted.

**Dashboard does not control the designed trade-off.** Urgent p90 amber at >18h is already the contractual breach; amber should sit *before* 18h, red at breach. There is no Routine p90/median (72h target) and no Complex-panel tail metric, even though section 6 explicitly accepts Routine-tail damage and spends a great deal of design effort on Complex-panel aging. You cannot govern a class-sacrifice policy without measuring the sacrificed class. Oldest-Urgent-waiting is a good leading indicator; it does not replace class-level outcomes.

**Trade-off table is directionally right and quantitatively empty.** Every effect is “better / slight / helpful / neutral.” Next-bottleneck and monitoring columns are the useful ones. Implementation burden collapses to Medium/Medium-high/High and does not discriminate.

**Smaller nits.** Repeat-after-failure is the worst tail in both periods (peak median 145h, p90 240h) and is only handled as a machine-quality loop, not as a case class. Combined S2+S3 is named as a residual risk and never given a policy. I5 is sold as “robust” when it mainly covers S1/S3. Physical “metering” of courier dumps into pre-an is left unspecified (samples have already arrived). Cancel rate rising 3.1% → 5.5% is unused.

The piece would be in the high 8s if the weekly-rate work were paired with a coherent constraint policy, a real capacity story for accession and technical validation, accurate exception multiples, and a dashboard that watches every class the policy is willing to hurt. It is not there.
