**7.4**

The answer is structurally complete, numerically accurate, and correctly refuses the two main failure modes (raw-gap = discrimination; case-mix = fully legitimating). Under a strict standard it is still not near-flawless. The main problems are incomplete use of the evidence, a few conceptual slippages, and a process-mining plan that is broader than it is precise.

**What is strong.** All eight required headings are present and on-topic. Section 1 separates raw parity from adjusted disparity and does not stop at approvals. The support-step argument is the right center of gravity: Language Support Contact and Accommodation Review are treated as rights-preserving *and* as possible sources of delay, handover, and specialist-queue burden. Section 2’s insistence on not blindly controlling away language need and accommodation request is the single best analytical move in the piece. Risk scores and documentation completeness are treated as potentially contaminated, not as neutral covariates. Mitigations keep the support steps and target the delay penalty. The bottom line is calibrated and does not overclaim intentional discrimination.

**Section 3 underuses the data it already has.** Top variants are treated as if they *are* the phenomena. Group B’s language-need share is 57% but V4 is 24%; Group C’s accommodation-request share is 62% but V5 is 31%; appeal rates are 13/10/16% while V6 is 8/5/13%. Those gaps are compatible with other variants containing the same activities, with under-provision of mandated support, or with both. A fairness assessment of accessibility *must* measure activity-level coverage (Language Support Contact among language-need cases; Accommodation Review among accommodation-request cases; Appeal Intake among denied cases), not only the share of one named path. The answer never does this. The same flattening happens for Group D: 51% high-risk versus 28% on V3 is left uninterpreted.

A related miss: reopen/rework rates (11/22/24/20%) are named in Section 1 and then almost dropped. That is one of the required fairness dimensions, and it is more diagnostic of procedural failure than approval rates. There is also no back-of-envelope decomposition of the residual +2.6 / +4.1 day gaps into path mix versus within-path waits versus the already-reported 3.8-day and 4.7-day support queues. Without that, “adjusted timeliness disparity” remains a black box.

**Appeals are partly mechanical and are treated as if they were not.** Lower approval produces more appeals even if notices and decisions are equally good. Group D’s adjusted appeal gap (+5.7 pp) sits next to a −3.1 pp approval gap; some of the appeal residual is composition, not a separate fairness finding. The answer lists “more denials” as one possibility among many and never disciplines that possibility.

**A few conceptual unclarities.** “Uncertainty” is listed as a fourth *kind* of finding parallel to disparity, legitimate routing, and unfair burden. It is an epistemic status, not a substantive category; mixing them blurs the identification strategy. The bottom line then says procedural fairness is “indeterminate with respect to intentional discrimination.” Intent is not the question that was asked. The support-capacity and queue-penalty evidence is already enough to say the process is *not fully procedurally fair*, while remaining agnostic about animus or biased individual decisions. Hedging the whole fairness judgment behind “indeterminate” is slightly misaligned with the evidence the answer itself assembled.

Smaller slips: common-support and mediator/collider issues are mentioned but not operationalized (no statement of which variables are pre-treatment, which are post-intake, which would be colliders). Intersectionality is absent (language-need cases that also request accommodation; Group B in the two high-handover regions). Channel is discussed as a delay mechanism but not as a possibly endogenous choice. Resource “overload” is taken from the scenario note rather than defined from the log.

**Section 7 is a good inventory, not a tight identification plan.** It touches essentially every listed PM4Py function, which meets the prompt. Several details are loose even as pseudocode. `filter_paths_performance` is called with a two-string list rather than a path tuple / list of tuples. `get_all_case_durations` is never joined back onto `case_df`, so the later stratified tables cannot actually use cycle time. `insert_case_service_waiting_time` / `get_service_time` are invoked with a caveat, but the plan then treats inter-event elapsed time as if the waiting-time decomposition were identified. Log-skeleton and DECLARE are discovered and then unused; the useful constraint checks are handwritten and do not report conformance against a discovered model. Placeholder resource IDs (`bilingual_1`, …) are flagged, but there is no step for *discovering* the specialist pool from activity–resource co-occurrence (which `discover_organizational_roles` and activity-based similarity are for). There is no difference-DFG or statistical comparison of performance DFGs across groups. The code is a tour of the API, not a procedure that would actually estimate the quantities Section 2 promised.

**Mitigations are comprehensive but unranked.** The table is better than a list, and the “do not remove support steps” principle is correct. It still does not say what to do first given scarce budget, nor how to test whether a fix worked (e.g., pre-register the support-step wait and coverage metrics, not only adjusted approval). Side effects are named but mostly generic.

**Net.** This is a strong exam answer: right framing, right constraints, right residual concerns, almost no numerical error. It is not a nearly flawless assessment. The process-mining evidence is read too coarsely (variants as proxies for coverage), rework and wait decomposition are under-analyzed, appeals are not separated from denial composition, the PM4Py plan is wide rather than identified, and the final verdict is slightly more agnostic than the support-queue evidence warrants. Those are enough, under a strict rubric, to keep the score well below the top band.
