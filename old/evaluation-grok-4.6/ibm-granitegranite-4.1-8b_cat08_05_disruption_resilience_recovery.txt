**5.6**

The answer looks complete and well-structured, but it is not a reliable reading of the case. Several claims are factually wrong, several labels contradict the problem’s own constraints, and the resilience / noncompliance distinction—the core of the question—is repeatedly blurred.

**Process-mining and metric errors**

T0 conflates fitness with the share of fitting traces: it reports “96% of traces fitting (fitness 0.96).” The source separates them (fitness 0.96, 91% fitting traces). T2–T4 state the two figures correctly, so this is not a rounding slip; it is a concept error.

T2 attributions are backwards. Traceability exceptions (9.5%) are assigned to C (skip authorization). Authorization gaps and scan/POD/quality gaps are different controls; the DFG and the move-on-model list point to dispatch-without-scan, missing quality/release, missing scan, and missing POD. Missing temperature documentation (4.8%) is assigned to E (manual pick list); the case text ties that leak to F and to missing quality/release records. Dual-control missing (2.9%) is assigned to F as if F caused it. F is the control that *limited* that failure.

Worse, F’s “evidence of resilience” is written as “reduced controlled-substance errors (2.9% vs baseline 0.2%).” That comparison shows a ~15× *increase*. Relative to an uncontrolled state F may have helped; the numbers cited do not show that.

T3 is described as cycle times “close to baseline.” Hospital-critical is close (9h vs 8h). Pharmacy-standard is not (38h vs 23h). Wholesale-bulk is not (84h vs 36h; p90 160h vs 72h). Calling T4 “operational metrics stable” repeats the mistake: wholesale median is still 58h vs 36h, p90 120h vs 72h, traceability 2.2% vs 0.8%, rework 5.8% vs 3.5%. That is incomplete recovery plus a hidden compliance tail, not stability.

Baseline percentages are sloppily rounded (0.8/3.5/2.1 → 1/4/2). Invented figures appear as if measured: 2-hour snapshot staleness, 96% baseline hospital fill, “HL7-tiered risk.” HL7 is a messaging standard, not a triage scheme.

**Resilience vs concealed noncompliance (section 3 is the weakest part)**

The prompt forbids treating after-the-fact audit remediation as equivalent to real-time compliance, and forbids treating throughput as resilience when critical controls disappear. The answer violates both.

L is listed as “true resilience.” L is debt collection after operational close. The stabilization DFG makes the concealment quantitative: 2,300 cases go Invoice close → Audit remediation versus 24,800 Invoice close → END, while cycle-time metrics already look recovered. That is the hidden-work finding. Calling it resilience collapses the distinction the grader was told to reward.

C is given “resilience in preserving revenue / throughput” in the table, then retired as a risky shortcut. Those two columns cannot both stand under the stated rule.

I is filed as a risky shortcut. I is load-shedding: it is why hospital fill stayed 91% while pharmacy fill fell to 61%. That is a real fairness and service-cost problem, not concealed noncompliance. Mislabeling it muddies the exact contrast the question asks for.

B is marked **Retire**. During a WMS outage, trained offline authorization is one of the few *control-preserving* adaptations. The case says B “preserved control quality but consumed scarce compliance capacity.” Retiring it and hoping standing authorizations will exist *in the system that is down* is operationally incoherent. Section 4 then puts B back into the recovery path. Keep/modify/retire is therefore not a stable judgment.

K is “true resilience.” It is necessary reconciliation after improvisation, not resilience during the shock.

A, C, G, and the hospital-versus-pharmacy split are handled more reasonably. That is not enough to salvage the taxonomy.

**Incomplete use of the DFG and segments**

Path volumes are mostly unused. T2 has a clear split: Manual authorization 2,500 / Skip 1,600 / Hospital fast lane 1,000; Manual pack → Dispatch without scan 1,800 versus Temporary release 1,650; 960 deferred into backlog with 320 cancels. Stabilization has 1,400 manual-reservation reconciles of which 300 cancel, and compliance holds roughly double versus baseline. None of this is used to weight which adaptations actually dominated.

Recall-sensitive products are in the process context and never appear again. Cold-chain is mentioned, but not as a hard contemporaneous control. Pharmacy fill 61%—the central fairness fact—is almost invisible next to the 91% hospital figure.

Move-on-log vs move-on-model vs temporal-profile deviations are listed once in T2 and then dropped. The long, variable Dispatch-without-scan → Phone POD delay and Backlog → Manual pick exceeding p95 are not interpreted.

**Recovery model (structurally present, substantively thin)**

Required headings exist: trigger, triage, evidence, backlog, reconciliation, audit loop. Missing or wrong content:

- No distinct playbook for T3 (intake up, inventory sync and scanning unreliable). The incident’s hardest phase after the outage is hybrid unreliability, not a binary “WMS down.”
- Quality/release check—the normative gate whose absence is a named move-on-model deviation—never re-enters the design.
- No non-negotiable floors: dual-control for controlled substances, contemporaneous temperature evidence for cold-chain, extra custody for recall-sensitive SKUs. Hospital-critical and controlled are lumped as one “high” bucket.
- Evidence capture “recorded in ERP” while the ERP/WMS is the failed system.
- G is retired with no specified dispatch proof that works when scanners are dead.
- B and I are used in the model after being retired / condemned.

The tree is a restatement of A–L with nicer labels, not a redesigned control regime.

**Indicators and recommendations**

Several requested dimensions appear, but leading indicators are almost all drills, not in-incident signals (snapshot age, % of dispatches with dual-control *before* handoff, audit-queue size/age, temperature-record completeness, conformance/fitness). There is no audit-queue indicator despite the hidden-work theme. Control-completeness is again blamed on C and G. Fairness is a survey score instead of the observed fill-rate and cancellation split.

Recommendations are generic tooling (standing auth, hourly snapshots, QR codes, courier app). They do not prioritize: never ship controlled or cold-chain without contemporaneous evidence; do not declare recovery while the remediation queue is open; protect a pharmacy floor so “hospital first” does not become 61% fill. Residual risks are boilerplate.

**Management statement**

Readable and decision-shaped, but too soft. It accepts “temporary lightweight shortcuts” without stating the one line the data support: accept hospital priority and capacity shedding; do not accept missing authorization, dual-control, temperature, or dispatch identity for regulated goods; do not treat median cycle time as compliance recovery. The “what would change our mind” tests (two-hour restore, six-month standing-auth pilot) are disconnected from the incident evidence already in hand (9.5% traceability exceptions, 4.8% missing temperature records, 2.9% missing dual-control, 2,300-case audit tail).

**Net**

Format compliance and a few correct calls (C and G as unacceptable, A as mission-aligned but leaky, hidden T4 workload mentioned) keep this out of the failure band. Material misreads, a violated resilience definition, an unsafe “retire B,” and a recovery model that omits the actual control floors keep it far from a high score. Under a strict reading it is a polished but unreliable advisory note.
