**6.2**

The answer is complete, well structured, and conceptually stronger than most attempts: it separates timing, routing, and decision-policy interventions; keeps four-eyes and final-compliance rules; treats queues as forecasts; distinguishes readiness from completion; and sees that a local win can displace other cases. Those are the intended hard points. They are not enough. Under a strict reading the write-up has several feasibility and consistency failures that a process-mining reviewer should not forgive.

**Process-feasibility errors.** The K-104 path puts `PrelimCompliance(C1)` on Monday morning in parallel with identity and documents. C1 is a singleton with a 18–20 h backlog. That start time is queue-jumping, not a reserved *later* slot, and it contradicts the answer’s own C7. The derivation never explains *when* C1 actually reads the file without pre-empting the two urgent Monday cases. “Reserve a provisional slot after Assessment” and “C1 reads evidence as it lands” are different mechanisms; the table blends them. For K-411 the path is labeled concurrent (`‖`) while Identity is 11:30–13:20 and Document Check is ~13:35–18:05. Those intervals do not overlap. An answer that lectures on false concurrency cannot publish a non-overlapping “parallel” path.

**Internal contradictions.** Section 2 allows K-104 Decision as early as Tue 22:00; C9 and the K-104 derivation correctly put M1 in business hours (Wed morning). Section 4 then says 100 K-411-like cases hit Assessment “half a day earlier”; the case analysis was ~1.4 h of readiness and 0–2 h of completion. Net C1 demand is “≈ neutral per case” in the capacity table and “300–600 h plus prelims” in the rollout paragraph. The 300–600 h is baseline review load, not incremental intervention load; reservations reorder a scarce server, they do not create that demand. The prose says this once and then argues as if it had not.

**Weak or wrong causal steps.** D2’s short queue is attributed to “overflow-sized demand.” The snapshots show the opposite: D1 is the congested specialist, D2 is the slack generalist. K-219’s Mon 22:30 D2 start applies the Mon 16:00 5 h forecast from originals receipt (17:30) as if no work cleared in between; 16:00+5 h is 21:00, and a working D2 would have burned part of that queue. Overnight D2 is inferred from D1’s night work on a different, complex file. That assumption carries the −10 to −18 h no-rework claim. A2’s Tuesday 08:00 queue is 6 h, yet Assessment is started as early as 11:00. K-337’s Friday 15:30–21:00 compliance review is possible on the clock and poorly stress-tested: a singleton reviewer may not open a harder-than-usual file at 15:30 Friday, which would push Decision later and shrink the advertised two-day gain.

**Calibration and precision.** Ranges are used, which is right, but several ranges sit on the optimistic side of the author’s own constraints (K-104 Decision, K-219 Assessment, K-411 concurrency). The 20–40 % D2 handwriting-rework rate is invented; labeling it an assumption does not make the “expected value near neutral to mildly positive” claim more than a guess. Freezing K-104 Assessment at the factual Tue 15:10–19:10 is a defensible minimal-change choice, but then the text still talks as if the reserved C1 slot is “just after the predicted Assessment finish” while C1’s calendar is already full.

**What still works.** K-337 is correctly treated as a decision-policy change, not a time cut, and the C1-issued RMI veto is identified. Weekend/M1-window effects are handled. The 100-case discussion hits reservation hoarding, D1/D2 substitution, rework, Assessment bunching, external/weekend delay, and timestamp fiction. The ranking does not collapse to cycle time. Pilots and the eight labeled assumptions match the spec.

The piece reads like a strong draft that was not checked against its own numbers or resource exclusivity. That is more than “minor.” It is not a 4: the normative skeleton and interference story are real. It is nowhere near a 9. **6.2** is already generous relative to a nearly flawless bar.
