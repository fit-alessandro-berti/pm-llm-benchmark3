## Material problems

**1. Classification is described as a routing prerequisite, then ignored in every portfolio.**  
Section 2 says A1/A2 shape KYC path, collateral path, document checklists, High-exposure controls, and contract rules. Classify has 8% errors that “misroute cases.” A5 is then ranked first and recommended first-wave, with an explicit dependency on product/exposure data—and no A2, no mandatory human classification check, and no rule for what happens when the 8% misroutes generate the wrong checklist. That is a broken dependency chain, not a minor omission. Automating document nudges on a dirty classifier is exactly the kind of encoded-control failure the brief warns about.

**2. The recommended four underweight the two largest delay pools, and the ranking looks reverse-engineered.**  
Median wait × frequency (rough inventory): signing ~321k case-hours, credit-analysis wait ~249k, financial review ~166k, contract ~167k, external valuation ~136k, packaging ~101k, normalization ~104k, entity mismatch ~54k. The answer repeatedly elevates A4’s 31-hour wait (correct as a *conditional* critical-path point) while ranking A7 sixth and A10 seventh, then dropping both from year one. Some of that is defensible—valuation is external, signing is partly customer-side—but the write-up never shows it did this comparison. The matrix then places the recommended four in ranks 1–4. That is too tidy. A7’s 58-hour sync point and A10’s 44-hour / 21% reminder loop deserved a harder quantitative fight with A9, not a narrative substitution.

**3. A9 is overloaded with controls it does not naturally own.**  
Four-eyes applies to *credit analysis vs committee packaging*, and only in High-exposure SME (5.8% at month-end). A9 is contract generation. The answer keeps parking four-eyes inside A9’s workflow, Phase 2 metrics, and “related packaging controls,” while admitting A8 would be needed later. That is a control-design smear. A9 *can* hard-gate KYC-before-signing and decision-to-contract match; it cannot honestly be sold as the four-eyes control. The compliance mandate could also have been met by treating A4 as the control-strengthening module and keeping A7 in the bottleneck set. That alternative is never seriously scored.

**4. A6’s downstream-bottleneck rating misreads the resource note.**  
The brief says clean inflow will overload analysts *unless normalization improves*. A6 *is* that improvement: 5,200 × 75 minutes is on the order of three analyst-FTE of transcription load. The matrix still scores A6 as creating credit-analysis overload. Section 4 is more careful (“time is released… credit analysis may become the next bottleneck”), so the piece disagrees with itself. The interesting risk is A5 without A6, or A1+A5 without capacity management—not A6 as a primary bottleneck generator.

## Structural and coverage gaps

**Segments and variants are flattened.** Committee packaging and decision are 3,900 cases; credit analysis is 8,900; contract preparation is 7,600. A large share of volume never hits the committee sync point. High-exposure vs Micro vs SME, and Equipment (71% collateral) vs Working-capital (18%), should have changed expected effects and possibly module mix. Section 4’s cycle-time claims are mostly process-wide. The 380 `Resolve entity mismatch → Contract preparation` edge and the 98% “register is position 2” fact are unused.

**A2, A3, A8, A12 rankings are weakly justified relative to their risks.** A3 sits above A7 and A12 despite “very high” false-negative severity and a 3-FTE specialist bottleneck already concentrated on two seniors. Putting a KYC pre-classifier in the top half of a first-year list, even with governance caveats, is aggressive. A8 is correctly delayed for GIGO, but the 26-hour packaging wait is then barely priced. A12 is the only candidate that systematically exploits the 650/510 remediation loop and “policy ambiguity,” yet it is ranked eighth and absent from the recommended set with little more than “later if exceptions remain.”

**RM friction is acknowledged, then the #1 pick is the customer-friction module.** Relationship managers already cause incomplete-document loops and “will resist changes that increase customer friction.” A5 can be designed as RM-helpful, and the answer says so, but first-year suitability is simply “High.” That is too clean given the political constraint the brief actually stated.

**Phase 3 quietly introduces a fifth module.** The hedge (“if the cap is strict, prepare A7 for year two”) is there, but the rollout still reads as a five-module year. For a constraint that was explicit, this is sloppy.
