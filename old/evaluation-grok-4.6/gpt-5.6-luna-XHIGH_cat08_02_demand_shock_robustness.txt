**7.5**

The answer is a strong, format-compliant operations design. It uses the DFG, queues, variants, and conflict hints as evidence, refuses blanket I1/I2/I3/I9, and keeps mix, WIP, and bottleneck migration at the center. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, several factual, logical, and design gaps keep it well below the top band.

**What is actually good.** Scenarios A–D match the required axes (mix, machines, medical/specialist, courier), and E is a legitimate compound test. Bottleneck-migration is the best section: peak 96% pre-analytical with the 8530/780/420 split, batch-wait 6→14h and 9→22h, urgent bypass falling 74%→58%, assay 98% and technical validation 101%, hold-for-specialist 18/48h, and the I3/I4/I10 side effects are all used as causal material rather than decoration. The robust package is a control system (class lanes, SLA-slack scoring, WIP caps, conditional batching, gated I4, selective I10), not “add staff everywhere.” Policies have on/off triggers. The table has the required columns and honest next-bottleneck cells. Residual risks include redesign-induced starvation and order-level logs hiding tube/assay sync. The dashboard separates leading from lagging and warns against average-only views.

**Factual and logical defects.** The specialist-hold comparison is wrong in the way that matters. 610 vs 290 over 3 vs 8 weeks is about **5.6× on a weekly basis**, not “more than twice … after adjusting for the shorter period.” That is a process-mining misread, not a rounding slip. Scenario C then contradicts itself: technical validation cannot “appear to improve because fewer cases leave it” **and** have “its output accumulate in front of Medical validation.” Those are opposite flow regimes. Recollection → receive is 42h/60h; any Urgent case that enters that loop has already burned the 18h contract, so I6 cannot “protect” Urgent SLA for defective samples unless the clock resets—an assumption never stated. Peak DFG closure is ignored: 9900 starts vs 8040+540 ends leaves a large open set; that is direct evidence of WIP accumulation and should have grounded the cap policy.

**Design is architecture without sizes.** “A bounded fraction” of prep/machine/medical capacity for Urgent **and** Complex-panel is not a redesign that can be shown to remain acceptable. At peak those classes are ~49% of volume and Complex-panel is 3.1× medical and 2.4× specialist; reserving for both is exactly the simultaneous-priority error the prompt forbids, unless the fraction is specified and stress-tested. No reservation %, WIP cap, or even a worked example shows Urgent staying near 18h or Routine near 72h under A or E. Repeat-after-failure as a fourth lane with a quality hold is plausible for M2/night clustering, but those cases already have p90 132h/240h; the answer never faces the risk that the lane makes the worst tail worse. I5/I10 still inject capacity; making them conditional does not estimate whether the remaining internal system is viable when they are off.

**Instruction nits that add up.** The “minimum” dashboard is a large control-room catalogue, not a few leading indicators. Several unused facts that should have constrained the design are missing: 4-week cross-train lead time (I4 is not available mid-wave), registration error after 9h shifts, overall rework 7.5%→11.8%, and 22%→54% of cases with a queue >12h. Triggers (27%, 18%, 8% repeats, 25% in two hours, 6h slack) are mostly invented bands sitting near the peak summaries rather than derived from the logs. Almost every candidate intervention survives in conditional form; robustness is bought with operating-model complexity, and implementation sequencing is not designed.

**Net.** This is competent, evidence-aware process reasoning with the right refusal to optimize the historical mean. It is not a tight, sized, internally consistent redesign. The specialist-rate error, the Scenario C contradiction, the unanalyzed recollection-vs-Urgent-SLA clash, and the unsized dual reservation are enough to bar any score in the high 8s–10s.
