**6.4**

The write-up is structurally complete and gets the main causal story right: March is mostly label/provenance drift; May onward is real rework and delay; staffing and the July priority rule mainly show up in queues, not Assess service time; October is seasonal case mix; December is mixed recovery. That is the analysis the rubric is looking for. It is not close to flawless.

**Format and coverage.** All ten required headings appear, with the specified tables and (barely) compliant word counts. Six timeline intervals is the minimum, and skipping September is a real miss: supplier recovery is a dated event with a visible drop in `supplier_z_pct` and rework, and it is needed to separate transient disruption from persistence.

**Quantitative errors.** These are the largest deductions. The claimed Manhattan distance of 48 for September is wrong (January vs September on the seven given rates is 28). October is 64 and is the true peak among supplied months; calling July and September the peaks is invented precision, which the prompt forbade. The Part A distance code also drops the first DFG column after `set_index` (`iloc[:,1:]`) while subtracting a 7-edge baseline, so the reproducible artifact would not match the prose. The change-point rule applies a threshold of 5 to `mapped_model_fitness` (which lives in \([0,1]\)), so it cannot flag mapped-fitness breaks. Rework’s cycle-time share is inconsistent with the author’s own “1–2 days per loop” assumption: a +17 pp rework increase at 24–48 h per loop is roughly 4–8 h, not 15–20% of a +17.5 h median shift.

**Process-mining mistakes.** Standard control-flow fitness does not use resource identifiers. Treating missing resources as a cause of the raw-fitness collapse is incorrect; the March drop is explained by the eligibility relabel and extra `Open Case` events. Logging change points at 2026-09-10 and 2026-12-05 are not logging events. June is described as the rework peak; July is higher (29%). Senior Assessment in October is treated as seasonal population drift only, despite the prompt’s warning that the activity was historically recorded inconsistently and that a priority rule began in July.

**PM4Py plan.** Required surface coverage is there; the API is not faithful. `pm4py.discover_declarative` and `pm4py.conformance_check_alignments` are not the manual names (`discover_declare`, `fitness_alignments` / `conformance_diagnostics_alignments`). `discover_temporal_profile` is given `case_id`/`timestamp` kwargs it does not take. `pm4py.Log` is not a type. Monthly DFG construction hard-codes `activity_name` instead of `concept:name` after `format_dataframe`. Label mapping and duplicate-`Open Case` removal — the actual normalization used in the study — are never implemented. `compare_raw_normalized_mappings` depends on a nonexistent `baseline.xes`.

**Causal and monitoring design.** Interaction and aggregate masking are acknowledged, which is good. Confidence is still too high and too lumped: May–July is one 80% blob instead of separable supplier (rework), staffing (queue), and priority (redistribution) signatures; December at 90% ignores the prompt’s own warning that load and interventions coincide. The primary monitoring population (standard, non-Z) conditions on the two mix variables that are drifting. Segmenting on queue-wait quartile conditions on an outcome. Decision rule 4 requires a complexity shift *without* volume change and would miss the actual October episode. Rule 6’s p90/median > 2× is already true in January (69/31.5 ≈ 2.2), so it is not a drift detector.

**Net.** Competent diagnostic narrative and usable monitor list, undermined by a fabricated DFG number, broken analysis code, several PM4Py/API errors, and a handful of process-mining misconceptions. Under a strict reading that is a mid-6, not an 8.
