The answer has the required skeleton and some of the right vocabulary (bottleneck migration, paired interventions, leading vs lagging metrics). Under a strict reading it is not a strong process-redesign. It repeatedly misreads the log, inverts stated intervention effects, and then contradicts its own package. The markdown looks disciplined; the reasoning is not.

**1. Stress scenarios — incomplete isolation, one invented “disruption” is an intervention, and a clear data error**

The four required disruption *types* appear, but they are mashed rather than varied. Scenario 1 stacks mix shift + courier compression + medical-validator absence + M2 failure. That prevents the reader from seeing which stress moves which bottleneck. Scenario 2 and 4 then restack equipment, staff, and courier again. Variation by case mix is asserted with round percentages that are not tied back to the 8-week / 3-week summaries.

Worse: Scenario 4 lists “1 technical validator cross-trained to accession correction” as a *disruption*. That is I4, a candidate intervention. Treating a design choice as an exogenous shock is a category error.

Scenario 3 states “20% of Complex-panel cases require specialist review (vs. 13% normal).” The 13% figure is the *normal Complex-panel case mix*, not a specialist-review rate. In the normal DFG, Hold for specialist review is 290 / 18,400 ≈ 1.6% of cases (610 / 9,900 ≈ 6.2% in peak). Complex-panel cases are 2.4× more likely to enter that hold, not “13% specialist review.” This is a process-mining miss, not a rounding issue.

**2. Bottleneck-migration analysis — right frame, several inverted or incoherent chains**

This section is where the answer should earn its score. It does not.

- **I5 is applied to the wrong station.** In Scenario 1 the primary bottleneck is pre-analytical check; the proposed relief is “Add evening shift (I5).” I5 is a temporary *evening medical-validation* shift. It does not destress pre-analytical check. The claimed migration (pre-analytical → medical validation via I5) is therefore false. Relieving pre-analytical would more plausibly load preparation, batching, and M1/M2 — which the peak table already shows at 89% / 98% with 11h / 18h queues.

- **I4 is inverted.** Conflict hint and intervention text are explicit: I4 *relieves accession correction* and *risks a technical-validation shortage*. Scenario 2 says the opposite: I4 makes the accession-correction queue grow 14h → 20h. The 20h figure is invented. In Section 5 the same inversion reappears as “next bottleneck: accession correction queue grows.” Section 4 / Scenario 4 briefly treats I4 as creating a validation bottleneck. The document does not have a stable model of I4.

- **Scenario 2’s machine logic is internally broken.** If M1 is out, every case is on M2. The sentence “Routine cases queue for M1 (but M1 is out)” is incoherent. The interesting migration is: all volume + Urgent bypass fragmentation + M2’s higher failure rate → repeat-assay loop (peak: Assay run → Repeat assay 1,260; Repeat → Assay 1,080) → technical validation already at 101% / 16h. That chain is not derived.

- **DFG edges are used as decoration, not evidence.** “780 9730 cases” is not an analysis of recollection. Normal recollection from pre-analytical is 760 / 18,120 ≈ 4.2%; peak is 780 / 9,730 ≈ 8.0%. Request recollection → Cancel also jumps (290 → 370) on a smaller base. Those *rates* are the mining evidence. Raw edge counts without denominators are misleading.

- **3.1× and 2.4× are swapped.** Variant text: Complex-panel is 3.1× more likely to need *medical validation* and 2.4× more likely to enter *specialist hold*. Scenario 3 applies 3.1× to specialist review.

- **“120 Complex-panel cases held for review” is the wrong edge.** 120 is peak Repeat assay → Medical validation. Hold for specialist review is 610 (560 onward to medical validation). Assay capacity is not “wasted” by a hold after the assay; the hold sits *after* technical validation. The WIP/aging problem is downstream of the machine, not a machine-block.

- **I5 “increases WIP for Routine”** is weakly argued. The dominant peak path is still Technical validation → Release result (4,050) versus Technical → Medical (3,420). Routine often never sees a medical validator. Adding evening medical capacity does not, by itself, inflate Routine WIP.

Several later numbers (recollections 1,200, repeats 1,500, validation 3.5h → 8h) are free inventions. That is not “grounded in the provided logs.”

**3. Robust package — violates the problem’s own constraints and contradicts later sections**

The brief forbids (i) optimizing only the average, (ii) prioritizing every case type at once, and (iii) recommending an intervention without the downstream overload. The “core, all-scenarios” package fails all three.

- **I1 as always-on is the exact anti-pattern in the conflict hints.** Urgent-first at prep and assay helps Urgent and starves Routine *and* Complex-panel, raising system WIP and fragmenting machines. Pairing it with I6 does not cancel that. I6 reduces bad-sample loops; it does not create capacity for two other case classes. Calling I1 “robust” is a design error, not a wording issue.

- **I3 + I2 as a standing pair is likely anti-robust on the given variants.** M2 already has the higher failure rate; repeats cluster after night shifts *and M2 usage*. Sending *all* Routine to M2 *and* doubling Routine batch size from 12 to 24 concentrates the failure-prone work into larger lots. Peak Repeat-after-failure is already 9% with p90 240h. This package can raise repeats and then reload technical validation (101%) and medical validation. The “use I4 if M2 fails” fallback is nonsensical: cross-training validators does not replace an assay line.

- **I5 + I10 cannot be both standing policy (Section 3) and trigger-gated (Section 4).** That is an internal contradiction. Always-on I10 also adds a 12h transport delay to Complex-panel as a *structural* wait. Complex-panel median is already 64h / 111h and p90 118h / 210h. A standing 12h tax is not a robustness feature; it is a peak overflow valve. The assumption “partner can take 20% of Complex-panel without violating Urgent SLAs” confuses case types: I10 is Complex-panel overflow, not Urgent.

- **I8 is the only clearly structural, mix-agnostic idea**, and even here the answer invents “60% digital” and “68% → 50% utilization.” The real conflict hint — I8 unloads registration and *exposes* pre-analytical (96%, 9.5h) and M1/M2 (98%, 18h) — is underplayed. Registration is 68% / 85% with 0.7h / 2.8h queues; it is not the peak constraint.

- **I7 is missing from the redesign** despite courier compression being a required scenario and courier intake moving from 1.0h to 7.5h with 10:00 / 17:00 spikes. I7 is one of the few interventions that destresses intake → pre-analytical → prep *without* picking a winner among case types. Omitting it while making I1 permanent is the wrong robustness philosophy.

- **Accession correction (2 people, 91%, 14h peak)** is the station that unblocks many cases and sits on the Urgent 18h clock. Leaving I4 out of the standing package, then misunderstanding I4, leaves the tightest scarce skill under-designed. Four-week cross-training also cannot be an *in-wave* lever; if it is used at all it must be pre-positioned. That timing constraint is not treated.

**4. Scenario-specific policies — some usable triggers, but they fight the “always-on” package and one policy endangers the Urgent SLA**

“Outsource when specialist queue > 12h and Complex-panel mix > 25%” is a reasonable shape. So is suspending large Routine batches when a machine is above ~95% and batch wait > 10h.

But:
- I10 cannot be always-on *and* gated.
- I1 cannot be always-on *and* “switched to Urgent-first only” in an outage.
- “Prioritize Complex-panel over Routine in medical validation” when specialist queue > 18h ignores that Urgent also requires medical validation and has an *18h contractual* penalty. A Complex-first medical rule during specialist absence is how you miss Urgent SLAs. The brief said not to prioritize all types at once; it did not say to sacrifice the only contractual clock.
- “Delay non-Urgent batches until courier arrival stabilizes” during a 24h delay / 10:00 dump risks a larger second-wave batch at prep/assay. The Repeat-after-failure > 20% trigger is also lagging (outcome mix), not a leading courier signal.

**5. Trade-off table — required columns present; several cells are wrong or unearned**

Structure matches the spec. Content does not.

- I6+I1 understates damage to Routine/Complex (I1 starvation and WIP), and “2–3% fewer repeats” is invented.
- I3+I2 “all except equipment outage” contradicts Section 3.
- I5+I10 puts the 12h transport risk on *Urgent*; the intervention is Complex-panel overflow. Rework column says “specialist backlog,” which is not a rework/repeat effect.
- I4 “no direct help” to Urgent is false: a 14h accession queue is almost the entire Urgent 18h budget. Unblocking accession is one of the few ways to protect Urgent *without* an Urgent-first rule at the machines.
- I7 never appears. I9 is unused (acceptable) but the courier-smoothing lever is not optional given the scenarios they themselves wrote.

**6. Residual vulnerabilities — one good redesign-induced point, several misses that follow from their own package**

I3 as a single point of failure is the right kind of residual. Night-shift / M2 repeat clustering is in the variant text and should have been designed for, not only listed as leftover.

What they do *not* own:
- Standing I1 as a source of Routine/Complex starvation and higher WIP — a vulnerability *caused by the redesign*.
- Standing I3+I2 as a repeat amplifier on M2.
- I8 pushing the bottleneck into 96% pre-analytical and 98% assay.
- Still only two accession specialists if I4 is not pre-trained.
- Recollection work consuming registration/clerk capacity (explicit in the brief).
- Specialist “unavailable every second Friday” as a *calendar* risk, not only a queue-length risk.

**7. Dashboard — leading/lagging split is correct; several signals fight the physics or are uncalibrated**

Good that they separate leading queues/failure rates from weekly SLA/rework. Weaknesses:

- Urgent bypass < 50% as an alarm that should *raise* I1 ignores the stated cost of bypass: it already falls from 74% to 58% in peak and *fragments machine schedules*. A dashboard that only pushes bypass up will worsen M1/M2 utilization and repeats.
- Courier queue > 4h will fire continuously against a peak median of 7.5h, unless the redesign actually adopts I7 — which it did not, except as a dashboard action pointing at an unused intervention.
- Medical-validation WIP > 500 is not derived from the 9,900 / 3-week volume.
- Missing the actual leading clocks: Urgent age toward 18h, accession-correction queue (14h), M1 vs M2 utilization *separately*, specialist-hold age, and same-day case-mix shift (Urgent/Complex share). Those are what the DFG and resource table say will move first.

**Net**

This is a formatted recap of the candidate list, not a redesign that stays acceptable across mix-and-disruption scenarios. The process-mining evidence is cited often enough to look grounded and used loosely enough to be wrong (13% specialist rate, 3.1× vs 2.4×, edge 120 vs 610, I5 on pre-analytical, I4 backwards). The “robust” core institutionalizes the intervention the brief warned against (I1) and the pairing most likely to raise M2 repeats (I3+I2), then contradicts itself on what is standing versus gated. A nearly flawless answer would have kept I1/I2/I10 off the default path, used I6/I7/I8 and *pre*-trained I4 as mix-agnostic structure, reserved I5/I10/machine dedication as triggered policies, and walked each scenario down the DFG with rates, not raw counts. This answer does not do that.
