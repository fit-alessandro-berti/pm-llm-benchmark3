**6.2**

The memo chooses the right family of action (hybrid, not A or B) and mostly follows the required structure, but it is not close to flawless. Several errors sit in the executive section, process-mining concepts are mishandled, and a few numbers are simply wrong.

**Strategic call.** E is defensible: simple houses clearly benefit, apartments and permit-sensitive buildings deteriorate, and the pilot mixes two interventions (coordinator ownership vs. survey-skip / post-billing activation). The memo correctly refuses national scale and full rollback. That is the strongest part of the answer.

**Material inaccuracies.** The executive memo invents a “16pp complaint increase” under national scale. The data show +3.7pp overall (6.1% → 9.8%), +5pp apartments, +7pp old buildings. Sixteen percentage points is not a rounding of anything in the packet. In the same section, D is called an “uncontrolled experiment” even though the option is a *controlled* regional experiment, and C is said to lose the handover benefit even though C keeps coordinator ownership. Section 4 later describes C correctly. Leadership would read the wrong versions first.

Segment signs are dropped throughout: simple-house complaint change is **−1pp**, not “1pp.” F7 reverses a DFG edge: the log shows Manual activation → Billing start (6% → 16%), not “billing-start followed by manual activation.” C’s “2 day gain at best” is invented.

**Process-mining errors.** High fitness / low precision is not “overfitting.” Overfitting is typically high fitness *and* high precision with weak generalization (spaghetti). Precision 0.51 is the opposite problem: the model is too permissive. The team already said this. Billing-before-activation is also an *intended* redesign rule (“activation checks were moved after billing start”), not mainly unofficial circumvention under 93% utilization. Treating designed behavior as rule-breaking muddles diagnosis.

**Measurement naivety.** A 6-week pilot with p95 = 62 days, 30-day complaints, and incomplete cost after week 4 needed explicit censoring / completion-bias caveats. Those tail and cost figures cannot be treated as clean facts. Sparse start timestamps are mentioned, then ignored when duration metrics are locked in as facts. Pilot mix (more apartments / older buildings) is parked in A3 and barely used to qualify the *overall* deterioration.

**Fact / interpretation split.** The table is the right idea and uses more than eight items, but I1–I3 over-claim, I5 is mostly restated facts, and A4 is correctly uncertain then treated as operationally settled when simple-house R is scaled with task merging *and* post-billing activation. Rural long-distance (11%) is left unassigned. Social-network centralization is almost unused.

**Conflicts and VoI.** The five conflicts are explained in the right direction (mixture of segments, front-loaded savings vs. back-loaded rework, ownership vs. single points of failure). Quality is uneven: 3.4 overstates that full-horizon rework “exceeds” labor savings despite incomplete cost; 3.5’s missing minus signs make apartment FTR look possibly improved. VoI ranking is plausible (M6/M3/M4 high, M8 low) but M1 is not “redundant” if M6 looks good—observational rule checks ≠ a stratified randomized skip test—and M2/M7 are undervalued given duration bias and the activation/permit compliance problem.

**Recommendation design.** Immediate / pause / scale / measure / gate is the right skeleton. Halt rules are poorly calibrated (FTR floor 75% is far below the simple-house result; p90 ≤ 22 days is not anchored to a known simple-house baseline). Scaling post-billing activation even for simple houses under-weights the alignment and complaint-type evidence. Two process variants are proposed without a serious operating-model cost.

Right decision, sloppy evidence handling. Not a 7+ under a strict read.
