**7.5**

The answer is a strong, format-compliant, evidence-heavy response. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it has several real design gaps, some missed process-mining structure, and a few places where the redesign logic is thinner than the diagnosis.

**What it does well**

Diagnosis is the best section. It uses well more than five artifacts: the frequency DFG (3,600 auto, 2,550 auto→fast approval), the performance DFG (0.8 / 1.0 / 3.0 on the happy path; 34 / 88 on evidence→manual; 22 / 60 and 30 / 80 on quote/approval), token replay (fitness 0.91, 68% fitting traces), alignment deviations, rework rates (9.2% / 14.1% / 3.3%; 31% then 9% cost), segment outcomes, and resource constraints. The core contradiction is stated correctly: the modal path is locally cheap and fast, and a false economy on high-value, incomplete, and reopened work. It does not treat the slow path as universally better, and it does not invent ROI.

A / B / C are materially different (compliance routing, upstream evidence, downstream remedy), not parameter tweaks. Sacrifices are named. The table stays qualitative. Failure modes and the experiment plan are grounded in the 82% utilization cliff, digital-literacy abandonment, the 6–18 hour gate, and long-tail complaint timing.

**Material weaknesses**

1. **Redesign A is mostly the prompt’s own hinted intervention.** “Make the outdated, non-segment-aware auto screen enforce R1/R2/R3” is the obvious move already implied by the rule text and the two-week auto-screen note. The four lanes are competent but not a deep redesign. Post-review remedy choice is underspecified. Blocking Fast approval *until* review is not the same as forcing the 96-hour reviewed/repair path, and the answer never makes that distinction crisp. For Premium high-value devices that is the actual trade-off: 20 hours / 22% reopen versus 96 hours / 5% reopen, plus “strict service expectations.” There is no expedited compliant lane, SLA’d review cell, or review-then-replace design. A therefore risks recreating the already-flagged temporal deviation that Manual eligibility → Repair quote is too slow for Premium.

2. **Fraud/risk is treated as a residual sink, not a designed resource.** Two specialists, Monday/campaign peaks, EUR 52/hour. Incoming already balances at 980 (400+360+220) with 210 denials. A says high-value work must hit Manual *or* Fraud, then lists Fraud overload only as a failure mode. There is no triage rule for what belongs with seven reviewers versus two specialists. That is a process-mining/resource gap, not a polish issue.

3. **Redesign C collides with the current use of Quality check.** Two inspectors are “underused except after repair campaigns,” but they also sit on Repair device → Quality check (360) and Quality check → Rework repair (45), with 12.5% rework on repaired high-value devices. Pulling them into a pre-dispatch gate is a real capacity transfer. The answer does not say who still inspects repairs, or whether pre-dispatch staffing increases escaped repair defects. It also bundles two mechanisms (replacement gate + part-price pull-forward) and is weaker on how the gate reduces repair rework than on how it reduces EUR 410 wrong replacements.

4. **Several DFG facts that should have shaped the redesigns are unused.** Fast approval → Quality/compliance close is 350 cases with no dispatch; Fast approval → complaint is 140 versus Dispatch → complaint 260. C only gates pre-dispatch, so it cannot touch the 140 early failures; A is the redesign that should. Frequency accounting is also incomplete (Manual eligibility incoming 1,400+890+310 vs outgoing 1,400; Dispatch incoming/outgoing similarly off). A serious PM reading would treat the DFG as partial and not design as if the map were closed. Low discovered-model precision is stated in the prompt and barely used.

5. **B is directionally right and still a bit generic.** Consolidated checklist, portal, assisted channel, and reason-coded second requests follow the 22% / 140-hour / 26% double-request evidence. The mechanism for *substantive* completeness versus a completed form is only a failure mode, not a design. R2 is about dispatch-before-completeness; B helps completeness but is not a hard dispatch interlock unless combined with A, which the standalone table somewhat blurs.

6. **Over-claim on effect size relative to the data.** “Strong expected reduction” appears in the table and body. The source numbers support *direction* (14.1% vs 3.3%; 22%/18% vs 5%/6%; 31% then 9%). They do not support calling the quality effect “strong” once A adds 15–35% manual inflow and C adds 6–18 hours. The experiment section is more careful than the redesign write-ups.

7. **Experiment sequence is defensible and still one-sided.** A-first is justified by R1/R2 and the two-week auto-screen. It is also the sequence most likely to slam the 82% utilization wall *before* B removes evidence loops. B-first, or a tiny A rule on >EUR 400 only, would have been the more conservative capacity logic. The answer sees the risk and still leads with the load-increasing change. Stop/go ranges (high single digits / low teens; p90 +20–30%) are invented thresholds presented with more precision than the evidence warrants.

**Net**

This is a good executive-sponsor memo: structured, segment-aware, and mostly faithful to the artifacts. It is not a near-flawless process-mining redesign. The first redesign is too close to the prompt’s suggested fix, Premium high-value speed/compliance is not actually redesigned, inspector and fraud bottlenecks are under-designed, and several DFG/conformance signals are left on the table. Those are enough, on a strict rubric, to keep the score well below the 9–10 band.
