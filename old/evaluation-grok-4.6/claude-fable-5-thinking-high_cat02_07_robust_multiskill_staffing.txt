6.4

The write-up is complete, well structured, and substantively on-task: labeled assumptions, two distinct objectives, block-level offered-load arithmetic that is mostly auditable, three named dispatch rules, a robustness-first recommendation that does not chase base-case mean delay, and a single self-contained Python scaffold that encodes the tables and calls the required PM4Py functions. Under a hypercritical standard that is not enough for a high score. The analysis overclaims precision from a simulation that is not faithful to its own assumptions, and several design choices make the policy comparison weaker than it appears.

**Capacity arithmetic is mostly right, not fully right.** Mixture means, block skill loads, day totals (~2,720 base-min vs 2,490 rostered), the 14:00–16:00 rho ≈ 1.14, and the evening coverage hole (no Mechanical after 18:00) are correct and are the right backbone for the later argument. Errors that should not have survived checking: U4 Mechanical mixture mean is 29.6, not 30.6; 16:00–18:00 staffed minutes are Deepa 120 + Emil 90, not the reverse (the 210 total is saved only by coincidence). Offered load is also booked entirely into the arrival block, so the table overstates intra-block rho and understates spill into Validation after 16:00. That is acceptable as a bound only if treated as one; the text slides between “approximate offered load” and operational bottleneck claims without keeping that distinction sharp.

**SPEC is close to a straw man.** A serious specialization policy would put Ben on Mechanical (multiplier 1.00), keep Ada on Mechanical/Intake, and not park Farah exclusively on Intake/Validation while diagnostic queues form. The implemented home map wastes the best Mechanical capacity on Intake/Validation and withholds Ada from Electrical even when Chen/Deepa are saturated. Escape-to-any-qualified only when *no* home resource is rostered is also harsher than “fixed queues” need to be. SPEC’s collapse under U1/U5 is therefore partly constructed. The specialization–pooling tension is real in this data; this particular SPEC does not give that tension a fair operational reading.

**HYBRID is underspecified where it matters.** The P1 “anchor” is the best *electrical* person (Chen → Deepa → Emil). Chen cannot do Mechanical, so reserved idle time does not protect P1 Mechanical at all. Before 12:00 the same rule can exclude Chen from non-P1 Software while Farah is gated by the 1.25/wait rule, leaving Software sitting beside an Intake-only or idle anchor. That hole is central to morning P1/P2 behavior and is not analyzed. Parallelization text and code also disagree: the narrative gives P1 a stronger “parallel if two resources can be found” rule; the code uses the same idle-now test for P1 and P2.

**The simulation does not implement the assumptions it cites.** A12 says ranking uses expected service; `choose()` multiplies the *drawn* `task.base`. Preemption remaining time is `(finish − now) / multiplier`, but `finish` includes handover/setup and the entire unused break pause, so a preemption near a lunch window can inflate remaining work by tens of minutes, then apply the 10% restart on that inflated base. Busy-time undo subtracts the same contaminated remainder and can mis-state utilization. `last_res` is updated only on completion, so a resume after preemption is charged handover again. Victim sort is `(prio_rank, −remaining)` with `reverse=True`, which selects *least* remaining work, opposite the comment and A8. Setup is charged on consecutive *tasks*, including sequential branches of the same case; the prompt charges setup between consecutive *cases*. Coordination is a free 8-minute clock delay, never assigned to a resource. In-progress work at 20:30 is dropped from the event log. These are not omitted-mechanism footnotes; they hit preemption, overhead, and utilization—the quantities used to rank P1 protection.

**Section 4 numbers cannot carry the weight they are given.** Eight seeds, ~4–7 P1 cases/day, and delay SDs of 16–50 minutes mean many 7–8 pp P1 gaps are within noise; that is mentioned and then ignored when HYBRID is said to “win P1 in five of six scenarios.” Mean/p90 delay are computed only on completers while 16–35 cases remain unfinished; in an over-offered day that is selection bias, and it is differential (POOL completes more). Incomplete-as-P2-breach is conservative, but then POOL’s P2 edge is hard to interpret against HYBRID’s larger leftover pile. O1 is misstated: HYBRID’s worst P1 (0.75) does not “match” POOL’s worst (0.69). O2 is overclaimed: HYBRID’s utilization SD is the *worst* of the three, which is exactly a term in O2. Resilience is a free-text tag, not an operationalized metric.

**What remains genuinely strong** is the framing: the day is over-offered, evening skill holes dominate routing, Electrical is the scarce skill, U5 is the reservation-failure case, mode-switch triggers are stated in observable quantities, Section 6 hits gaming, P3 starvation, skill erosion, and Validation as the next bottleneck, Section 7 correctly warns that PM4Py `@@waiting_time` is not stage wait, and Section 9’s shadow-then-interleaved pilot with P1 rails is the right validation posture. Those parts are why this is not a low score. They do not offset an untrustworthy DES, a stacked SPEC, an electrical-only P1 reserve, and a results table treated as more decisive than the design can support.

A near-flawless answer would have matched code to A12/A8, fixed remaining-time and victim selection, designed a non-caricature specialist policy, reported delay with incompletes (e.g., censored or makespan-inclusive), and separated estimation error from policy gaps. This does not.
