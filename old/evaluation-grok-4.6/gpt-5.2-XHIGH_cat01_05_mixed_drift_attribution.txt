**7.3**

The answer is structurally complete, numerically careful on the supplied tables, and directionally aligned with the intended reading (March as semantic/provenance, later movement as real process and performance change, multiple mechanism-specific onsets). Under a strict standard it is not near-flawless: identification is looser than the prose implies, a few method choices are confounded, and several format/operational details are sloppy.

**What is actually strong.** Section 1 sequences the right regimes and does not collapse them into one “migration broke the process” story. The March raw-fitness collapse is tied to labels, duplicates, and missing resources, while mapped fitness and the normalized fragment stay near baseline (L1 = 3). May is tied to Supplier Z and rework edges; June–August to queueing under lost capacity; July to unobserved redistribution; October to seasonal mix plus possible Senior Assessment coding change; December to mixed recovery. Alternatives and evidence scores are mostly well calibrated (July at 60 is the right kind of restraint). Section 2 is the right argument: mapping can restore fitness without proving the business process is unchanged, and post-May movement survives normalization. The baseline-relative table and fragment L1 distances are correct (May 40, July 59, October 64). Change points are mechanism-specific rather than global. Hypotheses 1–5 and 8, 10 match the required set. Monitoring is segmented, seasonal, and not a single threshold.

**Quantitative identification is the main failure.** The cycle-time split is presented as a decomposition into case mix, rework, service, and queueing, but the ranges are not a partition and can sum well above 100%. May’s 45–70% “control-flow/rework” share is not derived. Rework variants rise 11.5→24 pp; if a loop added on the order of 10–20h, the extra loop mass explains only a few hours of the +5.4h median, not the majority. Incomplete first-pass bundles can lengthen Collect Documents *without* a Request Info loop (`CollectDocs→Assess` falls 95→91). That is delay and quality, not rework. Service is correctly small (~0.6h on a 4.8→5.4h Assess time), but queueing and rework are then left to fight over the residual with overlapping bands and no identity. July/October splits have the same problem. “Ranges are OK” is not a license to report shares that are not identified from the aggregates.

Putting `Assess/SeniorAssessment→Decision` into the DFG L1 is a second identification error. That series can fall because of right-censoring/WIP (they later admit this as hypothesis 9), so October’s 64 and July’s 59 partly measure incomplete months, not routing. A lower-bound distance was the right idea; including a censored outcome rate undermines it. Adjacent distances are also “previous *shown* month,” not calendar-adjacent (Jan–Mar, Mar–May, then a real Sep–Oct step). The code repeats that ambiguity.

**Causal ranking fights the later text.** Interaction of supplier rework and staffing is the actual June–August story in Section 9, yet it is ranked 10. Persistent adaptation (rank 7) is only weakly supported: rework stays high in Oct–Nov when complexity is 41–45%, which is mix, not persistence; September already partially reverses. August is the tell they underuse: Supplier Z is already down (46%→38%) while median/p90 *peak* (45h / 110h). That is backlog/capacity inertia, and it should have been used to separate supplier share from queueing.

**Format and small defects.** Section 2 is at or above the 280-word cap; Section 9 is over 400. “Rework/escation” is a sloppy error in a discrimination table. Fast+rework never sum to 100%; residual variants are ignored. Primary monitoring on standard, non-Z cases is a reasonable quasi-control and a poor “primary” operational population: it systematically down-weights the cells where the process actually broke. L3 (`month × complexity × supplier × priority`) will not meet the stated ≥60 support in early months (high-complexity × Z in January is on the order of 20 cases); “collapse supplier” is mentioned but not designed.

**Monitoring and PM4Py are good, not exact.** There is no dedicated event-order/skeleton monitor in Section 7 (DFG/variants are a partial substitute). Same-month-last-year baselines are specified despite only 2026 being in hand; a first-year seasonal rule needed an explicit fallback. BH across highly correlated monitors is named, dependence is not. Part B’s `filter_time_range` omits `mode`; PM4Py’s default `traces_contained` will drop in-flight cases and bias monthly DFGs, waiting times, and decision rates—the exact artifacts this analysis depends on. `get_variants` return-type handling is defensive but version-fragile. De-duplication keeps the first `Open Case` rather than documenting why that matches `Open Case → Register`. None of this makes Part A unreproducible; the embedded tables and L1 implementation are the best part of the answer.

**Net.** This is a competent drift memo with correct arithmetic on the given summaries and the right multi-onset narrative. It is not a rigorous decomposition: cycle-time shares are asserted, DFG distance is partly a censoring artifact, interaction is under-ranked relative to the author’s own attribution, and several operational/PM details are loose. That combination caps the score well below the top of the scale.
