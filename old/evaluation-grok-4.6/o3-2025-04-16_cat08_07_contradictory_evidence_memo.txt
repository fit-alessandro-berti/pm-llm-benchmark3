**6.2**

The memo is structurally complete and lands in the right decision family (do not scale R as a bundle; do not full-rollback; harvest the simple-house win while containing tail/rework/control failure). That is not enough. Under a strict reading it has material internal contradictions, causal over-attribution, missing mandated artifacts, and a VoI ranking that fights its own recommendation.

**Internal contradictions (most damaging).** Section 4 labels C “our core recommendation,” then calls E “most balanced,” then Section 6 “adopts Alternative E.” Those are different policies: C puts coordinators on every case and only suspends skips; E deploys a segment-gated process. The memo also pauses survey-skip and post-billing activation *everywhere*, while Section 6 pauses them only for medium/high-risk segments. A decision memo that cannot state a single consistent next action fails the main job.

**Causal synthesis is too coarse.** R is four levers (coordinator ownership, merged validate/schedule, predictive survey skip, activation-after-billing), not two. Median gains for simple houses are attributed to ownership/queueing (E1) as if the merge and the skip rule were secondary. That unsupported collapse then licenses “scale coordinators, keep the median.” The data do not identify which lever produced the −6 day / −8 day p90 win. Treating ownership as the proven engine is an interpretation smuggled in as strategy.

**Facts, interpretations, and assumptions leak.** The three-column table is the right shape and clears the eight-item bar, but several “facts” are sloppy or incomplete: E8’s “simple houses p90 8 d” drops the minus and reads like a level, not a change; E7 omits the edge; E1–E3 offer single-cause stories where the log supports several. Important facts never enter the table: pilot mix bias (more apartments/older buildings), incomplete segment/risk attributes before week 3, cost truncated after week 4, sparse start timestamps, generalization 0.70 → 0.56, SLA 13% → 16% (better for simple houses), NPS +21 → +17, and the alignment “installation repeated without preceding survey.”

**Metric-conflict section is correct but thin.** The five required tensions are named and the intuition is right (mixture distribution; optional edges; single-owner fragility; cost horizon; skip-rule misfit). It does not use the artifacts the prompt required: temporal profiles (install → activation worse for apartments; failed visit → second visit delay under 93% utilization; billing → complaint as a new long-tail relation), generalization, or the working-together network concentrating on coordinators *and* activation specialists. High fitness / low precision is explained in governance language rather than as “the model can replay the log but over-generates unobserved skip × manual-activation × second-visit × early-billing combinations.”

**Alternatives A–E are sketched, not stress-tested.** A’s “locks in 24% median and 13% visible cost” treats a biased, incomplete pilot average as a transferable national gain. B’s rollback condition is narrowed to an acute regulatory penalty, ignoring operational inability to run dual processes. C’s claim that suspending skips “keeps the median speed gain” is unevidenced. Conditions under which D would dominate (cannot segment reliably; political need for causal identification before any scale) are underspecified. The C/E blur makes the evaluation internally unreliable.

**Value of information is the weakest analytic section.** Ranking M5 last is inconsistent with a plan to scale coordinator ownership: absence-stalls and 93% utilization are central findings, and M5 is the test of the asset being scaled. M2 is undervalued given sparse start times—the entire duration story (queue vs work, tail composition) is only half-identified. M8 is dismissed as “marketing,” though the complaint mix already shifted from wait-time to “installed but not working” / “billed before activation”; that split can change whether speed-oriented design is net-positive. For most items the write-up states a purpose, not a decision hinge (“if X, we do Y instead of Z”). M7 is both a measurement and a control; that distinction is not used.

**Recommendation hygiene.** Immediate national, segment-gated rollout assumes a risk/permit classification the prompt says was incomplete even in the pilot. The 80% utilization cap is invented and not labeled as a policy assumption pending M5. The go-wide bar “no SLA or billing-before-activation breach >1%” is ambiguous and, if read as SLA rate ≤ 1%, is incompatible with a 13% baseline. Treating post-billing activation as scalable once p90/FTR/complaint thresholds clear underweights a control/compliance failure (frequent move-on-log: billing before successful activation). Pilot selection bias is never used: a harder-than-average mix likely *overstates* harm and *understates* the national simple-house share, which should have informed confidence intervals around E, not been ignored.

The piece would be a solid 7.5–8.0 if C/E/pause scope were consistent, the four levers were kept separate, mandated PM artifacts were used, and VoI rankings followed from the decision. They are not. The recommendation is directionally defensible; the reasoning is not tight enough to trust.
