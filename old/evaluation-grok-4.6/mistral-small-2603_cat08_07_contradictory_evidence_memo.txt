**5.4**

The answer is in the right strategic neighborhood and covers every required section, but it is not close to a high-scoring memo. Several errors change the meaning of the evidence, misstate the decision set, and make the recommended experiment internally inconsistent.

**Format and memo quality.** The required structure is six labeled top-level sections, beginning with a standalone executive memo of at most 700 words. This response opens with a title block and a short bullet recommendation, then dumps the rest into later headings. The memo itself does not do the job the prompt assigned it: it is not a decision-oriented argument of the trade-off. It asserts a hybrid of D and E, then immediately specifies actions that are closer to C+E. The later “Final recommendation” largely repeats the opening, so the document is redundant rather than layered (memo for decision-makers, then supporting analysis).

**The recommendation is conceptually mixed and operationally contradictory.** The prompt’s best-supported path is E (or C plus E’s measurement), not “D with E refinements.” D delays harvesting a result that is already visible in 34% of cases. The answer then implements the opposite of a pure experiment: scale coordinator ownership for simple houses, pause skip and post-billing activation, *and* randomize survey-skipping in apartments and old buildings. Those last two instructions fight each other. Equipoise for skip does not exist in the worst segments; the segment table already shows large FTR and complaint deterioration there. Randomizing skip on the strata where it already looks harmful is poor experimental design. The right place for M1 is medium-risk addresses with genuine uncertainty, after controls are restored for permit-sensitive stock.

**Misreading of the alternatives is a serious flaw.** C is defined as scaling coordinator ownership *while suspending* survey skip and post-billing activation. The answer lists those same two features as residual risks of C. That is not a subtle miss; it means the alternatives table cannot be trusted. The failure path is equally sloppy: “roll back survey skipping and post-billing activation (Alternative B)” confuses a partial unwind with full return to baseline. A is not rejected hard enough given the tail, rework, and activation-order problems. E is treated as something you do *after* D confirms thresholds, which inverts E’s definition.

**Facts are garbled in the evidence table.** “Cases with 3 handovers” is not the metric given; it is 3+. “Median duration 6 days, first-time-right 3pp” and the apartment/old-building analogues drop the signs, so a reader cannot tell improvement from deterioration. Success criteria are written as “p90 cycle time  35 days,” “First-time-right  80%,” and so on; the inequalities are missing everywhere they matter. Those are not cosmetic defects in a decision memo. The table also relabels the required third column as “Uncertainties” rather than assumptions, and it contains only a thin slice of the prompt. Fitness/precision/generalization, alignment moves, temporal-profile deviations, DFG edge shifts, SLA, NPS, second-visit and manual-activation rates, survey vs second-visit utilization, truncated cost after week 4, incomplete attributes before week 3, sparse start timestamps, and the pilot’s heavier apartment/old-building mix are mostly absent. The prompt required separation of facts, interpretations, and assumptions using at least eight items; the spirit is met on count, not on quality or on use of the process-mining artifacts the constraints explicitly say not to ignore.

**Interpretations are over-certified.** “Survey-skipping misclassified medium-risk apartments, causing rework” is a plausible reading of the notes and alignments, not a measured causal finding. Alignments show installation without a preceding survey and missing permit checks; they do not by themselves prove the predictive rule’s classification error is the mechanism. “Post-billing activation reduced accountability” is likewise an inference, and citing NPS plus complaint volume does not establish that mechanism.

**Metric-conflict explanations are correct in direction and thin in mechanism.** Fitness vs precision is described at textbook level but not tied tightly to the offered interpretation (a high-fitness happy path plus an under-constrained model that permits skip × manual activation × second visit × bill-before-test). Median vs tail omits the obvious confound that the pilot mix is harder than baseline, so some tail movement may be composition rather than treatment. Visible vs full cost does not emphasize that complaint handling is truncated after week 4, which is why the EUR 198 vs EUR 207 comparison is not yet decision-grade. Handovers vs resilience is the strongest of the five paragraphs. Segment heterogeneity is listed rather than explained as a coherent system: skip rule plus missing permits plus bill-before-activate plus coordinator saturation producing a new billing→complaint long tail.

**Value-of-information ranking is only partly defensible.** M1, M4, and M6 belong near the top. Ranking M2 and M5 low is hard to justify given the rest of the memo. Start timestamps are sparse by construction; every duration claim, including the median/tail contrast, is weakened by that gap. If coordinator ownership is what they want to scale, M5 is not a side study—it tests the resilience claim they treat as material (93% utilization, stalls on absence, centralized working-together network). M7 is underrated relative to the alignment diagnostics that already flag billing-before-activation and absent permit checks as the compliance-like failure mode. The “how this would change the decision” column is generic and does not specify flip conditions.

**Thresholds are invented and still allow a worse process than baseline.** p90 of 35 days is still worse than 31; FTR of 80% is still worse than 82%; complaints of 8% are still worse than 6.1%. A 12-week horizon is asserted without linking it to the 6-week pilot, the 30-day complaint window, or the 60-day cost follow-up in M4. Broader rollout gated on “all thresholds met” is also in tension with the earlier claim that only a refined E would scale.

**What is still creditworthy.** The answer does not recommend national scale, does not do a full rollback just because some KPIs worsened, and does notice that simple low-risk houses are real beneficiaries. It pauses the two design choices most implicated in rework and activation-order problems. It lists A–E and M1–M8. Those are the minimum moves a competent memo must make. They are not enough to offset the misstated alternatives, contradictory experiment, missing operators/signs, weak fact–interpretation split, and the systematic underuse of the process-mining evidence the question was built around.

A near-flawless answer would have chosen E (with C’s suspensions as the default control set), written a true memo on heterogeneous effects and value of information, used a clean three-column evidence table packed with the given artifacts, treated mix-shift and truncated cost as first-class caveats, and ranked measurements by whether they could flip scale / pause / redesign. This is a complete but unreliable draft.
