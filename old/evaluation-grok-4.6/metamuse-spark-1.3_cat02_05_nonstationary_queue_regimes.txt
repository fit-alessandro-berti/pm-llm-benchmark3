**6.2**

The answer is structurally complete, operationally literate, and mostly honest about non-stationarity. It is not close to flawless. Several claims that look quantitative are wrong, internally inconsistent, or unreconciled with the supplied tables. Under a strict reading those are not cosmetic.

**What holds up.** The estimands are defined correctly: daily `completed` is throughput, not capacity; median/p90 service is not mean flow time; `mean_wip` is opening-hours inventory. Outage (2026-04-15) and recovery (04-16–04-17) are not treated as a sustainable high-throughput regime. Primary cuts by mix, backlog, capacity, and weekday/weekend are defensible; S2 (mix change-point with staffing as a covariate) is a real alternative, not a relabeling. Little’s Law is restricted, arrivals vs completions are both shown, and the R-A implied wait (~146 min vs ~25 min median service) is the right qualitative conclusion. Pooling fallacies on weekday/weekend composition, mix shift, and cancellation of outage deficit against recovery surplus are the right phenomena.

**Numerical and conservation errors.** R-A net flow is stated as “net −4 over 7d” in §§2 and 6. Arrivals 701 vs completions 697, so arrivals−completed = **+4**. The sign is wrong under the same convention used later (“net +1” on baseline weekends). Recovery surplus is **30** completions above arrivals (241 vs 211), not +29. Pooled `staff_fte` is **5.14**, not ~5.3. Mean of daily medians is **~31.0**, not ~31.5; 31.5 is the median of medians. The “cleaner” R-E two-day Little’s figure `34/9.83=3.46h` does not follow from arrivals (9.54/h) or completions (10.08/h); 9.83 is an undeclared blend of the two proxies the rest of the memo warns against mixing.

**False burstiness claims.** On 2026-04-15, completion hourly Fano is about **2.1** and CV about **0.65**, both *below* the arrival Fano (~3.8), not “Fano ≫ arrivals, CV >1”. The zeros are real capacity loss; the dispersion numbers were not computed. Completions on 2026-04-21 are described as “7–13 range”; the vector is **3–13**. These sit in the section whose job is dispersion.

**Unreconciled evidence.** Assumption A2 treats the hourly vectors as aligned truth for those dates because they sum to daily totals. They contradict `peak_08_10_pct` on every representative day (e.g. 04-07: 08:00–09:59 share 17/101 ≈ 17% vs tabulated 31%; 04-21: 11/115 ≈ 10% vs 47%). The 04-21 spike of 34 is in **10–11**, after the named peak window. The memo treats `peak_08_10_pct` and that spike as the same concentration mechanism. A data audit should have flagged the clash and stopped using both as simultaneous fact.

**Self-contradiction on service time.** §§1 and 6 correctly refuse to substitute median service for E[S] or W. §7 then scales FTE from “+~35% mean service” using the median shift (25 → 35 is **+40%**, and the 35%/40% figures are not even stable). p90/median also rose (~1.9 → ~2.4), so if anything the mean moved more than the median. The staffing +50% workload claim is therefore both methodologically forbidden by the author’s own rules and numerically loose. “Effective utilization >100%” on recovery is the same sloppiness: drain can exceed arrivals; server utilization cannot exceed 1 without a defined overtime/capacity model.

**Little’s Law and code.** The applicability matrix is the strongest section, but opening-hours W vs calendar elapsed time (overnight freeze of WIP) is only gestured at. Code hard-codes L and λ instead of deriving them from the regime masks it just built, computes Little’s Law only for R-A / R-Aw / pooled, and implements the “change-point heuristic” as a printed rolling mean plus a sentence. That meets the letter of the spec weakly. Copying the assignment’s seven bullets into §8 is noise.

**Net.** This is a competent regime analysis with the right qualitative skeleton and several results you could not trust without recomputing. Strictly, invented or mis-signed quantities in conservation, dispersion, and staffing arithmetic, plus failure to audit the hourly vs `peak_08_10_pct` contradiction, cap the grade well below the top of the scale.
