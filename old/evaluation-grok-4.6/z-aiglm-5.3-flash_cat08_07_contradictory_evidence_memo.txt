**7.5**

The memo is strategically strong and format-compliant, but it is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it has several real defects in causal discipline, value-of-information logic, and operational feasibility.

**What it does well.** The core call is the right one: do not scale R as piloted, do not roll back, unbundle the four design elements, keep coordinator ownership and merged scheduling for simple low-risk houses, suspend survey-skipping and post-billing activation where they fail, and buy evidence before wider rollout. That respects every stated constraint. The evidence table is large and mostly well labeled. All five metric conflicts are explained with the actual process-mining artifacts (fitness/precision/generalization, alignments, DFG, temporal profile, social/resource networks, segments). A–E are all evaluated. M1–M8 are all ranked with a decision-change story. Thresholds and a rollback contingency are present.

**Fact/interpretation leakage (explicit constraint violation).** The table is cleaner than the memo. In the executive memo, “the process became markedly more variable” is stated as what the precision collapse *means*, and resilience loss is treated as “an ownership-design flaw, fixable without abandoning ownership.” The source only says reduced handovers *may* have reduced resilience. Item 4 also files an incomplete cost *estimate* (EUR 207) as a fact. Several “why it matters” cells on Fact rows smuggle causal readings. That is not a complete merge of the three categories, but it is enough to fail a strict reading of “do not merge.”

**VOI is partly mis-specified.** M7 is mostly a control/guardrail, not information that changes beliefs. Ranking it #1 as “value of information” conflates risk reduction with VOI. M4 (full downstream cost) and M2 (start timestamps) are more decision-critical than the ranking implies: cycle-time tails and the 198 vs 207 cost split are the economic heart of the choice, and start timestamps are a prerequisite for trusting those tails. Ranking M5 last is also too casual given 93% utilization and observed stall-on-absence; the memo then scales coordinator ownership nationally on untested mitigations (notes, named backups, an invented 85% cap).

**Operational overreach.** “Scale nationally, but only for simple low-risk houses” assumes segment/risk attributes are production-ready. The pilot itself had incomplete customer-segment and building-risk data before week 3, and pilot regions were apartment/old-building heavy. Gating nationally on those attributes is asserted, not evidenced. Rural and business effects (harder rescheduling, billing disputes) are noted in the table and then barely operationalized in the hybrid. Allowing post-billing activation to remain for simple houses is a residual compliance-like risk that is not really argued.

**Smaller flaws.** The 85% utilization cap, FTR ≥ 80%, complaints ≤ 7%, and “within 10% of p95” are invented numbers presented as if they were natural thresholds. “Simpson’s-paradox-like” is loose (this is heterogeneous treatment effects plus mix, not a classic reversal). “E is the only option…” overclaims after correctly saying no alternative dominates all metrics.

The recommendation would still be defensible after those fixes. The work is well above a generic summary, but the causal overconfidence, VOI mix-up, and national subset-scale without a measurement-feasibility check keep it well short of excellent.
