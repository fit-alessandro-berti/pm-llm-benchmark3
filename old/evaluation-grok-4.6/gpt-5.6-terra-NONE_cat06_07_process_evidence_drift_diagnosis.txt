## What is correct and should be preserved

The answer meets the quality bar on the four months that matter. April is treated as a schema/anchor/feature-pipeline incident: unknown activity, missing queue, unmapped variants, and mean predicted risk rising to 0.28 while the mature breach rate stays at 0.19. June is a routing-mix change, not automatic concept drift: V2 jumps to 52%, remote-fix latency is stable, V2 breach falls to 0.09, and the aggregate rate drop to 0.15 is mix, not recovery. August–September are overlapping process, concept-from-the-model’s-feature-set, and Partner Z population effects. October is correctly treated as non-comparable because 55% maturity plus path-dependent outcome backlog can manufacture an apparent 0.16 breach rate.

The triangulation is real, not decorative. Predictive metrics are read against DFG edge times, matured variant rates, Partner Z mix, and pipeline audit findings. The answer repeatedly refuses a single-label diagnosis and states what would look different if the competing story were true. Containment, recalibration, feature repair, and retraining are staged rather than collapsed into “retrain the model.” The monitoring design correctly gates predictive alarms behind data-quality and label-maturity checks, and keeps supplier/process alarms independent of model retraining.

## Material defects

**1. The diagnosis table contradicts itself on April and June.**  
April is listed under covariate drift and process drift even though the same rows say the disruption is artificial and not an operational process change. That is the wrong place to put a schema rename. April belongs almost exclusively under data-pipeline failure; residual May feature staleness is the only related follow-on. Listing it in three rows makes the table look comprehensive while blurring the one diagnosis that is actually clean.

June is listed as population shift. It is not. A remote-routing policy reroutes existing demand onto V2. That is process / policy mix. Population shift starts in September with Partner Z. Section 2 gets June right; Section 1 does not. In an audit whose job is taxonomy, that inconsistency is not cosmetic.

**2. Covariate-drift timing is sloppy, and the confidence is too high.**  
“June onward” is not one regime. June is a V2 spike. July–October is V3 expansion plus supplier delay, then Partner Z. Those change different parts of \(P(X)\). Calling the whole span covariate drift, at High confidence, overstates how well feature-shift has been isolated from process and population change. The answer itself admits that aggregate metrics do not identify which features moved.

**3. Concept drift is slightly mis-scoped.**  
Partner Z is placed under concept drift as a “possible” September segment. There is no evidence yet that \(P(Y \mid X)\) differs for Partner Z after path, geography, device age, and maturity. That is a population-shift hypothesis, which the rest of the answer correctly treats as unproven. Conversely, July is undersold as an “early warning.” Matured V3 breach has already jumped from 0.35 to 0.46 and the parts edge from 42 h to 65 h. From a diagnosis-time model that cannot see future supplier delay, that is already a material change in \(P(Y \mid X)\).

**4. Falsification is only implicit.**  
The prompt asked what would falsify each explanation. Section 1 gives “contradicting or insufficient evidence,” which is not the same thing. The competing-hypothesis table in Section 3 does the real falsification work, but it is not mapped back onto the six drift types as required. A strict reading of the spec is only partly satisfied.

**5. September’s observed 0.31 is not fully interpreted.**  
For October, the answer correctly says 0.16 is biased downward because long, high-risk paths are missing. September is 82% mature with the same mechanism already visible (42-day delay, V3-heavy, Partner Z slower to mature), yet the text only calls the month “provisional.” It does not say that 0.31 is likely a lower bound. That is an avoidable inference gap in a piece that is otherwise careful about label maturity.

**6. July’s aggregate calibration is underused.**  
July is nearly calibrated in the aggregate (0.18 predicted vs 0.19 observed) while AUROC falls to 0.76 and V3 outcomes break. That pattern is stronger evidence of a localized ranking failure than of a global base-rate shift. The answer notes the AUROC drop but does not use the calibration/discrimination split as a diagnostic.

## Smaller technical and design nits

The Python sketch hits the required API checklist and correctly distinguishes event-calendar from case-start cohorts. It is not as auditable as advertised. `extract_temporal_features_dataframe` is computed once and then ignored. Monthly rows store raw `perf_dfg` objects rather than explicit median/p90 durations, and `discover_performance_dfg` often returns means unless configured otherwise—the business evidence is medians. Variant shares for V1/V2/V3 are never computed, despite being central to every later claim. Duplicate handling is only exact `(case, activity, timestamp)` collapse; the audit also involves `UNKNOWN_ACTIVITY` and source-specific dedupe after 20 April, which the sketch does not surface.

October’s outcome-API backlog is filed as both label delay and data-pipeline failure. Label infrastructure is related, but it is not the same incident class as the April rename. The double-booking is defensible only if the table says so more sharply.

Training-safety judgments are mostly sound. August as “conditionally safe” is a little aggressive: 93% maturity with path-dependent missingness can still bias the label toward non-breaches. The qualification is present; the residual risk is underweighted.

## Overall

The answer would be in the high 9s if Section 1 used the same precision as Section 2, if falsifiers were stated per mechanism, if September’s bias direction were named, and if the process-evidence code emitted the actual monthly statistics the narrative depends on. Those are not presentation nits. They are the difference between an excellent walkthrough and a defensible audit. Under a strict reading, that caps the score at **8.2**.
