**7.3**

The final write-up is a strong, format-compliant operations redesign: it uses the logs rather than the normal average, keeps case types in conflict instead of ranking everything first, and generally refuses naïve I1–I3 capacity theater. It is not nearly flawless. Several design choices are internally inconsistent, operationally late, or slightly misread the DFG.

**What holds up**
- All seven required sections are present and used as intended. Scenarios vary mix *and* disruption, not just volume.
- Process-mining use is mostly accurate: recollection 4.2%→8.0%, accession correction 2.8%→4.3%, batch-wait 4.4%→12.6%, repeat assay 6.7%→14.8%, specialist hold 1.8%→7.6%; peak urgent bypass falling 74%→58%; specialist-hold edge 18h→48h; recollection loop 42h→60h.
- Bottleneck migration is the real backbone: I6/I8 flood prep/assay; strict I1 starves Routine/Complex and inflates WIP; I2 collides with urgent bypass; I3 dumps Routine onto failure-prone M2; I5 moves work into specialist hold; I10 trades queue for transport/traceability. I4 is correctly gated against 101% technical-validation utilization.
- I9 is rightly rejected as a default because `Medical validation → Release` is only 2h/5h.
- Residual risks include redesign-caused Routine starvation and early-reject communication load. The dashboard separates leading queues/mix/repeats from lagging p90/SLA/rework.

**Material flaws**
- **Late Urgent control.** Escalating Urgent at “12h since registration” is a bad rule against this log. Peak `Register → Receive` is already 8h, then `Receive → Pre-analytical` is 9h. By 12h the contractual 18h clock is largely gone. Time-*remaining*-to-SLA or station-age triggers were the coherent choice; mix-share and queue triggers elsewhere only partially rescue this.
- **Auto-release overclaim.** Peak medical-validation load is already the Urgent/Complex mix (`Tech val → Medical` 3420 plus holds), while Routine’s dominant path is still `Tech val → Release`. “Auto-release low-risk Routine to free medical validators” treats a path that is largely already in the DFG as a new capacity lever. The real peak problem is more cases that *cannot* skip medical/specialist review.
- **Priority dressed up as capacity.** Protected “1–2h assay slots” sit next to 98% machine utilization and an 18h assay queue. Reordering and Routine throttling can protect a class; they do not manufacture 1–2h waits. Residual section 6.1 admits assay tightness; R1/P1 still write as if the slot exists.
- **Inconsistent Complex targets.** S1 wants Complex p90 ≤140h; S2 allows 150–180h; neither is anchored to a stated SLA. That is unclarity in the acceptance envelope the rest of the design claims to protect.
- **Missed DFG WIP signal.** Peak starts 9900 and closes only 8040+540=8580. ~13% of peak cases are still open or unaccounted. That is direct evidence of instability and should have informed CONWIP caps; it is unused.
- **Arbitrary knobs.** 60% M1 reservation, 10–15% repeat reserve, Routine mini-batch at 60h, batch 8–12 vs 16–18, dashboard red lines — little sensitivity to the actual queue table (pre-analytical 9.5h, accession 14h, specialist 36h).
- **Quiet capacity adds.** Temporary receive staff, evening medical shift, remote specialist, and a communication clerk are narrower than “hire everywhere,” but P4 still solves an intake spike by adding people. I5 is a given candidate; extra intake FTEs are not.
- **Control design noise.** P1 uses rolling Urgent p90 (lagging) as an on-switch after the dashboard correctly labels p90 as lagging. Eight leading indicators stretch “few.”

**Net.** This is competent, data-grounded process design with real bottleneck-migration discipline. The Urgent clock logic, the auto-release story, the 1–2h slot fiction, and the wobbling Complex targets keep it well short of a high score under a strict reading.
