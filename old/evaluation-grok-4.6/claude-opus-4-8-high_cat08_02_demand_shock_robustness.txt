**6.4**

This is a competent operations redesign write-up with the right architecture (mix-and-disruption scenarios, migration chains, always-on vs triggered policies, self-inflicted residues, leading vs lagging metrics). It is not nearly flawless. Several claims fight the log, several “guards” do not actually guard the station they name, and the supposedly robust core still tries to protect Urgent and Complex-panel on the same scarce assets.

**Process-mining misreads**

The peak log does not support the S1 opening mechanism. Urgent bypass of Batch wait *falls* from 74% to 58% in the peak period, while `Test preparation -> Batch wait` *rises* in absolute frequency (760 → 1180) despite fewer cases. The first pressure is therefore more Urgent *trapped in batches* and more schedule fragmentation from a *failed* bypass, not “more Urgent means more Batch-wait bypass.” Treating bypass as the load amplifier inverts the variant the log actually recorded.

The intro’s bottleneck story is also off. Normal utilization is already highest at technical validation (76%), then pre-analytical (74%), then assay (71%). Peak is simultaneous saturation (technical validation 101%, assay 98%, pre-analytical 96%) plus *queue explosions* at medical validation (2h → 22h) and specialist review (5h → 36h). That is not a clean hop “from pre-analytical check to assay to validation.”

R3 then mis-identifies the next bottleneck of I6. Early rejection of poor samples sits on the recollection branch (`Pre-analytical check -> Request recollection`), not on `Manual accession correction`. I6 should cut junk, shrink the 42h/60h recollection loop, and raise *good* flow into preparation and assay. Sending the overload to the two accession specialists is a different defect class. The I8 “guard” is equally weak: I8 never proposed skipping pre-analytical check. The stated conflict is that cheaper registration *floods* pre-analytical check and the machines. “Still run pre-analytical check” does not bound that flood.

**Design logic that does not survive the constraints**

R1 puts Urgent *and* Complex-panel on M1 and Routine on M2, then calls Complex “protected by cap.” The cap protects the Routine lane. Complex-panel shares the reliable machine with Urgent, and Complex-panel consumes *multiple* assays. That is simultaneous priority of two stressed types on one resource—the constraint the prompt forbids assuming away. M2’s higher failure rate is then waved off in the table as “Neutral (M2 quality risk if unbounded)” even though the I3 hint is that dumping Routine onto M2 *raises repeats*. Neutral is the wrong sign.

R1 is listed as robust across S1–S4 and, in the same breath, as failing in S2. A package item that collapses in one of the four required disruption classes is a scenario policy, not a robust control. P2 tries to patch this by running large Routine batches on M1 “during low-Urgent windows,” but S2 is defined on ordinary peak mix (Urgent already 29%). Those windows are the scarce thing.

R4’s trigger is operationally infeasible on the observed peak. Accession-correction peak median queue is 14h and technical validators are already at 101%. Amber >6h / red >10h would pull a technical validator for most of the peak, exactly when technical validation has no slack. “Only one at a time” still steals from the only station already over 100%. Claiming R4 “helps S3” is also wrong: S3 is medical/specialist absence, not accession.

P1’s “one reserved medical-validator slot per hour” for Complex-panel is undersized on the peak arithmetic (~20% of ~471 cases/day). P1 reserves Complex slots; P3 then gives remaining medical capacity to Urgent first. Under S5 they co-activate and fight over the same gate.

**Table, robustness, and local inconsistencies**

Scenario coverage in the trade-off table does not match the narratives: R1 does not cover S3 (assay lanes do not replace missing medical validators); R4 does not cover S3. S3’s “upstream looks healthy” trap is asserted while the same paragraph uses peak queues, when at peak upstream is not healthy. WIP caps are never quantified. I9 is silently dropped (reasonable) but several frequency shifts that would have strengthened the argument are unused: recollection cancel intensity (290/540 vs 370/520), specialist-hold counts (290 → 610 in a much shorter window), and the batch-wait frequency surge.

**What still holds**

Sections 2, 4, 6, and 7 are the reason this is not much lower. Migration is mostly traced with DFG edges and queue metrics; I1/I2/I3/I4/I8/I10 conflict hints are used rather than ignored; I5/I7/I10 are mostly scenario-gated instead of “add capacity everywhere”; Routine tail risk from the Urgent cap and R4 ping-pong are correctly named as redesign-caused residues; the dashboard separates mix/queue leading indicators from p90/rework lagging outcomes and gives bands.

Those strengths are real. They do not cancel the inverted bypass story, the I6/accession mix-up, the M1 dual-priority design, or triggers that fire on the exact peak the “guards” were supposed to survive. Under strict scoring that is a clearly incomplete control system, not a near-flawless one.
