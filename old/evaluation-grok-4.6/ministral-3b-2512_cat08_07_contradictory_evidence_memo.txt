**5.4**

The answer has the right *shape* (choose a segment-restricted hybrid, refuse national scale, refuse a full rollback, separate facts from inference, discuss metric conflicts, rank measurements). That is not enough. Under a strict reading of the rubric it is materially inaccurate, conceptually sloppy on the process-mining artifacts, and logically inconsistent in the alternatives and VoI sections. A complete memo that gets the headline decision right is not “nearly flawless.”

**Factual errors (the most serious problem)**

The evidence table is supposed to be the fact layer. It gets a core segment result backwards. Apartments are recorded as “median +2 days, p95 +12 days.” The prompt says apartment / medium-risk: **median −2.0 days**, **p90 +12.0 days**. Simple houses are also labeled with a p95 change; the source table is p90. The same inversion reappears in the memo as “apartments/old buildings suffer (+12–25 days),” which collapses tail movement into overall duration and hides that apartments still improved at the median. That is not a wording slip. It changes the story from “heterogeneous median/tail trade-off” to “apartments are slower,” which is false.

A second hard misread: “missed permits in 18% of old buildings.” Eighteen percent is the **pilot share** of the old-building / permit-sensitive segment, not a permit-miss rate. The alignment finding is qualitative (“expected Permit check not observed”). Treating segment mix as a defect rate is a basic data-handling error.

Several other numbers are used loosely: p90 and p95 are swapped; arrows/inequalities are dropped throughout (“14.2  10.8”, “Handovers  resilience”), so the reader cannot tell whether a relation is “less than,” “not equal,” or a missing character.

**Process-mining concepts are wrong in exactly the places the prompt tests**

Fitness vs precision is explained as “fitness = data fit; precision = generalizability” and “the model overfits simplified pilot paths.” That is incorrect. Precision is permissiveness / extra behavior, not generalization. The prompt even supplies a separate generalization score (0.70 → 0.56), which the answer never uses. Low precision here means the discovered pilot model allows too many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing-before-activation. Calling that “overfitting” and “not the intended workflow” mixes discovered-model quality with normative conformance.

Alignments are cited, but DFG edge shifts, the handover-of-work vs working-together distinction, and the temporal-profile pairs are mostly atmosphere. The prompt listed those artifacts as non-optional. Field-survey utilization falling 78% → 52% while second-visit utilization rose 66% → 92% is the actual resource-substitution mechanism behind “cheaper visible cost, more rework.” It is barely used.

**Facts, interpretations, and assumptions still bleed**

The table format looks compliant and there are nine rows, so the minimum count is met. The contents are not clean. “Fewer handovers → better resilience” is listed as an assumption even though the prompt already contradicts it (coordinator absence stalls cases; networks centralize). “The visible cost drop is an accounting illusion” in §3 is an overclaim: pre-complaint cost really fell; the issue is a truncated cost object, not a fake saving. “Manual activation is not a bug—it’s a workaround” is an interpretation parked in the assumption column. “Baseline has no proven resilience for high-risk cases” in the memo is not a fact, not supported, and sits badly next to better baseline FTR, complaints, second visits, SLA, and tails.

Selection and measurement limits are underweighted: pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings; segment/risk attributes are incomplete before week 3; complaint cost is missing after week 4; start timestamps are sparse. The memo treats median gains as “real” and segment effects as “clear” while those caveats are still open. That is exactly the fact/interpretation collapse the rubric penalizes.

**Decision-alternative logic is broken for C and too crude for B/D**

C is “scale coordinator ownership **and suspend** survey skipping and post-billing activation.” The answer says C’s tail risks “persist” and then adds that C is reasonable only if those features are paused. That is a misread of the alternative, then a circular condition. B is dismissed as “never recommended,” which violates the instruction to take segment benefits seriously without treating rollback as unthinkable. D is rejected because “we lack the data to justify a full regional experiment”—the opposite of value-of-information logic. “Pilot fatigue” is invented. Preferring E over D can be defended (capture simple-house value now, fence the harmful levers). The written reasons do not defend it.

The recommended package is roughly E with a C-style freeze of skip/post-billing activation. That is a defensible stance. The supporting argument is not.

**VoI ranking is backwards on the highest-leverage tests**

M1 is a **randomized** skip-rule experiment by risk stratum. Ranking it 6th for “bias” and “overkill if M6 validates the rule” inverts experimental design: the RCT is the stronger causal test; observational rule validation (M6) is the cheaper, more confounded one. M2 (start timestamps) is useful instrumentation, not the top decision-changing experiment, and it does not “validate whether M6’s predictive rule is reliable.” M3 (reason codes) is dumped as “enablement, not a decision driver,” even though the open causal question is *why* failed visits, manual activations, and complaints rose. M7 is undervalued relative to a known compliance-like failure (billing before activation; missing permits).

The rollout gate is also poorly designed: “M4 shows downstream costs stabilize (e.g., &lt;5% increase).” The already-incomplete estimate is EUR 198 → 207, about **+4.5%**, and is biased downward because post-week-4 complaint cost is missing. A 5% bar would already be nearly met by a number the prompt flags as untrustworthy.

**What still works**

The memo is decision-oriented, under 700 words, and refuses both naïve scale-up and naïve rollback. It sees that no lever dominates all metrics, that simple houses are the beneficiary, and that skip-survey plus post-billing activation are the main harm channels. Sections exist in the required order. Conflicts (median vs tail, visible vs full cost, handovers vs resilience, segment split) are named even when the explanations are imprecise.

Those are table-stakes for this prompt. They do not offset inverted segment facts, a wrong reading of 18%, a precision/generalization error, a mis-specified alternative C, and a VoI ranking that treats a randomized trial as noisier than an observational check. On a hypercritical scale this is a competent template filled with mistakes, not a senior decision memo.
