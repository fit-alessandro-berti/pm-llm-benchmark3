6.0

The memo is usable and structurally complete, but it is not close to flawless. It chooses the right decision family and hits every required heading, then undercuts itself with factual mistakes, an internally inconsistent design, and several missed confounders that a careful reading of the evidence makes hard to ignore.

**Factual and internal-consistency failures.** Simple low-risk houses are 34% of the pilot, not “60% of pilot volume.” That error appears in the executive memo and again under alternative B. The evidence table also reports “Simple houses: +6d median,” which reverses the sign of a −6.0 day improvement and sits next to “+12d p90,” so a reader cannot tell which numbers are gains. More serious is the recommendation itself: the memo claims to “scale Redesign R” for simple houses, then strips out survey skipping and post-billing activation for that same segment. That is not R; it is a narrower coordinator-only variant. The skip rule was aimed at medium-risk addresses, and simple houses are the one segment with better first-time-right and fewer complaints. Reverting skip there either discards a demonstrated win or assumes, without saying so, that skip never applied. The memo never resolves which.

**Causal overclaim and a leaky fact/interpretation split.** Section 2 has the right three-column idea, but several “facts” are already causal stories, and the executive memo states rule failure, lost survey expertise, and post-billing ambiguity as established mechanisms. Those are plausible; they are not shown. The table also omits the evidence that most threatens the hybrid bet: pilot regions were heavier in apartments and older buildings, segment/risk attributes are incomplete before week 3, complaint cost is cut off after week 4, start timestamps are sparse, and the observation window is only six weeks against a 62-day p95. Incomplete early attributes directly weaken the 34% “simple house” slice on which E depends. Mix shift is never treated, so overall tail deterioration is read as pure treatment effect rather than treatment plus a harder case mix (while the median still falling on a harder mix is actually stronger evidence than the memo notices).

**Process-mining artifacts are underused.** Fitness vs. precision is explained at a high level, but generalization (0.70 → 0.56), the specific alignments (billing before activation, repeat install without survey, missing permit checks, complaints after billing), the temporal profile (failed visit → second visit lengthening where coordinators sit at 93%), DFG edge shifts, and the simpler handover network vs. more centralized working-together network are only touched. SLA (worse overall, better for simple houses), NPS, idle survey capacity (78% → 52%) versus overloaded second-visit teams (66% → 92%), and the business/rural segments are largely unused.

**Alternatives and VOI are uneven.** A and B are framed reasonably. C is mischaracterized: it already suspends skip and post-billing activation, yet the memo warns that C still risks tails “if field surveys are skipped.” D is dismissed as delay even though D is the alternative built for the measurement holes the dataset actually has. The M1–M8 ranking is directionally sensible (M1/M3/M4/M7 belong near the top) but does not match the decision: if skip is already reverted everywhere, a randomized skip trial is oddly both “paused” and “highest value”; M5 is ranked 7th despite resilience being one of the memo’s headline conflicts; M6 is separated from M1 even though both test the same rule; M2 is undervalued given sparse start times. Thresholds (p90 within +5 days of baseline, €200/case, complaints <8%, manual activations “above 10%”) are invented, not derived.

**What still works.** The memo does not scale nationally, does not full-rollback, and does not pretend one intervention wins on every metric. The five metric-conflict explanations are mostly right in direction: happy-path compression vs. lost safeguards; ownership vs. single-point stall; visible cost vs. incomplete rework cost; heterogeneous segments. Immediate action / pause / scale / measure / threshold is the right closing shape.

Net: a competent executive draft with the correct strategic instinct, damaged by arithmetic and labeling errors, a recommendation that is not actually “R for simple houses,” and silence on the data defects that should most humble that recommendation. Under a strict standard that is a mid-score memo, not a strong one.
