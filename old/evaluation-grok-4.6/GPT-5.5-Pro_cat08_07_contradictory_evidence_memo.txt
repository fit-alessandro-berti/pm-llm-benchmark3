**7.3**

The memo is strategically coherent and hits the required structure, but it is not nearly flawless. Several analytical shortcuts, category errors, and overconfident causal readings keep it well below a top score.

**What works.** The recommendation (E, with C-style gates) is the only alternative that respects the prompt’s hard constraints: do not nationalize R, do not discard the simple-house gains, do not treat one metric as decisive. The five metric-conflict explanations are the strongest section—fitness/precision, median/tail, handovers/resilience, visible vs downstream cost, and segment heterogeneity are all explained in operational terms rather than as modeling curiosities. A–E are evaluated with conditions, not slogans. M1–M8 are all ranked with a decision-change story.

**Material analytical flaws.**

1. *Designed policy treated as illicit deviation.* R explicitly moved some activation checks after billing when the signal looked acceptable. The memo and table repeatedly frame “billing before activation” as a compliance-like failure and alignment artifact. That is a policy judgment, and a defensible one given complaints and disputes, but the write-up never distinguishes intended policy, process drift, and true control breach. That is a real misread of the intervention, not a wording nit.

2. *Overall deltas are attributed to R without the mix caveat in the memo.* Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings; segment/risk attributes are incomplete before week 3. Citing p90/p95, FTR, complaints, and SLA as “the redesign worsened X” overclaims identification. Segment tables are the cleaner evidence; the exec memo leans on confounded aggregates.

3. *Facts, interpretations, and assumptions still bleed.* The prompt forbids merging them. Examples: “improved accountability” is an unverified design intention, not an interpretation licensed by the handover drop; the complaint-mix shift (“waiting” → “installed but not working” / “billing before activation”) is a stated finding but appears only as paraphrase; several “assumptions” are measurement facts (cost truncated after week 4, sparse start timestamps). The three-column table is the right shape and then fails its own standard in multiple rows.

**Smaller but chargeable issues.**

- House gains are treated as evidence for *both* coordinator ownership *and* merged validate/schedule. The pilot does not identify components; C vs E depends on that gap.
- “Manual activation doubled” is false (7% → 15%).
- “Map directly” from alignments to FTR/rework/complaints overstates what alignments show.
- Low generalization (0.56), six-week window, and incomplete early attributes are underused when arguing that a segmented rule can be operationalized now. E *requires* a risk classifier the data say was incomplete.
- Volume is never sized: simple low-risk houses are 34% of the pilot. An executive memo should say how much of the book E actually captures.
- VoI ranks decision importance but ignores cost, speed, and complementarity (M7 is a cheap control already implied by the recommendation; M6 then M1 is a sequence, not two independent “very high” items). M8 last is defensible, but NPS and the complaint-type shift are left almost unused.
- Rollout thresholds stay qualitative (“agreed tolerances”) except for a few binary gates. Fine as direction, weak as a decision rule.
- NPS, business-customer billing disputes, and rural rescheduling are thin relative to the evidence provided.

**Constraint check.** No prohibited national scale; no pure rollback; no single-metric dominance. The process-mining stack is used, not ignored. Those are necessary conditions, not a high score.

The piece would be a solid consulting draft. Under a strict reading it still over-interprets treatment effects, mischaracterizes an intended activation-policy change, and only partly separates evidence tiers. That is well short of nearly flawless.
