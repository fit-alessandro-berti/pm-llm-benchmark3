**6.5**

The write-up is conceptually strong and hits the required shape of the task: it separates demand, mix, capacity, outage, recovery, and intervention; it refuses to treat 16–17 April as a steady high-throughput regime; it does not substitute median service for mean flow time; and the Little’s Law matrix is, in structure and verdicts, the right kind of analysis. Format compliance is high. Those are the reasons the score is not in the 4–5 range.

It is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading the quantitative layer is unreliable, and that is enough to keep the grade well below the 8–10 band.

**Numerical errors that are not rounding.** Hourly means, variances, Fano factors, and CVs in §5 are wrong on three of the four representative days. 15 April arrivals average \(105/12 = 8.75\), not 7.92; 21 April averages \(115/12 = 9.58\), not 9.25; 25 April averages \(4.25\), not 3.92. For 7 April the mean is right (\(\approx 8.42\)) but the variance is not \(\approx 18.6\) (sample variance \(\approx 27.2\)), so Fano \(\approx 3.2\) rather than 2.2 and CV \(\approx 0.62\) rather than 0.51. 21 April is overdispersed, but Fano is \(\sim 9\)–\(10\), not 12.3. The qualitative burstiness story survives; the claimed indicators do not.

Pooled and regime summaries drift in the same way. The 28-day arrival median is \(103.5\), not \(\sim 99\); the mean is \(\approx 90\), not \(\sim 86\). In §4 the R5 weekday arrival median is given as 111; the seven weekday counts sort to a median of **115**. §2’s R1 arrival point estimate of 98 matches neither the median (101) nor the mean (\(\approx 100\)). R1 completions are 94–105, not \(\sim 97\)–105. These are the exact quantities the task asked to estimate “at a defensible level of precision.”

**Internal inconsistency.** The Python block, if run, would not reproduce the Fano/CV/mean figures in §5 (it uses the correct hourly vectors and sample moments). §2 and §4 disagree on R1 arrivals. A final answer that contains both a narrative and a “reproducible” script that contradict each other has not been checked.

**A first-order conservation point is missed.** On R5 weekdays, completions run about 10–16 per day below arrivals (\(\approx +94\) over seven weekdays), yet `mean_wip` only moves from 37 to 50. That gap is large enough that either snapshot WIP is not a comparable stock, carry-over/opening inventory is doing something the aggregates cannot show, or the arrival/completion counts and WIP are not on the same population. The text notes mild upward drift and then applies \(L/\lambda\) anyway. That is the kind of stock–flow check a process-mining analysis is supposed to make before calling Little’s Law even “approximate.”

**Little’s Law: right caveats, one unmarked assumption, sloppy labels.** The refusal to apply the law to the outage day, the recovery window, and the pooled month is correct. The warning that implied \(W\) is calendar sojourn, not median service, is correct. The 12-hour vs 24-hour discussion is the right issue. But the conversion \(W_{\text{hours}} = 24L/\lambda_{\text{day}}\) is only justified if `mean_wip` is a **24-hour** time average. The prompt does not say that; for a centre that is closed overnight, 15-minute snapshots are at least as likely to be opening-hours only. That is an assumption and should have been labelled. Using \(L_{\text{open}}\) as if it were \(L_{24}\) biases every implied hour figure. Verdict strings also leave the required three-way set: “Applicable (approx)” is not `applicable` / `approximate only` / `not defensible`. R1 is a stretch for `applicable` given unknown 07:00 backlog and a one-week window.

**Smaller but real defects.** “WIP falls 5235” is a broken “52 → 35.” Operators disappeared from several displayed identities (`W  20 / 99  0.20`). The lag-1 claim of 0.6–0.8 on the **full** daily series is not shown and is hard to reconcile with four Fri→Sat and Sun→Mon jumps of order 50–70 cases; the text reads as an unsourced range. The “Simpson’s-paradox-like” point is aggregation bias / a step change, not a sign reversal. The 21 April hourly vector does not reproduce `peak_08_10_pct = 47` (08:00–09:59 is 3+8 = 11 arrivals, \(\approx 10\%\)); a careful audit would have flagged the supplied tables as mutually inconsistent instead of using both as if they agreed. Assumptions (snapshot clock, mix shift vs recoding, intervention clock) are discussed but rarely labelled as assumptions.

**What still works.** Regime design is sensible and not over-unique: weekday/weekend, outage, recovery, post-mix, intervention. The alternative pressure segmentation (`WIP/FTE`) is a real alternative, and the comparison of strengths is fair. Pooling distortions (composition, mix step vs smooth slope, recovery mistaken for capacity) are the right three. Operational advice is tied to those regimes, including a pooled policy that is dangerous once segmented (cutting weekend FTE; targeting 16 April’s 129 completions). The code skeleton matches the spec: in-memory tables, pooled vs regime summaries, Fano/CV plus lag-1, a clearly non-oracular split heuristic, Little’s Law only on chosen windows, and PM4Py comments that correctly refuse to treat these aggregates as an event log.

Net: this is a good conceptual memo with a complete required skeleton, undermined by unverified arithmetic, narrative/code mismatch, and a missed stock–flow contradiction in the main congestion regime. That is a mid-6, not an 8.
