**7.4**

The final write-up is a strong, format-compliant operations redesign. It uses the DFGs, queues, variants, and conflict hints; it does not “add capacity everywhere”; it does not optimize the historical average; and it treats case types as competing, not jointly prioritizable. It is not nearly flawless.

**What is solid.** All seven required sections are present and usable. The five scenarios vary mix *and* disruption type, not just volume. Bottleneck migration is the real analytic core: peak is treated as a hot chain (pre-analytical 96%, assay 98%, technical validation 101%, medical validation 93%), and several interventions are correctly shown to shove work onto the next saturated station (ungated I8 → pre-analytical; I2 → technical validation; I1 → WIP and schedule fragmentation; standing I4 → technical validation; I5 → clerk/query load). Variant evidence is used, not recited: Urgent bypass falling 74%→58%, Complex 3.1×/2.4× propensity, repeats clustering after night shifts and M2, clerks burned on recollection calls. Rejecting I1/I2/I3/I9 *as written* and converting them into capped, triggered, or inverted policies is the right move. Triggers have hysteresis. Residual risks include redesign-induced ones (priority inflation, auto-release rollback, flex-pool contention). Leading vs lagging is cleanly split.

**Factual / quantitative slips.** The S2 claim that “roughly 3–5% of daily assay capacity is burned on repeats” is wrong as written. Peak repeat initiations are 1,260 against ~8,500 assay completions (~14.8%), and repeat-loop re-entries are ~1,080 of ~10,250 assay starts (~10.5%). The 3–5% figure is, at best, the *incremental* gap versus the normal-period rate, and even that is a rough reconstruction. That is a material misstatement in a capacity argument that the rest of the design leans on. Repeat rate is also given as 6.9% normal; depending on denominator it is ~6.7% or ~7.2%. Small, but it sits next to the larger error.

**Process-reasoning gaps.** The prompt states that Complex-panel orders require *multiple assays*. The mix shift is analyzed almost entirely as a medical-validation / specialist-review problem (24.5%→47% of technical-validation outflow; ~26%→50% of releases). That is real, but it understates assay-load inflation: a 13%→20% Complex share plus multi-assay occupancy is a different machine stress than “+43% weekly volume.” P3’s ~30–35% Urgent machine-time cap is then poorly reconciled with peak Urgent ~29% *plus* Complex ~20%, both of which the text says must be protected. Routine as sole shock absorber is asserted more cleanly than the remaining machine budget allows.

**Overclaim and speculation.** “p90 back toward 18h” is unsupported. Normal Urgent p90 is already 19h; peak median is 17h; Register→Receive is 8h in the peak performance DFG and consumes a large fraction of the contractual 18h before the lab even starts. Smoothing and restoring bypass can help the internal segment; they cannot be sold as recovering the SLA without that budget decomposition. S2’s “samples age toward stability limits, feeding new recollections” is not in the log. Recollection growth is evidenced (4.2%→8.0%, abandonment 38%→47%); specimen-expiry as the mechanism is invented.

**Unclarities and looseness.** “No single bottleneck” overstates a chain that clearly has primary constraints (technical validation >100%, assay 98% / 18h, medical validation 22h, specialist 36h). P4’s Complex cell credits I10, which belongs to P6. “Acceptable” is never given numeric post-redesign targets by class and scenario (e.g., Urgent p90, Routine p90 vs 72h, Complex after 12h partner delay). The dashboard is good but is ten leading indicators, not “the few.” Time-to-breach dispatch assumes remaining-work estimates the flattened order-level log does not provide; this is admitted in residual #7, which helps, but the policy is still built as if that engine exists.

**Net.** This is a competent, evidence-using redesign with the right architecture (smooth/clean intake, kill repeats, adaptive not static priority, protect the end of the line, trigger overflow). Hypercritically, the quantitative capacity claim is inaccurate, Complex multi-assay load is under-modeled, the Urgent SLA claim is optimistic, and a few causal and packaging details are sloppy. That keeps it well below a top-band score.
