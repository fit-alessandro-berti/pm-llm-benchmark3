**6.2**

The answer is structurally complete and often operationally literate, but it is not nearly flawless. Under strict grading it loses substantial credit for arithmetic errors in the process-mining evidence, internally inconsistent policy design, hidden capacity, and an underspecified core control mechanism.

### What is actually strong
The seven required sections are present and used for their intended purpose. The four scenarios vary mix and disruption type, not just volume, and are tied to the logs. Bottleneck migration is the best part of the answer: recovery floods after an assay outage, phantom idle capacity in front of medical/specialist gates, Urgent-first raising total WIP, and I8/I2/I3/I4 pushing work onto the next saturated station. The redesign correctly refuses naive I1/I2/I3, treats assay and medical validation as constraints, and includes a redesign-caused vulnerability. The table covers the required columns without fake cycle-time claims. Leading vs lagging metrics are distinguished.

That is a competent operations memo. It is not a precise or fully coherent one.

### Process-mining inaccuracies
Several “evidence” percentages are computed on inconsistent denominators, and the errors all inflate the peak story.

- Technical→Medical is reported as ~24.5% → ~47.2%. The normal figure is 3900/15890 = 24.5% of technical outflows. The peak figure uses 3420/7240 (Assay→Technical), not technical outflows 3420+4050+610 = 8080, which is **42.3%**. Same mistake for Technical→Hold: 610/7240 = 8.4%, versus 610/8080 = **7.6%**.
- Assay→Repeat ~15.5% uses 1260/8140 (Test preparation→Assay). From assay outflows, 1260/(7240+1260) = **14.8%**.
- Preparation→Batch wait ~13.8% uses 1180/8530. From preparation outflows, 1180/(8140+1180) = **12.7%**.

Directionally the congestion narrative survives; as process-mining evidence it is sloppy. A judge asked to reward use of DFG evidence should penalize this. The answer also never notes incomplete peak flows (Batch wait 1180→1030, Hold 610→560, Repeat 1260→1200), which matter for interpreting queues versus dropped/censored work.

### Logical flaw: “scenario-specific” policies that are always on in the observed peak
This is the largest design failure.

Historical peak already has Urgent median 17h and p90 33h, medical queue 22h, specialist queue 36h, Hold→Medical 48h, Assay→Repeat well above 10%. The triggers are:

- P5: Urgent p50 > 16h or p90 > 24h
- P1: medical queue > 8h
- P2: repeat share > 10%
- P3: specialist queue > 18h

All of these fire for the entire documented winter wave. P5 is explicitly sold as a **short-lived, time-boxed override** of the guarded dispatch rule, yet it would be the default peak regime. That reintroduces the I1 starvation/WIP problem the answer itself warns about. P1–P3 would also run concurrently; there is no conflict resolution among evening medical staffing, larger Routine batches, Urgent-first, hold-criteria tightening, and outsourcing.

Trigger calibration is also internally inconsistent. P4 uses Register→Receive > 10h, which is **above** the peak median of 8h, so the courier playbook might not activate in the historical courier-stress period, while P5 activates immediately. That is not a usable control system.

### Hidden capacity and infeasible service levels
I6/R1 promises a 2-hour defect decision while pre-analytical peak utilization is 96% and median queue is 9.5h. That SLA is incompatible with the station’s own queue data unless poor samples skip the queue or staff are added. The “assumption” that “triage staffing covers arrival peaks” is extra capacity smuggled in after the constraint “do not simply recommend adding capacity everywhere.” I5 is at least labeled as peak-only extra capacity; I6 is not.

I4 is placed in the **robust, always-available** package, but technical validators are “cross-trainable in 4 weeks.” A 3-week peak cannot use I4 unless training was already done. Treating thresholded flex as a winter-wave control ignores the only training-time fact in the brief.

### The actual robust mechanism is underspecified
R3 is the center of the redesign: three streams, daily capacity shares, WIP caps before assay and medical validation. No shares, no cap-setting method, no statement of what gets cut when assay is at 98% and medical at 93% with Urgent 18h and Routine 72h both live. “Do not assume all case types can be prioritized simultaneously” is acknowledged in prose and then deferred to an unspecified board. Repeat-after-failure (worst p90: 132h/240h, double assay consumption, clustered after M2/nights) is folded into “Routine/Repeat,” which is the wrong aggregation given the variant behavior.

I9 is never accepted or rejected, despite being the clearest “optimize release work, damage waiting-time” trap in the candidate list.

### Other unclarities and residual-design gaps
- Tightening Hold-for-specialist criteria is proposed as a scarcity response with no safety/false-negative discussion in a diagnostic lab.
- Urgent “slack” uses median remaining step times; those medians are exactly what blow up in peak (Technical→Medical 8h→24h, Hold→Medical 18h→48h). Steering with unstressed medians is optimistic.
- The “minimum” dashboard is not minimum: a dozen-plus leading series. Thresholds are mostly data-aware, but quantity undercuts the requirement.
- Specialist 87% utilization with a 36h queue is treated like a ordinary high-util station; the data more strongly imply a tiny, highly variable pool (one person off every second Friday). The absence scenario is right; the utilization interpretation is incomplete.

### Net
The answer demonstrates real bottleneck-migration reasoning and does not optimize the average. It also misstates DFG shares, designs peak policies that collapse into “turn everything on,” hides capacity inside I6/I4, and leaves the only non-priority-conflict mechanism (stream shares / WIP caps) unspecified. Those are not polish issues. They are the difference between a strong draft and a reliable operating design.
