**6.3**

The answer is a strong, literate design that correctly refuses the lender’s ill-posed request, refuses to code rejects/withdrawals/opens as non-default, accounts for all 48,000 cases, and puts temporal validation and leakage control at the center. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, several issues are central to the task (honest estimands, process time scales, as-of maturity, and a skeleton that actually implements the design), not cosmetic.

### What is actually good
The core diagnosis is right: 90-day default is only defined after activation; treating the 21,600-row export as the population, or filling omitted cases with `Y=0`, would contaminate the negative class and encode underwriting selection as “safety.” The cohort table reconciles 18,900 + 2,700 + 3,600 + 9,100 + 5,700 + 1,740 + 1,160 + 5,100 = 48,000. The leakage table hits every required element with the right keep/repair/drop calls. Random splits are rejected for the right reasons (policy break, migration, label lag, time’s arrow). Section 8’s three performative-feedback stories are process-specific and decision-relevant. Short cases are correctly flagged as an immortal-time problem *in prose*.

### Major problems

**1. The primary estimand is not one quantity.**  
Section 1 names a five-state CIF \(\mathbb{P}(J=1\mid\mathcal{H}_k)\) and, in the same breath, the conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}(D_{90d}=1\mid A=1,\mathcal{H}_k)\) as “the” primary estimand. Those answer different questions and need different cohorts, clocks, and metrics. The product decomposition is algebraically fine for complete data, but it does not license treating both as the operational target. The CIF of *activated-and-defaulted* is mostly a funnel probability; it is a weak “early warning of default” signal. The text never chooses.

**2. IPSW contradicts the paper’s own identification claims.**  
Section 1 and the assumption table correctly brand \(Y(1)\perp A\mid\mathcal{H}_k\) as **fundamentally untestable**. Section 3 then puts inverse probability of *selection* weights \(1/\hat{e}(X_k)\) in the **primary** analysis of the activated-only model. That reweighting is exactly an attempt to transport activated outcomes onto the full intake—i.e., the counterfactual the authors just ruled out. IPCW for immature follow-up among the activated is coherent; IPSW as primary is not. Manski bounds are then offered as sensitivity without stating they apply to a different estimand than the observed-data CIF (for \(J=1\), rejects are observed competing events, not missing labels).

**3. One-clock competing risks smash two time scales together.**  
Rejection, withdrawal, and transfer occur on *process time from application start*. 90-day default lives on a clock that **starts at Account Activated**, a post-baseline event. A Fine-Gray / cause-specific Cox model with \(J\in\{1,\ldots,5\}\) and “\(T=\) process time or \(t_{\text{activation}}+t_{\text{default}}\)” is not a small simplification; it is a misspecified multi-state process. The honest structure is sequential: prefix \(\to\) {reject, withdraw, transfer, activate, still open} and, **only if activated**, a 90-day (censored) default clock. Jumping from a prefix-time probability \(\mathbb{P}(J=1\mid\mathcal{H}_k)\) to Cox/Fine-Gray without that nesting is a statistical-design error, not a notation choice.

**4. Validation maturity is tied to the wrong timestamp.**  
Splits are by **case start**. Label maturity is a function of **activation time** plus 90+35 days. Claiming 1 Jul–30 Sep 2025 starts are “fully matured” because 30 Sep + 125 d = 2 Feb 2026 is false: a case that starts in September and activates after 13 Oct 2025 is still immature on 15 Feb 2026, and some Jul–Sep starts will still be open. Train/val have the same hole for slow files. A serious design would filter or censor *inside* each split with an as-of rule on activation and `label_available_at`, not assume start-time windows buy maturity.

### Further deductions (each enough to move the score)

- **Asymmetric maturity is mishandled in the skeleton.** In survival/CIF analysis, an observed default at day 20 is an event even if 125 days have not elapsed; only non-events need the full horizon-plus-feed wait. The code sets `activated_immature` from `act_time + 125d` alone and never consults `label_available_at` after parsing it. That column is the delayed-label mechanism they spent a subsection on.
- **Section 4 vs Section 9.** Prose: never drop short cases; canonicalize migration names; collapse 5-second duplicates; support elapsed-time prefixes. Code: `get_prefixes_from_log(..., length=3)` (standard behavior drops \(n<3\)), no mapping, no dedup, no \(\Delta t\) prefixes, then `dataset["cohort_status"].value_counts()` printed as “Full Intake (N=48,000)”—which is only cases that survived prefix extraction. Activation time is computed and discarded, so the skeleton does not actually retain “start, activation, terminal, censoring, and label-maturity” as required.
- **`k=3` is not “Credit Review entry.”** That assumes a rigid Started → KYC → Review path. Document loops, skips, and the 15 Oct 2025 rename break the identification. Terminal detection via `"Reject" in act` / `"Transfer" in act` is the same fragility, and it is what the unimplemented canonical dictionary was supposed to prevent.
- **Collider language is overplayed.** Conditioning on future activation is a problem for using \(\mathbb{P}(D\mid A=1,\mathcal{H}_k)\) as a score on *raw intake* and for causal claims. It is not automatically “collider stratification bias” in a predictive model evaluated only on eventual activates. Overstating this blurs the predictive/causal line Section 8 is trying to draw.
- **Policy shift is under-modeled.** A dummy `is_post_july_2025_policy` is a level shift, not a different prefix–selection–outcome map. The prompt’s point is that the same prefix has a different selection probability and a different \(Y\)-relationship after 1 Jul 2025. That wants interactions, separate regimes, or explicit retraining—not one bit.
- **Dead or unjustified PM4Py.** `discover_dfg` is fit on length-3 training prefixes and never used; on truncated prefixes it does not recover real terminals. `documents_total` is repaired in the audit table and silently omitted (not repaired) in code. Partner permanently missing feeds never appear as a cohort row, despite being a stated MNAR risk.
- **Minor unclarities.** “Unresolvable multi-platform sync anomalies” is an exclusion with no operational rule (a knob for silent dropping). A macroeconomic index is not in the schema. Tenure repair assumes continuous employment. Transfer is absorbing \(J=5\) in the primary target and “external follow-up” for the 60% linked—those cannot both be true without a stated hierarchy.

### What keeps it well above a failing design
It does not commit the failure modes the prompt names as weak: it does not train blindly on 21,600 rows, does not call omitted cases non-default, does not keep `final_limit` / `decision_reason` / `collection_status`, and does not treat `pm4py.split_train_test` as the validation plan. The assumption table (testable / partially testable / untestable) is the most disciplined part of the write-up. Those are necessary for a high score; they are not sufficient when the estimand, the clocks, the test-set maturity rule, and the code/design contract are internally inconsistent.

**Net:** a serious, usable study outline with a broken identification story (CIF vs conditional vs IPSW-counterfactual), a misspecified competing-risk clock, a start-time split that does not guarantee label maturity, and a skeleton that drops the very as-of and completeness rules the prose insists on. That is a mid-6, not an 8–10.
