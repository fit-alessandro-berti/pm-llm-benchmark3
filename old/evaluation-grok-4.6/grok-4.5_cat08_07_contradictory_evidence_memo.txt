7.3

The memo chooses the right strategic posture (E, not A or B), keeps facts/interpretations/assumptions mostly separate, and actually uses the process-mining artifacts instead of name-checking them. Under a normal rubric this would score higher. Under the required hypercritical standard it is not close to flawless.

**Format and readability.** The executive memo likely exceeds the 700-word cap (roughly 800–900 words). It also dumps all of M1–M8 into the memo, which makes the “decision” section read like an appendix. Worse, comparison operators and arrows are systematically stripped: “EUR 184  161”, “p90 and complaint rates  baseline”, “full loaded cost  baseline”, “coordinator utilization sustainably  ~85%”, “billing-before-activation  0”. In a decision memo the go/no-go gates are the load-bearing sentences. Here they are ambiguous. “Addressappointment” / “installactivation” / “billingcomplaint” are the same defect. That is not cosmetic; it degrades the one artifact leadership would actually read.

**Logical tension in the recommended action.** E requires reliable case-start segmentation. The same memo states that customer-segment and building-risk attributes were incomplete before week 3 of a 6-week pilot, and it correctly lists “the skip rule is well-calibrated” and “ownership is separable from skip/billing” as *uncertain assumptions*. It then tells management to qualify cases immediately into simple low-risk houses vs. apartments/old stock and scale coordinator ownership plus merged validate/schedule on that cut. That is acting on the unvalidated classifier the VoI section says must still be tested (M6). The risk is named in the alternatives table and then underweighted in the action plan.

**Missing methodological caveats.** A 6-week pilot reporting p90 38.5 / p95 62.0 and 30-day complaints is an observation-window problem. Tail duration and post-close complaints are at least partly right-censored or dependent on unspecified follow-up. The answer never flags this, yet those tail/complaint figures are used as if they were fully observed national-quality facts. Pilot vs. baseline also differs in length, mix, and region; mix bias is handled well, duration/censoring is not. The invented 85% utilization cap and literal-zero conformance gates are presented as decision rules without being labeled as judgment.

**Conceptual slip on precision.** High fitness / low precision is explained serviceably, then blurred: low precision is a *model* property (extra behavior allowed relative to the log), not proof that “operating reality allows too much deviance.” Deviance is shown by alignments, DFG shifts, and outcome splits. Conflating the two is a real process-mining error, even if the operational conclusion is directionally right.

**Attribution is a bit too clean.** Item 10 treats survey-skip and post-billing activation as the “primary drivers” of apartment *and* old-building harm. The segment notes point to a miscalibrated skip rule for apartments and *missing permit checks* for old buildings—the latter is a different mechanism. Permits are handled later as a control, but the causal story in the evidence table over-bundles. Rural rescheduling and the SLA-improved-for-simple-houses detail are also underused.

**VoI ranking is defensible but inconsistent with the action.** Ranking M4/M6/M3/M1 high is reasonable. Ranking M5 last is not, given that coordinator ownership is the one design piece they will scale *now*, utilization is already 93% in two regions, and absence already stalls cases. If ownership is going into production this week, the resilience test is not a later “how” refinement; it is a condition of that scale-up. M7 as rank 5 is acceptable if treated as a gate rather than a learning experiment, and they do treat it that way.

**What still works.** A vs. B vs. C vs. D vs. E is evaluated without pretending a single intervention dominates. National scale is refused for the right reasons (tail, rework, billing-before-activation). Full rollback is refused for the right reasons (simple-house gains). The five metric conflicts are all explained, and DFG, alignments, temporal profiles, and both social networks are tied to the trade-off rather than listed. The evidence table’s type labels are mostly correct. The final recommendation’s structure (act / pause / scale / measure / threshold) matches the required shape.

Net: strong strategic synthesis with broken decision-critical notation, an over-eager hybrid rollout on incomplete segment data, and a few genuine analytical omissions. That is a good answer, not a near-flawless one.
