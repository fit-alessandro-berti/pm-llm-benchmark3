**6.1**

The memo picks a defensible option (E), follows the required structure, and does not blindly scale or blindly roll back. It is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading, several analytical errors, overclaims, and VoI mistakes keep it well below a high score.

**Strategic synthesis.** The core trade-off—fast standardized path versus control on complex work—is the right one, and the memo uses fitness/precision, alignments, and segment splits to justify stopping skip-and-bill-early while keeping simple-house gains. That is the strongest part of the answer. It then overreaches. It treats simple-house results as “proven,” recommends scaling coordinator ownership and merged scheduling **nationally** for that segment immediately, and underplays the comparability problem: 6-week pilot, four regions, more apartments/older buildings than baseline, incomplete segment/risk attributes before week 3, sparse start times, and truncated complaint cost. Alternative E says measure before wider scale; this answer converts that into a national rollout of the “good” slice. It also barely uses generalization (0.70 → 0.56), the working-together network, field-survey idle capacity versus second-visit saturation, SLA’s split result, or the rural/business deteriorations. Those are not ornaments; they change how far any hybrid can be pushed.

**Facts versus interpretation.** The three-column table is the right device and the eight items are mostly real metrics. Separation still leaks. Fact 4 fuses the old-building p90 move with the permit alignment into one “fact.” Fact 6 states coordinator utilization “reached 93%” as if it were the pilot-wide level; the source limits that to two regions. The memo says handovers were “successfully eliminated” (they fell from 34% to 12% of cases with 3+ handovers), that segment data “proves” simple-house gains, and that skip-and-permit bypass “leads to” unstructured rework. Permit absence is a move-on-model deviation, not a stated design of R. Survey skip was specified for some **medium-risk** addresses, not as the mechanism that made simple low-risk houses succeed; crediting those houses to “skipped survey logic” is a causal error. “Incentivized premature billing” is also wrong-register: early billing after an acceptable signal was a design choice, not an inferred incentive. Several assumption cells are generic data-quality caveats rather than the actual decision-critical uncertainties (mix bias, non-random pilot, incomplete cost after week 4).

**Metric conflicts.** All five required conflicts are addressed and the median/tail and cost-shift stories are directionally right. Precision is not a flower model and 0.94 fitness is not a log that “fits perfectly.” “Guarantees” unprepared arrivals, “destroying” resilience, and savings being “wiped out” are stronger than the evidence. The conflict section also omits the obvious composition channel already flagged in the table: a heavier apartment/old-building mix can fatten the tail even if the process change is only part of the effect.

**Alternatives A–E.** The advantage/risk/condition format is complete and A/B are correctly rejected. C is read fairly as dropping merged scheduling. Calling E “optimal” violates the instruction not to treat one intervention as dominant. The E write-up does not say how a split process will handle 93% coordinator load, 92% second-visit load, or absence-related stalling—the exact resilience failure the memo elsewhere treats as central. D is undersold: given the measurement holes, a controlled experiment is more reasonable than “only if we doubt simple-house benefits.”

**Value of information.** This is the weakest section. Ranking M1 near the bottom because “the pilot already showed the rule failed” ignores that the skip result is confounded. A stratified randomized skip test is high-VoI precisely because the current evidence cannot isolate the rule. Ranking M8 last is a strategic miss: the memo’s own trade-off is speed versus working service after install, and complaints already split along those two axes; M8 is not lagging decoration. M7 is mostly a control to implement now, not an uncertainty that would flip A versus E. M2 and M5 are undervalued relative to a plan that scales single-owner flow: sparse start timestamps leave wait-versus-work in the tail unidentified, and absence handling is the stated resilience failure. M4 as top-ranked is defensible for the money question, but full 60-day cost will not decide whether to keep billing-before-activation or skip on old buildings; those are already quality/compliance calls.

**Final recommendation.** The pause on survey skip for apartments/old buildings and on billing before activation is the correct immediate control. The 90% “accuracy in predicting zero-rework installations” gate is arbitrary and mixes classification accuracy with first-time-right. Broader rollout criteria ignore mix, SLA, NPS, and resource congestion. Immediate national scale of the simple-house path, while listing representativeness as still uncertain, is internally inconsistent.

The artifact looks like a competent executive memo and would likely be usable. Strictly, it overclaims causality, misattributes the skip rule, under-treats confounding, and ranks the next experiments in a way that would misallocate scarce measurement. That is well short of nearly flawless.
