**6.4**

The write-up is complete, well structured, and mostly faithful to the required headings. It separates hard rules from preferences, names override reasons, and is unusually strong on worker-facing explanation and audit design. It is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading, several decisions are inconsistent with the constraints the author themselves wrote down, the scoring system would not reproduce the table, and the baseline comparison is asserted rather than computed.

**Section 1 — mostly sound, slightly inflated “hard” set.**  
Objectives cover SLA, error/rework, continuity, fairness, training, and resilience without collapsing to throughput. Safety rules for skill 0, Security, Approval, unsupervised high-priority work, the batch cap of 4, and Quality batching are stated correctly. Two problems: (1) continuity-on-high-handover is promoted into a near-hard default, then immediately given an SLA override that is *not* in the original constraint 5 (original text: continuity unless owner skill is 0–1 *and* supervision is infeasible); (2) overtime avoidance is treated as hard in the narrative and soft in the scorer, so later assignments can “comply” with whichever version is convenient.

**Section 2 — good skeleton, not an executable policy.**  
Eligibility filtering, sequential case ordering, named score terms, tie-breaks, and exception codes are the right ingredients. The required components are all present. The algorithm still cannot be audited or replayed:

- Weights `0.35 / 0.20 / 0.20 / 0.15 / 0.05 / 0.05` never define domain, scaling, or normalization. Continuity (0.20) systematically beats overtime/fairness (0.15), which quietly nullifies constraint 4.
- Training is a 5% tie-breaker, not a constraint. Nothing in the scorer enforces “≥20% of normal/low assignments support documented training priorities.”
- SLA scoring depends on expected service, wait, and handover delay that are never estimated from the snapshot or from the historical medians (2.7h / 3.3h). “Score is high when slack is small but still recoverable” is circular.
- “High complexity,” “critically low hours,” and “severe SLA risk” are undefined, so Step 2’s continuity reject rule is not operational.
- SNA/collaboration appears only as the fourth tie-break, i.e. almost never.

The table format and reason/trade-off/risk columns are done properly.

**Section 4 — comparative claims without comparative work.**  
The question asked to compare the policy with first-available and specialist-only on this queue. The answer never instantiates those two policies. That is a major omission, not a presentation nit. Concrete gaps:

- First-available would send C002 (Approval) to Jonas (skill 1) and C003 to Jonas — exactly the unsafe pattern the prose gestures at, but never shows.
- Specialist-only would give Celine C003, C007, C009, C014, C016 (five cases, **breaking the batch cap**) and pile Farid on every Approval/Negotiation. The resilience critique is true and would have been decisive if computed.
- “Lower overall SLA risk” and “usually close to specialist-only on errors” are unearned. No expected wait, no rework multiplier, no hours check.

The trade-off framing is intellectually honest; the evidence is not.

**Section 5 — strongest section.**  
Non-punitive, layered (rules then weights), named reasons, invitation to override. This is actually usable on a shop floor. It still overclaims “the system checks… whether someone has already received too many new cases” while the batch itself stretches Eva, Celine, and Jonas without saying so.

**Section 6 — complete list, measurement recipes are thin.**  
All ten required indicator families are present, plus sensible cuts by `assignment_reason`. Good. Weaknesses: no formulas, no windows, no thresholds, no pre/post design, no handling of the fact that `reassignment_count` and `assignment_reason` are already in the log (so several “indicators” are just field checks, not mined discoveries). Handover-of-work vs. `current_owner` change is not distinguished. Training share depends on string-matching reasons — easy to game, which is ironic in an audit section.

**Section 7 — adequate sketch, not an implementation of the policy.**  
It touches every listed PM4Py symbol and the production logging advice is the right idea. Failures: `skill_matrix` is an empty stub; merge keys assume a long-format matrix that was never defined; several calls are used with optimistic signatures (`discover_batches`, `get_variants` on a formatted dataframe, `filter_event_attribute_values` without `retain`/level); there is **no candidate generator, no scorer, no “learn” step** beyond a comment about average service times. The section is audit glue, not a policy that could be trained or replayed.

**Cross-cutting.**  
The piece never defines complexity/handover cutoffs, never uses the 2.7h / 3.3h / 1.8× historical numbers in any calculation, and never checks hours-remaining against the urgent cluster (C002 2h + C011 4h on Farid with 6.5h is acknowledged as a sequencing risk and then left). Socio-technical content is mostly confined to Section 5. Explainability is real; reproducibility is not.

A generous reading of the same text is “strong design memo with a few contested calls.” Under the requested rubric — hypercritical, minor defects count — the C003/constraint-4 contradiction, the C015 training dodge, the non-operational scorer, and the missing worked baselines are not minor. This is a solid 6, not an 8–9.
