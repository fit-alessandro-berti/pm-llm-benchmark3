5.8

The answer is complete in format and often directionally sound, but it is not reliable enough to score much higher. It repeatedly misuses the event log, invents precision the data do not support, and then builds operating policies on those mistakes.

**Evidence handling is sloppy.** Several headline statistics use inconsistent denominators. Peak medical-validation demand is stated as 41.4% of volume and later as 47.2% of peak cases; the first counts mixed entries onto the medical/specialist path, the second is a share of technical-validation inflows, and neither is “percent of cases.” Direct technical-validation release is given as 55.9% in the peak period; against technical-validation outcomes it is about 50%. Specialist-review “8.4% of technical validation outputs” is actually 610/7240 inflows, not 610/8080 outputs. Assay-repeat risk is 6.2% of registered cases in the normal period and 14.8% of assay completions in the peak period; those are not the same rate. Scenario D claims “>12% of intake” goes to manual accession correction; 12.3% is pre-analytical defects combined, and accession correction is about 4%. Peak-period counts (420, 610, 780, 1,260, 3,700) are treated as if they were a single operating state, and historical peak p90s are written as if they were the causal effect of I1.

**Scenario B conflates two different shocks.** The peak DFG already includes both analyzers. Using the peak repeat edge (1,260/8,500) as the M1-outage failure rate understates a true M1 loss and overstates what the peak log can prove. There is no M1/M2 volume split in the materials, so the “>15% first-pass failure” and “failure feedback loop” are asserted, not measured.

**Bottleneck migration is structurally good and locally wrong.** The sequence intake → pre-analytics → accession/recollection → batch/assay → technical validation → medical/specialist is the right skeleton, and the critiques of raw I1, I3, I4, I8, and I9 match the conflict hints. That is the strongest part of the answer. It then proposes a “robust” fix that recreates the same error: pull preparation and registration staff into accession while preparation is already at 89% utilization with an 11-hour queue. “Off-peak hours” is not a resource that exists in Scenarios A or D. The binding 101% technical-validation constraint is mostly “protected,” not redesigned. I6 is oversold as a bypass of the 9.5-hour pre-analytical queue; it only fails bad samples faster. Same-day recollection is not implied by a 2-hour reject rule when redraw still depends on the patient and the courier.

**Two scenario policies are internally contradictory.** Policy B triggers on M2 error >10% and then “channel[s] 100% of Urgent… directly to M2.” If M1 is down that is tautological; if M2 is the quality problem it puts the SLA-critical work on the worse machine. Policy C sends “routine Complex-panel” work to the partner and keeps Urgent, abnormal, and inpatient work in-house. Those retained cases are exactly the ones that need the missing specialist; overflow then relieves the wrong queue. “Routine Complex-panel” also blurs case types the prompt kept distinct.

**Much of the redesign is unearned numerical theater.** 45-minute urgent caps, 3-hour routine time-boxes, batch size 16–18 “capturing 80% of setup efficiency,” 150 cases/hour metering, 140% instantaneous intake, 70% of volume in a 3-hour window, and 4-hour M2 calibration are not derived from the DFG or the queue table. The 4-hour wash cycle would cut capacity on the already fragile analyzer. Courier color-coding, optical serum checks, and autovalidation help the narrative more than they are supported by the log. Autovalidation is especially thin: most routine cases already leave from technical validation (73.6% normally).

**The table and dashboard inherit the overclaims.** I6 does not give Urgent cases a “major” bypass of pre-analytics. Courier smoothing does not “eliminate” Scenario D delay. Cross-training’s “next bottleneck” is listed as technical validation, which the design is trying to spare; the real next station is preparation. Complex-panel p90 target <80 h is stricter than the normal-period p90 of 118 h. Green post-analytic WIP <100 is not calibrated to Little’s law on the stated peak flows. Defect “interception rate” is a confounded leading indicator: under I6 a rising rate may mean the gate is working.

The answer avoids the worst failure mode—optimize mean cycle time and add staff everywhere—and it does separate standing design from triggered policy. That is why it is not a low score. It is not a high score because a process-mining redesign that misreads its own frequencies, routes work onto the failing resource, and exports the cases that do not need the missing specialist is not “nearly flawless.”
