**6.0**

The final write-up is complete, on-format, and clearly better than a generic Lean dump: it uses the frequency DFG, performance DFG, alignment/fitness figures, rework rates, segment outcomes, and resource constraints, and it keeps the core contradiction (fast path 31% cheaper before rework, only 9% after; 14.1% vs 3.3% complaint rates) in view. That is not enough for a high score under a strict rubric. Several design claims rest on misread artifacts, invented parameters, and a forbidden dominance claim.

**Diagnosis is the strongest section, but it overreaches.** Fitness 0.91 with 68% fitting traces is treated as “significant policy breaches” without separating partial alignments from wholesale non-conformance. The claim that outdated auto-screen rules “force many low-risk cases into manual review” is not supported by the DFG; the alignment evidence points the other way (high-value dispatch without risk review, fast approval before evidence completeness). R3 is ignored despite a concrete leak: 120 reopenings go to the escalation board, which can deny or fulfill without a guaranteed manual-eligibility pass. Idle quality-check capacity and the Monday/campaign fraud bottleneck are also unused.

**Redesign B is built on a material process-mining error.** Premium high-value “96 hours reviewed/repair path” is end-to-end case time, not repair duration. The performance DFG already shows quote wait (mean 22h, p90 60h), customer-approval wait (mean 30h, p90 80h), and repair itself (mean 20h). Targeting “express repair in 48 hours vs. current 96 hours” therefore misstates both the baseline and the bottleneck. A 48-hour end-to-end high-value path is not a redesign of repair; it would require collapsing quote, customer wait, and parts confirmation—none of which is designed. The same 96h figure appears earlier as `Dispatch replacement → Customer complaint/reopen` mean time; using two different 96-hour artifacts interchangeably is sloppy.

**Several numbers and policies are invented.** A EUR 200 Standard cutoff has no basis in R1 (EUR 400) or the segment tables. “Adds ~10 hours median” for mandatory review is a guess. Putting a 6–18 hour pre-dispatch gate on *all* replacements, including Premium low-risk accessories already at 8-hour median with a 24-hour credit cliff, is not segment-aware and can create the service-credit exposure the diagnosis itself flags. Staffing a “dedicated Premium team” on a path that already meets the 24-hour bar, while seven reviewers queue above 82% utilization and fraud has only two specialists, fights the resource data.

**Redesign C is the most generic of the three.** Real-time risk scores, 30/70 cutoffs, weekly retraining, and a customer-visible “risk score” (then relabeled Standard/Premium, which is already an attribute) is stock analytics routing, not a mechanism grounded in these traces. Reserving 20% of already-saturated manual capacity as an “escalation buffer” would worsen the documented queue, not relieve it.

**The table violates an explicit constraint.** Marking the current process “dominated? Yes” claims Pareto dominance the prompt forbids. Several cells are also incomplete or one-sided (A’s cycle-time row is a fragment; C’s cycle-time line is barely a claim).

**Experiments are directionally right and then poorly specified.** A/B testing and a 30-day lag check for displaced complaints are the right causal idea. Framing “increase cycle time by 15 hours” as a *target*, and using <10% complaints / <5% violations as first-pilot go-gates without a control-relative formulation, treats noisy levels as proof. Splitting A (rules+gate, then portal later) after already selling A as one package weakens the learning design.

**What still works:** A, B, and C are architecturally different (static segment rules + gate vs. two-tier repair-first vs. dynamic routing). Sacrifices are stated. Failure modes for A correctly reuse the prompt’s 15–35% manual inflow and portal-abandonment risks. The answer does not optimize only average cycle time and does distinguish Premium/Standard and accessory/high-value more often than not.

Those strengths keep it well above a failing or purely generic answer. The 96-hour misread, the dominance claim, invented thresholds, resource-inconsistent staffing, and a generic third design are enough to keep it far from “nearly flawless.”
