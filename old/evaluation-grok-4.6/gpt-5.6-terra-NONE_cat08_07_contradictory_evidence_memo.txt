8.2

The memo is strategically coherent and chooses the only alternative that satisfies the constraints: do not nationalize R, do not discard the simple-house gains, keep facts/interpretations/assumptions distinct, and treat the process-mining artifacts as decision inputs rather than decoration. Recommendation E, hardened with C’s control suspensions, is the right call and is argued as a trade-off rather than a winner-take-all story.

What keeps it from the top of the scale is not a wrong decision; it is incomplete use of the evidence the prompt made load-bearing, plus a few places where confidence outruns the data.

The segment result that the entire rollout boundary rests on is weaker than the memo admits. Customer-segment and building-risk attributes are incomplete before week 3 of a six-week pilot, so a large share of the 9,200 cases cannot cleanly support “simple low-risk houses are clear beneficiaries.” That limitation is listed in the evidence table and then underweighted in the recommendation. A stricter memo would treat “scale for simple houses” as a provisional eligibility rule, not a demonstrated operating model, until the segment cut can be reconstructed on complete attributes.

Several process-mining artifacts are used only atmospherically. Fitness/precision, alignments, handover vs. working-together networks, and the segment table are handled well. DFG edge shifts, the temporal-profile relations (especially Billing start → Complaint escalation as a new long-tail, and Failed visit → Second visit delay under 93% coordinator load), SLA’s split (worse overall, better for simple houses), the field-survey utilization collapse (78% → 52%) against second-visit saturation (66% → 92%), and the complaint-mix shift from wait-time to “installed but not working” / “billed before activation” are either omitted or mentioned too lightly. Those are not flavor; they are the mechanism that explains why the median improved while the tail, rework, and downstream cost deteriorated.

Methodological silence on the duration metrics is a real gap. A six-week intake with p95 = 62 days implies either a follow-up window that is never specified or completed-case bias that would distort tails. Sparse start timestamps are acknowledged in the VOI list (M2, ranked 7th) but never treated as a threat to the cycle-time comparison itself. Ranking M2 that low is defensible if one cares only about waiting-vs-processing design; it is not defensible if one is using p90/p95 as a go/no-go gate.

The evidence table is mostly clean and exceeds the eight-item floor, but fact rows leak interpretation (“simplified transfer of work,” “created resource concentration,” “weakly constrained”). Manual activation 7% → 15% never appears as its own fact, despite being one of the clearest rework signals.

VOI reasoning is ranked and causal, which is the right structure. Two weaknesses: (1) the final action list launches M1–M8, which flattens the ranking into a measurement program rather than a decision-information portfolio; (2) an 8–12 week study cannot mature 60-day cost (M4) on more than the earliest cases, so the economic threshold they set cannot actually be scored on the timeline they propose.

Thresholds are directionally correct but underspecified (“agreed tolerance” on p90/p95; no numeric utilization cap despite 93%/92% being the resilience finding). Opening language (“expand the redesign” for simple houses) is slightly broader than the later, more surgical list (coordinator + merged validate/schedule only). A reader of section 1 alone could think full R, including survey skip, is being scaled for that segment.

None of this makes the answer unsound. It is a strong, usable executive product. Under the stated bar—hypercritical, minor defects count, very high scores reserved for near-flawless work—it is very good rather than excellent.
