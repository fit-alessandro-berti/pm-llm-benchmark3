3.2

The answer has the right skeleton and a defensible high-level stance (no single “X%” claim; expand only with evaluation), but it is not close to the standard implied by the rubric. It fails quantitative discipline, misstates the panel, leaves required identification and process content incomplete, and treats uncertainty as a disclaimer rather than a constraint on claims.

**Section 1.** The availability vs intensity vs selection distinction is present and no explicit causal effect is asserted. That is the high point. The contemporaneous-change summary is still wrong in a policy-relevant way: the May complexity jump is described as concentrated in adopting branches, but every branch moves up by about 5pp. Routing, the fraud desk, and the product change are flattened into one fog of “policy shifts,” so the reader cannot see which shocks are common and which are bundled with treatment.

**Section 2.** The table columns exist, but the estimands are labels, not estimands. Horizons are “12 months” on an 8-month panel. ITT is restricted to “adopted branches,” which is not an ITT. Intensity is written as a linear “+10%” effect in a sample whose never-treated units have almost no intensity variation. `MEDIAN_Tail` names the wrong location measure. Rework is recoded as “error rates.” Heterogeneity is a regression interaction with a time-varying, post-shock covariate, not a defined subgroup or dynamic effect. The required discussion of interference (shared fraud desk, rerouted arrivals) and treatment versions (availability vs fast-path use vs local practice change) is missing. “Partially, with bias” / “Not identifiable alone” is not an identification statement.

**Section 3.** Calling the design an event study does not make it one. Month fixed effects do not absorb the April routing campaign that sent extra volume specifically to Alpha and Beta. Conditioning on post-treatment complexity and arrivals risks adjusting away part of the bundled intervention. The write-up never names a staggered-adoption estimator that is robust to heterogeneous/dynamic effects; it only says TWFE can be bad. The synthetic-control alternative is invalid on its face: Gamma is a later-treated unit, not a donor pool, and one donor cannot support a synthetic control. The third row targets an intensity estimand with a binary DiD. The “why primary is better” paragraph is generic and does not wrestle with five clusters, two adoption dates, and a treatment that is not a binary switch.

**Section 4.** Two required mechanisms are absent: tail amplification and survey selection. Several filled rows are factually off. Beta’s p90 does not “drop slightly”; it rises from the low 90s into the mid-100s after April. Alpha’s median is not “stable” through the FTE loss (48.3 → 49.5 → 53.5). Fast-path “harmful” is specified as the median falling, which is not a harmful signature. The table never separates substitution (easy cases leaving the manual queue) from capacity creation, which is the central process question.

**Section 5.** This section should have been the empirical core and is the largest failure of care. Alpha post-period p90 is ~108h, not 106.1. Delta’s Apr–Aug median change is about +5.0h, not +4.8 from a 39.3 post mean. Epsilon’s analogous change is on the order of +4h, not +1.4. Gamma is labeled “Apr–Aug avg” while the 37.2 figure is Jul–Aug, so the table header and the number do not match; using Jan–Mar as Gamma’s pre-window also throws away the informative not-yet-treated months. The intensity claim “r ≈ −0.6” is unshown and collinear with calendar time and the August volume drop. Pre-trend “stability” over three months does not address selection on historically bad performance or regression to the mean. The complexity adjustment (+5pp → +2h) is an invented elasticity. The useful staggered contrast—Gamma still untreated in Apr–Jun while Alpha/Beta are treated, then Gamma treated in Jul–Aug, with August improving for never-treated units too—is not computed.

**Section 6.** Coverage of the named threats is adequate as a checklist. The logic is not. Selection is assigned “downward bias in gains” and the consequence “overestimate gains” in the same row. If controls also deteriorate after April, that does not, by itself, bias DiD toward finding losses. Survey weighting is proposed after the prompt already said response counts are unavailable. “Check p90” is not a test of ecological aggregation. Clustered SEs with five branches are mentioned, then ignored when later confidence numbers are issued.

**Section 7.** Sign language is inconsistent (“negative” as worse for medians, “negative” as more routing for escalation). Confidence of 50–70 is too high for an unidentified bundled treatment in five units. The safe CSAT claim is not safe: means move by ~0.1 in untreated branches, response rates are 20–45% with no counts, and selection into surveys is unmeasured. Alpha rework does not “revert in Aug”; it stays at 14.5. Escalation’s March fraud-desk jump is not separated cleanly from the April adopter increment. The option to say “not identified” is barely used where it is most warranted (median, CSAT).

**Section 8.** `expand with an embedded evaluation` is the least-wrong category, and that is credited. The design itself would not identify much. Delta is the easiest, lowest-complexity branch, so success there does not answer whether the policy works for the weak-branch population that was actually targeted. A 4-week ramp with monthly KPIs, a 2% median bar (~0.8h at Delta’s baseline), and a 0.15 CSAT tripwire are noise-dominated rules. There is no freeze or independent assignment of routing and staffing, no wait-list or case-level randomization, no plan to keep a never-treated series, and no fraud-desk queue instrumentation beyond a sentence. Operational caution is gestured at; causal learning is not designed.

**Section 9.** This is not a reproducible analysis of the supplied panel. Gamma and Epsilon are omitted from the CSV, so staggered adoption cannot be estimated from the code as written. There is no validation of a 5×8 balanced panel, no trend display of the five outcomes, no relative-time indicators, no descriptive panel model, no intensity model, no clustered standard errors, and no statsmodels (or equivalent) fit. `pd.io.StringIO` is not the usual loader. A print about small N does not substitute for the required models or for a warning about interpreting branch-month medians as if they were case-level effects. Relative time is a comment saying “(conceptual).”

**Section 10.** The ladder format is correct; the content is not. Controls did not show “identical” cycle-time and rework increases. Intensity–median correlation is not a strong “suggestive” claim once time and volume are acknowledged. The “conditionally causal” bullet invents “March routing changes” (routing is April and targeted; the fraud desk is March and global) and then concludes a null that those assumptions would not actually identify. The final “Not supported” bullet is the only one that is cleanly right.

**Net.** Leadership would correctly be told not to announce an X% cycle-time win, but they would also be given wrong arithmetic, a mutilated control set, a fake synthetic-control design, overconfident CSAT/median reassurance, and code that cannot reproduce the study. Under strict grading that is a structured, partially oriented answer with material factual, identification, and reproducibility failures, not a high-quality causal-policy analysis.
