**6.3**

The write-up is complete, well structured, and unusually honest about trade-offs. The hard/soft split, worker explanation, audit list, and PM4Py sketch are the strongest parts. Under a strict reading it is not nearly flawless: the algorithm is not reproducible, and the actual roster for the critical cases does not survive the evidence the answer itself cites.

**Section 1** is mostly sound. Safety rules are separated from preferences, and the text does not pretend there is a single optimum. The rest of the answer does not consistently obey that split.

**Section 2** names every required scoring family, but it is not an executable policy.

- Terms are declared normalized to \([0,1]\). `CONT` and `FAIR` go negative; `SLARISK` is unbounded. Urgent weights then subtract an unbounded risk term, so the “explainable weighted sum” is not on a stable scale.
- `sla_reference`, `est_effort`, the numeric mapping of handover bands, and the high-complexity threshold are undefined. Two auditors can replay the same log and get different eligible sets and different winners.
- Eligibility allows `role == "preparer_only"` for Final Approval, but that role is never defined as a resource state.
- Queue order is greedy by urgency with no backtrack. That design choice is fine only if later capacity collisions are detected. They are not.

Because no case is scored with the formula, Section 3 cannot be shown to be an output of Section 2. That is a serious explainability failure for a policy whose selling point is auditability.

**Section 3 has material assignment errors.**

*C001 is the clearest break.* The answer cites a 2.7 h median wait for an owner change on high-complexity work and a 3.3 h service inflation when a weak owner is kept. The SLA is 3 h. “Hugo leads execution” *is* an owner change (2.7 h). “Celine executes, skill 1” *is* a weak owner (3.3 h). A supervised pair does not cancel either penalty; it adds coordination cost. Calling this a way to “dodge the 2.7 h handover penalty” contradicts the answer’s own evidence. Fallback “Hugo takes it solo” admits the design is already expected to collapse. `org:resource` for a pair with a designated lead is also undefined, so the audit indicators in Section 6 cannot be applied cleanly.

*Capacity is not checked against hours.* Farid gets two urgents (C002 at 2 h, C011 at 4 h) plus C010, with 6.5 h left and five open cases. If C002 uses most of the 2 h window, C011 is already in breach territory. Celine is given C001 (complexity 0.88), C007 (0.81, 6 h SLA, already three reassignments), and C009 review, with 6 h left. Eva gets two high-priority cases in 5 h. Hugo is lead on C001, plus C014, C015, supervision, and shadows, with 5.5 h left. The batch check only counts assignment *cardinality* (cap of 4). That is not a feasibility check.

*Rule 4 is broken on C003.* David is overtime-risk high, 14 open cases, 3 h left. The case is normal priority with 18 h slack. Rule 4 allows high-OT load only when SLA risk is urgent and no safer alternative exists. Celine is Diagnosis 3 with low overtime risk. Continuity and the reassignment threshold are real concerns, but they are soft. Treating them as sufficient to override a non-urgent OT rule collapses the hard/soft distinction from Section 1.

*The 20 % training claim is not supported.* Normal/low cases are nine. Only C009 is a real documented-priority execution. C015 is Hugo executing while Eva and Jonas *observe*; both have Security 0, so under the answer’s own H1 they cannot execute even supervised. Observation is not an assignment that “supports documented training priorities.” Without C015 the share is \(1/9 \approx 11\%\). C012 is labeled “suitable for trainee” and is given to a Quality 3 specialist. C010 is labeled “potential trainee approval under supervision”; Amal, Gita, and Jonas have Approval 1 and may prepare under rule 7, but the text only considers David and Hugo (Approval 0) and drops the idea. Hugo is then “invited to shadow” an approval he is forbidden to perform.

*C001 is also mislabeled as training.* Celine’s documented priority is negotiation, not security.

**Section 4** reasons well at the level of regimes and correctly refuses a single winner. It is still incomplete: first-available and specialist-only are never instantiated on C001–C016, so claims about queues, fairness, and SLA risk are unfalsifiable. Specialist-only on C001 would simply be Hugo or Gita; first-available would ignore the 0.88 complexity and the high handover band. Showing those two rosters was the obvious way to make the comparison auditable.

**Section 5** is the best section: non-punitive, rule-first, and it invites dispatcher override. It overstates cleanliness (“the same six questions, in this order”) relative to the actual weighted sum, and it repeats the untrue story that Farid received both urgents because the fit was clean rather than because the policy stacked them.

**Section 6** hits every required indicator and ties them to drift vs declared policy. Good. Residual issues: replay cannot work until `org:resource` for pairs is defined; “unjustified” OT assignments need a closed-form test; C001-style pair assignments would scramble handover and skill-mismatch measures.

**Section 7** mentions the required PM4Py surfaces and the learning loop is the right idea. It is still a sketch with holes: `filter_event_attribute_values` is applied to `case_complexity_band`, which is not in the log; `hard_filter`, `score`, `extract_assignment_decisions`, and `resource_state_at` are unspecified; reconstructing resource state at each historical timestamp is the actual hard problem and is hand-waved. That would not audit the policy as written.

Net: a strong socio-technical essay with a policy-shaped algorithm and a roster that does not implement it. The decisive case (C001) is internally inconsistent with the cited time penalties; training and overtime constraints are satisfied on paper more than in the assignments; parameters needed for replay are missing. That is well above a thin or single-objective answer, and well below a nearly flawless one.
