**6.0**

The answer is complete, well structured, and stylistically strong. It hits every required heading, refuses a single-optimum claim, and is unusually good on worker explanation, audit design, and PM4Py coverage. Under a hypercritical reading, that is not enough. The core applied test is the queue, and that is where the answer breaks.

**Section 3 contradicts the problem’s own evidence and constraints.** C001 is an urgent, 3h, complexity-0.88 Security Review whose notes say “needs specialist.” The answer keeps performer R3 (Security = 1) with R8 sign-off. That is *rule-legal*, but it ignores the historical fact the answer itself quotes: keeping a weakly skilled owner adds a median **3.3 hours of service time**. That extra time is larger than the remaining SLA. Constraint 5 prefers continuity when supervision is feasible; it does not require making the weak owner the performer of a 3h enterprise security task. The better reading is specialist execution (R8 or R7) plus a structured handover. The answer never seriously prices coordination delay, dual-person occupancy, or the collision with C007.

That collision is the worst operational error. C007 cannot move: Rework skill 3, high priority, complexity 0.81, **3 reassignments**, high handover cost. Celine also “keeps” C001, supervises C009, and receives C014. She has 6.0 hours left. Urgent security plus a high-complexity rework due in 6h is not a sequencing footnote; it is an infeasible plan. Giving C001 fully to a Risk specialist would free the one person who must keep C007. The policy text warns against blind continuity and then does exactly that.

C003 is a cleaner constraint breach. Constraint 4: do not load `overtime risk = high` unless SLA risk is **urgent** and **no safer alternative exists**. C003 is normal priority, 18h remaining. R4 already has 14 cases, 3.0h left, and high overtime risk. R3 is Diagnosis 3, low overtime, 6 cases, 6.0h. The answer still assigns C003 to David and relabels constraint 4 as a mere preference. That is rewriting the spec to defend the table. The continuity argument (reassignment count = 2) is real, but it does not satisfy the stated exception test. The same section then *removes* C014 from David for overtime reasons. The OT rule is applied opportunistically, not consistently.

Capacity is not checked anywhere with numbers. R6 gets two urgents (C002 at 2h, C011 at 4h) plus C010 on 6.5h remaining. R5 gets two high-priority items (C005 at 8h, C016 at 7h, complexity 0.79) on 5.0h remaining. “Sequence explicitly” is not a capacity model. Hours remaining were given precisely so this check could be done.

**The algorithm cannot produce the table.** Section 2 is a reasonable rule skeleton, but only `sla_urgency` is numeric. Skill, continuity, workload, training, and resilience stay at “strong positive / large penalty.” Weights, scales, and the “small tolerance” for keeping the current owner are unspecified. A second analyst cannot reproduce C001–C016, and the audit indicator “deviations from assignment policy” is therefore not operational. The table reads as case-by-case advocacy after the fact, not as the output of the stated scorer. For a policy that must be explainable and auditable, that is a structural failure, not a presentation nit.

**Training is cosmetic.** The 20% target is met only by counting 2/9 ≈ 22%, and one of those two is C015: Jonas has Security **skill 0** and may only shadow. Constraint 6 allows supervised work at skill 1–2, not skill 0. Shadowing is fine pedagogically; treating it as a full training assignment to clear the quota is generous. Meanwhile the log itself flags unused, safer development opportunities: C010 “potential trainee approval under supervision” (Jonas is Approval 1 and may prepare), C012 “suitable for trainee,” C015 also names Eva. C010 is instead stacked onto already-loaded R6. The policy says training must be planned and visible; the batch treats it as a remainder.

**Section 4 is directional, not a comparison.** Dimension-level contrast is what the prompt asks for, and the qualitative trade-off language is good. But the answer never materializes first-available or specialist-only assignments for the same 16 cases, so claims about throughput, SLA, and fairness are unfalsifiable. The few concrete contrasts are mostly right (first-available would put C001 on R3 unsupervised and C002 on a skill-1 approver such as R10; specialist-only would pull C016 off Eva and pile Diagnosis/Rework onto R3, likely past the 4-assignment cap). That is not a full comparison. Expected throughput / SLA / rework are never even roughly quantified.

**Smaller defects that still matter under a strict bar**

- Hard vs soft constraints are reshuffled. Assignment cap, security, and approval are hard; overtime and the 20% training rule, which the prompt lists as constraints, are demoted. The design should have said why it downgraded them, then still obeyed them.
- Supervision and shadowing are excluded from the cap in the writeup but still consume scarce specialist time. R8 is on the clock for C001 and C015; R3 is on the clock for C001 and C009. The cap check is therefore incomplete.
- Section 5 is the strongest socio-technical writing in the answer, but it omits the one assignment workers would challenge: more work to David despite high overtime and 3 hours left. An explainable policy that hides its most contentious exception is not fully explainable.
- Section 6 is the best section: indicator definitions, log fields, reason-code stratification, continuity and supervision-quality extras. Even here, the skill-mismatch code marks every skill-0 event as a violation, which would flag the intended C015 shadow as a defect. Approval preparation at skill 1 would also false-positive. The audit does not implement the policy it claims to audit.
- Section 7 names the required PM4Py surface and is usable as a sketch. It is not clean. `filter_case_performance` typically requires `max_performance` as well as `min`; `get_service_time` / `insert_case_service_waiting_time` are sensitive to lifecycle/start-complete data the sketch does not establish; discovered SNA, roles, similarity, and batches are imported and then not used to form or calibrate the current decision. “Learn the policy” collapses to a bullet list of conditional expectations with no estimation procedure, no leakage control, and no link back to weights.

**What remains genuinely good.** Objectives are multi-criteria and not throughput-only. Eligibility filtering, exception logging, and reason codes are the right control points. Several assignments are correct and well justified (C002, C004, C005, C007, C008, C011, C016). Batching C006/C012 respects rule 8 and avoids R9. The worker memo is non-punitive. The answer never pretends there is one obvious optimum.

Those strengths make this a competent design essay. They do not make it a reliable assignment policy. The live queue is where explainability, constraints, and trade-offs have to cohere; here continuity is over-weighted against the paper’s own 3.3h finding, overtime is violated when a safer specialist exists, the scorer is not specified enough to audit, and at least one worker’s day (Celine) does not fit in the hours remaining. That is why this is a 6.0, not an 8–9 writeup that merely needs polish.
