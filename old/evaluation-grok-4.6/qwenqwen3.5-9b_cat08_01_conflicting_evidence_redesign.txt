4.5

The write-up is complete, on-format, and clearly aimed at the right problem (fast path vs. quality/compliance). It is not a generic Lean checklist and it does cite many of the given artifacts. Under a strict reading, that is not enough. The process-mining reasoning and several design choices are wrong in ways that would mislead a sponsor.

**Diagnosis errors.** Sequential DFG edges are added as if they were case counts (`3600 + 2550 + 2300`), which is invalid; those are successive hops on overlapping cases, not 50% of 5,000. Token-based replay is misstated: log fitness is 0.91 and only 68% of traces fit; calling that “68% fitness” is a basic conformance mistake. R3 is overclaimed: 310 of 430 reopeners already go to manual review, and the alignment finding is reopen-after-close, not systematic bypass. Fraud/risk is described as underused; the prompt says two specialists and a Monday/campaign bottleneck. The 72h figure is treated as “the complaint edge,” but it is only `Fast approval → Customer complaint/reopen`; `Dispatch replacement → complaint` is 96h.

**Redesign logic vs. the data.** Redesign A (and C’s Premium rule) sends Premium low-risk accessories onto the slow path. That contradicts the given economics and policy: median 8h vs 46h, high service-credit exposure after 24h, and R2’s explicit exception for Premium low-risk accessories. The 3.3% complaint rate is for `Repair quote → Customer approval → Repair device`, not for “manual review.” Redesign B applies a hard evidence gate to all segments, including the R2-excepted Premium accessory stream, and then says the 140h unclear-defect median would get worse—an evidence portal that cuts repeat requests 30–45% should move that segment the other way. Redesign C puts the pre-dispatch quality gate on repair quotes, but the given failure mode is wrong replacement selection on the dispatch path; repair already has `Quality check`. Forcing repair for all Premium cases again collapses accessories with high-value devices. A and C also target largely the same population (Premium / high-value off the fast path), so they are less “materially different” than claimed.

**Table and experiments.** Marking the current process `dominated? yes` violates the instruction not to claim Pareto dominance from this data. Cycle-time “improved for Standard” under A is unsupported if Standard stays on the fast track. The 100h stop rule for unclear defects is already failed at the 140h baseline. The 24h stop for Standard uses the Premium-accessory credit threshold, not the stated 72h tolerance. “Abandonment < 5% of current baseline” is ambiguous. Sequencing is three parallel-ish pilots, not a learning path that first tests the cheap two-week auto-screen update or isolates downstream shift via R1/R2 rates, reshipments, credits, and repeat evidence—not only escalation-board volume.

**What is still good.** The contradiction is named; 14.1% vs 3.3%, 31% vs 9% cheaper, 26% repeat evidence, 140h unclear defects, and EUR 410 wrong-replacement cost are used; each option states a sacrifice; failure modes include bottleneck migration. Those strengths keep it out of the bottom third. The factual and causal mistakes keep it far from a high score.
