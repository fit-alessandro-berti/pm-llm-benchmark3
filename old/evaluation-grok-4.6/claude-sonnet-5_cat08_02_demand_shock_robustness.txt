7.0

The submission is a competent, well-structured operations redesign that hits every required section and generally respects the anti-patterns in the brief (no blanket capacity add, no average-only optimization, explicit downstream overload, case-type conflict). It is not close to flawless. Several claims misuse the log, one design parameter is internally inconsistent with the mix the author themselves cite, and a few migration stories are smoother than the evidence allows.

**What is actually strong.** Scenarios S1–S4 match the required axes; S5 is a legitimate compound case rather than a fifth volume shock. Conflict hints on I1/I2/I3/I4/I8/I10 are converted into modified policies instead of being ignored. I9 is correctly rejected. Dynamic caps, hysteresis on machine-mode switches, and on/off triggers with off-ramps are the right shape of control logic. Residual vulnerabilities include redesign-caused harm (R6 starving technical validation; R3 losing the informal registration catch). The dashboard separates leading queues/mix/machine flags from lagging SLA/p90/rework, and several thresholds are anchored to the published peak medians rather than invented round numbers.

**Material misuse of process-mining evidence.**

1. Urgent batch-wait bypass does **not** “rise to 58%.” The variant states 74% bypass in the normal period and 58% in the peak. Bypass **falls**. Peak DFG confirms the opposite mechanism: Test preparation → Batch wait rises from 760/17,310 ≈ 4.4% to 1,180/9,320 ≈ 12.7%, and the Batch wait → Assay run edge slows from 9h to 22h. The S1 story that mix shift fragments benches *because more Urgent cases bypass* is inverted. The peak problem is that Urgent cases lose the bypass and sit in batch wait, while remaining bypasses still fragment schedules. That is a different control problem than the one analyzed.

2. Repeat-assay “6.6% → 13.7% of assay throughput” is a convenient ratio (1,140 / 17,270 and 1,260 / 9,170), not completions. Completions are 1,140 / 17,030 ≈ 6.7% vs 1,260 / 8,500 ≈ 14.8%. Worse, this jump is attributed to S1 mix shift, but Repeat-after-failure only moves 8% → 9%. The extra repeats are a quality/M2/night-shift effect, which belongs in S2 (and is only partly used there).

3. Peak utilizations (assay 71% → 98%, technical validation 76% → 101%) are treated as S1 mix-only outcomes. Peak weekly volume is 9,900/3 ≈ 3,300 vs 18,400/8 ≈ 2,300 (~+43%) *and* a mix shift. The author first says volume “may or may not rise,” then loads volume-inclusive peak queues onto a mix-only scenario. That overstates how far mix alone moves the bottleneck.

4. S3’s backward migration into registration rests on clerks “explaining delays.” The log ties clerk load to *explaining recollection errors*, not SLA chasing. Plausible, but it is not process-mining evidence.

**Bottleneck-migration overclaim.** S5 concludes that technical validation, medical validation, and specialist review are “the structural choke points every scenario eventually migrates to, regardless of where it starts.” That is false on the author’s own S2 and S4 write-ups. Assay outage keeps the primary constraint on M1/M2 (with bursty arrivals into technical validation). Courier compression hits pre-analytical check, the two accession specialists (91% / 14h), and the 42h/60h recollection loop. Validation gates are common *downstream* attractors under mix and absence shocks, not a universal terminus.

**Design calibration errors.**

- R5’s single-machine fallback reserves “e.g., 30%” of slots for Urgent+Complex. The author’s own trigger treats >40% Urgent+Complex as the S1 alarm, and the observed peak mix is 29%+20% = 49%. A 30% reservation would systematically under-serve the classes the policy is meant to protect—the exact starvation mode they correctly criticize in static I3.
- R1 is sold as reducing recollection-driven rework in *every* scenario. Early reject/flag shortens time-to-recollect and avoids wasting prep/assay on bad tubes; it does not automatically cut recollection *volume* and can raise Request recollection / Cancel order if sensitivity is high. The table’s “reduces recollection-driven rework” blurs faster detection with fewer loops.
- Repeat-after-failure is the worst tail (median 70h/145h, p90 132h/240h), consumes assay twice, and clusters after M2/night shifts. It almost disappears from the package except as a capacity footnote. A redesign that insists case types cannot all be prioritized at once should still have an explicit policy for the class that recycles the scarce machine.
- I10’s “remaining SLA budget >24h” invents a Complex-panel contractual window the brief never gives (only Urgent 18h and Routine 72h are specified). Complex-panel median is already 64h/111h.
- Technical validators are “cross-trainable in 4 weeks.” R6’s medium burden and peak-triggered use understate that this pool cannot be created inside a 3-week wave unless training is done in the normal period—an assumption that should have been explicit.

**Smaller unclarities.** R4’s wording (“priority … only up to a WIP cap for Routine/Complex-panel”) is easy to implement backwards. R6’s trade-off row marks Urgent/Routine/Complex all “Neutral,” which undersells the cases actually stuck on the 2-person, 14h correction queue. Several “critical” dashboard bands are set at the *already observed peak median* (pre-analytical 9.5h, medical 22h / specialist 36h), which is late for a leading indicator. Registration overtime error (clerks after 9h) is unused. Hold-for-specialist performance (18h/48h) is thinner in the analysis than the 36h queue statistic.

**Verdict.** This is a strong exam answer with real command of bottleneck migration, intervention conflicts, and trigger design. Under a strict process-mining standard it loses a full quality band for inverting a key variant, conflating mix with volume, over-unifying every scenario onto the validation gates, and proposing a 30% reservation against a ~49% critical mix. Those are not polish issues; they change which policies one would actually deploy.
