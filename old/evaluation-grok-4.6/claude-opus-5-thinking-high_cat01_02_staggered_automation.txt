**7.2**

This is a strong causal-policy memo with the right structure, mostly careful arithmetic, and a decision that tries to buy both operations and identification. It is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading, several internal contradictions, one overstated “strongest” claim, and a few design gaps are enough to keep it well below the top of the scale.

**Format and craft.** The ten headings and required tables are present. Estimands, strategies, mechanisms, threats, and outcome assessments hit the mandated coverage. Word counts are mostly respected; the S1 justification is slightly bloated. The Python block does what was asked: embedded CSV, validation, separate (not subplot) trend figures, adopter pre/post, relative time, a labeled descriptive panel model plus an intensity model, clustered SEs, and explicit non-causal comments. That scaffolding is better than most answers. It is also where a strict grader should stop being impressed and start checking whether the prose is consistent with the numbers.

**What is done well.** Section 1 stays descriptive and correctly separates availability from use, including the 4–6% pre-availability fast-path floor. Selection on poor baselines is named. The shared March escalation jump, May complexity jump, April routing spike, August de-load, and FTE moves are all real in the panel. The estimand list is the best part of the memo: ITT availability versus dose, median versus tail, rework jointly with escalation, interference through the fraud desk, dynamic ATT(e), and the unidentified heterogeneity that actually decides expansion. S1 is a defensible primary design; the critique of naive TWFE under staggered, dynamic effects is correct in spirit. Mechanisms are process-literate. Uncertainty is treated as first-class: five clusters, no honest p-value, spillover bounds, pre-trend adjustment, and a refusal to issue “automation reduces cycle time by X%.” The safe leadership sentences in section 7 are more disciplined than the final ladder.

**Factual and logical problems.**

1. The strongest claim is not true as written. Section 10 says that in every month after switch-on, rework fell by about 2pp. Alpha drops 13.2 → 11.2 in April, then 14.0 / 14.5 — *above* baseline. Beta is 10.4 in April, then 12.7 versus 12.8. Only the adoption month is a clean step; the post-period average is +0.54pp at Alpha and −0.56pp at Beta. The DiD is mostly “avoided the May complexity-driven rise,” not a durable 2pp cut. Leading the claim ladder with the first-month cells is selective.

2. Median and p90 are collapsed in the same bullet after the memo spent pages forbidding that. Alpha/Beta p90s are about +10% versus own baseline. Calling them “roughly flat” contradicts section 5.1, which correctly treats that absolute tail worsening as the central arithmetic fact.

3. The intensity story contradicts the event study. ATT(e) for the median is weakest at e = 0 (−6.6 lp) and larger later (−9 to −13). Section 5.3 and the Suggestive bullet say almost all of the gain appears at first switch-on at ~25% use. Those cannot both be the preferred reading. The low-use band is also compositionally stacked (it includes Gamma’s two post months), so the “step versus slope” conclusion is not identified from that split.

4. Identifiability is oversold in places. E5/E6 mark direction as identified because the adoption-month discontinuity is sharp. A go-live package of training, WIP rules, and manager attention can produce the same step. The memo knows this (Hawthorne threat) and then forgets it when filling the “identifiable?” column.

5. Several quantitative details are slightly loose: Alpha load is 32.4 then 30.1 then a 42.7 peak depending on the paragraph; p90/median 2.01 → 2.34 is an endpoint contrast, not the window average (~2.23); “comparators deteriorated 12 to 17%” overstates Epsilon (~11%); distant Gamma placebos use already-treated Alpha/Beta in the CS baseline, which the prose admits and the code still does.

**Design and policy.** “Expand with an embedded evaluation” is the right family. Within-Delta case randomization plus holding a never-treated branch is serious about identifiability. Gaps remain. Case-level assignment inside one branch inherits shared specialists, shared FTE, and the same fraud desk — a SUTVA problem the memo analyzes across branches and ignores within branch. Eight weeks of 50/50 then a ramp sits poorly next to a six-month case-level endpoint and p90s of 80–100 hours. Holding Epsilon because it is “in distress” uses its worst month (CSAT 3.75 in June; 3.90 by August) and sacrifices the operational half of the objective: if the live hypothesis is congestion relief, the distressed high-complexity branch is an odd control. A null at already-fast Delta cannot, by itself, license “reposition as congestion relief for high-volume branches” unless that hypothesis is actually tested on a congested unit.

**Net.** The memo understands the scientific problem: endogenous staggered adoption, partial uptake, mixed median/tail, substitution versus capacity, and a shared queue. It refuses the headline number leadership wanted. Those are the things that should score highly. Strictly, the final claim ladder misstates persistence, the intensity and dynamic analyses disagree, and the recommended experiment underplays interference and the operations–learning tradeoff. That is clearly above a middling answer and clearly not near-flawless.
