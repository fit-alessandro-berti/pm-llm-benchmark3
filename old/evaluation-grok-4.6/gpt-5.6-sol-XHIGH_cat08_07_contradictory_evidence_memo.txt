**7.4**

The answer is strategically sound and format-compliant, but it is not nearly flawless. It correctly rejects national scale and full rollback, treats R as separable components, and keeps facts, interpretations, and assumptions mostly distinct. The remaining defects are small but real: technical sloppiness in the process-mining claims, internal inconsistencies between sections, and false precision in the rollout gates.

**What it gets right.** Alternative E is the defensible choice given the constraints. The memo is decision-first, stays under 700 words, and does not let the median, handover cut, or €161 visible-cost figure dominate. Segment results are used properly: simple houses are the only clear beneficiary; every other reported segment has a worse tail and worse first-time-right. The evidence table is the strongest part of the memo. Coverage, mix imbalance, incomplete attributes, sparse start times, and truncated complaint cost are recorded as facts; survey-skip error, permit gaps, and post-billing activation are kept as causal hypotheses; national generalization and rule reliability are left uncertain. All five metric conflicts are explained in the right causal register. Fitness vs precision, median vs tail, handover vs resilience, visible vs downstream cost, and house vs apartment/old-building effects are not collapsed into a single story. A–E are evaluated with advantages, risks, and enabling conditions. M1–M8 are all ranked with a decision-change clause.

**Process-mining accuracy is not clean enough.** Low precision and low generalization are mashed into one diagnosis of a “flexible, unstable exception process.” That is wrong in the details. Precision 0.51 means the *discovered model* is too permissive. Generalization 0.56 means the model is a weak predictor of unseen cases. Those are different model properties, and neither is identical to operational instability. The memo places that interpretive claim in the facts-leaning process-evidence paragraph. The same blur appears when model-allowed combinations are treated as if they were the same thing as observed DFG exception growth. Section 3 is more careful than Section 1; the memo should have been equally careful.

**Internal inconsistencies.** Section 1 allows “explicitly authorized and auditable exceptions” to billing-before-activation. Section 6 says post-billing activation remains prohibited unless separately tested. Those are not the same rule. Section 1 recommends E; Section 6 recommends “E, supplemented by controlled elements of C and D.” E already contains targeting, controls, and measurement. Relabeling the choice after the fact makes the decision look less crisp than the operational plan actually is.

**The leading logical tension.** M6 is ranked as the highest-value measurement because the risk rule may not identify safe cases. The same document lists rule reliability outside the pilot as uncertain, then scales survey skipping for “verified” simple houses anyway. “Verified” is doing too much work. The simple-house cell is encouraging, but it is still a bundled, non-randomized, mix-confounded contrast. If classifier failure is the top uncertainty, skip-scale should be shadow-validated or held to a concurrent-control cell first. The memo almost says this, then does not do it.

**VOI ranking fights the memo’s own decision logic.** M7 is ranked fourth, yet the text says persistent unauthorized billing-before-activation or missing required permits should trigger segment rollback. That is a decision-flipping measurement, not a hygiene item. Ranking it below M1/M4/M6 while also saying it can force rollback is inconsistent value-of-information reasoning. The urgency-versus-decision-value distinction is intelligent, but it is used to bury a control that the rest of the memo treats as a hard stop. M3 is similarly undervalued relative to the claim that mechanism codes would determine whether to change the rule, staffing, activation policy, or permit routing.

**False precision.** The 95% sensitivity rule, 2-day p90 / 3-day p95 / 1-point quality bounds, 85% utilization cap, and “two consecutive cohorts with 60-day follow-up” are invented numbers with no justification. The prompt asked for thresholds; it did not ask for spurious statistical exactness. “Upper 95% confidence bound” language is especially overfitted to a six-week, four-region, incompletely labeled pilot. An 85% utilization gate is a reasonable ops heuristic, not a scientific cutoff.

**Smaller bluntness and omissions.** Rural cases are parked with apartments and old buildings under a survey-skip pause, even though the stated rural mechanism is rescheduling under coordinator load, not a bad skip rule. That is conservative, not careful. SLA improvement for simple houses is in the source and unused. The complaint-mix shift (“waiting for appointment” → “installed but not working” / “billing before activation”) and activation-specialist ownership complaints are underused in the memo itself. Old-building median is +1.0 day; “improved or slightly worse medians” is technically true and slightly cosmetic.

**Not a failing answer.** It does not scale nationally, does not roll back on aggregate deterioration alone, does not merge the evidence table, and does not ignore fitness, precision, alignments, temporal profiles, DFG edges, or social/resource networks. It would be a strong executive product. Under a hypercritical reading it is still a 7-range memo: the recommendation is right, the synthesis is good, and the remaining defects are exactly the kind of unclarity and overclaim a decision-maker should not have to debug.
