**6.5**

The final write-up follows the required six-heading skeleton, uses the mandated table columns, and generally obeys the anti-naïve-clustering constraints: complexity, authority, substitution, and risk are treated as first-class signals, and shadow roles are discussed as useful but costly. That structural compliance is the main reason the score is not lower. Under a strict reading, however, the answer is well short of “nearly flawless.”

**Data fidelity.** Almost all matrix, similarity, and substitution figures are used correctly. The exception is material: Fatima’s absence is said to have made median pricing duration “doubled **(3874 min)**.” The log says 38 → 74 minutes. That is not a rounding issue; it is a garbled number in a required evidence section. Smaller slips compound the impression of carelessness: “technical **tierment** program,” and “dual-signoff … **(R13R08)** … **(R13R05)**.”

**Role structure.** The ten-role split is mostly the right grain: Nora, the two temps, Fatima, Erik, Hana, Mateo, and Giorgio are correctly *not* collapsed into frequency clusters. Two weaknesses remain. First, Chen and Dina (and Amira/Ben) are still parked in single formal roles even though the benchmark’s point is that near-identical activity mixes hide different complexity and risk exposure; the caveat is in the table text, not in the structure. Second, Giorgio is defined as a commercial bridge, yet A7/A8 (72 and 88 events) are labeled *peripheral*. Those counts are among his largest activities; treating them as occasional work contradicts the role name and the substitution story.

**Core vs peripheral / shadows.** Section 2 mostly restates the table rather than separating frequency, complexity, substitution, and authority as distinct tests. Mateo’s “core” work is described as a **Coordinate** activity that does not exist in A1–A14; that is inferred from the prose scenario, not from the log schema. The required shadow/bridge trio (Giorgio, Mateo, Erik) is handled substantively, with both benefits and risks, and the automation/temp add-ons are appropriate. The 38→74 error sits in the middle of the Giorgio analysis and weakens it.

**Ambiguity.** R13 and R07 are given two credible readings and falsifiable deciding evidence. R04’s second reading — “generalist service agent with technical training” — is not plausible on the matrix (255 / 235 / 220 on diagnose–configure–implement, almost no front-office volume). That is a weak alternative, not a serious competing hypothesis.

**PM4Py code (largest technical failure).** Illustrative code is allowed; incoherent code is not. `discover_organizational_roles` returns role objects (activity sets + resource weights), not a frame keyed by `org:resource`, so the `.merge(..., on='org:resource')` cannot work as written. `similarity_df['complexity_diff']` is never computed. `format_dataframe(..., object_id=None, variant='pm4py')` invents parameters. The “valid roles” filter then **hard-codes** Hana, Erik, and Mateo — encoding the conclusion instead of discovering it. Temporary substitution is summed as if it were a clean numeric flag, with no reconstruction of the week-level episodes the scenario actually gives. Networks are called but not interpreted against the six sequence fragments. This section does not show a trustworthy method for “avoiding over-interpretation”; it asserts the warning and then implements a circular filter.

**Operations.** Staffing / training / risk / resilience are all present, and the two cautions match the stated constraints. “1:1 backup for high-complexity roles” is an unsupported leap from the evidence.

Net: a complete, constraint-aware organizational reading with the right roles in the right places, pulled down by one clear numerical error, an inconsistent Giorgio core/periphery split, one implausible ambiguity, and a required code block that would not run and partly begs the question.
