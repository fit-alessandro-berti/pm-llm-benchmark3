**6.5**

The answer shows real process-level thinking and is far stronger than a frequency-ranked automation list. It uses waiting times, DFG loops, resource networks, and control findings to argue against A1-first, treats committee packaging as a synchronization point, keeps humans on exceptions, and refuses unsafe downstream bots. Those are the right instincts. Under a strict reading, however, the deliverable is not nearly flawless: it contradicts its own constraints, invents precision the prompt forbade, and contains several reasoning errors that would mislead a steering committee.

**Constraint and internal consistency failures (largest deduction).**  
Budget is “at most four automation modules in the first year,” and Section 7 insists that limit is respected. The Section 6 roadmap does not respect it. Phase 1 already deploys A5, A2, and A4; Phase 2 adds A6 and A12; Phase 3 adds A3 plus pilots of A9/A10 and expansion of A7. That is a seven-to-nine-module year, not a four-module portfolio. A2 is not even in recommended portfolio D, yet it is a Phase 1 go-live. A recommended set of A5+A4+A6+A12 cannot simultaneously be the year-1 plan and a plan that also ships A2, A7, and A3. For a portfolio exercise defined by scarce slots, this is a structural failure, not a wording slip.

**Dependency contradictions.**  
The matrix states A5 “needs A2 for correct checklist,” then the recommended four omits A2. If that prerequisite is real, D is infeasible; if it is overstated, the matrix is wrong. A4 is also tied too tightly to A3. Entity mismatch is a specialist activity after KYC (`KYC → Resolve` 1750; `Resolve → KYC` 1160); A3 is a hit pre-classifier on the much larger KYC pool. Related queues, not a hard prerequisite. Treating them as one bottleneck blurs the handover network the answer otherwise uses well.

**Analytical errors.**  
A6’s isolated “downstream bottleneck risk” is backwards. The case text warns that *upstream* automation increases clean inflow to the 9 FTE analysts *unless normalization is improved*. A6 is the relief valve (75 minutes of analyst work on 5,200 cases), not the source of overload. Later sections describe the A1/A5 inflow risk correctly, so the matrix row is inconsistent with the author’s own later reasoning.  
A4’s 31-hour median wait versus 55-minute service time is almost entirely queueing, rework (`Resolve → KYC` 1,160), and specialist concentration, not task duration. Claiming an assistant cuts that wait to “~10–12 hours” treats a capacity/handoff problem as a keystroke problem.  
A9 is written as if contracts wait on committee (`3650 → 7300`), but contract preparation is 7,600 against 3,900 committee cases. Many contracts are a non-committee variant; the dependency is overstated.

**Unsupported precision.**  
The prompt asked for qualitative ratings, not invented ROI. The text still asserts ~30% cut in the 44-hour signing wait, 15–20 hours off the 58-hour external valuation, 25–35% Equipment-finance cycle-time reduction, ~60% auto-clearable KYC hits, 8.5% → 3–4% remediation, and similar figures. None of these are in the mining table. The 60% KYC auto-clear is especially unsupported: KYC rework is 11%, and hit resolution is described as manual. Portfolio C’s “provisional signing → 0” assumes a perfect gate.

**Weaker process-mining use than the best parts imply.**  
The 58-hour external wait is the longest delay and a true synchronization point (`Wait → Committee packaging` 1,980; required in 71% of Equipment finance). Recommended year 1 drops A7 for A12. That trade-off is defensible given the control mandate, but the cycle-time claims for D still lean on B’s A7 effects. Weekly committee cadence for high-exposure cases—the batching reason early prerequisite completion matters—is barely used. A5’s “Very High” process-level benefit also overreaches: notes say customer-response delay dominates, so a checklist/nudge may not move the 8-hour wait as much as implied, and RM resistance is a first-class constraint.

**Presentation.**  
Comparison operators and arrows were stripped (`Frequency bottleneck`, `36507300`, `KYC wait ,`). In a management artifact this is not cosmetic; several effect sentences are unreadable without reconstruction.

**What still works.**  
Ranking A1 and A11 at the bottom is correct: register is high-throughput with 1.5-hour wait and back-office utilization already below 65% after 15:00; activation cannot run if controls are incomplete. Portfolios Q/B/C are cleanly differentiated on what they optimize and sacrifice. Controls (KYC-before-signing, four-eyes 5.8%, decision–contract match, audit trail) are treated as design constraints, not afterthoughts. Shifted demand, WIP before credit analysis, and a kill-switch if analyst wait rises are the right operations ideas. A5 as the *political* quick win instead of A1 is a sharp reading of the stakeholder constraint.

The piece would be in the high 8s if the year-1 plan matched the four-module recommendation, dependencies were consistent, and effect sizes stayed qualitative. As written, strong process reasoning is undercut by a plan that violates its own budget, a few inverted resource logics, and fabricated decimals. That is a mid-6 under a strict bar, not a near-flawless 9.
