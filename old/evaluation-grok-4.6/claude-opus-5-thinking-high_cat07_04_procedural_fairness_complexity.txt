6.4

The answer is unusually complete and mostly constraint-compliant, but it is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it has material statistical overreach, several analysis-plan errors that would distort the fairness conclusions, and repeated over-interpretation of coarse controls.

**What it gets right.** It uses the required headings, covers all listed fairness dimensions, and does not treat raw approval gaps as discrimination or case-mix differences as automatic justification. It treats `risk_score_band` as potentially contaminated, treats rights-preserving steps as a source of burden rather than a reason to drop them, and keeps outcome fairness separate from procedural burden. The two-specification idea (gross burden vs within-need), the positivity problem on V4/V5, the negative-control edge, and the calibrated bottom line are the strongest parts.

**Serious inferential flaws.** The “controls explain 63%/76%/85%” figures are not a proper decomposition; they treat a residual from an unspecified (likely nonlinear) model as if it were a Kitagawa–Oaxaca share. Worse, crude two-proportion standard errors are applied to *adjusted* differences. Those SEs are already slightly wrong for Group B (~1.5 pp, not 1.4), and they are the wrong SEs entirely for a covariate-adjusted estimand. From that the answer declares C’s −2.2 pp “not reliably distinguishable from zero” and D’s −3.1 pp “borderline.” That is invented precision. The same overconfidence appears when +9.5 pp Request Missing Documents at constant `documentation_completeness` is called nearly decisive. Completeness is a coarse, process-generated band, not a sufficient statistic for file quality; residual confounding inside the band is exactly what one should expect. Saying “none of this is messier files, because that variable is controlled” is false.

**Resource arithmetic is overstated.** “3,600 A cases / 52 workers ≈ 69” versus B “concentrated on 7 bilingual staff” treats assignment as exclusive and converts “disproportionately assigned” into a headcount monopoly. The independence claim about accommodation specialists also overreaches: dual role membership does not establish that the same person reviews the same case.

**Section 7 would not support the assessment as written.** Service/waiting insertion assumes a `start_timestamp` the log does not have, so service vs wait is not identified. Handovers are coded as `n_events - 1`, which counts activity steps, not resource changes. The “expectation” decomposition then plugs in *medians* and DFG frequency shares; that quantity is not a cycle-time gap and cannot separate routing from unequal service. Learning a log skeleton / DECLARE model on Group A and scoring B/C against it pathologizes legitimate Language Support and Accommodation routes. Conformance outputs are stored but not summarized. Several API details are guessed. These are not cosmetic: they would mis-measure the exact disparities the rest of the memo relies on.

**Smaller issues.** Group A is implicitly treated as the fairness norm rather than as one observed process. “Nominal access is satisfied” in 1.6 sits awkwardly next to the skipped-step checks. Mechanism 4.10 is a generic Simpson’s-paradox hedge with no evidence. The preliminary model’s inclusion of region/month is first narrated as a complexity adjustment, then later (correctly) as something that can hide office effects. Appeal-quality and historical-bias cautions are good but repeated at a length that blurs what is shown versus speculated.

The bottom-line verdict is still roughly the right one: outcome discrimination is not identified; support-step queues and thin specialist capacity are the clearest procedural problem. The memo is strong policy analysis with a broken measurement appendix and several claims that outrun the tables. That is well above a weak answer, and well below a clean one.
