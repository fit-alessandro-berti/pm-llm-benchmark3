**6.3**

The write-up is complete and correctly shaped: all seven required headings are present, hard rules are separated from preferences, the policy names every required scoring family, the queue is fully assigned, baselines are compared as trade-offs rather than a fake optimum, and the worker/audit/PM4Py sections exist. That is the ceiling. Under a strict reading, several core design and assignment errors keep it well below “nearly flawless.”

**Assignments vs. stated evidence and capacity**

Several legal assignments are still poor decisions given the historical numbers the prompt supplied.

- **C001** keeps a Security skill-1 owner on an urgent, 3h, complexity-0.88 enterprise review. Constraint 5 can be read as allowing this because supervision exists, but the same brief says weak-skill ownership adds a median **3.3h of service time** — more than the remaining SLA. Changing owner costs ~2.7h waiting, also almost the whole SLA. The text treats R7 takeover as a fallback instead of making the specialist the primary with R3 as knowledge-continuity support. For a 3h security SLA that is the wrong default.
- **C003** is the worst call. A case already in a diagnosis loop, complexity 0.66, two prior reassignments, is given to a **skill-1** resource. The brief says >2 reassignments raise rework 1.8× and appeals 1.4×; this assignment *creates* the third reassignment **and** a skill downgrade from R4 (skill 3) to R6 (skill 1). R8 (Diagnosis 2, low OT, 5.5h) is ignored. Hitting the 20% training quota is a real constraint, but the prompt only requires training “if SLA risk remains acceptable.” Forcing the quota onto this case is optimizing the metric, not the process.
- **Hours remaining are almost unused.** R3 gets C001 (slow junior security), C007 (high-complexity rework, 6h SLA), and supervision of C003, with **6.0h** left. R5 gets two high-priority jobs (8h and 7h SLAs, one of them complexity 0.79) plus an approval, with **5.0h** left. R6 gets two urgents (2h and 4h) plus the training diagnosis, with **6.5h** left. R7 has 4.5h, 10 open cases, a high security review, and must supervise C001. The matrix includes hours specifically so the policy can refuse infeasible same-day loads. The score uses open-case counts instead.

Other assignments are fine and sometimes sharp: C002/C011/C005/C007/C008/C016 respect ownership and skill; high-OT staff (R2/R4/R9) are correctly shielded; C015 correctly **refuses** Eva/Jonas because Security skill 0 is not “1–2 under supervision”; C006/C012 batching matches rule 8; the 4-assignment cap holds.

**The algorithm is not actually a usable policy**

Section 2 lists the right ingredients, then combines them in a way that cannot be audited or recomputed.

- Weights are 0.30 + 0.25 + 0.30 + 0.20 + 0.15 = **1.20**, plus an unweighted bottleneck term.
- Components live on incompatible scales (urgency 1–10, skill 1–3, continuity −3…6, workload ≈ −4…0). Multiplying by weights does not produce a defined ranking.
- `capacity threshold` is referenced and never defined.
- Tie-breaks are a pile of criteria with no order.
- The table is justified in prose; **no scores are ever computed**. Publishing two-decimal weights and then not applying them is cargo-cult optimization.

A sequential greedy pass (priority, then SLA) is also never examined as a limitation, even though it is exactly why R6 is packed with both urgents plus training.

**Comparison is directional, not empirical**

Section 4 is the right shape (throughput, SLA, rework, fairness, development, continuity, resilience) and correctly refuses a single winner. It does **not** instantiate first-available or specialist-only on C001–C016. Without those counterfactual assignments, claims about this queue are generic textbook contrasts, not a comparison of the designed policy to the two required baselines.

**PM4Py sketch is thinner and sloppier than the prompt asked**

Required families are named, but the code would not run as written and misses part of the asked surface:

- A PM4Py event log is not `for event in log`.
- `filter_case_performance` filters case **duration**, not SLA remaining; the comment treats it as an SLA filter.
- `pm4py.view_sna` / `save_vis_sna` and `get_variants` are listed in the prompt and unused.
- “Learn the policy” is a sentence at the end; the sketch is an audit dump, not a method for estimating handover penalties, service times, or bottleneck loads from the log and feeding them back into scores.

**Smaller but real gaps**

- C009 is a skill-1 diagnosis with only informal “review,” while C003 gets a named supervisor — inconsistent supervision discipline.
- R8 (one of two Security-3 people) is given extra rework while C001 still needs a security backstop.
- R10 receiving new-case Eligibility is legal but never explained to a Quality worker.
- Section 5 is the right tone and not punitive, but it does not explain the assignments workers would actually question (R6’s extra diagnosis next to two VIP/urgent items; R3 keeping urgent security as a junior; R10’s cross-role eligibility case).

**What still works**

Objectives vs. constraints are clean. Hard rules on Security, Approval, high-priority skill floors, the batch cap, OT avoidance, and Quality batching are understood. The Eva/Jonas skill-0 catch is better than many otherwise stronger answers. Training share is counted (2/9). Continuity is not ignored. The piece does not pretend there is one optimum.

That is a complete, mostly constraint-aware design with a non-operational scoring rule, two assignments that fight the prompt’s own cost evidence, unused capacity data, and a comparison/PM4Py layer that stays at sketch quality. On a harsh scale that is mid-6, not 8+.
