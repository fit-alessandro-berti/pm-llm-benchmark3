**6.2**

The answer is structurally complete and lands on a defensible hybrid (E), which is the right family of choices. It does not scale R nationally, does not roll back solely because some metrics worsened, and it covers the five required metric conflicts. That is the floor of a competent memo, not the ceiling. Under a strict reading of the brief, the memo is incomplete, internally inconsistent, and sloppy in places that matter for an executive decision.

**Format and decision orientation.** All six required sections exist and the memo is well under 700 words. The recommendation is decision-oriented. That is the strongest part of the submission.

**Fact / interpretation / assumption separation is thin and sometimes wrong.** The evidence table meets the eight-item minimum and then stops. Five of eight rows are obvious headline metrics; DFG edges, temporal-profile deviations, alignment move-on-model (missing permit checks), generalization (0.70 → 0.56), first-time-right, second-visit/manual-activation rates, resource-utilization shifts, and the data-coverage defects are absent. Item 8 is misclassified: “Downstream complaint costs will exceed visible savings” is a forecast, not an assumption. The supported statements are (a) fact: estimated full cost is already 198 → 207, and (b) assumption: the incomplete post–week-4 complaint series may make the gap larger or smaller. The memo then treats “skip rules are inaccurate for older buildings” as established fact rather than a plausible reading of segment notes plus missing early-week risk attributes.

**The most important confounder is ignored.** Pilot regions had more apartments and older buildings than the baseline mix; segment and risk attributes are incomplete before week 3; start timestamps are sparse; complaint cost is censored after week 4. None of this appears in the memo, the conflict section, or the go/no-go thresholds. Without it, aggregate “R is worse in the tail” is over-interpreted, and segment effects themselves are less certain than the memo claims. That is a material failure of incomplete-evidence reasoning.

**Process-mining artifacts are underused.** Fitness vs. precision is explained in the right direction but imprecisely (high fitness is replay of the log; low precision is excess model behavior, not merely “unique exception paths”). Alignments are cited once. Temporal profiles (apartment install→activation deterioration; new billing→complaint long-tail; failed-visit delay under 93% utilization), DFG share shifts, working-together centralization, and generalization are essentially unused in the decision logic.

**Metric-conflict explanations are adequate, not sharp.** Median-vs-tail and simple-vs-apartment are the best paragraphs. Visible-vs-downstream cost never states the actual estimated full-cost reversal (198 vs 207). Handover-vs-resilience is fine but does not use the social-network finding. Fitness/precision does not connect low precision to the specific illegal/undesirable combinations the alignments already flag (billing before activation; install without survey; permit check absent).

**Alternatives A–E are sketched, not stress-tested.** C is undersold: coordinator ownership is the cleanest benefit (handovers 34% → 12%) while survey-skip and post-billing activation are the parts that produce rework, complaints, and conformance failures. D is reduced to “risk-averse on compliance,” which misses that D is the value-of-information option given broken measurement. Conditions under which A or B would be reasonable are perfunctory.

**Value-of-information ranking is the weakest analytical section.** The brief asked which uncertainty each item resolves *and how it could flip the decision*. Most entries do not do the second. M6 (validate the risk rule against survey outcomes) is the cheapest test of the central causal claim and is ranked below reason codes. M5 is dismissed as “organizational design” even though E/C both scale coordinator ownership and the log already shows stalling on absence. M2 is underrated given sparse start times and a 93% utilization bottleneck. M8 is dismissed on the grounds that NPS already fell—exactly the wrong reason. Complaint mix already shifted from wait-time to “installed but not working” / “billed before activation”; splitting CX would tell you whether median-speed gains are valued at all.

**Internal inconsistencies and wording errors in the action.** Section 1 scales coordinators only for low-risk segments; section 6 rolls the role out nationally. “Route … to … pre-activation Billing” is the opposite of the intended control (billing must follow successful activation). Thresholds (p90 < 35 days, complaints < 7%, full cost below baseline) ignore the conformance defects the analysis itself treats as fatal: billing-before-activation rate, missing permit checks, first-time-right, and precision/generalization. An 8-week review is asserted without tying it to the 60-day cost window in M4.

**Bottom line.** The skeleton of a good memo is here: do not scale R, do not scrap the simple-house gains, contain skip-survey and post-billing activation, measure before a wider bet. The execution is a mid-quality consulting draft with a thin evidence table, a missed selection-bias problem, a VOI section that mis-ranks decision-critical measurements, and an action line that is either inconsistent or literally inverted. That is not near-flawless work.
