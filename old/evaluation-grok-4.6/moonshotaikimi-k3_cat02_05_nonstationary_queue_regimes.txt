**7.3**

The final write-up is a strong, well-structured process-mining analysis: it hits the required eight headings, keeps open-hours units consistent, isolates outage and recovery instead of treating them as normal high-throughput days, labels assumptions, and generally separates observation, estimate, hypothesis, and recommendation. The T8 audit (hourly vectors vs `peak_08_10_pct`) is a genuine data-quality finding, not invented color. That is not enough for a high score under a strict reading.

**Numerical and internal-consistency failures**

The most damaging error is the lag-7 claim. Section 5 and the Section 8 “headline outputs” state that daily arrivals have lag-7 autocorrelation **≈ 0.99**. That figure is a windowed Pearson correlation of two 21-day slices (different means, 21 paired products). The code implements standard ACF: full-series demeaning and denominator \(\sum_{t=1}^{28} (x_t-\bar x)^2\). That estimator is about **0.72**, not 0.99. The reproducible block will not print the number the narrative treats as established. For a section whose job is serial dependence, that is a real inconsistency, not a rounding issue.

Related: the residual lag-1 “≈ 0.0” uses all residuals in the denominator, including days that are not in any adjacent same-regime pair. That estimator is biased toward zero, so “memory lives in the backlog, not demand” is stronger than the calculation supports. The claim that WIP lag-1 is “depressed by regime jumps; within-regime persistence is higher” is not shown in the final tables and is not obviously true on the short within-regime slices.

Smaller slips: pooled implied \(W \approx 3.98\) h is described as sitting “near” R5w (4.66–5.28 h); it is closer to R6w. “Simpson” is used for reverse causality and mixture masking; the task allowed “Simpson-like,” but the staffing–WIP point is not a Simpson reversal.

**Queueing-law overclaim**

R1 is marked **applicable**. The same section lists unknown 07:00 backlog, open-hours-only \(L\), no case-level flow times, and a large unexplained gap (implied ~146 min vs \(\hat E[S] \approx 28\) min at \(\rho \approx 0.66\)). Under the stated quality bar, that is **approximate only**. Little’s Law can still be a useful open-hours descriptor, but the aggregates do not identify a sojourn well enough for “applicable.”

Capacity pressure is also pressed harder than the identification allows. \(\rho \approx 1.17\) in R5w is a useful bound under A2/A3, and backlog growth is independent corroboration, but R1 already shows that the same \(\rho\) machinery cannot explain observed WIP. The 77% jump in “workload minutes” and the “needs ~7 FTE” recommendation inherit that fragility; the median-as-mean bound (\(\rho \approx 0.93\)) is given, then under-used.

**What remains solid**

Regime cuts are defensible; a serious alternative (congestion state) is compared rather than forced. Pooling is shown to hide weekday/weekend intensity, mix-driven service, and a near-balance ratio that averages overload with drain. Hourly Fano vs completion smoothing on 2026-04-21 is the right burstiness contrast. Recovery days are not treated as sustainable capacity. The Python block is complete relative to the spec (in-memory tables, pooled vs regime summaries, two dispersion measures plus a serial indicator, a labeled split/CUSUM heuristic, Little’s Law only on named windows, PM4Py comments that correctly refuse to treat the daily table as an event log).

Those strengths keep the score well above a mediocre answer. The lag-7/code contradiction, the nonstandard residual ACF, the unsupported within-regime WIP claim, and the too-strong R1 Little’s Law verdict keep it well below “nearly flawless.”
