**3.2**

The submission is directionally cautious (avoid national scale, avoid blanket rollback) but fails multiple hard requirements, contains factual errors, and is too sloppy to be an executive decision product.

**Format and memo quality.** The spec requires six exact top-level sections, with section 1 a decision-oriented memo of at most 700 words. The answer uses one top-level heading and nests everything else under it. The actual memo is a single generic paragraph: it names Alternative D, asserts “balance,” and does not state the trade-off in operational terms (median/handover/visible-cost gains vs. tail, first-time-right, complaints, SLA, activation-before-billing, and coordinator saturation). An executive could not act from that memo alone.

**Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are not separated cleanly.** The evidence table is the weakest required artifact. It lists five facts, not “at least eight” well-specified items; omits the numbers that drive the decision (fitness/precision/generalization, cost 184/161 vs. 198/207, FTR 82%→76%, second visit 11%→18%, manual activation 7%→15%, handovers 34%→12%, SLA, NPS, DFG shifts, alignments); and then cites nonexistent “Fact 4/5/8.” Interpretation 2 is false: the pilot model has *higher fitness* (0.94) and *lower precision* (0.51), not higher precision. Assumptions 1–2 treat the skip rule as an open accuracy question while the segment table already records “skip rule often wrong.” Assumption 3 (“complaint handling improvements”) is incoherent because complaints rose 6.1%→9.8%. That is not a minor labeling issue; it collapses the required fact/inference boundary.

**Required metric-conflict analysis is incomplete and partly wrong.** The prompt explicitly requires an explanation of high fitness but low precision. It is absent. The process-mining interpretation is also unused: the discovered model replays the common coordinator path (fitness) while permitting too many combinations of skipped survey, manual activation, second visit, and billing start (precision). Median-vs-tail is oversimplified. Overall median improved, and four of five segments improved on median; only old/permit-sensitive buildings got slower. The conflict is mixture plus exception tails (apartments +12 p90, old buildings +25 p90, new billing→complaint relation, failed-visit→second-visit delay under 93% coordinator load), not “only the 34% simple-house slice improved.” Visible-cost vs. downstream cost is asserted without citing the cost figures or the measurement censoring after week 4. Handover-vs-resilience is mentioned but not tied to the simpler handover network, more centralized working-together network, or survey-team underload vs. second-visit overload (78%→52% vs. 66%→92%).

**Process-mining artifacts are mostly ignored.** Alignments (billing before successful activation; repeat install without survey; missing permit checks on older apartments; complaint after billing), temporal profiles, selected DFG edges, generalization drop (0.70→0.56), and resource/social-network findings are not used as decision evidence. Data-coverage limits (6 vs. 10 weeks, apartment-heavier pilot, attributes incomplete before week 3, sparse start timestamps, truncated complaint cost) are not treated as reasons to discount naive before/after comparisons.

**Alternatives A–E are too thin and mis-rank the feasible set.** Advantages, risks, and *conditions* are sketchy. C is undersold: suspending survey-skip and post-billing activation is exactly how one would attack the tail, rework, and compliance-like failures while keeping coordinator ownership. E is dismissed as “fragmentation,” which is the point of a heterogeneous effect, not a bug. D is treated as the safe default without pricing the cost of continued harm: the pilot is already producing more dead-on-arrival installs, billing-before-activation, and complaints. Pure experimentation without first stopping known-harm mechanisms is a weak recommendation under the stated constraints.

**Value-of-information reasoning is contradictory.** The table marks M8 “High” and then ranks it last. M1 is said to “validate or invalidate M2 and M3,” which is not how those measurements relate. Highest-value items are claimed to resolve “downstream cost,” but M4 (the actual 60-day cost instrument) is ranked medium. M7 is undervalued given the alignment diagnostics the memo is required to take seriously. The write-up does not state, for each Mi, a decision flip (e.g., M6/M1: salvage skip-by-stratum vs. ban skip; M4: if fully loaded cost stays above baseline, do not scale even the “cheaper” path; M5: if absence stalls cases, do not scale single-owner coordinators without a backup model).

**Final recommendation and thresholds are poorly specified.** Immediate action is more measurement (M1–M3) with no pause of survey skipping or billing-before-activation, and no limited scale of the one cell that already wins (simple low-risk houses). The rollout bar—“median improvement must be sustained across *all* segments”—conflicts with the instruction that segment-specific benefits matter and would likely block any rollout (old buildings are already +1 day median). The 85% utilization cap is invented. Success is conditioned on M1 and M3 “confirming effectiveness,” which those two instruments cannot do alone.

Net: a loosely organized preference for more study, not a defensible synthesis of conflicting process-mining evidence. The factual inversion of precision, the missing fitness/precision explanation, the broken evidence table, and the refusal to stop or ring-fence mechanisms already shown to create tail risk and rework are each independently disqualifying for a high score.
