The answer is structurally complete and substantively on the right side of the problem: it treats rules 6–7 and 9–10 as hard constraints, ranks A/B last, refuses to let SLA forecasts legalize a shortcut, keeps re-screening from substituting for second-officer review, and prices P-901 as a real cost. That is the core of a strong answer. It is not nearly flawless. Several quantitative and planning errors sit in the justification for the actual ranking, and under a strict reading those are enough to keep the score well below the top band.

**Central ranking is oversold.** P1 is a reasonable composite, but the table’s “decision + execution by T+75–90” contradicts section 2’s T+75–130 and is not supported by the given durations. Alternate screening is 15–30 minutes; S4 is 35–65; review is 35–75 plus 5–15 to record/execute. The favorable half of that convolution is not inside 90 minutes. Residual breach “~0.4–0.6” and “preserves most of the €20k–€35k … (~0.4–0.6)” are invented, not derived. C, by contrast, is 40–90 minutes against a 90-minute deadline. If those bounds are the support, C often finishes on time; “≈50% inside deadline” is unjustified and quietly underrates the main rival. P1’s S2 fallback, started only at T+30–40, is strictly slower than immediate C. Ranking P1 first can still be defended on spillover and information, but the write-up claims a deadline performance P1 does not have.

**The S4 sensitivity analysis names the wrong contingent plan.** If S4 is known to arrive at 65 minutes, waiting until T+30–40 and then interrupting P-901 is dominated by starting C immediately with D in parallel. The answer says P1 “collapses into C at T+30–40” and that standalone S4-wait drops below F. It never states the plan that actually becomes first. “Reserving S4” is also assumed without support in the case.

**Section 3 contains a clear arithmetic error.** “A 10% chance of the low-end penalty (€10k expected) nearly cancels the maximum deadline benefit” is false: 0.1 × €100k = €10k, which does not nearly cancel €35k. The dominance argument can survive on the upper tail and non-monetary harm; this particular calculation does not. The F interval 0.55–0.75 × €15k–€80k ≈ €8k–€60k is a naïve corner product, and using the observational approval rate as P(false rejection | reject now) is left undefended.

**Counterfactual tree is incomplete on the one branch that changes the control set.** If alternate screening kills the fuzzy match *and* the refreshed score is below 70, rule 6’s second-officer trigger may lapse. Auto-clear still needs score < 40 and no alert, so a 40–69 band is legally distinct from both “still mandatory” and “auto-clear.” That branch is deferred to a rerank trigger instead of being traced. The tree also writes “~0.75+,” which steps outside the given 0.55–0.75 band after the prompt warned that estimates may not transport.

**Internal plan inconsistency.** P1 escalates at T+30–40, but the fallback is “if by ~T+55–60 no second officer has started … move to F.” At T+55 a 35–75 minute review cannot meet a 90-minute deadline, so that trigger is both too late and incompatible with the stated checkpoint. Rule 6 is also written as “risk ≥70 AND unresolved fuzzy match each independently,” which garbles OR.

**Smaller defects.** Benefit/harm cells are not in comparable units. The confidence column mixes “confidence this rank is right” with outcome confidence. G is correctly labeled an unenabled exception, but ranking a path the author says must not be pursued above two forbidden actions is fine; treating it as a live sixth-best *plan* in the table is slightly muddy. None of these would sink an otherwise tight answer; they add to an already leaky quantitative case.

The conformance table, the deontic-versus-forecast paragraph, the four-eyes / fabricated-control distinction on B, the API value-of-information note, and the SAR non-disclosure handling are all good. The grade is limited by errors in the numbers and timelines that are supposed to justify *which* conformant second-review plan wins.
