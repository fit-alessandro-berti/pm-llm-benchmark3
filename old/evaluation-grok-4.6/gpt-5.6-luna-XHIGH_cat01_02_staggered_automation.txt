**7.3**

A complete, well-structured answer that hits the intended scientific targets: endogenous targeting, availability vs intensity, staggered timing, median/tail divergence, fraud-desk interference, small-cluster uncertainty, and a learning-oriented rollout. It is not nearly flawless. Several quantitative, identification, and design slips keep it well below a high score under a strict rubric.

**What works.** Section 1 stays descriptive and separates availability, uptake, selection, co-shocks, and measurement. The estimand table correctly refuses point identification and distinguishes ITT-style availability from endogenous fast-path use, including treatment-version and interference notes. The primary design (cohort/group-time event study, not naïve TWFE) is the right class, and the post-table rationale is clear. Mechanisms are process-aware. Pre/post windows for Alpha/Beta (Jan–Mar vs Jul–Aug) are a good attempt not to treat ramp months as mature performance. Most arithmetic I checked is correct (Alpha/Beta/Gamma pre/post levels; Gamma vs never-treated median and p90 residuals of about −3.9 h and −7.1 h; intensity contrasts). Effect assessments are appropriately hesitant. The claim ladder is well ordered. The code is self-contained, validates the panel, avoids subplot grids, clusters by branch, and labels regressions as descriptive.

**Material flaws.**

1. **Arithmetic error presented as fact.** Never-treated rework, Apr–Jun vs Jul–Aug, is about +0.73 pp (Delta) and +1.03 pp (Epsilon), so ~+0.9 pp, not “+0.5 pp.” The residual vs Gamma is therefore about −2.1 pp, not the implied ~−1.7. Escalation “+0.2 pp” is also a slightly low rounding of ~+0.27.

2. **Uncontrolled 15% is smuggled into a causal-looking range.** Gamma’s raw −14.8% median change includes the same Jul–Aug easing seen in Delta/Epsilon. After the control comparison the incremental signal is closer to ~9% (−3.9 / ~43.7 h). Writing “near zero to roughly 9–15% for a Gamma-like branch” treats a contaminated pre/post as a plausible effect scenario.

3. **Identification strategy and code disagree.** Section 3 rejects TWFE under heterogeneous dynamic effects and nominates group-time ATTs. Section 9 then estimates the exact two-way FE spec that was criticized, plus a relative-time TWFE that omits leads −6, −5, −4, −3, and −1. Distant pre-period months are absorbed into the reference class with −1, so the “event study” is not a clean lead/lag design and is also collinear with month FE. No cohort-specific or stacked estimator is actually computed. The never-treated DiD residuals that carry Section 5 are not reproduced in the script.

4. **Weaker design than the setting allows.** With five branches and a shared fraud desk, randomizing only which of Delta/Epsilon goes first still yields one treated cluster and almost no power. A lead process-mining design should have embedded case-, day-, or team-level randomization of automation eligibility inside the next branch (to identify use vs availability) while still holding a branch-level control for the policy ITT and measuring desk spillovers. That option is absent.

5. **Process quantification is thinner than the data permit.** Arrivals/FTE (load) is never computed; Alpha’s implied load rises from ~31 to ~40 cases per productive FTE. Rework and escalation are piecewise-constant for months, which looks like a coarse or lagged measurement process, not a smoothly observed loop rate; that artifact is never flagged. Baseline `automated_fast_path_pct` of 4–6% under `automation=0` is unused.

**Smaller issues.** Escalation is given confidence 55 because the share rose, while the table is framed as an *effect* assessment—descriptive certainty and causal certainty are mixed. ITT-A bundles routing and local practice changes into the intervention even though the write-up elsewhere treats them as confounders/mediators. HET-D is underspecified. Guardrails (e.g., 10% p90 for two weeks) would likely have fired in May from the product/staffing shock alone; false-trip risk is not discussed.

**Bottom line.** Leadership would be steered away from a single “X% cycle-time” claim and toward a measured expansion, which is the right policy posture. The answer is strong relative to a typical write-up, but the rework miscalculation, the 9–15% range, the TWFE/event-study implementation gap, and the low-power branch-only experiment are enough that it is clearly not near-flawless.
