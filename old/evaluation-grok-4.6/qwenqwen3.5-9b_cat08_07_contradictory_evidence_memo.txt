The answer is structurally complete and lands on the right strategic call (E, not A or B), but it is not close to flawless. Several material evidence-handling errors and one clear misread of the decision set keep it well below a high score.

**What works.** The memo is decision-oriented rather than a recap. It refuses national scale without addressing tail, rework, and activation-order problems, and it does not treat the simple-house gains as disposable. The five metric-conflict explanations are mostly the right causal stories (easy-path speed vs. hard-path stall; ownership vs. single-point failure; visible vs. downstream cost). M6/M3/M4 as high-value measurements is a defensible VoI ranking. The final recommendation has the required slots: pause, scale, measure, threshold.

**Material errors.**

1. **29% is treated as a skip-rule failure rate.** In the memo and again in the evidence table, “the skip survey rule failed for 29% of apartments” / “segment-level failure rate (29%)” is wrong. 29% is the apartment share of the pilot, not a measured failure rate. That is a basic fact/denominator error in a memo whose whole case is segment-specific.

2. **Alternative C is misread.** C already suspends survey skipping and post-billing activation. The write-up claims C “does not address the flawed survey-skipping logic or activation timing.” That collapses C into a straw man and makes the A–E comparison unreliable. The live contrast is national coordinator-only (C) vs. segment-gated hybrid plus controls (E).

3. **Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are mixed**, which was an explicit hard constraint. Survey skipping is a design fact, not an interpretation. The 7%→15% manual-activation increase is a fact; “signal checks were unreliable” is the interpretation. Cost exclusion after week 4 is a data-coverage fact; “total cost impact is unknown” is the implication. Model precision 0.79→0.51 is a fact, but it is later used as if low precision *means* non-compliance.

4. **Process-mining concept slip.** High fitness + low precision means the discovered model replays the log well while remaining too permissive. Non-compliant sequences (billing before activation, missing permit checks) come from *alignments*, not from the precision score. The memo treats 0.51 precision as evidence of non-compliant paths. That is the wrong artifact.

**Other defects that should cost points under a strict rubric.**

- Median improvement is ~24% ((14.2−10.8)/14.2), not 27%.
- Memo date is October 2023 against a 2026 setting.
- A–E evaluations are thin: advantages are implied, and “conditions under which each would be reasonable” are mostly missing. A would be more reasonable if tails and first-time-right had moved with the median; B if simple-house gains were illusory or non-transferable; D if even the simple-house effect were confounded by mix/measurement.
- VoI does not say how each measurement would *change the decision*. M8 is undervalued: complaints already shifted from wait-time to “installed but not working” / “billed before activation,” so splitting CX is high-value for whether to keep speed-oriented pieces. M2 is dismissed because completion times exist; sparse start times are exactly why waiting vs. processing (failed visit → second visit, coordinator stalls) cannot be diagnosed. M1 is not merely an overlap with M6.
- Process-mining artifacts are only partly used. Temporal profiles, DFG edge shifts, permit-check move-on-model, field-survey utilization falling 78%→52%, second-visit utilization 66%→92%, and the centralized working-together network are barely synthesized. Business and rural segments are ignored.
- The rollout gate (p95 within 5% of 45 days and complaints &lt;7%) is arbitrarily tight on p95 and silent on first-time-right, SLA, billing-before-activation rate, and permit-check conformance.

Right recommendation, incomplete and sometimes inaccurate use of the evidence. Not a 7.
