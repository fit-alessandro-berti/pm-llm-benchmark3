The memo is strategically coherent and mostly well disciplined: it refuses national scale, refuses a blanket rollback, keeps facts/interpretations/assumptions separated in the table, uses the process-mining artifacts, and lands on a gated hybrid that matches the segment evidence. It is not near-flawless. Under a strict reading the defects below are enough to keep it well short of a high score.

**Repeated mis-aggregation of harm.** Apartments (29%) and old buildings (18%) are treated as “the 47% of volume where R is doing harm.” Business and rural cases also lost first-time-right, gained complaints, and lengthened p90. That is 66% of pilot volume, not 47%. The error appears in the executive memo, the metric-conflict section, and the closing trade-off, so it is not a slip. It makes the win/lose split look cleaner than the evidence is and overstates how much of the book is a proven beneficiary.

**Over-claim that coordinator ownership is innocent.** The memo states that ownership “is not the source of the harm” and that damage traces to “two separable design choices.” That is stronger than the evidence. Utilization at 93%, absence stalls, a centralized working-together network, and unclear post-billing ownership are ownership-model effects. Later sections quietly contradict the claim by imposing an 85% cap, backups, and M5. A decision memo cannot assert innocence in the recommendation and then treat ownership as an open design risk.

**Skip rule kept for simple houses without causal isolation.** Simple houses improved on every metric, but the prompt does not show that *survey skipping* caused that gain. Skipping was aimed at medium-risk addresses; simple-house gains are at least as consistent with merged validate/schedule and fewer handovers. Retaining skip for 34% of volume while calling skip “the documented driver” of the damage is an attribution error, and it is in tension with ranking M6 as the highest-value measurement.

**Value-of-information logic break on M6.** The memo says a poorly discriminating rule would “remove the main argument against broad rollout and make A-with-controls plausible sooner.” A failed rule strengthens the case against skipping; it does not dissolve the remaining objections (tail, complaints, billing-before-activation, permits, load). “A-with-controls” is also a confused label for C/E. The rest of the VoI ranking is sensible; this sentence is not.

**Softer but real problems.** “Cheap scheduled vs expensive unscheduled” and “EUR 207 is a lower bound” are interpretations stated as fact. High fitness is explained almost entirely as model permissiveness; the mining team also tied fitness to the common simplified path. “Forgoes roughly half of the headline speed gain” is unsupported arithmetic. Rural/business are both “paused” and given scaled coordinator ownership, so the action is ambiguous. Several gates (85%, EUR 190, 5% false-skip, 3,000 cases, precision 0.75) are invented without a sentence of why those cutoffs rather than nearby ones. An extra H1 sits above the required six top-level sections.

**What still works.** The evidence table is the strongest part: sixteen items, columns actually do different jobs, and data defects (mix, missing attributes, sparse starts, truncated cost) are carried as assumptions rather than laundered into facts. The five metric conflicts are explained as different questions, not as noise. Alternatives A–E have conditions, not just pros/cons, and A/B are correctly rejected for the reasons the prompt requires. Thresholds and a stopping rule make the recommendation decidable.

The piece reads like a strong executive product with a few load-bearing inaccuracies. Those inaccuracies are why it is a 7, not a 9.
