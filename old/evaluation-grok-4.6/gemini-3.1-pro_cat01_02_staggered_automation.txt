6.1

The memo is well organized and hits the required headings, tables, and motifs (staggered adoption, intensity versus availability, median versus tail, fraud-desk interference, small clusters). That architectural competence is real. It is not close to flawless. Under a strict reading, quantitative discipline, identification logic, and internal consistency fail in several places that matter for the assigned task.

**Quantitative errors and selective comparisons.** Beta’s Jan–Mar median is 45.9h, not 46.1h. Gamma’s Jan–Jun escalation average is 8.7%, not 8.2%. These are small, but they sit in the only explicit pre/post table. More serious is the April DiD narrative. The text treats Delta (+2.4h) and Epsilon (+1.9h) as “the” secular trend and concludes treated branches beat controls by 1.0–3.5h. Not-yet-treated Gamma moved +0.1h in the same month. Against Gamma, Alpha’s DiD is about −1.2h and Beta’s is about +0.8h. Omitting the one other untreated unit is cherry-picking, not robustness. Gamma’s July contrast is also window-dependent: June is the complexity-shock peak, so −7.5h versus a “3–4h” control drop overstates a break. Alpha’s Jan–Mar path (50.4, 46.9, 49.4) is not “remarkably flat,” so the regression-to-the-mean dismissal is overstated. The intensity analysis is asserted (“high-intensity branches absorbed the shock better”) without a reported coefficient, contrast, or even a two-bin table, so requirement 5.3 is only weakly met.

**Identification is described better than it is used.** Callaway–Sant’Anna / event-study language is appropriate, and the TWFE “forbidden comparisons” paragraph is correct. Then the primary design conditions on `arrivals`/`fte`. The April routing campaign sent extra volume *to adopters because they were adopters*. Arrivals are post-treatment and part of the policy bundle; adjusting for them is a bad control and can hide the operational contrast leadership actually cares about. Intensity DiD is written as if never-treated units have “zero dose”; the series is 4–6% everywhere pre-adoption. The code then fails to implement the proposed design: `relative_month` is built and unused; the “descriptive panel model” is pooled OLS of median on `automation` + complexity + workload, with no branch FE, no month FE, and no event-time dummies. That specification compares Alpha’s ~50h level to Delta’s ~35h level and is worse than the TWFE the prose rejects. Panel validation is `df.shape` only.

**Logical inconsistencies.** Section 2 marks tail, CSAT, intensity, and CATE as not identifiable (or only partial). Section 7 still assigns 75 confidence and a −1.5h to −4.0h interval to the median effect; section 10 upgrades this to “Conditionally causal: 1-to-3 hour reduction.” Those ranges do not match each other. Section 1 states as fact that aggregates “mathematically obscure the underlying bimodal distribution” and that “spillovers exist” via unrecorded fraud-desk waits; both are hypotheses, not observations. The intra-branch 50/50 RCT is sold as “bypassing the SUTVA issues inherent in branch-to-branch comparisons.” Shared specialist and fraud-desk queues produce within-branch interference; randomization of cases does not remove it. The later spillover-monitoring paragraph quietly admits this, which makes the earlier claim a contradiction rather than a nuance. An 8-week post window is too short relative to the same memo’s 4–5 month intensity ramp. The scale-up rule (`p < 0.05` and `>5%` median drop, “no statistically significant increase in rework”) treats non-significance as evidence of no harm and ignores the tail risk the memo itself flags.

**Narrower defects.** Rework and escalation are jammed into one estimand despite opposite signs and different mechanisms. Treatment-version / compound-treatment issues (availability + routing + local practice change + fraud-desk rules) are noted, then the ITT is still interpreted as “system availability.” Confidence 75/70 on median and rework is not disciplined given five clusters, endogenous selection, and a bundled April shock. The Epsilon-next recommendation is reasonable; the claim that case-level randomization “solves the small-cluster confounding problem” is loose.

The piece would be a solid 8 if the April control set included Gamma, the event-study were actually estimated, post-treatment workload were not treated as a clean covariate, the RCT-SUTVA argument were stated correctly, and the numeric claims lined up. Those are not nits. They are the core of estimands, identification, and quantitative discipline.
