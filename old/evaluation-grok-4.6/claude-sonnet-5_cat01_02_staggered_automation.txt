**5.3**

The causal framework in §§1–8 is substantively stronger than the score implies. The score is pulled down hard by missing required deliverables, sloppy quantitative presentation, and a few identification gaps that a lead process-mining scientist should not leave on the table.

## Completeness (disqualifying for a high score)

Two of ten required sections fail.

- **§10 is absent.** The claim ladder is not optional decoration. It is the epistemic summary the rest of the memo is supposed to collapse into. Omitting it leaves the reader without the mandated `Observed:` / `Suggestive:` / `Conditionally causal:` / `Not supported:` ranking.
- **§9 is a stub.** The code block ends after imports. None of the eight specified tasks are implemented: no embedded CSV, no validation, no trend output, no pre/post contrasts, no relative-time indicators, no panel or intensity model, no clustered SEs, no cluster/median warnings. A header and a causal disclaimer are not a reproducible analysis.

On a format-constrained evaluation, that alone caps the grade well below the quality of the prose.

## What is actually strong

§§1–4 and §6 are mostly the right memo. Availability is distinguished from `automated_fast_path_pct`; selection on historically weak, high-volume branches is treated as design, not a footnote; co-shocks (fraud desk, routing, FTE, May complexity) are named before any contrast. Estimands cover ITT-like availability, intensity, median, tail, rework/escalation, CSAT, and dynamics/heterogeneity. SUTVA and treatment-version problems (routing interference, fast vs escalated automated paths, shared fraud desk) are explicit. Rejecting naïve TWFE via Goodman–Bacon, with the Alpha/Beta dip-then-recovery as the concrete reason, is the correct instinct. Mechanisms are process-shaped rather than generic DAG labels. The threat table hits every required item and adds routing. The safe leadership claims in §7 correctly refuse “automation reduces cycle time by X%.” Choosing **expand with an embedded evaluation**, holding one never-treated arm, and asking for case-level path data plus fraud-desk waits is the right policy class.

Arithmetic on the pre/post means and the Alpha/Beta DiD components is largely correct where it can be reconstructed.

## Quantitative presentation is not disciplined enough

The §5 tables are hard to use as evidence.

Numbers are jammed without operators (`48.948.5`, `25%48%`, `40.637.2h`, `97113120110101`). Minus signs are dropped systematically on improvements, so the first table and most of the DiD table do not encode direction. Only escalation’s `+0.90pp` is unambiguously signed. A reader who uses the tables and skips the paragraph will reverse several results. For a memo whose job is quantitative discipline, that is not cosmetic.

Other small quantitative faults compound this: “p90 and escalation rise everywhere” is false for Gamma’s p90; Gamma DiD is left as em-dashes for rework, escalation, and CSAT; relative and absolute units are mixed in the p90 interval; Gamma’s rework change is slightly off.

## Identification and process interpretation: good structure, incomplete confrontation with the data

The primary design is conceptually right and practically oversold. Callaway–Sant’Anna / Sun–Abraham with cohort × event-time, month FE, and three covariates on 5 clusters × 8 months is not a credible estimator here. The memo notes “3 clusters” and “not point-identified” for E6, then still installs that estimator as the lead strategy without a degrees-of-freedom or saturation discussion. That is a real methodological miss.

A larger process miss: **the late-summer arrival drop is common, large, and timed exactly with the alleged learning recovery.** Arrivals fall in July–August in every branch; FTE and complexity are flat in that window. Alpha’s p90 path 119.9 → 109.6 → 101.2 is therefore not clean evidence of implementation fidelity. Delta and Epsilon also improve then. Gamma’s attractive pre/post window is almost entirely those shared recovery months, with May–June congestion sitting in the pre-period. DiD nets out some of this, but the mechanism table and the “matures / FTE-complexity stabilize” story do not. Load (`arrivals/fte`) is the obvious process covariate—Alpha peaks near 43 cases per FTE in June, the worst p90 month—and is never computed, only named later as a possible control.

Related gaps:

- Mean reversion is correctly flagged, then §7 still reports a signed median effect of “possible modest relative improvement” bounded at **−10% to 0%** with confidence 40. If selection on poor history can generate the entire favorable DiD, the interval should include a small harmful region, or the cell should say **not identified**. The bound is asymmetric in automation’s favor.
- `automated_fast_path_pct` is 4–6% when `automation=0` in every branch. That is a measurement or treatment-version issue, not a harmless “baseline.”
- Averaging branch-month medians is not a pooled median; this is mentioned as a future `print` warning and never used to discipline §5.
- Next-branch choice (Delta, hold Epsilon) maximizes a cleaner Gamma-like contrast, not the joint objective. Epsilon is the higher-complexity, worse-CSAT remaining site—the policy-relevant generalization. The memo does not treat that tradeoff.

## §8 is the right option with a circular decision rule

Stepped-wedge plus a possible within-branch contrast, six-month windows, event-log collection, p90/escalation guardrails, and keeping a never-treated arm are all sensible. The scale-up rule—“replicate the direction and rough magnitude seen in Alpha/Beta/Gamma”—treats those magnitudes as a target after the same memo argued they are not cleanly identified. Within-branch randomization by case type is proposed without noting that case types are not exchangeable.

## Bottom line

This is a strong causal-policy sketch that understands staggered adoption, intensity versus availability, tails versus medians, and queue spillovers. It is not a finished answer to the specification that was given. Missing §10, an empty §9, unreadable signed tables, an overparameterized “primary” estimator, and an under-analyzed common demand decline are enough, under a strict rubric, to keep it near the middle of the scale rather than in the 8–10 band a complete, tight version of the same argument might have reached.
