**7.4**

The memo is strategically competent and format-complete, but it is not nearly flawless. Under a strict reading it overstates causal certainty, misclassifies at least one finding, underuses several first-class process-mining artifacts, and leaves a material ambiguity in what is actually being scaled.

**What it gets right.** The recommendation correctly rejects national scale (A) and full rollback (B). It treats Redesign R as a bundle of interventions with heterogeneous effects, preserves the simple-house path, suspends the two most suspect design choices, and attaches explicit rollout gates. Facts, interpretations, and assumptions are mostly separated. All five required metric conflicts are explained. A–E are evaluated with conditions, not slogans. M1–M8 are ranked by decision value, not convenience, and the “good redesign / bad targeting vs. bad component” frame is the right one.

**Causal overclaim in the memo.** The executive section treats overall deterioration (p90 31.0→38.5, p95 45.0→62.0, FTR 82%→76%, complaints 6.1%→9.8%) as clean treatment effects—“harms too large and too concentrated to ignore,” “not just data quirks.” The same document knows the pilot mix is heavier in apartments and older buildings, that segment/risk attributes are missing before week 3 of a 6-week pilot, and that cost after week 4 is incomplete. Those caveats stay buried in the table. Calling simple-house gains “proven” and “commercially meaningful” is too strong: there is no segment NPS, total cost may be worse, and early-week labels are incomplete. A decision memo that will be judged on separation of fact from inference should have led with composition risk, not only listed it.

**Fact / interpretation errors.** Item 3 puts the complaint-mix shift (“installed but not working,” “billing before activation”) in the interpretation column as something customers “may be” experiencing. That shift is a stated organizational finding, not a conjecture. The same cell then says NPS “may be influenced by … incomplete post-week-4 cost coverage,” which is a category error: a cost-window gap does not affect NPS measurement. Item 12 treats “skip rule often wrong” as a fact; in the source it is a segment note, i.e. an interpretation that still needs M6.

**Omitted or underweighted evidence.** Generalization (0.70→0.56) is a listed model-quality metric and is ignored. SLA breach (13%→16%, better for simple houses) is a core conflict metric and is absent from the memo, the table, and the rollout thresholds. The survey-team collapse (78%→52%) against second-visit overload (66%→92%) is the cleanest “work was displaced, not removed” result and is barely used. Learning-curve / short-pilot effects (6 vs 10 weeks) are not raised as an assumption. These are not decorative details; they would have strengthened both the hybrid recommendation and the VOI case.

**Internal inconsistency.** The opening says suspend survey skipping and post-billing activation “for higher-risk segments,” which implies those practices can continue for simple houses. The later memo and Section 6 pause post-billing activation as a *standard* path (global) and survey skipping only outside validated low-risk strata. Management cannot tell from the first page whether delayed activation is allowed for simple houses. Given that billing-before-activation is a frequent alignment violation and a complaint driver, that ambiguity is material.

**Shallower than it looks.** Section 3 is accurate but thin: it never notes that the p95 deterioration (+17 days) swamps the median gain (−3.4 days), nor that low precision plus new DFG mass on exception edges is the same phenomenon as the tail. VOI ranking is sensible (M1/M6/M4/M7 first) but does not weigh cost or speed of information; M7 is both a measurement and a necessary control for the recommended hybrid, and ranking it fourth understates its immediate operational value. The 85% utilization gate is invented without justification.

**Bottom line.** This is a good consulting memo with the right decision and the right skeleton. It is not a 9+: it overreads the overall metrics, misfiles at least one fact, drops generalization/SLA/capacity displacement, and is not fully consistent about which design elements survive. Those are enough, under a strict standard, to keep it well below the top band.
