**What it gets right.** The mechanism map is the best part: pooling versus coordination, hub-and-spoke replacing local loops, Legal/Risk already at 91–93%, common-mode failure at the hub, informal advice disappearing from the log, and the observation that management’s own handover table does not show a real reduction in coordination load. **Material flaws.**

1. **The pilot cannot identify the treatment it claims to test.** A Technical Shared Service is a shared capacity pool. Randomizing or sampling cases inside North/South while “controls” remain in those teams violates SUTVA: once N_Tech/S_Tech/E_Tech are merged, control cases no longer have an embedded specialist, and pilot demand still congestes the same queue. A team-level stepped wedge (keep one region on the embedded model) would identify the merger; case-level randomization identifies a routing rule, not the reorganization. The answer names both designs and does not resolve the contradiction.

2. **The utilization claim is left half-closed.** The text flags that Legal/Risk cannot absorb a 15% utilization lift, but it never does the obvious arithmetic. Tech-pool utilization weighted across three specialists is roughly the mid-70s; ×1.15 is plausible. Legal 93% ×1.15 is infeasible. The interesting forecast is therefore not “queues may worsen” but that management’s headline benefit is physically undefined unless the 15% applies only to N/S/E tech *and* legal/risk/QA are kept off that pool. The pilot later separates Legal/Risk, which is the right move, but the mechanism and scenario sections still treat the 15% as a coherent organizational target.

3. **Scenarios mostly restated management’s table.** Optimistic ≈ estimates hold; central ≈ estimates plus some loops; adverse ≈ estimates break. There is little independent forecasting from the given volumes (820/760/520), current waits (3.2h local tech vs 9.6h legal), or implied occupancy. A stricter answer would have added the listed human handovers (about 251 → 267 per 100 cases) and said plainly that nominal simplification is already falsified by the proposal’s own numbers.

4. **The PM4Py sketch has analysis bugs, not just API looseness.** `queue_summary` groups by event-level `team`. After centralization that field will be the shared-service team, so North/South/East wait comparisons collapse. Origin team has to be case-level (`first` or a dedicated attribute). Several public `pm4py.*` signatures are guessed (`insert_case_service_waiting_time`, `get_service_time`, `extract_temporal_features_dataframe`, SNA kwargs). The comment about version drift is honest; it does not make the calls correct. `local_expertise_flag` and `handover_reason` are in the schema and almost unused—the two fields that would most directly test informal-advice loss and absence cover.

5. **Smaller but real sloppiness.** The mechanism table mixes actions with outcomes (“nominal handover reduction,” “loss of local expertise”). Optimistic “early warning signs” are just the absence of problems. Several “leading: weeks” items (role drift, deskilling, capability) are lagging. SLA in code is priority-only after the indicator text promised segment-appropriate SLAs. Resource-similarity under “standardization” is stated too flatly: specialists may converge while local staff diverge from technical work.
