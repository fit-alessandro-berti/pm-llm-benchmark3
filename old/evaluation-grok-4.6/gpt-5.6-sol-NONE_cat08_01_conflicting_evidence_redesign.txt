**7.8**

This is a strong, artifact-grounded redesign memo, not a generic Lean dump. It still falls well short of “nearly flawless.” Under a harsh reading the misses are small but real, and they compound.

**What holds up.** The diagnosis is the best part. It uses the frequency DFG, performance DFG, token replay (0.91 vs 68% fitting traces), alignment deviations, temporal profile, rework rates, segment outcomes, and resource constraints as *interacting* evidence, not a checklist. The central contradiction is stated correctly: the dominant `Intake → Auto → Fast approval → Dispatch` path is fast (medians 0.5 / 0.4 / 1.8 hours) and cheaper *before* failure cost (31% → 9%), but it externalizes defects (14.1% vs 3.3% reopen; 260 dispatch→complaint edges; p90 180 hours; €410 high-value mis-picks). The answer does not crown the slow path as universally better. Capacity math (7 × 36 = 252 hours; 82% inflection; 15–35% inflow risk; two fraud specialists) is used as a design constraint, which is the right move.

A / B / C are actually different mechanisms—hard segmented routing, evidence-completion architecture, and decoupling restored service from final settlement—not parameter tweaks of one idea. The table refuses fake ROI. Failure modes and the staged experiment (instrument → A → B → C, 150–180 hour complaint horizon, control groups) are specific.

**Process-mining reasoning is slightly sloppy.** Low *discovered-model precision* is treated as if it were the same thing as policy violation. Precision is model-vs-log permissiveness. Forbidden auto/risk/repair combinations in operations are primarily fitness/alignment issues against the normative net (and a discovery-overgeneralization issue only secondarily). “Hard routing reduces the discovered model’s low precision” is the same conflation in to-be language. Fitness 0.91 vs 68% fitting traces is used well; the precision point is not.

**Several given artifacts are underused or smoothed over.** Fast approval → Quality/compliance close (350) is ignored; a large share of “fast approvals” never dispatch, which matters for any replacement-gate design. Complaint in-edges (510) do not match complaint out-edges (430); the DFG is leaky, and a careful diagnosis would say so rather than treat edge counts as a closed system. The temporal-profile finding that `Manual eligibility → Repair quote` is *specifically too slow for Premium* is absorbed into generic queue commentary. Repair rework at 12.5% on high-value devices is diagnosed and then left without a real mechanism in any of the three redesigns (C’s “diagnostic cell” is a hope, and the answer itself admits diagnosis may not be the cause).

**Redesign A has specification holes.** “Clear mid-risk devices” land in the assured replacement lane with only a lightweight gate. Mid-risk is not costed in the prompt, and any mid-risk claim > €400 would violate R1 if it skips manual/fraud review. The two-week rule-update fact is stretched into “segment-aware auto rules can be deployed in two weeks.” Forcing every reopen through manual review before escalation implements R3 but loads the 7-reviewer pool and delays the one scarce CSAT mechanism (daily 45-minute board); that trade-off is not analyzed.

**Redesign C is the weakest evidential fit.** Loaners, card holds, advance exchange, and a centralized diagnostic cell are classic warranty operations, not inferences from this log. That can be a valid redesign, but the write-up does not bound inventory-loss, authorization, or inspector-skill assumptions tightly enough for the same evidence standard applied to A and B. It also quietly changes the KPI from cycle time to time-to-restored-service—legitimate, but the current-process diagnosis still frames success mostly in throughput-time terms, so the comparison is not fully commensurate until Section 5.

**The Pareto table is conceptually right and locally inconsistent.** Marking A as “No; conditional on capacity and classifier accuracy” is having it both ways. If dominance depends on those conditions, the cell is *conditional*, same as Current and C. Several cells also smuggle implementation commentary into effect columns.

**The experiment sequence has a real logical gap.** A is piloted first, which is justified by the two-week deploy and R1/R2 leakage. Go/stop gates protect Premium *accessories* (24-hour credit line) and manual utilization, but not Premium *high-value* experience. The diagnosis already established that moving those cases onto the reviewed path is 20 hours / 22% complaints versus 96 hours / 5%. Phase A can therefore “succeed” on compliance while inflicting the worst identified CX failure on the most sensitive high-value segment, and C—the only design that tries to resolve that contradiction—comes last. There is also no explicit guard against campaign weeks, when fraud/risk is already known to spike.

**Smaller nits.** Arrow formatting in A’s flow collapsed to whitespace. “First three transitions” is accurate only if one accepts Fast approval → Dispatch as part of eligibility speed; the approval itself is faster than fulfillment. Assisted channels in B are a good digital-literacy mitigation, but they reintroduce the labor the portal was meant to remove, and the resource story is thinner than A’s. Parallel part-price lookup is well targeted, then left unconnected to the three quote technicians’ actual capacity.

**Net.** Evidence use, trade-off handling, and non-generic redesign logic are clearly above competent. Process-mining conceptual precision, completeness of DFG reading, specification of mid-risk/R1, and experimental protection of the segment the diagnosis itself flagged as the hard case are not at the same level. That is why this is a high-7 answer, not a 9.
