**3.2**

The answer has the requested section skeleton and a few correctly copied statistics, but it is not a defensible process-mining redesign. It misreads core artifacts, proposes at least one conceptually broken flow, fails a required table column, and treats three “different” options as if they all improve speed, cost, quality, and experience at once. Under a strict rubric that is far from a passing professional advisory note.

**Diagnosis is a restatement, not a synthesis.** The contradiction is copied from the stem instead of being proven from the log. The decisive cost artifact—fast-approval cases 31% cheaper before rework, only 9% cheaper after complaints, reshipments, and credits—is never used. Frequency volumes (3,600 auto / 2,550 fast approval / 2,300 dispatch), token replay (fitness 0.91, 68% fitting traces), alignment move-on-log deviations, low model precision, the 26% repeated-evidence loop, and the segment table (Premium high-value 22% reopen on the fast path vs 5% on reviewed/repair) are almost entirely unused. Naming five numbers is not the same as using five process-mining artifacts.

**Factual errors in the diagnosis are disqualifying.** Rework-repair at 12.5% of *repaired high-value devices* is attributed to the fast path; that activity sits on Quality check → Rework repair, not Fast approval → Dispatch. Dispatch replacement → Quality/compliance close is reported as “mean 3.2, p90 14” and framed as a close-out delay; the source edge is mean 5.0 / median 3.2 / p90 14.0 and is one of the *faster* edges. The long-tail quality failure is Dispatch → Customer complaint/reopen (mean 96 / p90 180), which is ignored. Intake → Manual eligibility (mean 7.5 / p90 18) is described as “wait times of manual review,” which is the wrong interpretation of that performance edge.

**Normative rules are mishandled.** R1 is invoked as if any unnamed “risk assessment” satisfies “Manual eligibility *or* Fraud/risk review before Fast approval.” An automated screen almost certainly does not. R2 (no dispatch before evidence completeness, except Premium low-risk accessories) and R3 (reopened claims must pass Manual eligibility before close or denial) are never designed for. Complaint/reopen → Manual (310) vs Escalation (120) is invisible in every redesign.

**Redesign A is a thin relabel of the existing auto-screen.** Adding “Risk assessment” without saying whether it is a two-week rule update, a new human queue, or Fraud/risk review leaves the mechanism unspecified. The prompt already warns that rule updates can raise manual inflow 15–35%; that number is unused. Targeting Premium low-risk accessories—the segment with a 24-hour service-credit cliff and an explicit R2 exception—and then adding a latency step is the wrong segment choice. The flow omits evidence completion, denial, abandonment, replacement dispatch, and reopen handling.

**Redesign B is the largest design error.** Routing low-risk devices to “Direct Repair quote” collapses the dominant, cheap fulfillment mode (2,300 Fast approval → Dispatch replacement) into the slow repair commercial path. Low-risk accessories are not mid-life repairs; forcing quote → customer approval (mean 30h) → repair would destroy 8–13 hour accessory medians and Premium credit exposure. “Reduces unnecessary steps for low-risk” is the opposite of what the flow does. Mid-risk is invented without outcome data. High-value is nailed to Manual + Fraud + Repair with no remaining reviewed-replacement path, even though the escalation board already sends 65 cases to dispatch. The sacrifice line (“complexity for mid-risk”) is not a real trade-off.

**Redesign C is internally incoherent.** The happy path is “evidence in 24 hours → Repair quote,” while the quality gate is “before dispatch replacement.” Those are different fulfillment modes. A 24-hour evidence cutoff is unsupported and punitive for the 22% unclear-defect cohort (median 140h, highest abandonment). The portal’s 30–45% repeat-request benefit and literacy/abandonment risk are mentioned, which is the only well-grounded sacrifice in the memo. The 6–18 hour gate delay and the fact that two quality inspectors are underused except after repair campaigns—the actual resource design lever—are not used. A quality gate does not satisfy R1 or R3.

**The three options are not materially different in a usable sense.** A, B, and C all drift toward more review and/or more repair. None keeps a *segment-legal* fast replacement path, a *reviewed* replacement path for high-value, and a distinct treatment for unclear-evidence cases. That is the actual design space in the DFG.

**The Pareto table fails the spec and the economics.** The required `dominated?` column is missing. Ambiguous labels (“Quality/Rework Effect = High” on the current process) make the table unreadable. All three redesigns are shown as lower cycle time, lower cost, better experience—exactly the “we can have everything” claim the prompt forbids. Given the designs as written, A should add latency vs today’s fast path, B should worsen accessory cycle time and Premium credits, and C should add 6–18 hours plus abandonment risk. Resource-pressure and complexity columns are nearly identical and therefore non-informative. No row should be called a known Pareto winner from this log; the missing dominance column also means that caveat is never stated.

**Failure modes are circular and resource-blind.** “Increased complexity” restates the sacrifice; “may not address root causes” is generic. “High-risk Standard low-risk cases” is self-contradictory. B’s failure “may not address high-value compliance” contradicts B’s own mandatory Fraud/risk step. Bottleneck migration never uses the actual constraints: 7 reviewers and the 82% utilization cliff, 2 fraud specialists after campaigns, 3 quote technicians waiting on part-price confirmation, 2 inspectors, or the non-scalable 45-minute daily escalation slot.

**The experiment plan cannot identify real vs displaced improvement.** Piloting A and C on the same Standard low-risk slice at once confounds attribution. That slice is not the highest-risk or highest-complaint cell. Stop/go is three copies of “go if everything improves,” with no ranges despite usable prompt bounds (manual inflow +15–35%, complaint 14.1% vs 3.3%, gate +6–18h, portal abandonment). There is no holdout long enough for complaint tails (p90 150–180h after dispatch), no R1/R2/R3 replay check, no service-credit or wrong-replacement cost (EUR 95 vs 410), and no plan to see whether faster close simply moves failures into reopen (310 back to Manual). “Use statistical analysis” is not a learning design.

**What little is salvageable.** Section order is mostly right. A few statistics are quoted correctly (9.2% / 14.1% reopen, 82% reviewer threshold, portal abandonment risk). The memo does not invent a fake ROI. That is the level of a shallow first draft, not an executive redesign.

The grade is low because the errors are not cosmetic: they invert the fast-path quality problem, break the accessory fulfillment model, omit a required comparison field, and never resolve the speed–cost–compliance contradiction the sponsor actually faces.
