**6.2**

This is a well-structured, mostly competent fork-join analysis that still fails a strict reading on accuracy, internal consistency, and use of evidence that is sitting in the table.

## What holds up

The delay algebra is the right one. \(j_c=\max_i e_{c,i}\), \(FJ_c=\max_i L_{c,i}\), and \(T_c=U_c+FJ_c+D_c+F_c\) correctly avoid double-counting calendar time. The proof that \(\sum_i(Q_{c,i}+S_{c,i}+W_{c,i})=3\cdot FJ_c\) is explicit, and the F01 illustration (calendar sync window 22 minutes vs summed waits 27) is the right intuition. Ranking **B** over **A** despite A’s 50-minute service, calling out zero-benefit cases, and labeling Assumption 1 are all on target. Format compliance is strong; F02/F04/F07/F11 worked examples (except as noted below) check out.

## Material errors

**F01 is wrong, and the error is treated as a finding.** Upstream elapsed is \(08{:}10-07{:}55=15\) minutes, not 35. Total flow time is \(09{:}28-07{:}55=93\) minutes, not 113. The identity they advertise then fails for F01 unless one keeps both mistakes. They further write that upstream “averages about 21 minutes (35 minutes in F01, 20 minutes otherwise).” That is not a typo in a dense table; it is a fabricated exception. The Python block uses `07:55` and would print 15 and 93, so Section 2 and Section 6 contradict each other. A reproducible analysis that was actually run would have caught this.

**Downstream “queueing” is largely not identified as such, yet it drives a recommended redesign.** `final_resource` plus F intervals make occupancy recoverable. F1/F2 are idle during much of \(D_c\): F01 waits 8 minutes with no prior F work; F02 waits 15 minutes on a free F2; F03 waits 15 minutes after F1 has been free since 09:28; F06/F07/F12 similarly wait after the assigned resource is already free. Only some cases (F05, F08, F11, part of F04/F10) look like genuine resource contention. Treating every \(D_c\) as a queue, cutting it 50% as “added capacity,” and recommending more F staff is not supported by the same timestamps. Section 7 then claims final-station server count is unknown, which is false: the scenario and the log both give F1 and F2.

## Smaller but real competence gaps

- **Concurrency is unused.** Overlapping A intervals (and overlapping B intervals) are visible. A’s zero queue is the natural consequence of parallel capacity, not a mysterious scheduling virtue. B still queues despite concurrency. That is the process-mining explanation for “long service ≠ critical path,” and it is missing.
- **CF2 ties are not reported**, despite an explicit tie-break and a request to report ties honestly. After halving B’s queue, F02 is an A/B tie at 09:15; F06 is an A/B tie at 10:15. The write-up says B “remains critical” for F06, which violates their own A-then-B-then-C rule.
- **Masking is described, not tested.** With F1/F2 occupancy in hand, one can see which branch speedups would pile into a busy final resource and which would not. The quantitative CFs keep \(D_c\) fixed and wave remaining interactions into “secondary effects.”
- **Section 3 over-claims.** “Every case experiencing some final-station queueing” is an assumption, later walked back in Section 7. Observations and hypotheses are mixed.

## What this is not

It is not a weak or confused answer. Definitions, max-join logic, non-critical-branch inertia, and the three counterfactual *shapes* are what the task asked for. It is also not nearly flawless: the primary case table is already inconsistent with the data and with the accompanying code, and the second recommended redesign rests on a delay type the log can already show is often idle time, not congestion.

Those are not cosmetic issues. Under a strict standard they cap the grade well below the top of the scale.
