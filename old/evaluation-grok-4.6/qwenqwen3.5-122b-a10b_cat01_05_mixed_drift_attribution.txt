**5.6**

The high-level story is the right one: March is mostly semantic/provenance drift, later months are real process and performance change, supplier disruption tracks rework, staffing and the priority rule sit on the queue, October is seasonal mix, and December is mixed load-plus-intervention. Format compliance is also mostly there. That is not enough for a high score under a strict rubric, because the quantitative core is unreliable, several claims are unsupported, and specified PM artifacts are not actually used.

**Quantitative failures (largest penalty).** Section 3 advertises a Jan–Feb baseline, then mixes January, February, Jan–Feb averages, and November-to-December month-on-month changes in the same table. December `cases` is reported as **−12.7%**; versus baseline it is an *increase*. The same column’s complexity, rework, and cycle-time figures are MoM declines, not baseline-relative deteriorations. March median cycle time is given as **+1.0%**; versus January it is a small *decrease* (31.5 → 31.2). Mapped-fitness May change is wrong. The stated Euclidean DFG distance does not reproduce the reported 0.05 / 0.18 / 0.22 under either the 7-edge vector in the prose or the 4-edge vector in the code (actual distances are ~1.7 / ~19 / ~29, or ~0.02 / ~0.19 / ~0.29 if scaled). Cycle-time shares of 30/35/10/25% are point estimates with no period, no arithmetic, and no ranges, which violates “do not manufacture precision.” Inventing a 2026-03-15 logging change point is unsupported.

**Factual and categorical errors.** Section 2 calls later mapped fitness “low.” It is not: mapped fitness stays 0.86–0.91. That sentence undermines the one distinction the section exists to make. October is filed as *behavioral* drift in the change-point list; it is population/seasonal (with a possible logging component for `Senior Assessment`). July’s “observed” rise in standard-case wait time is explicitly *not* visible in the monthly aggregates. “General rework rise in non-Z cases” is used as evidence against the supplier hypothesis; that split is not in the data.

**Causal restraint is uneven.** “Purely” semantic, “proving” behavioral drift, and “strong causal linkage” overstate what aggregates can carry. Supplier Z was already more complex; priority and staffing are concurrent; December mixes season and intervention. The answer sometimes says this and sometimes forgets it. The historically inconsistent `Senior Assessment` label is almost unused, even though October’s 4 → 22 rate is exactly where logging-consistency and mix are confounded.

**Process-mining / spec misses.** Part A never computes raw-to-mapped *recovery* (`mapped − raw` or equivalent). Part B ignores the required `pm4py.format_dataframe` and `pm4py.filter_time_range`, substitutes a brittle month loop, and calls a non-standard `discover_declare_constraints`. Event-ordering is absent from the monitor set. Monitor 2 (`raw − mapped > 0.1`) would alarm every month after March. Rule 7 (`mapped < 0.80`) never fires on the given series. Recurrence vs new drift is mentioned as YoY, but there is no prior year and no explicit seasonal baseline.

**What remains solid.** Timeline structure, the multiple-change-point argument, the supplier–rework and staffing–queue links, priority-as-redistribution, the discrimination/segmentation tables, and most of §9’s mediation discussion are competent and usable. They keep the score out of the failure range. They do not offset broken arithmetic in the required quantitative section or the specification misses in the PM4Py plan.
