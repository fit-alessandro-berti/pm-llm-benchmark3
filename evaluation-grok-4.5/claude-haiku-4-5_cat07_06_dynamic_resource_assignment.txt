# Evaluation

The submission is long, complete in structure, and rhetorically strong (especially §5 and much of §6–7). Under strict grading it still fails on core correctness: the policy math is inverted in places, several recommended assignments violate stated constraints, and the worked queue is not a faithful output of the stated algorithm.

## Severe issues (major deductions)

### 1. Assignment scoring is directionally wrong (§2)
Composite score **adds** `score_continuity` and `score_workload`, but both are built as **penalties**:

- Non-owners get `score_continuity = reassign_penalty + handover_penalty` (higher when handover is costly / reassignments already high). Adding this **prefers breaking continuity** on the worst cases—the opposite of the comment and of constraint 5.
- `score_workload = open_case_ratio penalty + time penalty + overtime penalty`. Adding it **prefers overloaded / high-OT resources**.

`urgency_weight * sla_urgency` is constant across resources, so it does not rank anyone. Resilience term is weak/ambiguous. A policy whose scalarization systematically favors overload and costly handovers is not a small implementation nit; it undermines §2 as a usable algorithm.

## Bottom line

Completeness and prose quality are high-school-to-professional; **technical core is not**. An explainable dynamic policy whose published score **rewards** overload and expensive handovers, paired with a queue that repeatedly assigns **non-urgent** work to **high OT-risk** staff and barely meets training/resilience goals, cannot score in the upper band under hypercritical rules. Fix the sign of continuity/workload terms, make urgency modulate weights only, enforce OT and max-load as hard filters before scoring, re-solve C003/C010/C013/C014/C011/C016 with R10/R6/R3/R8 capacity, and align §3 to a dry-run of §2—then the same structure could approach 8+.

**Grade: 5.5 / 10.0**
