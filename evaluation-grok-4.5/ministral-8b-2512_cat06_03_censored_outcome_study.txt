**4.0**

The submitted design shows basic awareness of selective observation (activation) and avoids the worst failure modes (e.g., it does not casually relabel rejected/withdrawn/open cases as non-default, drops several post-outcome fields, and attempts a temporal split). However, under hypercritical review it contains numerous factual errors, internal inconsistencies, incomplete rules, statistically weak choices, and a non-functional implementation skeleton. These accumulate into a substantially flawed answer that would not support a credible study.

**Section 1.**  
Primary target (default | activation) is defensible and correctly refuses to treat non-activation as non-default. However, the stated population (“all 48 000”) immediately contradicts the unit/estimand (“activated accounts only”). Prediction time (“first event after KYC or earliest”) is underspecified, not guaranteed to be early, and not expressed as a true as-of timestamp. Horizon and estimand language mix conditional probability with the original business request for “every application.” The alternative multi-state sketch is only a one-sentence gesture; competing-risk assumptions and the fact that default is undefined without activation are acknowledged but not developed. Overall the section is conceptually pointed in the right direction yet imprecise and internally inconsistent.

**Section 2.**  
The cohort-flow table is simply wrong on its face: it lists “Activated (labeled) 18 900 Default=0/2 700” instead of the given 21 600 (18 900 + 2 700). The subsequent rows therefore cannot sum to 48 000 cleanly, and the status column mixes counts with garbled label text. Inclusion/exclusion/as-of rules are only bullet-like statements, not explicit predicates on event timestamps, activation time, maturity windows, or extract date. Competing risks, selective unobservability of transfers, and “not-yet-mature” activated accounts are named but never turned into operational filters. A correct cohort table that accounts for every one of the 48 000 cases with mutually exclusive, timestamp-aware labels is absent.

**Section 3.**  
Ordinary right-censoring, delayed labels, activation selection, and permanent missingness are listed—an improvement over naïve complete-case analysis. The primary strategy (survival on activated cases only) matches the chosen target. However, the sensitivity analysis (impute transferred cases with the global 12.5 % rate, or with the linked-transfer rate) is crude, ignores the 40 % unlinked transfers and partner-originated permanent missingness, and does not examine the policy-change interaction or informative censoring. Testability claims are superficial; the fundamental untestability of counterfactual default under rejection is stated but not linked to any concrete bounding or principal-stratum approach. No clear distinction is drawn between administrative censoring at extract date versus label-arrival delay of 7–35 days.

**Section 4.**  
Fixed-count (first 5 after KYC) and elapsed-time (7 days) prefixes are mentioned, yet both cut-offs are arbitrary and unmotivated by the process. Short-case handling (“pad with nulls”) and document-loop handling are vague; activity renaming after the October 2025 migration is ignored (“use concept:name as-is”), guaranteeing broken variants. The embedded code fragment uses deprecated importers, hard-codes a meaningless cutoff, applies an incorrect filter, and calls `get_prefixes_from_log` with non-existent arguments. No guarantee is given that post-prediction events or same-day document uploads are excluded before feature construction.

**Section 5.**  
A few sensible process-aware ideas appear (document loops, KYC-to-review time, policy-period flag). Most others are either ill-defined (queue length at event time requires a full historical reference set that is never constructed), leaky (employee experience is known to be recorded at extract time, yet is treated as if it were event-time), or non-operational (`path_variants` via `get_variants` on prefixes is not shown). Static / prefix-derived / historical-reference distinctions are named but not enforced. No feature is given an explicit “available-at” timestamp check.

**Section 6.**  
The leakage table covers the required columns and correctly drops `final_limit`, `decision_reason`, `collection_status` and future events—the strongest part of the answer. Nevertheless several rows are incomplete or incorrect: `documents_total` “repair” is only a verbal hope; updated `risk_score` and resource experience are flagged yet the repair logic is never implemented; `label_available_at` is kept without an as-of join. No audit is performed for `source_system`, partner-origin flags, or the nightly snapshot semantics. The table therefore lists risks without demonstrating that they have been removed from the eventual feature matrix.

**Section 7.**  
A chronological split around the two known change points is a good instinct and correctly rejects random case splits. However the concrete windows ignore label maturity: many applications started in the proposed test period (Nov–Dec 2025) cannot have a matured 90-day outcome plus feed delay by the 15 Feb 2026 extract date, so the test set is heavily right-censored or empty of labels. No rolling-origin or walk-forward scheme, no explicit maturity filter, and no calibration or subgroup analysis stratified by the policy and migration regimes are supplied. Metrics (Integrated Brier, AUC, calibration) are appropriate to survival yet are listed without reference to how censoring indicators or competing risks will be passed to the scorer.

**Section 8.**  
The can/cannot-claim paragraph correctly separates predictive association among activated accounts from counterfactual default risk—an essential distinction. The three deployment-shift examples are generic and do not engage the concrete mechanisms already present in the data (policy threshold change, activity renaming, partner feed failure, transfer re-identification). The boundary language remains high-level rather than operational (e.g., no statement of the form “scores may be used only for applications that reach Credit Review under the pre-July 2025 policy and only while the model is refreshed before the next threshold change”).

**Section 9.**  
The skeleton is the weakest component. It is far shorter than the requested 80–160 lines of coherent pipeline, contains multiple hard errors, and fails almost every concrete requirement:
- Deprecated or non-existent PM4Py import paths and function signatures.
- `build_cohort` never computes activation time, terminal state, maturity date, or proper censoring indicators from the event log.
- Prefix extraction hard-codes a migration date, filters incorrectly, and does not call `pm4py.get_prefixes_from_log` with a length or time argument that matches the earlier design.
- Feature code treats a list of events as a DataFrame, computes `any(...)>1` (type error), and pulls non-existent experience values.
- No temporal train/validation/test split by case start time is implemented (only a comment).
- No leakage-audit assertion that actually inspects the feature table for forbidden columns.
- No cohort-count table that reconciles to 48 000.
- Survival/competing-risk modeling is mentioned only in a final comment; no data structures (start/stop times, event indicators, competing-risk labels) are prepared.
- Several required demonstrations (`format_dataframe`, proper prefix generation, DFG/variants/temporal features used only when justified, prevention of post-prefix columns) are either missing or broken.

Collectively these defects—incorrect cohort arithmetic, contradictory target/population statements, immature test-set construction, naïve imputation, ignored migration renaming, and a non-executable skeleton—make the design unsuitable for the stated evaluation criteria (statistical design, process-aware cohort construction, leakage prevention, honest target semantics, and PM4Py implementation quality). A score above 4 is unwarranted under the mandated strict standard.
